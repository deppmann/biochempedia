    contribution: >-
      Studied families with alkaptonuria, a condition in which urine darkens on standing, and
      noticed that affected people were often siblings with unaffected parents who were first
      cousins. Read with William Bateson as a rare recessive Mendelian trait, it became one of the
      earliest human conditions interpreted in Mendel's terms.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Garrod, 'The incidence of alkaptonuria: a study in chemical individuality', The Lancet (1902); Garrod, Croonian Lectures, 'Inborn errors of metabolism', The Lancet (1908)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Garrod was a London physician interested in the chemistry of urine. Alkaptonuria gave him a
      condition he could measure: affected people excrete homogentisic acid, which turns the
      urine dark when it stands. In 1902, two years after Mendel's work was rediscovered, he
      published in The Lancet an analysis of the families he could trace. Affected people tended
      to cluster among brothers and sisters, their parents were usually unaffected, and an
      unusually large share of those parents were first cousins. William Bateson saw what this
      meant in Mendel's terms: a rare recessive allele is most likely to meet a second copy when
      the two parents share an ancestor. Garrod went further and proposed that alkaptonuria
      reflects one blocked step in the body's chemistry, an idea he later called an inborn error
      of metabolism. In 1996, nearly a century later, the gene for the missing enzyme,
      homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase, was cloned. Garrod's pedigrees, read with Bateson, gave early evidence that
      human traits follow the same rules as peas, and that a gene can be read through the reaction it controls.

    story: >-
      Huntington was a young physician whose father and grandfather had practised medicine on the
      eastern end of Long Island, New York, and had seen the same families over decades. His short
      paper of 1872, titled simply "On chorea", set out the features of a hereditary form of the

      changes in mind and behaviour. He also described how it passed down families. It appeared

      allele, written down before Mendel's rules were known to medicine. In 1983 the laboratory

      laboratories found the cause: an expanded run of CAG repeats in the gene now called HTT.
      The families used for that mapping, including a large Venezuelan kindred, were not the ones he described, but the disorder carries his name.

techniques:
  - name: "Pedigree construction with standardized nomenclature"
    blurb: >-
      A family history is drawn with agreed symbols for sex or gender, affection status,
      relationships, deceased members and the proband. A shared code lets any reader test an
      inheritance model against the same chart.
  - name: "Conditional and Bayesian risk calculation"
    blurb: >-
      Genotype probabilities are enumerated under a stated model, then conditioned on what is
      observed, such as an unaffected phenotype or a set of unaffected children. The result is a
      probability for an untested person under those assumptions.
  - name: "Linkage analysis with DNA markers"
    blurb: >-
      A variable DNA marker is typed in as many family members as possible and tested for co-inheritance with the
      disease. A marker that travels with the disease far more often than chance places the gene
      near it, as for Huntington disease on chromosome 4.
  - name: "Deep sequencing of several tissues"
    blurb: >-
      A variant is sequenced to very high depth in blood, saliva, sperm or other tissues of each
      parent. The fraction of reads carrying it estimates the level of mosaicism or heteroplasmy in each tissue, which a single
      blood test can miss.

    choices:
      - "Autosomal dominant"
      - "Mitochondrial"
      - "X-linked dominant"
      - "X-linked recessive"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      A father gives his X to every daughter and his Y to every son, so a fully penetrant
      X-linked dominant allele in an affected father reaches all daughters and no sons. Autosomal
      dominant would give each child a 1/2 chance regardless of sex, so six children split exactly
      by sex is unlikely under it. Mitochondrial inheritance passes through mothers, so an
      affected father should have no affected children through it. X-linked recessive would leave
      the daughters as unaffected carriers unless the mother also carried the allele, and then about half of the sons would be affected too.
  - stem: "Which single observation, under full penetrance and no new mutation, rules out X-linked recessive inheritance for a trait?"
    choices:
      - "An affected man's son is affected, and the son's mother carries no copy"
      - "An affected woman's sons are all affected, and her daughters are not"
      - "Two unaffected parents have an affected son and an unaffected daughter"
      - "Affected men in two generations are linked through unaffected women"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      A son receives his father's Y chromosome and his mother's X, so an X-linked allele cannot
      pass from father to son; an affected son whose mother has no copy rules the mode out. An
      affected woman under the simple model carries the allele on both X chromosomes and gives an affected X to every son, while
      her daughters receive a normal X from an unaffected father, so that pattern fits. Two
      unaffected parents with an affected son is the classic case of a carrier mother. Affected
      men linked through unaffected women describes passage through carrier daughters, the usual
      X-linked recessive pattern.
  - stem: "A man's sister has an autosomal recessive disorder. Both parents and the man are unaffected. Assuming full penetrance and no new mutation, what is the probability that he is a carrier?"
    choices:
      - "1/2"
      - "1/4"
      - "3/4"
      - "2/3"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      His affected sister shows that both parents are carriers, so before his phenotype is known
      his genotype is AA, Aa, aA or aa, each 1/4. He is unaffected, which removes aa and leaves
      three equal outcomes, two of them carriers: 2/3. The value 1/2 is the carrier chance before
      conditioning on his being unaffected. The value 1/4 is the chance of an affected child of two
      carriers. The value 3/4 is the chance of being unaffected, not of being a carrier.
  - stem: "An autosomal dominant allele shows 80% penetrance: 80% of heterozygotes develop the condition. A heterozygous parent and a parent without the allele have a child. Assuming no other cause of the condition, what is the probability that the child is affected?"
    choices:
      - "0.20"
      - "0.50"
      - "0.40"
      - "0.80"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The child inherits the allele with probability 1/2 and, having it, shows the condition with
      probability 0.8, so the product is 0.5 x 0.8 = 0.40. The value 0.50 is the chance of
      inheriting the allele and ignores penetrance. The value 0.80 is the penetrance alone and
      ignores the chance of not inheriting the allele. The value 0.20 is the chance that a
      heterozygote stays unaffected.

  - stem: "A woman's mother is a known carrier of an X-linked recessive allele that is fully penetrant in males, and her father is unaffected. The woman has not been tested. She has three sons, and none is affected. Using her prior and this evidence, what is the probability that she is a carrier?"
    choices:
      - "1/9"
      - "1/8"
      - "1/5"
      - "1/2"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Her prior is 1/2. If she is a carrier, each son is unaffected with probability 1/2, so three
      unaffected sons have likelihood 1/8; if she is not, the likelihood is 1. The posterior is
      (1/2 x 1/8) / (1/2 x 1/8 + 1/2 x 1) = (1/16) / (9/16) = 1/9. The value 1/8 is the likelihood
      alone, without the prior or the comparison. The value 1/5 is the answer for two unaffected
      sons, not three. The value 1/2 ignores the sons altogether.

Human geneticists cannot set up crosses, so they work backwards from existing families. A **pedigree** is a family tree drawn to a shared code.

> **The one idea.** Fitting predictions support a model; one contradiction can rule it out.
>
> *Scope:* the five simple, single-gene models used here, assuming full **penetrance** (everyone with the genotype shows the trait), no new mutation and correct family relationships. A model that fits is supported, not proved.

## Read the evidence first

The first film used three symbols; the full code has more. In the 2022 revision of the National Society of Genetic Counselors standard, a square is a man or boy, a circle is a woman or girl, and a diamond is a nonbinary person or someone whose sex or gender is not specified. The revision ties shape to gender and records sex assigned at birth separately. A filled symbol means affected by the condition named in the key; a diagonal slash marks someone who has died.

A horizontal line joins partners. A vertical line drops to a horizontal sibship line, from which each child hangs. A double partner line marks **consanguinity**: the partners are related by descent, as first cousins are. Roman numerals label generations, and people are numbered left to right, so II-3 identifies one person. An arrow marks the **proband**, through whom the family came to attention.

A **carrier** is heterozygous for a recessive allele and usually does not show the trait. Many textbook charts put a central dot in the circle of a woman known to carry an X-linked recessive allele. The 2022 revision shows carrier status differently; check the key.

**Predict.** Two partners are joined by a double line. Can you conclude that both carry the allele being studied?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The line records shared ancestry, not genotype. Consanguinity raises the chance that both carry the same rare allele but does not establish carrier status or prove recessive inheritance.

</details>

## Test five inheritance models

| Model | Prediction under the simple model | A contradiction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Autosomal dominant** | One copy in a heterozygote is enough. The trait usually appears in successive generations and both sexes; father-to-son transmission is possible. An affected heterozygote and unaffected partner give each child a 1/2 allele risk. | An affected child has two unaffected parents. |
| **Autosomal recessive** | Affected people have two pathogenic alleles at one locus, identical or different. Unaffected carrier parents can have affected children of either sex, so cases often cluster among siblings, with few affected relatives elsewhere. Shared ancestry is more common because relatives are likelier to carry the same rare allele. | Two affected parents have an unaffected child, if the same gene causes both parents' trait. |
| **X-linked recessive** | A male has one X, so one copy can affect him. Affected men are much more common than affected women. An affected man passes the allele through carrier daughters to half their sons. Heterozygous women can show signs, partly through X-inactivation. | Demonstrated father-to-son transmission. An affected father-son pair establishes this only if the mother carries no copy. |
| **X-linked dominant** | An affected father passes the allele to every daughter and no son. An affected heterozygous mother passes it to half her children of either sex. | An affected father has an unaffected daughter or transmits the allele to a son. |
| **Mitochondrial** | Human mitochondrial DNA normally comes from the mother. An affected mother can have affected children of both sexes; an affected father transmits the trait to none. | Transmission from an affected father. |

Recessive deafness shows why that row's qualification matters: different genes can cause deafness, so two deaf parents can have a hearing child. Mitochondrial DNA encodes only a handful of respiratory-chain subunits (see [oxidative phosphorylation](/lessons/oxidative-phosphorylation/)); most mitochondrial proteins are nuclear-encoded, so many mitochondrial diseases instead follow Mendelian patterns.

**Predict.** An affected father has an unaffected daughter. Under the scope above, which of the five models does that one observation rule out?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only X-linked dominant, because every daughter receives her father's X. The other four models all allow an unaffected daughter.

</details>

Do not use “skips a generation” as proof of recessive inheritance; reduced penetrance can make a dominant allele appear to skip. Rett syndrome and fragile X syndrome, introduced in [synapse disorders](/lessons/synapse-disorders/), are X-linked examples complicated by new mutations and repeat expansion.

Once a model survives those tests, the next question is how strongly each possible genotype is supported.

## Conditional probability: the 2/3 rule

The second film reweighted schematic tiles; now put exact numbers on them. A woman's brother has cystic fibrosis, an autosomal recessive disorder caused by two pathogenic *CFTR* alleles. Her parents and she are unaffected. Her partner has no family history. What is the probability that their first child is affected?

Within the scope above, assume a partner carrier frequency of 1/25, a figure often quoted for northern European ancestry and lower in many populations; it is an exercise assumption, not a universal number.

Her affected brother shows that both parents are carriers, Aa. Before her phenotype is used, Aa x Aa gives four equally likely outcomes: AA, Aa (A from the mother), aA (A from the father) and aa, each 1/4.

**Predict.** She is unaffected. Which outcome disappears, and what fraction of the remaining outcomes are carriers?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The affected aa outcome disappears. Of the three equal outcomes left, Aa and aA are carriers:

> P(carrier | unaffected) = (1/2) / (1/4 + 1/2) = (1/2) / (3/4) = 2/3

</details>

If both partners are carriers (her partner with probability 1/25), both pass the recessive allele with probability 1/4. Therefore

> P(affected child) = 2/3 x 1/25 x 1/4 = 2/300 = 1/150

As a decimal check, 1/150 is about 0.0067, and 0.667 x 0.04 x 0.25 = 0.0067. The tempting 1/2 was her carrier probability before her unaffected phenotype removed one outcome. These are logic exercises, not risk figures for a real family.

## Updating with evidence: Bayes and unaffected sons

Here evidence reweights the hypotheses instead of deleting one, so Bayes' rule from the second film applies. A woman's mother is a known carrier of a fully penetrant X-linked recessive allele, and her father is unaffected. The untested woman has two unaffected sons.

Her unaffected father gave her a normal X and her mother one of two X chromosomes, so her prior is 1/2 carrier and 1/2 not carrier.

**Predict.** Are two unaffected sons more likely if she is a carrier or if she is not? Should her carrier probability rise or fall?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

They are more likely if she is not a carrier, so her carrier probability falls. If she is a carrier, each son is unaffected with probability 1/2, so the likelihood for two is 1/4. If she is not a carrier, the likelihood is 1.

</details>

Multiply each **prior** (the weight before the evidence) by its **likelihood** (the chance of the evidence under that hypothesis): carrier gives 1/2 x 1/4 = 1/8; not carrier gives 1/2 x 1 = 1/2. Normalize the two weights:

> P(carrier | two unaffected sons) = (1/8) / (1/8 + 1/2) = 1/5

Prior odds of 1 : 1 become posterior odds of 1 : 4, because the evidence is four times likelier if she is not a carrier. Under the same model, her next son's affected risk is 1/5 x 1/2 = 1/10.

## Why real pedigrees mislead

One contradiction rules a model out only inside the scope, and real families break the scope in four ways.

- **Genotype may not predict one visible phenotype.** Huntington disease is dominant, but symptoms usually begin in adulthood, so a young unaffected child of an affected parent may carry the allele. Penetrance also depends on CAG repeat length in *HTT*: about 36 to 39 repeats is usually described as reduced penetrance, and 40 or more as full penetrance. **Variable expressivity** means the same genotype produces different clinical pictures.
- **A variant may be new or mosaic.** A new pathogenic variant can produce a dominant disorder in a child of unaffected parents, which looks exactly like the observation that rules out autosomal dominant inheritance. In **germline mosaicism**, a parent carries a variant in only some cells, including some sperm or eggs. In a study of 58 families with one affected child and an apparently new variant, deep sequencing of several parental tissues estimated recurrence below 0.1% in 35 families; 5 had parental mosaicism associated with higher risk, and three fathers had the variant in 5.6 to 12.1% of sperm DNA.
- **Mitochondrial genotype can be a mixture.** With **heteroplasmy**, a cell contains normal and mutant mitochondrial DNA. A mother can transmit different proportions to different children, so severity varies and some children may be unaffected.
- **A family may be too small to reveal the pattern.**

**Predict.** If two carrier parents have only two children, is seeing no affected child surprising enough to reject autosomal recessive inheritance?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Each child has a 3/4 chance to be unaffected, so the chance that both are unaffected is (3/4)^2 = 9/16, more than half the time. Small families can look as though nothing is inherited.

</details>

Apparent exceptions also need molecular checks: among 11,035 trios, all seven apparent paternal mitochondrial signals were explained by a mitochondrial-derived nuclear insert shared by father and child.

### Design problem

An affected woman has five children, all affected. Her affected son has three children, none affected. Her affected daughter has two children, both affected. Severity varies. Decide between mitochondrial and X-linked dominant inheritance, and identify data that would settle it.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Test the affected son.** He transmits no mitochondrial DNA, so three unaffected children fit mitochondrial inheritance. Under fully penetrant X-linked dominant inheritance every daughter receives his affected X, so they exclude that model only if at least one is a daughter.

**The rest does not discriminate.** Both models fit an affected mother with affected children. A mother transmits mitochondria to every child; under X-linked dominant inheritance, five affected children of a heterozygous mother has probability (1/2)^5 = 1/32: possible but unlikely.

**Variable severity fits both models, so it cannot decide between them.** Under heteroplasmy children receive different mutant fractions; X-inactivation in women can vary an X-linked dominant phenotype.

**Settle it molecularly.** An affected father with an affected daughter whose mother lies outside the maternal line and lacks the family's mitochondrial variant would exclude mitochondrial inheritance. Or sequence affected and unaffected relatives, seek a mitochondrial variant confined to the maternal line, and measure heteroplasmy in several tissues. Heteroplasmy tracking severity, with the variant absent from the son's children, would support the mitochondrial model.

</details>
