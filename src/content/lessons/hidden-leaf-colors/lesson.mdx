## The big idea

In fall, green leaves turn yellow, orange and red, as if the tree paints them. Your child compares leaf prints and uses a sample coffee-filter strip to see that yellow was already inside a green leaf. They practice making a guess, looking closely and checking what they see.

> **Say it together:** "Green leaves are packed with a green color called chlorophyll. Yellow and orange colors hide underneath all summer. In fall, the green fades and the hidden yellow shows."

Green leaves hold **chlorophyll**, a green pigment (a colored substance) plants use to catch light energy and make food. Leaves also hold yellow and orange pigments called **carotenoids**, hidden under the green all summer.

When days grow shorter and cooler in fall, the tree stops making chlorophyll and breaks down what is left. The green fades and the yellow shows. Focus on that green-and-yellow reveal first. Later, add a second twist: most reds and purples are made new in fall from sugars trapped in the leaf, not hidden all summer.

<SafetyNote>
- **Outdoors:** An adult goes with your child. Gather fallen leaves only where you have permission and only from plants you know. Stay away from roads. Dress for sun and weather. Avoid poison ivy, which can turn red or yellow in fall. Check for ticks afterward.
- **Never taste:** Keep leaves, plants, berries and mushrooms out of mouths, including the spinach. Wash the spoon and everyone’s hands with soap when you finish.
- **Choking hazard for children under 4:** Keep small leaves and leaf pieces away from them. Call 911 if choking.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Predict.** Show your child a fresh green leaf and a yellow fall leaf. Ask, "Which colors do you think will rub off onto the paper?" Have them draw or write a guess.
2. **Make leaf prints.** Fold white paper over a green leaf. Have your child rub the leaf with the back of a spoon until a green print shows through. Repeat with a yellow and a red fall leaf. Compare the prints and check the guess.
3. **Sort and count.** Sort 5 to 10 fallen leaves into piles by color, then count the leaves in each pile.
4. **Compare with the sample strip.** Point to the worked observation below. It shows a wide green band and a thin yellow band from fresh green spinach. Ask, "Where do you see yellow on the strip and on the fall leaf?" Then ask, "If that yellow was inside the green leaf all summer, why couldn’t we see it?" Read the **Say it together** lines again.

### Original chromatography procedure

1. **Predict first.** Hold a fresh green leaf beside a yellow fall leaf. Say, "Let's guess before we test." Ask, "Do you think there is any yellow hiding inside this green leaf?" Have your child draw or write a guess.
2. **Cut the strips.** Your child uses child-safe scissors to cut 2 or 3 coffee-filter strips, about 2 cm wide and 12 to 15 cm long. Draw a pencil line 2 cm from the bottom of each strip.
3. **Tear the leaves.** Your child tears the spinach into fingernail-sized bits onto a plate. You hold the glass jar and add the bits, about 2 to 3 cm deep. Ask, "What color do you see right now?"
4. **Add the alcohol (adult).** Put on safety glasses and take the jar to your work spot, out of reach. Pour enough alcohol to cover the leaves, about 1/4 cup. Mash firmly but gently with the metal spoon for 2 to 3 minutes so the glass does not crack. Watch the liquid turn dark green. Cap the jar and let it stand 15 to 30 minutes, mashing once or twice more. Darker liquid gives clearer bands.
5. **Hang the strip (adult).** Push the leaf bits aside with the spoon. Tape the top of a strip to the pencil and lay the pencil across the jar mouth. Roll the strip so only its tip dips into the liquid, with the pencil line just above the surface and the paper off the jar walls. Cover loosely with foil.
6. **Wait, predict and check.** The liquid climbs over 30 to 90 minutes. Keep the jar on its tray while your child looks without touching. Check every 15 minutes. Before each peek, ask, "Which color do you think will climb highest?" Look for liquid creeping past the pencil line. Expect a wide green band. You may see a thin yellow or yellow-orange band above it or along its top edge.
7. **Dry, compare and debrief (adult).** Lift out the strip and lay it on a plate to dry for a few minutes, away from flames. Cap the jar and clean up as the safety notes say. Tape the dry strip beside a yellow fall leaf. Close with two quick questions:
   - Ask, "Where do you see the same yellow on your strip and on the fall leaf?"
   - Ask, "If that yellow was inside the green leaf all summer, why couldn't we see it?"
   Read the **Say it together** lines again.

## Why it works

Paper chromatography separates a mixture as liquid climbs through paper. Each pigment dissolves in the alcohol and sticks to the paper fibers to a different degree. Pigments that stick less travel farther, so the colors spread into bands. Coffee-filter results vary, and the bands are often fuzzy rather than sharp.

Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light and drives photosynthesis, making sugar from carbon dioxide and water. Carotenoids help catch light and protect chlorophyll from damage. As nights grow longer in fall, the leaf begins to shut down. The tree stops making new chlorophyll, breaks down the old chlorophyll and pulls useful parts back into the branches. Some carotenoids last longer, so yellows show through. Later, a layer of cells at the base of the leaf stalk lets the leaf fall.

Red **anthocyanins** form in fall in some trees, such as red maples and dogwoods, when sugars build up in the leaf. Sunny days and cool nights help. Scientists think these pigments shield the leaf while the tree pulls nutrients back, though this is still studied.

## For older children (ages 8 to 10)

Compare prints from a fresh green leaf and a fall leaf from the same tree. Record the colors side by side, then use the sample strip to discuss why the fall leaf may show less green.

In the original extension, the adult set up two glass jars at the same time, with green leaves and fall leaves from the same tree. Both jars used the same amounts of leaf and alcohol, about 1/2 cup of alcohol in all. The adult handled the glass and alcohol and labeled, stored and disposed of both jars as described above. Children measured how far each band climbed in cm, recorded the results in a table and explained what the fall leaf had lost.

### Reading the original demonstration results

If the strip showed only a green smear, the liquid may have been too weak. The original method used more leaves, less alcohol and more mashing to correct this. If the color washed to the top in one blur, the strip sat too deep. The original method dipped only the tip. The original guide notes that 91% alcohol may give clearer bands than 70% and catches fire even more easily. Fall leaves give pale results, so the original method used several.
