## The big idea

In fall, your child sees green leaves turn yellow, orange and red. It looks as if the tree is painting them. This activity shows that much of the color was there all along.

Green leaves are packed with **chlorophyll**, a green pigment (a colored substance) that plants use to catch light energy and make their own food. Leaves also hold yellow and orange pigments called **carotenoids**. They are there all summer, but there is so much green that it hides them.

In fall, shorter days and cooler weather signal the tree to stop making chlorophyll and break down what is left. The green fades and the yellows show through. Reds and purples work differently. Most of them are made new in the fall from sugars trapped in the leaf, so they were not hiding all summer.

Grade 2 links leaf color to seasonal change. Grade 4 asks children to know that plants use chlorophyll and sunlight to make food.

<SafetyNote>
- The adult handles the rubbing alcohol from start to finish and stays in the room while the jar is out. Children aged 5 to 7 do the leaf-rubbing version only. Children aged 8 to 10 tear leaves, cut strips and look at the jar, but the adult does all the pouring, mashing and moving of it.
- Rubbing alcohol catches fire easily. Work far from stoves, candles, gas heaters, fireplaces and anything that sparks, in a room with an open window or a fan. Never heat the jar or set it in warm water.
- Rubbing alcohol is poisonous to swallow, for children and for pets. Keep the bottle capped and out of reach, and label the jar "rubbing alcohol, do not drink." While the strip stands in it, keep the jar covered with foil on a tray out of reach, with the adult beside it; your child looks but does not touch. Wear safety glasses or goggles while you pour and mash. Mash gently so the glass does not crack; if the jar breaks, the adult cleans it up.
- If a child is choking or struggling to breathe, call 911. If anyone swallows alcohol or gets it in their eyes, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 right away, and rinse eyes with running water while you call. Call 911 if the person is hard to wake or struggling to breathe. For a pet, call your vet.
- When you finish, keep the leftover liquid and leaf bits together in the capped, labeled jar, out of reach, and ask your local household hazardous waste program (your county or city solid waste office) how to dispose of it. Wash the spoon and your hands with soap. The spinach used here is not for eating.
- Use child-safe scissors for cutting strips. Collect fallen leaves only from plants you know, never from poison ivy, which can turn red or yellow in fall.

## What to do

1. **Predict.** Show your child a fresh green leaf and a yellow fall leaf. Ask, "Do you think there is any yellow hiding inside the green leaf?" Have them draw or write a guess.
2. **Cut the strips.** Your child cuts 2 or 3 strips from coffee filters, about 2 cm wide and 12 to 15 cm long. Draw a pencil line across each strip, 2 cm from the bottom.
3. **Tear the leaves.** Your child tears the spinach into bits about the size of a fingernail and drops them in the jar. Fill the jar about 2 to 3 cm deep with leaf bits.
4. **Add the alcohol (adult).** Take the jar to your work spot, away from your child's reach. Pour in just enough alcohol to cover the leaves, about 1/4 cup. Mash firmly but gently with the spoon for 2 to 3 minutes, so the glass does not crack. The liquid should turn dark green. Cap the jar and let it stand for 15 to 30 minutes, mashing once or twice more. Darker liquid gives clearer colors.
5. **Stand the strip (adult).** Push the leaf bits to one side with the spoon. Tape the top of a strip to the pencil and lay the pencil across the jar mouth. Roll the strip around the pencil until only its tip dips into the liquid, with the pencil line just above the surface. Keep the strip off the sides of the jar. Cover the jar loosely with foil.
6. **Wait and watch.** The liquid climbs the strip over 30 to 90 minutes. Check every 15 minutes, with the jar staying put on its tray and your child looking, not touching. Before each check, ask, "Which color do you think will climb highest?" Expect a wide green band. Above it, or along its top edge, you will often see a thin yellow or yellow-orange band.
7. **Dry and compare.** Lift out the strip and lay it on a plate to dry for a few minutes. Then cap the jar and clean up as the safety notes say. Tape the dry strip next to a yellow fall leaf. Ask, "Where do you see the same yellow?"

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "What colors do you think are hiding in this green leaf?"
- "I wonder which color will climb the paper fastest."
- "What do you think would happen if we used a yellow fall leaf instead?"
- "If the yellow was there all summer, why couldn't we see it?"
- "What do you think a red leaf would show on the strip?"
- "Why do you think the tree stops making green in the fall?"

## Why it works

Paper chromatography separates a mixture by letting a liquid carry it up through paper. Each pigment dissolves in the alcohol and also sticks to the paper fibers to a different degree. Pigments that stick less travel farther, so the colors spread into bands. Results with a coffee filter vary, and the bands are often fuzzy rather than sharp.

Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light and drives photosynthesis, making sugar from carbon dioxide and water. Carotenoids help catch light and protect chlorophyll from damage. In fall, as nights lengthen, a layer of cells forms at the base of the leaf stalk. The tree stops making new chlorophyll, and the old chlorophyll breaks down. Some carotenoids last longer, so yellows appear.

Red **anthocyanins** form in fall in some trees, such as red maples and dogwoods, when sugars build up in the leaf. Sunny days and cool nights help. Scientists think they shield the leaf while the tree pulls nutrients back, though this is still studied.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Skip the alcohol. Fold a sheet of white paper over a fresh green leaf and rub hard with the back of a spoon until a green print soaks through. Do the same with a yellow and a red fall leaf. Compare the prints. Then sort 5 to 10 fallen leaves into piles by color and count each pile.

### Ages 8 to 10
Run a fair test with one change. The adult still handles both jars and all the alcohol, as in the main version. Make two jars at the same time, one with green leaves and one with fall leaves from the same tree, using the same amount of leaf and alcohol. Measure in cm how far each color band climbs. Record the results in a table, then explain what the fall leaf has lost.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If you see only a green smear, the liquid was probably too weak. Use more leaves and less alcohol, and mash longer. If all the color washes to the top in one blur, the strip sat too deep; keep only the tip in the liquid. If you have 91% alcohol, try it; it may give clearer bands than 70%. Fall leaves give pale results, so use several.
