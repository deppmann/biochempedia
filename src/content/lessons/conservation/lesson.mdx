techniques:
  - name: "Population viability analysis"
    blurb: >-
      A stochastic model of a population's births, deaths, variation between years and
      catastrophes is run many times from a given starting size. The fraction of runs that go
      extinct, or fall below a threshold, within a chosen horizon estimates the risk, and
      re-running under different management scenarios compares the options.
  - name: "Retrospective predictive validation"
    blurb: >-
      A model is fitted to the first part of a long time series and its forecasts are compared
      with the later observations it never saw. It tests whether a method's predictions track
      reality, within the kinds of populations and time spans that have long records.
  - name: "Genetic and demographic monitoring after translocation"
    blurb: >-
      Released animals and their descendants are typed with genetic markers to measure
      heterozygosity and ancestry, while counts, survival and recruitment are tracked. Comparing
      admixed with non-admixed individuals, and the period after release with the period
      before, tests whether a genetic rescue changed fitness.
  - name: "Randomized landscape experiment"
    blurb: >-
      Habitat patches of equal area are created, and which ones are joined by corridors is
      assigned at random. Repeated censuses of which species occupy each patch measure
      colonization and local extinction, so differences can be attributed to connection rather
      than to patch size or site.

**In 1995, managers released eight female pumas from Texas into southern Florida, where a small, inbred population of Florida panthers still lived. By 2010 panther numbers had about tripled, and heterozygosity, a measure of genetic variation, had doubled.**

Was that a success, and how would you know? The first film's rule is the yardstick.

> **The one idea.** A population's **trend** is its average yearly change in numbers on a log scale. If the trend is zero or below, the population reaches the **floor** (zero, or a number too low to recover from) in the long run, and size only buys time; a trend above zero makes lasting possible, though not certain. How soon, and how likely within a given time, depends on the trend, the size and the **swings** (the ups and downs from year to year) together. So conservation starts by removing the cause of decline. Alongside it, numbers, connections and genes can lift the trend or cut the risk.
>
> *Scope, stated once:* this holds in a simple model of one population whose log numbers wander with a steady trend and steady swings, with no immigration and no return from the floor. Real analyses add crowding, Allee effects, catastrophes and immigration, so the rule sorts causes; it is not a law.

Graeme Caughley argued in 1994 that most populations are small because something, such as habitat loss or overharvest, drove them down, so finding and removing that cause usually comes first. The risks of small numbers may still need managing at the same time.

## Why small populations are fragile

Mark Shaffer (BioScience, 1981) sorted the chance events that threaten small populations into four kinds. They interact, and several act on both the trend and the swings.

**Demographic stochasticity** is chance in individual fates: which animals happen to survive, and how many offspring, of which sex, each produces. Suppose a population's last breeding female has five young. The chance that all five are male is (1/2)^5 = 1/32, about 3%. In a population of thousands such runs of luck average out.

**Environmental stochasticity** is variation from year to year in weather, food and the populations of competitors, predators and disease that a population depends on.

**Predict.** A drought year hits a population of 10 and a population of 10,000. Does the larger one escape the drought the way it escapes an unlucky run of all-male litters?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. A drought hits every individual at once, so it does not average out with size the way demographic chance does. Size gives a bigger cushion, not a smaller shock.

</details>

**Catastrophes**, such as fires, hurricanes and epidemics, are the extreme swings. The fourth kind is genetic. In a small population **genetic drift** (chance change in allele frequencies) erodes variation, the raw material for adaptation, and mating among relatives raises **inbreeding**, which exposes harmful recessive alleles and feeds the first film's vortex. Drift's arithmetic belongs to [population genetics](/lessons/population-genetics/) (rate of loss in Going deeper).

Michael Gilpin and Michael Soulé named the first film's loop an **extinction vortex** in 1986. Some species also show an **Allee effect**: at low density, individuals find mates or defend against predators less well, so the trend itself falls just when numbers are lowest. The rates that drive these models come from [life tables](/lessons/demography/) and [population growth](/lessons/population-growth/).

## MVP and PVA: risk as a probability

How many individuals are enough? Shaffer's answer: none, until you choose a standard. He defined a **minimum viable population** (MVP) as the smallest isolated population, in a given habitat, with a chosen probability of persisting for a chosen time. As a tentative example he suggested a 99% chance of persisting for 1,000 years. Change the probability, the horizon or the habitat, and the MVP changes.

A **population viability analysis** (PVA) estimates that probability as the film did, by running a model many times, but with a real species' complications: survival and birth rates by age or stage, their variation between years, **density dependence** (rates that change with crowding), catastrophes and, sometimes, inbreeding. Like a forecaster's "30% chance of rain", it reports the fraction of runs that go extinct, or fall below a chosen threshold, within the horizon.

Are such forecasts right? In 2000, Barry Brook and colleagues fitted PVA models to the first half of each of 21 long-term ecological data sets and predicted the second half. Predicted risks of decline matched observed outcomes closely, with no significant bias. That supports PVA within populations and spans like those tested, not any single forecast. PVA is most often recommended for comparing management options rather than for its absolute numbers, and even rankings need checking (Going deeper).

**Worked problem, part A.** For one hypothetical species in one habitat (invented numbers), a PVA is run 1,000 times from each of three starting sizes, over 100 years. Extinction means zero individuals by year 100. Assume the model's vital rates (survival and birth rates) and their variation are correct and the habitat stays as it is.

| Starting size N0 | Runs extinct | Extinction probability |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 20 | 120 | 120 / 1,000 = 12.0% |
| 50 | 37 | 37 / 1,000 = 3.7% |
| 100 | 8 | 8 / 1,000 = 0.8% |

Probabilities are extinct runs divided by total runs. Suppose managers choose at most 5% extinction risk in 100 years. N0 = 20 fails. N0 = 50 passes, with persistence 1 - 0.037 = 96.3%. So 50 is the smallest *tested* size that meets the target. Sizes between 20 and 50 were never run, so the true threshold could lie anywhere in that range.

**Predict.** 3.7% is below the 5% target, but only 1,000 runs stand behind it. Would you sign off on 50 animals?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only narrowly: 1,000 runs are too few to be sure. A fraction counted from runs has sampling error, the **Monte Carlo error**. Its standard error is the square root of (0.037 x 0.963 / 1,000), about 0.006, so a rough 95% range (two standard errors either side) is 2.5% to 4.9%. The upper end sits just under 5%, so more runs would be needed to be confident.

</details>

Problem A held the vital rates fixed. If inbreeding keeps accumulating, survival or fertility can fall, lowering the trend; a PVA must model that or say it leaves it out. The second film follows a well-known **genetic rescue**: adding animals from another population to lift fitness that inbreeding has lowered.

## Census size and effective size

The second film left open how far a head count overstates genetic size, how firm the causal reading is, and whether one cat is typical.

The **census size** N is the number of individuals you count. The **effective size** Ne is the size of an idealised population, with equal sexes, random mating and family sizes that vary only by chance, that would lose genetic variation at the same rate. Ne is usually smaller than N, because uneven sex ratios, unequal numbers of offspring and swings in population size all speed drift: if a few males father most young, fewer parents pass on genes than the count suggests. For an unequal sex ratio alone, with Nm breeding males and Nf breeding females:

> **Ne = 4 Nm Nf / (Nm + Nf)**

**Worked problem, part B.** Suppose 50 adults breed: 10 males and 40 females. Assume discrete generations and that sex ratio is the only departure from the ideal.

**Predict.** Fifty animals breed. Is the population's effective size 50?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Ne = 4 x 10 x 40 / (10 + 40) = 1,600 / 50 = **32**, so Ne/N = 32 / 50 = **0.64**.

</details>

*Convert a genetic guideline.* Frankham, Bradshaw and Brook (2014) recommended Ne of at least 100 to keep the loss of total fitness to 10% or less over five generations, and at least 1,000 for long-term evolutionary potential. At Ne/N = 0.64, Ne = 100 needs about 156 adults (rounded up to whole animals in Going deeper).

### How firm is the panther reading?

Both of the second film's comparisons come from one population with no replicated control, and habitat, prey and road deaths changed alongside ancestry. The associations between admixture and fitness are strong, but how much to credit to genes rather than habitat or road deaths remains inference.

### Is one cat typical?

Genetic rescue does not rest on the panther alone: a 2015 meta-analysis found that outcrossing inbred populations improved fitness in 92.9% of 156 cases judged to carry a low risk of **outbreeding depression** (reduced fitness after crosses between genetically different populations, sometimes appearing only in the grandchildren of the cross).

## Habitat tools: changing the trend and the swings

Removing the cause of decline often means protecting or restoring habitat, though for some populations it means curbing harvest, invasive species, disease or pollution; diagnose the driver first. Protection and restoration aim to raise survival, births and the number of animals the land can hold; corridors allow immigration, recolonisation and gene flow. Each can lift the trend or cut the risk, depending on species and landscape.

**Protected areas.** Across terrestrial sites worldwide, Gray and colleagues (2016) found sample richness (species recorded per sample) 10.6% higher and abundance 14.5% higher inside protected areas than outside. Most of that advantage was explained by land-use differences between the sites, and rarefied richness (species counts adjusted for sample size) and endemism (a measure weighted toward species with small ranges) did not differ significantly. The comparison is observational, since reserves are not placed at random; matching protected sites with similar unprotected ones gives a cleaner estimate. In Costa Rica, Andam and colleagues (2008) found that about 10% of protected forest would otherwise have been cleared, and that simpler comparisons overstated that benefit by more than 65%.

**Corridors.** At the Savannah River Site in South Carolina, in restored longleaf pine savanna, Damschen and colleagues followed plant species for 18 years in patches of equal area, some joined by corridors. Connected patches gained species faster, lost them more slowly and ended with more species. The study was replicated in ten experimental landscapes, but at one site in one ecosystem, so it does not establish how corridors work everywhere. The prediction comes from [island biogeography](/lessons/island-biogeography/) and [spatial populations](/lessons/spatial-populations/).

**Restoration.** The Society for Ecological Restoration defines it as assisting the recovery of a degraded, damaged or destroyed ecosystem. In southern Costa Rica, Karen Holl and colleagues compared natural regeneration, applied nucleation (planting tree islands) and plantations at 13 sites. After about a decade, planting raised recruitment (new young trees establishing) of animal-dispersed trees, yet tree-recruit composition in every treatment remained distinct from reference forest (intact forest used for comparison).

Not on average. Action in general still beats doing nothing: a 2024 meta-analysis of 665 trials with no-action comparisons found that it improved biodiversity or slowed its decline in about two-thirds of cases.

## Decisions with competing goals

Evidence says what an option is likely to do, not which outcome matters most. For endangered sockeye salmon of Cultus Lake in British Columbia, Gregory and Long (2009) described a structured decision process that set out biological, social and economic objectives, the alternatives and their predicted consequences, so that the fishery agencies, First Nations and commercial fishers involved could see the tradeoffs explicitly. Climate change shifts all of these options ([global change](/lessons/global-change/)), and the final lesson, the [evidence workshop](/lessons/ecoevo-evidence-workshop/), practises weighing such studies together.

### Design problem

A small, isolated mammal population shows signs of inbreeding (heart defects, low sperm quality, poor survival of young), and its habitat is still shrinking. Managers propose bringing in animals from another population of the species. Starting from the panther teams' two comparisons, what would you add so the result says more than one population can?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Keep the panther design (a baseline of counts, survival, recruitment, heterozygosity and defect rates, then ancestry for every animal you can type), and record habitat loss, prey and road deaths alongside ancestry, to weigh their effects. If several isolated populations need help, release into some sooner than others, so the later ones serve as a comparison. State whatever limit remains.

Weigh outbreeding depression and disease carried by the newcomers. If the habitat keeps shrinking, a genetic gain may be temporary.

### The rule as a formula

The trend averages change in *log* numbers, so it never exceeds the average proportional change and can sit well below it when swings are large: a year that halves numbers and one that adds half average no proportional change, but a log trend of about -0.14.

For log numbers moving as a smooth random walk (a diffusion) with steady trend and swings and an absorbing floor, the chance of ever reaching the floor is 1 if the trend is zero or below. Above zero, a standard formula gives it in the smooth model. For yearly steps, as in the films, it is only an approximation, to be checked by simulation.

### Drift and the loss of variation

In the ideal model, expected heterozygosity falls each generation by 1/(2Ne) of what remains. In Problem B, at Ne = 32, that is about 1.6% per generation, roughly 15% over 10 generations.

### Effective size and the genetic guidelines in numbers

At Ne/N = 0.64, Ne = 100 needs 100 / 0.64 = 156.25 adults. With whole animals at one male to four females, 32 males and 128 females (160 adults) give Ne = 4 x 32 x 128 / 160 = 102.4, which clears 100. Frankham and colleagues give meta-analytic averages of Ne/N of about 0.1 to 0.2, which would put Ne = 100 at roughly 500 to 1,000 adults. A standardised median MVP of 4,169 individuals across 212 species (Traill and colleagues, 2007) summarises a literature and is not a target for any species.

### The corridor experiment in numbers

At the Savannah River Site, Damschen and colleagues followed 239 plant species for 18 years. Annual colonization rates were 5% higher and local extinction rates 2% lower in connected patches, and connected patches ended with about 200 species against 176. Unconnected outer patches were of two kinds: rectangular, or with dead-end side strips ("wings").
