      the Weizmann Institute, Galit Lahav built human cell lines in which p53 carried a cyan

    story: >-
      Kazutoshi Mori, working in the Dallas laboratory of Mary-Jane Gething and Joseph Sambrook,

      reticulum turned blue. He then searched for mutants that stayed white. In San Francisco,
      Peter Walter's students Jeffery Cox and Caroline Shamu ran their own screen for yeast that

      Mori shared a Lasker Award in 2014. Their pathway is now studied wherever cells secrete
      heavily, from antibody-making cells to the insulin-making cells of the pancreas.

    story: >-
      Paul Talalay's laboratory at Johns Hopkins ranked plant extracts by how strongly they
      raised a set of protective enzymes in cultured cells. In 1992 that assay led Yuesheng
      Zhang and Talalay to sulforaphane, a compound from broccoli. The assay could not say which
      protein the compound touched. In Tsukuba, Ken Itoh in Masayuki Yamamoto's laboratory
      showed in 1997 that mice lacking the transcription factor Nrf2 largely failed to induce those
      enzymes, and in 1999 reported Keap1, a protein that keeps Nrf2 in the cytoplasm. The two
      groups then worked together. Papers in 2002 and 2004 showed that inducers react with
      particular cysteines on Keap1, whose sulfur atoms give up electrons easily, and that an
      altered Keap1 no longer restrains Nrf2. Later mice from Yamamoto's laboratory, carrying
      Keap1 with single cysteines replaced, showed that different inducers need different
      cysteines and that sulforaphane needs cysteine 151. The result was a sensor that detects
      a whole chemical class by reacting with it, and a target for drugs that raise a cell's
      own defenses.

techniques:
  - name: "Live single-cell fluorescence imaging"
    blurb: >-
      Fuse the protein you care about to a fluorescent protein, keep the cells alive on a
      microscope, and photograph the same cells again and again for hours. Brightness tracks
      the amount of protein in each cell, so you see timing that an average over millions of
      cells hides.
  - name: "Reporter gene screen in yeast"
    blurb: >-
      Join the control sequence of a stress-responsive gene to a gene whose product gives a
      color, mutate the yeast at random, and look for colonies that fail to change color under
      stress. Each such colony carries a mutation in a gene the pathway needs. Screens of this
      kind tied IRE1 to the unfolded protein response.
  - name: "Photoaffinity labeling"
    blurb: >-
      Make a version of a drug that carries a radioactive atom and a group that light converts
      into a reactive one. Let it bind, flash it with light so that it bonds covalently to the
      protein it is sitting in, and purify the labeled protein. This names the target of a
      compound whose effect is known but whose partner is not.
  - name: "Cysteine substitution"
    blurb: >-
      Replace one cysteine at a time with serine or alanine, which cannot react in the same
      way, and test whether the protein still senses its trigger. If the response to one
      compound is lost and the response to another survives, that cysteine is the site the
      first compound needs.

    choices:

      - "The p53 gene stays silent until the DNA of the cell is damaged"
      - "Keap1 holds p53 in the cytoplasm, far from the genes it controls"
      - "MDM2 tags p53 with ubiquitin and the proteasome destroys it"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      p53 is made continuously, and MDM2, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, marks it for the proteasome,
      so each copy is short-lived. A silent p53 gene is the wrong picture, because the speed of
      the response depends on p53 already being made. Keap1 restrains Nrf2, a different
      responder, and does not handle p53. Phosphates added after DNA damage help free p53 from
      MDM2, and p53 does not need them in order to fold.
  - stem: "In the standard pathway, what must happen to p38 to switch it fully on?"
    choices:
      - "An inhibitor bound to it must be tagged with ubiquitin and destroyed"
      - "A single serine in its tail must receive a phosphate from ATM"
      - "A threonine and a tyrosine in it must each receive a phosphate"
      - "A GTP-bound Ras protein must bind it at the plasma membrane"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      p38, like ERK and JNK, is switched on in the standard pathway when a MAP2K phosphorylates both a threonine
      and a tyrosine that sit one residue apart. Binding of GTP-bound Ras is how Raf, at the top
      of the growth-factor cascade, is recruited, and it is not how a MAP kinase is switched on.
      Destruction of a bound inhibitor is how NF-κB is freed from IκB. ATM acts on p53 and MDM2
      after DNA damage and is not the kinase directly above p38.
  - stem: "IRE1 sits in the ER membrane and reports a backlog of unfolded protein. What does its activated cytoplasmic end do?"
    choices:
      - "It adds ubiquitin to an inhibitor that holds a transcription factor"
      - "It cuts a segment out of a messenger RNA in the cytoplasm"
      - "It is cut off in the Golgi and then travels to the nucleus itself"
      - "It phosphorylates a transcription factor that then enters the nucleus"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Clustered IRE1 switches on an RNA-cutting activity and removes a segment from HAC1
      message in yeast or XBP1 message in animals; a ligase joins the ends, and only the spliced
      message gives an active transcription factor. Phosphorylating a transcription factor was
      the first guess, made because IRE1 looks like a kinase, and it turned out to be wrong.
      Being cut in the Golgi and sending a piece to the nucleus describes ATF6. Adding ubiquitin
      to an inhibitor describes how NF-κB is released, and IRE1 is not a ubiquitin ligase.

      - "Nrf2 target genes are induced about as strongly as in normal mice"

    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Sulforaphane needs cysteine 151: in cells taken from mice carrying serine at that position,

  - stem: "A blot made from ten million irradiated cells shows p53 rising once and then fading over a day. What does this tell you about p53 in any one of those cells?"

      - "It rose once and fell once, in step with all of the other cells"
      - "It rose only in the few cells whose DNA had actually been broken"
      - "Little, because out-of-step pulses give the same average"
      - "It stayed at one steady level that was set by the radiation dose"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      An average over millions of cells looks the same whether every cell rises and falls

      cell did. One synchronized rise and fall is possible but is not shown by the blot, and

    rationale: >-
      p53 that cannot bind DNA cannot activate the MDM2 gene, which accounts for the low MDM2

  - stem: "Cells are given cycloheximide, which stops all new protein synthesis, and Nrf2 is then measured on blots at intervals. In untreated cells Nrf2 falls from 100 units to 25 units in 40 minutes. In cells exposed to sulforaphane beforehand, Nrf2 falls from 400 units to 200 units in 40 minutes. What do these data show?"
    choices:
      - "Half-life rose from 10 to 40 minutes, so destruction stopped"
      - "Half-life rose from 20 to 40 minutes, so destruction slowed"
      - "Half-life fell from 40 to 20 minutes, so destruction sped up"
      - "Half-life stayed at 40 minutes, so only synthesis increased"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Falling from 100 to 25 is two halvings in 40 minutes, a half-life of 20 minutes. Falling
      from 400 to 200 is one halving in 40 minutes, a half-life of 40 minutes. A longer
      half-life means slower destruction, which fits a Keap1 that, as part of the ubiquitin ligase, no longer gets

      cells as one halving when there were two. A fall from 40 to 20 minutes swaps the two
      conditions. A starting half-life of 10 minutes comes from dividing 40 minutes by the
      fourfold drop, and destruction has slowed, not stopped, because Nrf2 still falls.

    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      With PERK, eIF2α is phosphorylated and translation slows; without PERK neither happens

## Built to turn on fast: p53–MDM2 and Keap1–Nrf2

Think of a car at a red light with the engine running and a foot on the brake: lifting the foot beats starting the engine. You have met two versions before. Either the responder itself is made constantly and destroyed until a signal protects it—like HIFα while oxygen is present (the lesson on ubiquitin) and β-catenin until Wnt arrives—or an intact responder waits quietly until its partner is removed or occupied, like NF-κB held in the cytoplasm until IκB is destroyed.

Under constant synthesis and first-order destruction, a responder's steady-state pool obeys:

> **steady-state level = synthesis rate ÷ destruction rate constant**

Because half-life varies inversely with that constant (half-life = ln 2 ÷ k_d), lengthening half-life raises steady state by the same factor with no new transcription. At 60 units per hour and a rate constant of 2 per hour (half-life ~21 min), steady state is 30 units; slowing destruction fourfold to 0.5 per hour (half-life ~83 min) lifts it to 120 units. From p53's ~20-minute half-life, halting destruction lets steady synthesis double the pool in under half an hour.

### DNA breaks: p53 and MDM2

**p53** is a **transcription factor**, a protein that binds DNA and activates genes. Its restraint is **MDM2**, an **E3 ubiquitin ligase** that grips a short helix at p53's front end, covering its gene-activating region and tagging p53 with ubiquitin for the proteasome. Crucially, p53 switches on the *MDM2* gene itself—ordering its own destroyer in a negative feedback loop (as in the lesson on signaling circuits).

A break across both DNA strands activates the kinase **ATM**. ATM and the kinases beneath it phosphorylate both p53's MDM2-binding stretch and MDM2 itself, loosening their grip so destruction slows sharply. Accumulated p53 activates **p21** (blocking kinases that drive cell division), DNA repair genes, and—under severe damage—cell-death genes (the next lesson).

**Predict.** You add a small molecule that plugs MDM2's p53-binding cleft to healthy cells with intact DNA. What happens to p53 levels over the next hour?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

p53 climbs sharply without DNA damage: synthesis continues while MDM2 cannot grip or tag p53 for the proteasome—the mechanism of nutlin-3, which returns below.

</details>

Cells without working p53 keep dividing with broken chromosomes, making *TP53*, the gene for p53, the most frequently mutated gene in human cancer, mostly in its DNA-binding domain. Some tumors, sarcomas among them, keep p53 intact and carry extra copies of *MDM2* instead, keeping the brake pressed down.

### Chemical threats: Keap1 and Nrf2

Oxidants and **electrophiles**—molecules short of electrons that react with DNA and protein—threaten cells chemically. Their responder, **Nrf2**, is a transcription factor that activates protective enzyme genes, including those joining an electrophile to glutathione for excretion. Made constantly, Nrf2 is gripped by **Keap1** (part of a ubiquitin ligase), keeping its half-life well under an hour in calm cells.

Keap1 senses electrophiles by getting hit. Cysteine side chains end in sulfur atoms that give up electrons easily, and mouse Keap1 has 25 cysteines among 624 amino acids, several unusually reactive. Electrophiles such as **sulforaphane** (from broccoli) react with them. On the current view, altered Keap1 stops passing Nrf2 on for destruction, so Keap1 catchers fill up and newly made Nrf2 enters the nucleus.

**Predict.** On that model, modified Keap1 keeps holding older Nrf2 without delivering it for destruction. If you also block all new protein synthesis when an electrophile arrives, will much Nrf2 reach the nucleus?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Very little: because altered Keap1 still grips the older Nrf2, nuclear entry depends on newly made Nrf2 escaping once Keap1 is occupied.

</details>

Unlike a shaped receptor pocket, Keap1 reads hunger for electrons, sensing a broad family of unrelated chemicals. Which cysteine responds depends on the compound: in mice with serine replacing cysteine 151, sulforaphane does much less to Nrf2 while other inducers still work. Dimethyl fumarate, used for relapsing multiple sclerosis, is an electrophile that activates Nrf2, though how much of its benefit comes that way is debated. *(See Going deeper for how Talalay, Itoh and Yamamoto traced sulforaphane to Keap1.)*

## Three-tier stress relays: p38 and JNK

Stress pathways also co-opt the three-tier MAPK plan (Raf, MEK, ERK from the lesson on Ras and MAPK), ending in **p38** and **JNK**. Above p38 sit the MAP2Ks **MKK3** and **MKK6**, above JNK sit **MKK4** and **MKK7**, and above those sit many MAP3Ks wired to distinct stresses. In the standard route, p38 and JNK—like ERK—require a MAP2K to phosphorylate both a threonine and a tyrosine one residue apart.

Triggers include ultraviolet light, heat, sudden salt changes, oxidants, bacterial products, TNF and interleukin-1. One MAP3K, **ASK1**, repeats partner restraint: **thioredoxin** holds ASK1 off until oxidants make thioredoxin let go.

**Predict.** If a mutation stops thioredoxin from binding ASK1, what happens to p38 and JNK in an unstressed cell?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Freed from thioredoxin, ASK1 would be expected to switch on its MAP2Ks without oxidants, so p38 and JNK would gain phosphates in an unstressed cell.

</details>

- **p38** resembles yeast **HOG1**, which helps yeast survive high salt *(see Going deeper for its 1994 discovery)*. Remember two jobs: in immune cells p38 raises TNF and interleukin-1 output partly through **MK2**, and MK2 phosphorylates **Hsp27**, a small chaperone protecting stressed proteins.
- **JNK** (**c-Jun N-terminal kinase**, found in the early 1990s as a UV- and stress-activated kinase) phosphorylates serines 63 and 73 of **c-Jun**, the AP-1 partner from the previous lesson, strengthening gene activation. Persistent stress pushes JNK toward cell death (the next lesson). In neurons **DLK** feeds JNK: Ghosh and colleagues (2011) reported that cultured sensory neurons from mouse embryos lacking DLK resist both axon degeneration and cell death after losing nerve growth factor.

Starting a stress pathway fast is only half the problem; how a cell reads a signal's pattern over time to choose between repair and permanent arrest or death is the subject of the second film.

## Pulses, not a dial: reading the timing of p53

The second film showed why dish blots blur single-cell pulses—like averaging a thousand out-of-step strobe lights into a steady glow. Working with Uri Alon at the Weizmann Institute, Galit Lahav tagged p53 cyan and MDM2 yellow in human cells and filmed single cells for hours after gamma irradiation. Individual cells fired discrete p53 pulses of fixed average height and width; higher doses raised the average pulse count, while genetically identical neighbours gave none, one, two or more.

**Predict.** Since p53 activates the *MDM2* gene and MDM2 destroys p53, should yellow MDM2 peak before, with, or slightly after cyan p53 during each pulse?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Slightly after cyan p53: p53 must rise first to drive new MDM2 synthesis, and after that lag yellow MDM2 builds up and knocks cyan p53 down.

</details>

The pattern depends on the stress. Fixed-size pulses belong to gamma rays (double-strand breaks). Ultraviolet light gives one pulse whose height and length grow with dose, and in chemotherapy-treated cancer cells death depends on how high p53 climbs and how soon—so level carries information too.

In 2012 Lahav's laboratory used timed doses of the MDM2 blocker **nutlin-3** to hold p53 steady at about the height of one natural pulse. In that cell line, pulsing cells tended to recover and divide, while steady cells more often entered **senescence**, a permanent exit from division. The protein and its peak level were the same. Holding p53 up also meant more p53 in total, but at equal total p53, sustained p53 still switched on senescence genes more strongly: signal shape mattered.

### Design problem

After radiation, p53 in a single cell fires pulses of roughly fixed size, with more damage adding more pulses. Design a two-protein circuit that pulses instead of rising steadily, and explain how a cell could use pulse count to choose between recovery and permanent exit from division.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Pair activator A with destroyer D: A activates the gene for D, and D marks A for destruction. With enough delay and a steep response, negative feedback oscillates: making D takes time so A overshoots, arriving D knocks A down, D decays with A gone, and A climbs again.

To let damage set pulse count, gate the loop with a damage kinase that loosens D's grip while breaks remain. To count pulses, give A short-lived arrest-and-repair targets that rise and fall with A, plus a long-lived permanent-arrest target that accumulates bit by bit across pulses until many pulses push it over threshold.

</details>

## Three sensors in the folding compartment: the unfolded protein response

The **unfolded protein response (UPR)** applies both halves of this lesson's rule inside one organelle: lifting a restraint starts the alarm, and backlog duration helps steer repair toward death.

Surface and secreted proteins fold in the **endoplasmic reticulum (ER)** with chaperones like **BiP**. When unfolded chains outpace folding, three ER-membrane sensors—each with one end inside the ER—respond. On the leading accounts, BiP holds them quiet until unfolded chains pull BiP away, and unfolded chains also bind IRE1 directly.

Going past the second film's repair-then-death overview, each sensor uses a distinct mechanism:

- **IRE1 (cytoplasmic RNA splicing):** Clustering switches on IRE1's cytoplasmic RNase, which cuts a segment out of one mRNA—*HAC1* in yeast, *XBP1* in animals—before a ligase rejoins the ends; only the spliced message yields an active transcription factor ordering chaperones *(see Going deeper for the 1993 screens)*.
- **PERK (slowing translation):** PERK phosphorylates **eIF2α**, a ribosome initiation factor, slowing global protein synthesis so fewer new chains enter the ER.
- **ATF6 (regulated cleavage):** Like Notch, stressed ATF6 moves to the Golgi, where proteases release a fragment that enters the nucleus to activate chaperone genes.

**Predict.** In mammalian *XBP1* mRNA, IRE1 removes 26 nucleotides before ligation. Why does that change the protein's entire downstream tail instead of simply deleting eight or nine amino acids?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Codons are read in threes, and 26 is not a multiple of three (remainder 2). Removing 26 nucleotides shifts the reading frame, giving the protein a new tail that makes it an active transcription factor.

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/stress-signals/upr-three-sensors.webp" width={1200} height={752} alt="AI-generated illustration: a horizontal ER membrane with three labelled sensor blocks, IRE1, PERK and ATF6, each with BiP on its lumen end and unfolded chains nearby. Two insets show that each sensor first drives repair, and that PERK and IRE1 branches can turn toward death-promoting outputs under long or severe stress, depending on cell state." caption="IRE1, PERK and ATF6 first cut load and add chaperones, then can favour death-promoting outputs via CHOP and ASK1–JNK under long or severe stress." />

All three branches repair first. If the backlog lasts, PERK's branch switches on **CHOP**, a death-promoting transcription factor, and IRE1 recruits an adaptor feeding **ASK1 and JNK**. As with p53, timing carries part of the information: brief stress mainly calls for repair, while long or severe stress suggests repair is failing, though stress strength and cell state matter too and adaptive and death signals can overlap. Heavy secretors depend critically on this balance: children born without working PERK develop diabetes within months of birth, and mice lacking PERK develop diabetes with too few insulin-producing pancreatic cells.

## Going deeper

### How p38 was found from four directions in 1994

At Scripps, Jiahuai Han in Richard Ulevitch's laboratory cloned a protein gaining tyrosine phosphate after macrophages met bacterial lipopolysaccharide or high-solute medium, resembling yeast HOG1. At SmithKline Beecham, John C. Lee's group used labeled anti-inflammatory compounds as bait and recovered the same kinase. Two more groups reported it that year as a stress- and interleukin-1-activated kinase.

### Tracing broccoli sprouts to Keap1's cysteines

Paul Talalay's laboratory in Baltimore isolated sulforaphane from broccoli in 1992, and Ken Itoh and Masayuki Yamamoto in Tsukuba reported Keap1 in 1999. Collaborating, the groups showed that inducers react directly with reactive cysteines on Keap1.

### The 1993 yeast screens behind IRE1

Kazutoshi Mori in Dallas and Peter Walter's laboratory in San Francisco independently found IRE1 in 1993 yeast screens for mutants unable to report ER folding stress. Because IRE1 looked like a membrane-spanning kinase, both first expected phosphorylation before cytoplasmic RNA cutting was discovered.
