## The big idea

A dry bean from the kitchen jar looks like a pebble. It does not grow, move or seem to need anything. Yet it is alive. It is resting, a state often called **dormancy**: alive, but using almost none of its stored food.

Inside each bean is a tiny plant and a large food store. Give the bean water and a warm room, and the tiny plant starts to grow. This start is called **germination**. A root comes out first and grows down. A shoot follows and grows up.

The activity puts two bags side by side, one damp and one dry. Only one thing differs, so your child can see that water is what wakes the seed. Checking each day also shows a plant's first steps of growth, from seed to root to shoot. Virginia's kindergarten standard asks children to notice changes like these. Naming every stage in order is a grade 2 goal.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child while the beans are out. You do any splitting of the soaked bean.
- Dry beans can be swallowed, breathed in or pushed into a nose or ear. Keep beans away from babies, toddlers and children under 4, and watch that older children do not hand them to younger ones. Count the beans out and back in when you finish. Keep the plastic bags away from babies and toddlers.
- Do not taste any bean or sprout, even after soaking or sprouting. Raw beans contain natural toxins that proper cooking destroys, and raw lima beans contain compounds that release cyanide when chewed. Red kidney beans have the most: as few as four or five undercooked ones can cause severe vomiting, so skip them. Sprouts grown in a warm, damp bag can also grow germs, and health agencies say children should avoid raw sprouts. Throw the beans away when you finish. If a child is choking or struggling to breathe, call 911. If a child chews or swallows beans, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222.
- Use beans from the grocery store, not garden seed packets. Some garden seeds are coated in a brightly colored pesticide.
- Mold often grows in a damp bag, so look at the beans through the plastic. If you see fuzz, press the bag shut, do not open or sniff it, and have an adult throw it away. Anyone with asthma or a mold allergy, child or adult, should stay away from a moldy bag and should not do the daily checks once mold shows, because mold can cause severe reactions in them.
- Wash hands with soap and water after handling the beans and paper towels. Beans are food, so check first for any bean, pea, peanut or soy allergy and for your school’s rules on food in class. A child with such an allergy should not handle the beans.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Look first.** Give your child two dry beans. Ask, "Is this alive? How could we find out?" Write down their answer in their words.
2. **Set up the bags.** Fold a paper towel to fit inside a zip bag. Wet it with spoonfuls of water until it is damp but not dripping. Place 4 to 6 beans on it, about 2 cm apart, so you can see them through the plastic. Set up the second bag the same way with a dry towel. Label the bags "water" and "no water."
3. **Predict.** Ask, "What will happen in each bag? Where will something come out of the bean? Which way will it grow?" Have your child draw their guess.
4. **Place the bags.** Close each bag almost all the way, leaving a gap about as wide as a finger for air. Tape both to a bright window that does not get hot afternoon sun, or lay them on a counter. A sealed bag in hot sun can get too warm.
5. **Check every day.** Spend 2 or 3 minutes a day looking and drawing. Add a few drops of water if the towel starts to dry. In a warm room, most beans swell within a day and grow a white root in 3 to 7 days. In a cool room, it can take up to two weeks. The shoot, a curved stem, comes out a few days after the root. Expect nothing in the dry bag.
6. **Split a bean (optional).** The night before you want to split it, soak one extra bean in a small bowl of water, out of reach of younger children, for 8 to 12 hours. Peel off its skin. You split it along the seam with a thumbnail. Look for the tiny plant tucked at one end, with a root tip and two folded leaves. The two thick halves are the food store.
7. **Add water to the dry bag.** After about a week, ask, "What if we wet the dry bag now?" Wet its towel and keep checking. Most of those beans will start to grow too, which shows they were alive all along.

With a class, give each group of four one pair of bags and one daily checker.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder if this bean is alive. What could we do to find out?"
- "What do you think will happen in the dry bag?"
- "Where do you think the first part will come out? Will it grow up or down?"
- "What do you think will happen if we put one bag in a dark cupboard?"
- "The two bean halves are shrinking. Where do you think their food is going?"
- "What do you think will happen if this plant stays in the bag for a month?"

## Why it works

A ripe seed dries out as it finishes growing on the parent plant. With little water inside, its cells slow almost to a stop. That lets many seeds wait months or years. A bean's two thick halves are its **seed leaves**, also called cotyledons. They hold starch and protein for the young plant.

When the seed soaks up water, it swells and its cells start working again. Enzymes break down the stored food. The embryo's root, the radicle, pushes out first, which anchors the seedling and takes up water. Then the shoot grows, often in a hook that later straightens.

Germination needs water, air and suitable warmth. Beans germinate best in soil around 65 to 85°F, and more slowly when cooler. Light is not needed to sprout, but the seedling soon needs it, because its leaves must make food once the store is used up. Not every grocery bean sprouts, because some are too old.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use one bean type and just the two bags. Let your child check every day but draw only every other day. Ask one question each time: "What is new?" Point to the root and say its name. Keep the word list short: seed, root, shoot, leaves. If waiting is hard, check twice a day for the first three days, when the beans swell fast.

### Ages 8 to 10
Make it a fair test. Change one thing only, such as light (window bag and cupboard bag) or bean type (lima and pinto). Keep everything else the same. A damp bag in a closed cupboard is easy to forget, so look at it every day and follow the mold rule above. Measure the root in centimeters each day and record it in a table. Make a bar graph of day 7 root lengths. Afterward, have your child read their graph and explain what the test shows and what it does not.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If nothing happens after a week, the room may be too cool or the beans too old. Move the bags to a warmer spot and try fresh beans from another bag or store. If beans rot or turn slimy, the towel was too wet: use less water next time. If mold appears, press the bag shut and throw it away without opening it, then start again with fresh beans and a fresh towel. Some beans in every bag usually fail, so plant more than you need.
