summary: >-
  In mouse and rat visual cortex, the ocular dominance window follows the inhibitory cells, not the calendar: maturing inhibition starts it, and added brakes close it.
  Closing one eye strongly reshapes the cortex during a few weeks of youth; adults keep only a weaker, different form of this plasticity.
  This lesson asks what sets those weeks. You will see the evidence that a threshold level of inhibition
  triggers the window, the molecular brakes that later close it, and the rodent experiments that
  reopen it. Afterwards you can read a plasticity experiment as a test of a regulated state
  rather than an age, and judge what such results do and do not promise for people.

techniques:
  - name: "Monocular deprivation with an ocular dominance readout"
    blurb: >-
      One eyelid is sutured shut for a few days, then responses of visual cortex to each eye are
      measured with single-unit recording, visual evoked potentials or optical imaging. A shift
      toward the open eye shows that the cortex was plastic during the deprivation.
  - name: "Knockout and knock-in mice"
    blurb: >-
      A knockout mouse lacks a working copy of one gene; a knock-in carries a chosen change in
      it. GAD65 knockouts tested whether activity-dependent GABA release was needed for the window, and knock-ins
      that make one GABA-A receptor subunit insensitive to diazepam tested which subunit carries
      the drug's effect.
  - name: "Local drug and enzyme infusion"
    blurb: >-
      A drug or enzyme is delivered into one region of cortex through a minipump or an injection.
      Diazepam infusion tested whether boosting inhibition is enough to open the window, and
      chondroitinase ABC injection tested whether the extracellular matrix holds it closed.
  - name: "Interneuron transplantation"
    blurb: >-
      Inhibitory neuron precursors are taken from the embryonic medial ganglionic eminence and
      injected into the cortex of a young host, where they migrate and mature. Testing the host
      later asks whether the age of the inhibitory cells, rather than the age of the animal,
      sets the window.

      Diazepam is a positive allosteric modulator: it binds at an alpha-gamma interface and makes

**A mouse that lacks one of its two enzymes for making GABA can go through the whole of youth without the critical period that should have reshaped its visual cortex. Give it a few days of a benzodiazepine, even as an adult, and the window opens.**

Start with the picture behind the word "window". Close one eye of a four-week-old mouse for a few days, and its visual cortex shifts: it now answers that eye much less. Do the same to a two-month-old mouse, and the cortex changes much less. You met this result, in kittens as well as mice, in [the lesson on ocular dominance](/lessons/ocular-dominance/). A stretch of time like this is a window of opportunity and of vulnerability, and it is one reason amblyopia, a weak eye from childhood, is best treated early. This lesson asks how such a window is regulated, and the knockout mouse in the bold line is where the answer starts.

## The readout, step by step

Most of the evidence comes from one system: mouse primary visual cortex, with monocular deprivation as the readout. The mouse earns its place because you can change its genes.

1. **Where to record.** In the binocular zone of visual cortex, most neurons answer both eyes, though some answer only one. In the mouse, most cells answer the **contralateral** eye, on the opposite side of the head, more strongly than the **ipsilateral** eye, on the same side.
2. **Put a number on it.** The ocular dominance index is (C − I)/(C + I), where C and I are a cell's responses to the contralateral and the ipsilateral eye. An illustrative cell that fires 30 spikes per second to the contralateral eye and 10 to the ipsilateral eye has an index of 20/40 = 0.50. For responses of zero or more, the index runs from +1 (contralateral eye only) to −1 (ipsilateral eye only).
3. **Deprive.** Suture the contralateral eyelid shut for about four days at postnatal day 28 (P28), then record again. Responses shift toward the open eye, so the index falls, and a cell like this one can fall below zero: it now prefers the eye that stayed open. In young mice, most of the shift comes from the closed eye's responses weakening.
4. **Vary the age.** The shift is largest around P28, and much smaller before about P19 or after about P32 (Gordon and Stryker, 1996).

The first MCAT question at the end works one cell through. For this lesson, then, the window is the range of ages in which four days with one eye closed move the index toward the open eye. Is that range set by age itself, or by something that age brings with it?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** The window follows the inhibitory cells, not the calendar. In the working model, when maturing inhibition, especially in circuits involving parvalbumin basket cells (fast-firing inhibitory neurons), passes a threshold, it helps start the window. As inhibition goes on maturing, and other brakes build up around these cells, the window closes. In a normal mouse the two agree: inhibition reaches the threshold at a typical age.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which plasticity.** The juvenile ocular dominance shift in mouse and rat visual cortex, in which a few days of monocular deprivation weaken the closed eye's responses. Adults keep a [weaker, different form](#the-adult-form-of-ocular-dominance-plasticity). Song, language and people are outside it.
- **What "threshold" means.** In young cortex, crossing it **starts** a window; the evidence below is that a brief drug boost was enough. Whether normal inhibition must stay high to keep a window open has not been tested. Once the window has closed, lowering inhibition helped reopen it in adult rodents.
- **What it does not say.** Inhibition is not the only clock; other parts of the circuit mature too. Nor does it say which eye wins.

## Is it the calendar? The GAD65 test

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

If the window simply followed age, every mouse would have it from about P19 to P32, whatever else differed. The first test came from a mouse with weaker inhibition.

A **knockout** mouse lacks a working copy of one gene. Inhibitory neurons make GABA, the main inhibitory transmitter of the cortex, from glutamate with two enzymes. **GAD67** makes most of the GABA present at rest. **GAD65** sits at the synaptic terminal and adds to the supply when the neuron fires hard. A GAD65 knockout still has GABA, but it releases too little during strong activity.

**Predict.** Inhibition quiets neurons. Close one eye of a GAD65 knockout at P28. Should its cortex shift more than a normal mouse's, about the same, or less?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Less: not at all. In 1998 Takao Hensch, Michela Fagiolini, Michael Stryker and colleagues reported that GAD65 knockouts showed no ocular dominance shift after deprivation at the normal age. Their neurons still responded to vision, with normal basic response properties. What failed was the change.

If you guessed "more", that is a natural guess: a less inhibited cortex sounds easier to change. [A section below](#why-would-more-inhibition-help) takes up why it is wrong.

</details>

Then the rescue. Diazepam is a benzodiazepine, a positive modulator of the GABA-A receptor. It binds at a site between an alpha and a gamma subunit and makes the receptor's chloride channel respond more strongly to GABA, without opening it alone (see [the lesson on ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/), and [the lesson on drugs that act on one part of a receptor](/lessons/drugs-against-a-part/)). So it amplifies whatever GABA the knockout still releases. Infused into the visual cortex of the knockouts during deprivation, diazepam restored the shift.

Notice what the drug did not do. Boosting inhibition did not itself shift the cortex; the shift went toward the eye that stayed open. In the terms of [the lesson on the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/), inhibition here is **permissive**: it lets the cortex respond to deprivation. The difference between the eyes is **instructive**: it decides which eye wins.

**Predict.** Two experiments separate "age opens the window" from "inhibition opens the window."

1. Raise a GAD65 knockout to P60, long past the normal window. Give diazepam and close one eye.
2. Give diazepam to a normal mouse at about P15, around the time the eyes open and before its window would open, and close one eye. In a second group, give the same early diazepam, stop it, and close one eye at P28, the usual peak.

What does each idea predict for each experiment?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If age sets the window, the knockout at P60 is too old and should not shift. The P15 mouse is too young and should not shift, and the P28 group should shift normally.

If inhibition sets it, the knockout's window has never opened, so it should still be waiting at P60, and diazepam should open it. The P15 mouse should shift, because diazepam lifts its immature inhibition over the threshold. And if a window that opens early also closes early, the early-treated mice should have little left by P28.

The results follow inhibition.

| Mouse | Diazepam | Age at eye closure | Shift? | What it says |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | none | P28 | yes | the normal window |
| Normal | none | P60 | little | the window has closed |
| GAD65 knockout | none | P28 | no | too little inhibition; the window has not opened |
| GAD65 knockout | during deprivation | P28 | yes | raising inhibition is enough (1998) |
| GAD65 knockout | during deprivation | P60 | yes | the capacity waited into adulthood; age did not close it (Fagiolini and Hensch, 2000) |
| Normal | early | P15 | yes | the window can open early (Fagiolini and Hensch, 2000) |
| Normal | two days, just after eye opening | P28 | much less | it opened early and closed early (Iwai and colleagues, 2003) |

</details>

The last row matters most. The same study found that in knockouts, two days of diazepam near the start of deprivation were enough to switch plasticity fully on, even though the drug did not overlap the days the eye was closed (Iwai and colleagues, 2003). So diazepam acts as a trigger: it **starts** the window. What ends it is not shown here to be an internal timer. The early-treated windows closed sooner, and [the brakes section](#closing-the-window-brakes-not-decay) shows that further maturation of inhibition and added brakes help end it.

## Why would more inhibition help?

Inhibition quiets neurons, so why would a cortex with too little of it be *harder* to change?

The inhibition implicated here is thought to come largely from fast-firing basket cells, as the next section discusses. Their synapses cluster on the cell bodies of pyramidal neurons, the cortex's main excitatory cells, close to where the axon starts and spikes begin. That placement gives them strong control over whether and when a pyramidal cell fires. One leading idea is that maturing basket-cell inhibition sharpens *when* pyramidal cells fire, so that the circuit can tell which eye reliably drives each cell, and deprivation can then weaken the closed eye's input. Consistent with this, neurons in GAD65 knockouts fire prolonged discharges, and the benzodiazepine that unmasked plasticity selectively reduced them (Fagiolini and Hensch, 2000).

This is a hypothesis, not a settled mechanism. What the experiments establish is the order of events: inhibition first, then plasticity.

## Which inhibition: follow the basket cells

Cortex has many kinds of inhibitory interneuron. The ones implicated here are parvalbumin-expressing basket cells. Like most cortical interneurons, they are born in the medial ganglionic eminence (MGE) of the embryonic forebrain and reach the cortex by [tangential migration](/lessons/tangential-migration/).

Part of the case for these cells comes from receptor subunits. GABA-A receptors are built from five subunits, and the alpha subunit varies. Receptors with alpha1 are enriched at the synapses basket cells make on cell bodies. In 2004, Fagiolini, Hensch and colleagues used knock-in mice, which carry a chosen point mutation, here one that makes a single alpha subtype insensitive to diazepam. When alpha1 was made insensitive, benzodiazepines no longer opened the window early. When alpha2 was made insensitive, they still did. In mouse visual cortex, then, the drug acts through alpha1 receptors. That is consistent with basket-cell synapses carrying the effect, but it is not proof, because alpha1 is also the most widespread alpha subunit in cortex. The next experiment tests the broader idea directly.

**Predict.** Take inhibitory neuron precursors from the MGE of an embryo and transplant them into the visual cortex of a young mouse. The cells disperse and mature into interneurons, including parvalbumin cells. Weeks later the host is past its own window. If the window follows the animal, what should closing one eye do now? If it follows the inhibitory cells, when should a second window appear?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A second window appeared, timed by the cells. In 2010, Derek Southwell, Sunil Gandhi, Stryker, Arturo Alvarez-Buylla and colleagues found that when the transplanted cells reached about the age of native interneurons during the normal critical period, the host had a second critical period.

</details>

This is the most direct evidence that the window follows the inhibitory cells: an animal past its own window got a second one from a young cohort of inhibitory cells. It does not single out parvalbumin cells, because the donor cells became several kinds of interneuron.

### What sets the pace of the basket cells

If the maturation of these inhibitory circuits helps set the timing, changing when they mature should move the window. Three findings fit.

- **BDNF.** In mice built by Josh Huang, Susumu Tonegawa, Alfredo Kirkwood, Lamberto Maffei and colleagues (1999), cortical neurons make extra BDNF, a neurotrophin that among other effects promotes inhibitory synapses. Their inhibition matured early, and their critical period ended early.
- **Darkness.** Rearing animals in darkness from birth, which slows the maturation of inhibition, delays the window in rodents and in cats.
- **Otx2.** Otx2 is a homeoprotein, a transcription factor with a homeodomain. In 2008, Sayaka Sugiyama, Hensch, Alain Prochiantz and colleagues reported that Otx2 protein builds up in parvalbumin cells of mouse visual cortex although these cells make little Otx2 RNA. Its build-up depended on visual experience, and infusing Otx2 early opened plasticity early. Its source is [uncertain](#where-cortical-otx2-comes-from).

## Closing the window: brakes, not decay

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

If the window simply faded as the machinery for plasticity was lost, removing one molecule in an adult should not bring it back. Experiments in adult rodents suggest instead that it is actively held shut.

**Predict.** As the critical period ends, a **perineuronal net**, a dense lattice of extracellular matrix rich in chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans, condenses around many parvalbumin cells. Dark rearing delays it. Inject an enzyme that cuts chondroitin sulfate chains into the visual cortex of an adult rat, then close one eye. What does "decay" predict? What does "brake" predict?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Decay predicts no shift. A brake predicts a shift once it is removed. In 2002, Tommaso Pizzorusso, Maffei and colleagues in Pisa did the experiment with chondroitinase ABC, a bacterial enzyme. Monocular deprivation then shifted ocular dominance, which it did not do in untreated adults. The nets help hold the window shut.

</details>

Two more brakes have been found by deleting a gene and finding plasticity that lasts past the usual closing age.

**Myelin signals.** Myelin, the insulating wrap on axons ([its own lesson](/lessons/myelination/)), carries proteins such as Nogo that inhibit axon growth through the Nogo receptor. In 2005, Aaron McGee, Stephen Strittmatter and colleagues reported that mice lacking the Nogo receptor kept ocular dominance plasticity after the usual closing age.

**Lynx1.** Lynx1 is a small membrane protein that binds nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and damps their response. Its level in mouse visual cortex rises in adulthood. In 2010, Hirofumi Morishita, Hensch and colleagues reported that adult Lynx1 knockouts kept strong plasticity, and that blocking nicotinic receptors prevented it.

**Inhibition itself: two jobs at two times.** Here a second puzzle appears. Inhibition opened the window, so how can it also help close it? Reaching a threshold starts the window. Inhibition that goes on maturing after that becomes one of the brakes. In GAD65 knockouts given diazepam, plasticity was eventually lost after a long delay, and the loss came with improved function of their own inhibitory circuits (Iwai and colleagues, 2003). In adult rats, the Pisa group partly restored ocular dominance plasticity by reducing GABA transmission in visual cortex (Harauzov and colleagues, 2010). That is why some of the reopening treatments below work partly by lowering inhibition.

## Reopening the window, and what it promises for amblyopia

The reason to reopen it is amblyopia, in which one eye (occasionally both) sees poorly because its input to the cortex was degraded in childhood, for example by a misaligned or blurred eye. Patching the good eye works much less reliably in adults than in young children. A drug that reopened the window could, in principle, give adults another chance.

**Predict.** In 2008, José Fernando Maya Vetencourt, Maffei, Eero Castrén and colleagues gave adult rats weeks of fluoxetine, an antidepressant. It restored ocular dominance plasticity and lowered intracortical inhibition. What should diazepam, given along with the fluoxetine, do? Compare it with what diazepam did in the GAD65 knockout.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Diazepam blocked these effects, the mirror image of the knockout rescue. In the knockout, diazepam raised inhibition that was too weak, and the window opened. In the adult, fluoxetine lowered inhibition that had become a brake, and diazepam put the brake back. The same drug had opposite effects because the circuit was in a different state.

</details>

Paired with visual experience through the weak eye, fluoxetine also allowed recovery of vision in rats made amblyopic by long deprivation in early life. Elizabeth Quinlan's laboratory reported that a period of complete darkness restored plasticity in adult rats (He and colleagues, 2006) and, in a later study, allowed recovery of vision after long deprivation (He and colleagues, 2007). With chondroitinase ABC and the interneuron transplant, that makes several routes back to a plastic cortex in rodents.

Read these as evidence about mechanism, not as treatments. Each result is in rats or mice, mostly in visual cortex, with deprivations that differ from human amblyopia. Reopening plasticity can also reopen vulnerability, since a plastic cortex can be changed for the worse. Small trials of drugs in adults with amblyopia have so far given mixed results, and none of these approaches is an established therapy. Song learning and language have their own windows, taken up in [the lesson on sensitive periods](/lessons/sensitive-periods-behavior/).

The evidence points the same way across genes, drugs, rearing and transplanted cells. In mouse and rat visual cortex, the ocular dominance window tracks the maturation state of inhibitory circuits: a threshold starts it, further maturation and added brakes end it, and both steps can be moved. It is set by the state of a circuit, not simply by age.

## Going deeper

### Critical period or sensitive period

A sensitive period is a limited time when experience has unusually strong effects on a circuit. A critical period is the stricter case, in which the experience is needed for normal development and its effects last. Usage varies between fields. The mouse ocular dominance window is usually called a critical period, though its edges are softer than the name suggests.

### Where cortical Otx2 comes from

The source of the Otx2 that builds up in parvalbumin cells was first proposed to be the retina; later work pointed to the choroid plexus, the tissue that makes cerebrospinal fluid, as a source as well. The full answer is still debated, so hold this part loosely. Otx2 uptake into parvalbumin cells has been reported to depend on perineuronal net sugars, which, if it holds, would link the opening and closing steps.

### The adult form of ocular dominance plasticity

Adult mouse visual cortex is not completely fixed. Sawtell and colleagues (2003) found an adult form of ocular dominance plasticity in mice, mostly a strengthening of the open eye's responses, which differs from the juvenile weakening of the closed eye's responses. It depends on NMDA receptors. So "closed" means reduced and changed, not absent.

### How the brakes might overlap

The Nogo receptor, Lynx1, the extracellular matrix and mature inhibition each restrain plasticity: in rodents, removing or reducing any one of them lets some plasticity persist or return. It is not settled how they interact, or whether they are partly redundant.

### Why the drug's lack of effect on the index is a finding

It is tempting to say that boosting inhibition could never move the index, because it acts on both eyes' inputs. That holds only for one kind of effect. If inhibition scaled both responses by the same factor, the index would not change: 30 and 10 spikes per second becoming 18 and 6 still give 0.50. If it subtracted the same amount from both, the index would change: 30 and 10 becoming 25 and 5 give about 0.67. So the statement that diazepam alone did not shift ocular dominance is an experimental result, not a consequence of the arithmetic.
