techniques:
  - name: "Field disturbance manipulation"
    blurb: >-
      You change the disturbance a patch experiences, for example by stabilizing boulders so
      waves cannot overturn them, and compare the communities with untreated and handling-control
      patches. It separates disturbance from variables that travel with it in the natural pattern.
  - name: "Factorial microcosm experiment"
    blurb: >-
      You cross several levels of disturbance frequency with several levels of intensity, with
      replicates in each combination. It shows whether the effect of one factor depends on the
      level of the other, which a single gradient cannot reveal.
  - name: "Chronosequence (space-for-time substitution)"
    blurb: >-
      You sample sites that differ in time since disturbance or abandonment and read the series as
      a trajectory. It is fast, but it assumes the sites differ only in age, which repeated
      censuses of the same plots can check.
  - name: "Rarefaction and effective numbers of species"
    blurb: >-
      You standardize samples to the same number of individuals (or the same completeness) before
      comparing richness, and convert indices to effective numbers of species. It stops a larger
      sampling effort from looking like a richer community.

The first film scored two plots as effective numbers of species. Here is where those numbers come from, and then how a **community** (the set of species living and interacting in one place) rebuilds after a disturbance.

> **The guiding idea.** Any of the diversity indices here can be turned into an **effective number of species**: how many equally common species would give the same value. At order 0 it is simply the **richness** (the number of species present). Above order 0 it equals the richness only when every species is equally common, and it falls as a few species take over. These indices use only counts, never names, so succession and disturbance can change them only by changing which species are present or how evenly individuals are shared among them.
>
> *Scope:* this holds for the count-based measures in this lesson (richness, Shannon, Simpson and their effective numbers); a similarity index that compares species lists, like the one in the forest study below, does see identity. The idea does not say whether disturbance raises or lowers diversity: that depends on how often it strikes, how hard, and in which system, and settling it needs a manipulation.

## Counting a community: richness, evenness and effective species

Start with a species-by-count table: how many individuals of each species were counted. **Richness**, S, is the number of species present. **Relative abundance**, p_i, is the proportion of individuals that belong to species i. **Evenness** describes how equally the individuals are spread among species.

Two indices combine richness and evenness. **Shannon entropy**, H, measures the uncertainty about which species the next individual you pick belongs to: zero if there is one species, larger as species are many and evenly spread. With the natural logarithm, as here, its unit is the nat. **Simpson concentration**, lambda, is the probability that two individuals drawn at random (with replacement) belong to the same species.

> **H = -sum of (p_i x ln p_i)        lambda = sum of p_i^2**

**Worked problem.** The first film's two plots, counted completely, 100 individuals each: sample A has 25, 25, 25 and 25; sample B has 40, 40, 10 and 10. Both have S = 4.

1. *Shannon entropy.* In A every p_i = 0.25, so H_A = -4 x (0.25 x ln 0.25) = ln 4 = 1.386 nats. In B, 0.4 x ln 0.4 = -0.3665 and 0.1 x ln 0.1 = -0.2303, so H_B = 2(0.3665) + 2(0.2303) = 0.733 + 0.461 = 1.194 nats.
2. *Simpson.* lambda_A = 4 x 0.25^2 = 0.25. lambda_B = 2(0.16) + 2(0.01) = 0.34. The complements are 0.75 and 0.66.
3. *Back to species.* exp H undoes the logarithm and turns nats into species: 4.00 for A, 3.30 for B. 1/lambda turns a matching probability into species: 4.00 for A, 2.94 for B. Simpson's evenness E = (1/lambda)/S is 1.00 for A and 2.94/4 = 0.735 for B.
4. *Read it.* "Simpson index" can mean lambda, 1 - lambda (the Gini-Simpson index, the probability that two draws differ) or 1/lambda. Reported as "Simpson index", A = 0.25 and B = 0.34 make B look more diverse, though a larger lambda means less diversity. Name the convention.

The **order**, q, is the first film's dial: as it rises from 0, rare species count for less, and at q = 2 for much less. Effective numbers of each order are called **Hill numbers**.

> **effective species: order 0 = S     order 1 = exp H     order 2 = 1/lambda**

Why convert? Ten equally common species give a Gini-Simpson index of 0.90; twenty give 0.95. The community doubled and the index barely moved; the effective numbers read 10 and 20.

## Succession: three ways one community replaces another

**Succession** is the change in species composition at a site over time after a **disturbance**, an event that kills or removes organisms and frees space or resources. **Primary succession** starts with no previous community: new lava, rock bared by a retreating glacier, a new seawall. **Secondary succession** follows a disturbance that leaves soil, seeds or roots behind: an abandoned pasture, a burned forest.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/diversity-and-succession/primary-vs-secondary-succession.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: on the left, bare rock exposed beside a retreating glacier with no soil and a single tuft of green; on the right, an abandoned pasture whose cut-away shows soil with seeds and roots under short grass. A small strip marks soil, seeds and roots as present only on the right." caption="Primary succession starts on surfaces with no previous community, such as rock bared by a retreating glacier. Secondary succession follows a disturbance that leaves soil, seeds or roots behind, as on an abandoned pasture." />

Microbes take part too: adding nitrogen and phosphorus together to young glacier soils shifted their bacteria toward those of much older soils, in one forefield (Going deeper).

Why does one set of species replace another? Joseph Connell and Ralph Slatyer (1977) set out three alternative models.

- **Facilitation:** early species change the site (adding soil, shade or nitrogen), and later species can establish only after that change.
- **Tolerance:** early species neither help nor much hinder later ones; later species persist because they can grow at lower resource levels.
- **Inhibition:** whoever arrives first holds the space and keeps later colonists out until it is damaged or dies.

All three produce early species followed by later ones, so watching the sequence cannot tell them apart. Removing the early occupants can.

**Predict.** Clear the early colonists from a plot as they settle. Under each model, do later species establish sooner, later, or about as soon as in untouched plots?

Real results are often mixed: early colonists can help some later species and hinder others, and tolerance is hard to tell from inhibition in the field, so a removal result usually tests particular species, not one model for a whole site. The same logic runs removal experiments in [competition](/lessons/competition/) and [predation](/lessons/predation/).

Nothing in these models has a goal: early species alter conditions, and later species do better or worse as a result. Nor does succession necessarily run to one fixed endpoint: where it goes depends on which species arrive, in what order, and how often the site is disturbed again.

Many succession studies use a **chronosequence**, a set of sites of different ages read as one trajectory (a space-for-time substitution). In Neotropical secondary forests read this way, richness recovered much more than species composition (Rozendaal and colleagues, 2019; numbers in Going deeper): the count can look recovered while the species are still different.

All three models describe interactions that unfold between disturbances. Repeated disturbance can interrupt or redirect succession, and the second film follows that on a boulder shore.

## Disturbance: Sousa's boulders and the debate over the hump

The second film did not name the hypothesis its boulders tested. The **intermediate disturbance hypothesis** (IDH) predicts that the diversity of competing species peaks at intermediate disturbance frequency or intensity: too little disturbance, and the best competitors exclude the rest; too much, and only fast colonizers survive. Connell's 1978 paper in Science on tropical forests and coral reefs is usually cited, though J. Philip Grime (1973) and others gave earlier versions; Sousa's (1979) boulders are a classic test of it.

### Frequency and intensity together

Sousa varied how often a patch is reset. A disturbance also has an **intensity**: the share of organisms each event kills. Alex Hall and colleagues (2012) crossed five frequencies with three intensities, from 10% to 99.999% of cells killed per event, in **microcosms** (small unshaken laboratory cultures) of the bacterium *Pseudomonas fluorescens*. It quickly evolves several **morphotypes** (heritable colony forms), so the community is variants of one strain, not separate species.

**Predict.** Hold intensity fixed and vary frequency. Will diversity against frequency have the same shape at all three intensities?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. At 10% mortality, frequency made no difference to diversity. At 99.9%, diversity was greatest at intermediate and high frequencies. At 99.999%, it peaked clearly at an intermediate frequency. Read the other way, diversity rose with intensity when disturbances were rare but peaked at an intermediate intensity when they were frequent. Frequency and intensity **interact**, so a study that varies only one of them gives an incomplete answer: it holds at the level of the factor it kept fixed.

</details>

The experiment measured outcomes, not mechanisms: diversity might move through the types that regrow fastest after each hit, or through rare types lost by chance when few cells survive, but neither was tested. Gibbons and colleagues (2016) added time: in a lake-water bacterial community, a diversity peak appeared only briefly, while community states mixed ("A peak that did not last").

Is the hypothesis right? Jeremy Fox (2013) argued it should be abandoned: its usual mechanisms are logically flawed, and humped curves are found only rarely. Michael Huston (2014) replied that critics had ignored the linked predictions his model makes for disturbance and productivity together. The IDH is neither confirmed nor refuted. Treat a humped curve as a prediction to test in a named system, at a stated frequency and intensity, not as a law.

## Description, mechanism and scale

Keep three questions apart. **Description** reports a pattern: diversity is highest on intermediate boulders. **Mechanism** needs a manipulation or a tested alternative: hold boulders still and see whether communities change. **Scale** says where the comparison sits: within one boulder or plot, among plots, or the pooled species list of a region. A hump among plots can coexist with no hump within them, so state the level, the plot size and the sampling effort.

### Design problem

In a hypothetical pine savanna, low wet hollows burn less often than dry ridges and hold fewer plant species. Design a test of whether fire frequency, rather than soil moisture, causes the pattern.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Choose matched plots in both hollows and ridges, and within each moisture class randomly assign them to burn intervals (often, rarely, never), so fire frequency is crossed with moisture. Add a handling control: fire breaks cut the same way but no burn. Record every fire each plot actually received, and census all plots at fixed intervals with the same quadrat area and effort, reporting an effective number of a named order.

Compare changes in diversity, not single censuses. If diversity rises more in often-burned hollows than in rare-burn and no-burn hollows, while handling controls resemble untouched plots, fire frequency has a causal effect within hollows. If hollows and ridges burned at the same interval end up equally diverse, fire frequency explains the pattern; if a gap remains, moisture or something tied to it matters too, and the crossed design shows whether moisture changes the response to fire. No change may also mean the right species have not arrived yet. Any result holds for that site and those years.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Rankings that swap between orders

Counts of 52, 18, 18, 12 and of 38, 33, 28, 1 both have S = 4. At order 1 the first wins (3.36 effective species against 3.11); at order 2 the second does (3.01 against 2.86). Samples can also be standardized to a common sample coverage (completeness) rather than a common number of individuals.

### A nutrient experiment on young glacier soils

In the forefield of the Puca Glacier in southeastern Peru, Knelman and colleagues (2014) randomly assigned 16 plots of soil, exposed by deglaciation about three years earlier, to control, nitrogen, phosphorus or both. After one year, bacterial communities given both nutrients most resembled those of soils exposed for about 85 years. They identified bacteria by gene sequencing (see [sequencing and genomes](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/)). That is a shift in bacterial composition in one forefield, not recovery of a whole ecosystem.

### Sousa's stabilization experiment

Sousa fixed 16 small rocks to beams anchored in the shore and left 16 loose ones nearby at matched tidal height, sampling them from July 1975 to January 1977.

### The bacterial microcosm experiments

Hall and colleagues (2012) disturbed each microcosm 0, 1, 4, 8 or 16 times in 16 days, at 10%, 99.9% or 99.999% mortality per event, with three replicate microcosms for every combination, and measured diversity as 1 - lambda among the morphotypes one strain evolves in each microcosm.

### Recovery of richness versus composition in secondary forests

Rozendaal and colleagues (2019) pooled 56 Neotropical secondary-forest sites. In the 45 sites with old-growth reference plots, the model's mean predicted tree richness 20 years after abandonment was 80% of old-growth values, but similarity in composition to old growth was only 34% of the similarity found among old-growth plots. Both are model estimates from sites of different ages, not one forest followed for 20 years, and the two numbers measure different things.
