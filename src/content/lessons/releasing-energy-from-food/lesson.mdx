## Explain: how cells release energy from sugar

<WordBank terms={['glucose', 'cellular respiration', 'oxygen', 'carbon dioxide', 'mitochondria', 'breathing', 'thermal energy', 'rate of respiration']} />

A peanut, a slice of bread and a pea seed all store energy in their molecules. Your cells must break the food down to use it.

The fuel this lesson follows is **glucose**, a sugar that stores energy in its molecules. Cells can also release energy from fats and proteins, such as those in a peanut. Plants make sugar (glucose) by photosynthesis, as you saw in [that lesson](/lessons/photosynthesis-feeds-the-world/).

**Cellular respiration** is the process in which cells break down sugar (glucose) with oxygen and release energy. **Oxygen** is a gas in the air that cells use in this process. **Carbon dioxide** is a gas that cells give off when they respire.

> sugar (glucose) + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy

Inside a cell this happens in many small steps, not one big burst. The energy comes out in a form the cell can easily use.

Most of this work happens in the **mitochondria** (one is a mitochondrion). These are small organelles where cells release energy from food. Plant cells and animal cells both have them. Chloroplasts let a plant make food. Mitochondria let a cell use food for energy. Almost all plant, animal and fungus cells have mitochondria, with a few exceptions.

**Breathing** is moving air into and out of your lungs. It is not the same thing as cellular respiration.

| | Breathing | Cellular respiration |
|---|---|---|
| Where it happens | Lungs (in you and many animals) | Inside cells of plants, animals and fungi |
| What it does | Moves air in and out | Breaks down sugar and releases energy |
| How they connect | Brings in oxygen, carries out carbon dioxide | Uses the oxygen, gives off the carbon dioxide |

Yeast is a fungus, and its cells can respire with oxygen. But yeast in sugar water also breaks sugar down another way, called fermentation, which you will study in Biology. Baker’s yeast ferments even when air is around, so most of the gas in the balloon comes from fermentation, not from respiration that uses oxygen. Both ways give off carbon dioxide. Photosynthesis and respiration work as partners, as you will see in [that lesson](/lessons/photosynthesis-and-respiration-partners/).

## Explain: where the energy goes and what changes the speed

Cells spend the released energy on work, such as contracting muscle cells or building new cell parts.

Some energy always leaves as **thermal energy**, the energy of moving particles that you feel as warmth. That is why the sprouting peas warmed their thermos. The thermos kept the thermal energy from escaping quickly.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/releasing-energy-from-food/warm-pea-thermos.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two thermoses, one full of sprouting peas with a higher thermometer reading and one full of boiled peas with a lower reading, and a zoom of a pea cell with mitochondria giving off thermal energy." caption="Living pea cells respire, and some of the released energy leaves as thermal energy, so the thermos of sprouting peas can get warmer than the room. Boiled peas are dead and stay close to room temperature." />

Your body usually loses thermal energy to the cooler air around it. Respiration in your cells keeps releasing more. This is a big reason your body stays near 37 °C, even on a cold day.

Food does not turn into energy, and energy is never used up. It changes form. In respiration, energy stored in sugar becomes energy for cell work, plus thermal energy.

The **rate of respiration** is how fast cells carry out respiration. You can measure it as how much oxygen is used, or how much carbon dioxide is given off, in one hour. For yeast, oxygen used is the better measure, because yeast also gives off carbon dioxide by fermentation. Three things change the rate:

- Temperature. Within a suitable range, warmer cells usually respire faster. Too much heat damages cells, and respiration slows or stops. Boiled peas are the extreme case: their cells are dead.
- Oxygen. With less oxygen, respiration that uses oxygen slows down.
- Sugar (glucose). With too little sugar, respiration slows. Adding more sugar helps only up to a point. In yeast, a lot of sugar makes cells ferment more and respire with oxygen less.

### Design problem

Plan an investigation to test whether sprouting peas release thermal energy. You have dried peas, water, four or more thermoses, thermometers that read in °C, and a teacher who can boil water.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do living, sprouting peas warm their surroundings?
- **Independent variable:** the peas in each thermos: living peas soaked for a day, or soaked peas that your teacher boils and then cools to room temperature (the control).
- **Dependent variable:** the temperature inside each thermos in °C, read once a day for three days.
- **Constants:** the same mass of peas (for example 200 g), the same kind of thermos, the same room, the same thermometer depth and the same reading times.
- **Repeated trials:** at least two thermoses of each kind. Find the mean temperature for each kind on each day.
- **What results would mean:** if only the living peas get warmer than the room, that is good evidence the warmth comes from their respiration. Microbes on the peas can also release thermal energy, so a warm control would mean the test needs fresh, clean peas.
- **Safety:** only the teacher boils water or handles hot peas. Do not screw the thermos lids on tightly, so carbon dioxide can escape. Do not eat the peas. Wash your hands afterwards and throw away mouldy peas.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The equation in symbols

The word equation for respiration, written in symbols, is C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy. You do not have to learn it.

### Cells without mitochondria

A few cells do not have mitochondria, such as mature red blood cells in mammals.

### Where the pea numbers come from

The USDA handbook gathers earlier measurements (ASHRAE 1985; Suslow and Cantwell 1998).
