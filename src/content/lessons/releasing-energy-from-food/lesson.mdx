## Go further: the pathway inside cells

<WordBank terms={['glucose', 'cellular respiration', 'oxygen', 'carbon dioxide', 'mitochondria', 'breathing', 'thermal energy', 'rate of respiration']} />

The first film's pathway, as a word equation:

> sugar (glucose) + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy

Plants make **glucose** by [photosynthesis](/lessons/photosynthesis-feeds-the-world/). **Cellular respiration** is the process in which cells break down sugar (glucose) with oxygen and release energy.

The equation sums up many small steps. The energy comes out in a form the cell can easily use. Cells can also release energy from fats and proteins, such as those in a peanut.

The **cytoplasm** is the jelly-like fluid that fills the cell around the **mitochondria** (one is a mitochondrion). Almost all plant, animal, and fungus cells have mitochondria, with a few exceptions.

## Follow the released energy

As the first film showed, released energy powers cell work, such as making muscle cells contract. Some of it leaves as **thermal energy**, the energy of moving particles.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/releasing-energy-from-food/warm-pea-thermos.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two thermoses, one full of sprouting peas with a higher thermometer reading and one full of boiled peas with a lower reading, and a zoom of a pea cell with mitochondria giving off thermal energy." caption="Living pea cells release thermal energy during respiration, so sprouting peas can warm a thermos while boiled peas stay near room temperature." />

Respiration warms you, too. Your body usually loses thermal energy to cooler air, while respiration keeps releasing more. Respiration is a big reason your body can stay near 37 °C even on a cold day. Staying there depends on balancing heat made with heat lost.

Food does not turn into energy, and energy is never used up; it only changes form.

Living peas keep a thermos warm only while their cells keep respiring. What makes that release go faster or slower?

## Read the evidence the second film did not show

The second film's gauges were schematic. Real **rate of respiration** data report oxygen used or carbon dioxide given off in a set time, such as one hour. **Fermentation** is a way to break down sugar without oxygen. Because yeast ferments even in air, as the second film showed, oxygen used is the better measure for yeast. A balloon is also a rough measure: cells that respire with oxygen use about as much gas as they give off.

A second study tested oxygen. Scientists grew a lab strain of baker’s yeast at 30 °C, fed it sugar in a slow, steady trickle, and changed only the oxygen in the gas bubbled in. With sugar arriving slowly and 20.9% oxygen, the yeast did not ferment. At 1.0% and 0.5% oxygen, it also fermented. Even so, oxygen used counts only the respiration that uses oxygen.

### Design problem

Plan an investigation to test whether sprouting peas release thermal energy. You have dried peas, water, four or more thermoses, thermometers that read in °C, and a teacher who can boil water.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do living, sprouting peas warm their surroundings?
- **Independent variable:** the peas in each thermos: living peas soaked for a day, or soaked peas that your teacher boils and then cools to room temperature (the control).
- **Dependent variable:** the temperature inside each thermos in °C, read once a day for three days.
- **Constants:** the same mass of peas (for example 200 g), the same kind of thermos, the same room, the same thermometer depth and the same reading times.
- **Repeated trials:** at least two thermoses of each kind. Find the mean temperature for each kind on each day.
- **What results would mean:** if only the living peas get warmer than the room, that is good evidence the warmth comes from their respiration. Microbes on the peas can also release thermal energy, so a warm control would mean the test needs fresh, clean peas.
- **Safety:** only the teacher boils water or handles hot peas. Do not screw the thermos lids on tightly, so carbon dioxide can escape. Do not eat the peas. Wash your hands afterwards and throw away mouldy peas.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Yeast can use fermentation

Yeast, a fungus, can respire with oxygen or ferment; you will study fermentation in Biology. A lot of sugar makes yeast ferment more and respire with oxygen less. Photosynthesis and respiration work as partners, as you will see in [that lesson](/lessons/photosynthesis-and-respiration-partners/).

### The equation in symbols

The word equation for respiration, written in symbols, is C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy. You do not have to learn it.

### Cells without mitochondria

A few cells do not have mitochondria, such as mature red blood cells in mammals.

### Where the pea numbers come from

The USDA handbook gathers earlier measurements (ASHRAE 1985; Suslow and Cantwell 1998).
