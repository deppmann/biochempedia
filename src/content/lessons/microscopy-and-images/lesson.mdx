  - name: "Ernst Abbe"
    contribution: >-
      Published in 1873 a theory of image formation in the microscope, which tied the finest
      detail a lens can render to the wavelength of light and to the cone of light the objective
      collects.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Abbe, Beiträge zur Theorie des Mikroskops und der mikroskopischen Wahrnehmung, Archiv für mikroskopische Anatomie 9 (1873); discussed in Latychevskaia, Applied Optics 58:3597 (2019)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Microscope makers in the nineteenth century worked to grind better lenses, and it was easy
      to suppose that a good enough lens would show anything. Abbe, a
      physicist in Jena who worked with the instrument maker Carl Zeiss, argued that this could not
      be so. In his 1873 paper in Archiv für mikroskopische Anatomie he treated a specimen as
      something that diffracts light, sending it off at angles that depend on how fine its detail
      is. Only light the objective actually collects can rebuild that detail in the image. Finer
      detail throws light at wider angles, so there is a spacing below which the light misses the
      lens altogether. That spacing scales with the wavelength divided by the objective's
      numerical aperture, the quantity that combines the collection angle with the refractive index
      of the medium. Abbe's argument explained why immersion oil and wide-angle objectives help,
      and why a bigger image alone does not. It also set the target that every super-resolution
      method since the 1990s has had to find a way around.

    story: >-
      A focused laser spot cannot be made smaller than diffraction allows, but the region that
      fluoresces can. That was the idea Hell and Jan Wichmann set out in Optics Letters in 1994.
      Excite the dye with one beam, then hit it at once with a second, ring-shaped beam that forces
      excited molecules back to the ground state by stimulated emission before they fluoresce. Only
      molecules at the dark centre of the ring are left free to glow. In 2000 Thomas Klar, Stefan

      fluorescence microscope, and Hell shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Eric Betzig
      and William E. Moerner.

    story: >-
      A single glowing molecule still looks like a blur a couple of hundred nanometres wide, but
      the centre of that blur can be found far more precisely than its width, provided no
      neighbour is glowing at the same moment. The PALM paper, published in Science in 2006 by
      Betzig and colleagues including Harald Hess and Jennifer Lippincott-Schwartz, used
      photoactivatable fluorescent proteins to arrange exactly that. A weak pulse of violet light
      switched on a sparse random few; the team located each one, bleached it, and repeated the

      positions, not from the width of the blur. They reported a localization precision of roughly
      2 to 25 nm for single molecules, in thin sections of organelles such as lysosomes and

      Betzig shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Stefan Hell and William E. Moerner.

      A new microscope needs a test object whose answer is known in advance. In their 2006 paper in
      Nature Methods, Michael Rust, Mark Bates and Zhuang built one out of DNA. They attached two
      switchable dye pairs, each made of Cy3 and Cy5, to a double-stranded DNA molecule at points
      46 nm apart along its length. Because DNA bends a little, they predicted that the average

techniques:
  - name: "Widefield and confocal fluorescence microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      A fluorescent label absorbs light of one colour and emits a longer wavelength, which is
      filtered and recorded. Widefield imaging collects light from the whole depth of the sample;
      a confocal pinhole rejects out-of-focus light, giving optical sections that can be stacked
      into a three-dimensional image. Both remain limited by diffraction.
  - name: "Indirect immunofluorescence with specificity controls"
    blurb: >-
      Cells are fixed and permeabilized, a primary antibody binds the target, and a fluorescent
      secondary antibody binds the primary. The image means something only alongside controls: a
      knockout or knockdown that loses the signal, a secondary-only sample, and single-label
      samples to check bleed-through between channels.
  - name: "Thin-section transmission electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Cells are chemically fixed, dehydrated, embedded in plastic, cut into sections about 50 to
      100 nm thick, and stained with heavy metals. The electron image is a two-dimensional
      projection of membranes and organelles in dead, prepared tissue, so artifacts of fixation
      must be ruled out.
  - name: "Single-molecule localization microscopy (PALM, STORM)"
    blurb: >-
      Fluorophores are switched on a few at a time so that no two nearby ones glow together. Each
      one's centre is located from its blurred image, and the positions from thousands of cycles
      are plotted as the final picture. Precision improves with the number of photons collected.

  - stem: "What does the pinhole in a laser-scanning confocal microscope do?"
    choices:
      - "It shrinks the focused spot below the diffraction limit"
      - "It blocks out-of-focus light to give a thin optical section"
      - "It converts the fluorescence into a shorter wavelength"
      - "It removes photon noise from the background of the image"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The pinhole blocks light that comes from above and below the focal plane, so each scan is an
      optical section and a stack of them gives a three-dimensional image. It does not beat

  - stem: "A student images GFP-labelled vesicles 150 nm apart with a widefield microscope whose ideal lateral resolution is about 227 nm, then enlarges the picture fourfold in software. What does the enlarged picture show?"
    choices:

      - "One merged spot, because enlargement adds no new detail"
      - "Two separate spots, because each pixel now covers less space"
      - "No spot at all, because enlargement spreads out the signal"

    rationale: >-
      The two vesicles are closer than the resolution, so their blurred images overlap into one

  - stem: "A researcher compares protein levels in control and treated cells by mean fluorescence intensity. In the treated images many pixels in the brightest cells sit at the camera's maximum value. What is the problem?"
    choices:
      - "The camera added extra signal to every pixel above a threshold"
      - "The treated cells must have bleached during acquisition"
      - "The saturated pixels no longer report how much label is present"
      - "The background was not subtracted from either image set"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      A pixel at the detector's maximum has stopped responding in proportion to the light, so any
      amount of label above that level gives the same number and the treated mean is
      underestimated. Bleaching lowers signal over time; it does not pin pixels at the maximum.
      Background subtraction is a separate step and would not repair clipped values. The camera
      does not add signal above a threshold; it simply cannot record more.

    rationale: >-
      d = 0.61 × 600 / 1.20 = 366 / 1.20 = 305 nm, which is larger than 280 nm, so the puncta are
      not resolved. Comparing 280 nm with a fixed 200 nm limit ignores the wavelength and the
      numerical aperture; the limit depends on both, and with red light and a water lens it is
      well above 200 nm. About 250 nm is the Abbe spacing, λ/(2NA) = 600 / 2.40, a different
      criterion from the Rayleigh one the question specifies. About 500 nm comes from dividing
      the wavelength by the NA without the 0.61 factor (600 / 1.20).

  - level: "college"

      within the resolution volume.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      The diffraction limit applies to the illumination and detection point-spread functions, not
      to information in general. STED and related methods break it by making the fluorescent state
      nonlinearly dependent on light intensity, so the effective spot narrows as depletion power

The first film showed why two 150 nm sources become one recorded peak; now put numbers on that picture. The rest of the page tests camera sampling, methods that change the measurement, and what an image can support; each follows what enters the record.

## Put numbers on the blur

Here λ is the label's fluorescence **emission wavelength**, and **numerical aperture** (NA) describes the cone of light the objective collects. **Resolution** is the smallest separation at which two point objects can be distinguished under a stated criterion. The Rayleigh criterion calls them resolved when one Airy pattern's centre falls on the other's first dark ring.

Use the ideal widefield setup behind the first film's 227 nm figure: GFP-like emission at λ = 520 nm, an oil objective with NA 1.40, and 100 nm of specimen per camera pixel. These are exercise values, not a measured **point-spread function** (the instrument's response to a point); aberrations and thick samples can do worse.

**Step 1, lateral resolution.**

> d = 0.61 × 520 / 1.40 = 317.2 / 1.40 = 226.6 nm, about 0.23 µm

The vesicles are 150 nm apart, below 226.6 nm, so they are unresolved by this criterion.

**Step 2, camera sampling.** Optics can preserve detail that a camera then samples too coarsely. A useful introductory rule is at least two camera pixels across one resolved distance.

**Predict.** Which camera samples d = 226.6 nm adequately by that rule: 100 nm specimen pixels, 150 nm specimen pixels, or both?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The 100 nm camera gives 226.6 / 100 = 2.27 pixels across d, so it just meets the rule. The 150 nm camera gives only 1.51 pixels across d, so the camera loses detail that the optics could pass.

</details>

**Step 3, change the objective.** With the same 520 nm emission but NA 1.00, d = 317.2 nm. Losing 0.4 in NA costs about 90 nm of lateral resolution in this model.

**Step 4, look along the optical axis (the depth direction).** The first film used the simple lateral case; here is the fuller three-dimensional one. With immersion index n = 1.515, the approximate axial estimate is

> dz ≈ 2λn/NA² = 2 × 520 × 1.515 / 1.40² = 804 nm

That is about 3.5 times the lateral value. A point's three-dimensional blur is therefore elongated along the optical axis.

## Change the measurement, not the zoom

**Reminder:** in the scoped widefield model, λ and NA set the Rayleigh distance. Once the camera samples finely enough, further magnification and software zoom only enlarge the record: magnification without resolution is empty magnification. To do better, change the measurement.

### Remove out-of-focus haze

Widefield collects fluorescence throughout the illuminated depth, producing haze above and below focus in thick, densely labelled specimens. A **confocal microscope** scans a focused spot; a detector pinhole blocks most out-of-focus light. Each scan is an optical section, and a stack can reconstruct a three-dimensional cell, one way to measure organelle volumes. The [previous lesson's volumes](/lessons/measured-eukaryotic-cell/) came from other methods, including areas in electron-microscope sections.

**Predict.** If a smaller confocal pinhole rejects more out-of-focus light, why not close it as far as possible?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Below one Airy unit—a pinhole as wide as the Airy pattern's central disc—a smaller opening can buy modest resolution but costs signal. The main gain is optical sectioning and contrast; a conventional confocal remains diffraction limited, unable to separate arbitrarily close points.

</details>

### Use electrons for finer structural detail

**Electron microscopes** focus an electron beam with magnetic lenses. Transmission EM (TEM) sends it through a thin specimen. Conventionally, cells are fixed, dehydrated, plastic-embedded, cut into 50 to 100 nm sections and stained with heavy metals. TEM can show membranes and organelles, but its image is a two-dimensional projection of dead, prepared tissue; fixation can create artifacts. Scanning EM (SEM) rasters a coated surface to show its shape.

In **cryo-EM**, rapid freezing traps water as glass-like vitreous ice rather than crystals. Most near-atomic cryo-EM structures come from averaging many images of purified particles. Thinned frozen cells show molecules in place, usually at lower resolution. Averaging many copies of an abundant complex such as the ribosome has produced near-atomic structures inside human cells, but not an atom-by-atom whole cell.

### Encode close fluorescent emitters

Super-resolution changes what reaches the record without making diffraction disappear.

- **STED** uses a ring-shaped depletion beam to force excited dyes around the focus back to the dark state by stimulated emission, leaving a smaller centre able to fluoresce.
- **SIM** uses striped illumination to shift finer detail into the range the objective can pass, then computes an image.
- **PALM and STORM** switch on sparse fluorophores so nearby molecules do not glow together, estimate each isolated blur's centre, and combine positions from thousands of cycles.

**Predict.** One isolated molecule makes a broad blur, but its centre is estimated to 10 nm. Does that mean two molecules 50 nm apart are resolved when they glow together?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The 10 nm value is **localization precision**, the uncertainty in one isolated centre, not resolution between simultaneous emitters. PALM and STORM separate neighbours in time. Final resolution also depends on label density and size, drift and blinking; one fluorescent protein may be counted several times. With enough photons, an isolated centre can be found to a few nanometres, and shot-noise-limited uncertainty falls as 1/√N for N detected photons.

</details>

## Read the image as a claim

The second film named the controls for each link from label to claim; here are the cases it did not work through.

### 1. Is the signal the claimed target?

An antibody can bind the wrong protein (see [protein purification](/lessons/protein-purification-techniques/)). Signal loss after target knockout supports specificity; with knockdown, the signal should fall with independently verified target reduction. A secondary-only sample checks nonspecific signal from the fluorescent secondary. A genetic tag such as GFP avoids antibodies but can alter protein location ([hacking the cell](/lessons/hacking-the-cell/) and [detecting a signal](/lessons/detecting-a-signal/)).

**Predict.** An antibody still gives the same mitochondrial pattern after its target gene is deleted, although a blot detects no target. The secondary-only sample is dark. What failed?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The primary antibody is detecting something other than the target at mitochondria. The dark secondary-only control rules out the secondary antibody, but it cannot rescue the primary antibody's specificity.

</details>

### 2. Are the channels separate?

Single-label samples measure **bleed-through** (one dye's light leaking into another channel), and multicolour beads test whether the channels are aligned, or **registered**.

### 3. Are intensities comparable?

The second film gave the rules: identical acquisition, pixels below the detector maximum, and background subtraction that leaves photon noise. The hard case is saturation.

**Predict.** Treated cells contain many pixels at the detector maximum. Can their mean fluorescence be compared quantitatively with unsaturated control cells?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. A saturated pixel has stopped increasing with the light that reaches it, so its value no longer reports how much label is present. Background subtraction cannot restore clipped information.

</details>

### 4. What does overlap support?

After the label, channel and acquisition controls pass, an in-focus two-colour overlap can support proximity at roughly one resolution volume; widefield haze can create apparent overlap from farther away. For the first film's green-light exercise, that volume is about 230 nm laterally by 800 nm axially. A red channel usually has a longer emission wavelength and a larger blur, so a real two-colour analysis needs the measured resolution of each channel. Treating the green-light volume as an ellipsoid gives roughly 0.02 µm³, which contains tens of thousands of protein molecules at typical cellular protein density. Colocalization supports proximity, not binding. The design problem asks what it would take to claim more.

### Design problem

A paper shows an antibody to protein P staining mitochondria. Yellow overlap with a mitochondrial marker is offered as proof that P binds the marker. What would you require before accepting either claim?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

First test the stain. In P knockout or knockdown cells imaged identically, mitochondrial signal should fall with P on a blot. Add a secondary-only control and independent placement: tagged P checked for function or biochemical fractionation placing P in mitochondria.

Then test proximity with single-label samples, registration beads and unsaturated raw images, so any overlap coefficient uses real intensities.

Passing those tests places the proteins only within one resolution volume (about 230 nm laterally by 800 nm axially here). Co-immunoprecipitation supports the same complex, and a proximity method supports close approach; neither proves direct contact. Purified-protein binding tests direct interaction, and interaction-breaking mutations should remove binding without changing protein amount or location.

</details>

## Going deeper

The optical assumptions, sampling convention and experimental exceptions behind the main line.

### Where NA and the Airy pattern come from

Light diffracts through the objective's circular opening, so one point forms a bright disc with faint rings, an **Airy pattern**. Two independent emitters produce patterns whose intensities add. Ernst Abbe's 1873 theory described fine detail sending light to wider angles; an objective can rebuild only the detail carried by light it collects.

Numerical aperture is

> NA = n × sin θ

Here n is the refractive index between sample and lens, and θ is half the collected cone angle. In air n = 1.00, so NA cannot exceed 1. Immersion oil has n ≈ 1.515, allowing high-NA oil objectives near 1.4. Deep in thick, watery samples, a water objective that better matches the sample can give a sharper image. A fluorescent label absorbs one colour and emits a longer wavelength; that emission wavelength sets the detected blur.

That is why “the light limit is 200 nm” is incomplete: state the wavelength, NA, direction and criterion.

### Rayleigh and Nyquist are different questions

The two-pixel rule in the main line is introductory. Strict Nyquist sampling follows the finest spatial frequency the objective passes. Here λ/(2NA) = 186 nm, so pixels should be about 93 nm or smaller. Thus 100 nm pixels give more than two pixels across the 226.6 nm Rayleigh distance but still slightly under-sample by this stricter rule; careful work often uses smaller pixels.

### Evidence and history behind super-resolution

Hell and Wichmann proposed STED in 1994. In 2000 Klar, Jakobs, Dyba, Egner and Hell reported a fluorescent spot about 90 to 110 nm across, with smaller gains in live yeast and *E. coli*. Gustafsson reported a doubling of lateral resolution with SIM in 2000. PALM, STORM and FPALM were published in 2006 by Betzig and colleagues, Rust, Bates and Zhuang, and Hess, Girirajan and Mason, respectively. MINFLUX reported about 1 nm localization precision in 2017, but on DNA origami, not in a cell. The 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Eric Betzig, Stefan Hell and William E. Moerner; no one person invented super-resolution.

### Electron wavelength is not specimen resolution

At 100 kV an electron's wavelength is around 0.004 nm, but preparation, contrast and beam damage make practical biological resolution far coarser. The probe's wavelength is only one link in the measurement chain.

### Labelling can move the target

Fixation is an experimental variable, and incomplete fixation can let labelling move proteins. In primary human lymphatic endothelial cells made to overexpress LYVE-1, antibodies clustered the receptor on unpermeabilised cells fixed with formaldehyde alone; adding glutaraldehyde largely prevented the clustering. The long argument over Golgi's silver stain in nerve tissue, described in [the neuron doctrine](/lessons/neuron-doctrine/), was likewise an argument about what a stain reveals.
