## The big idea

Here is a sorting game for a hard question: what makes something alive?

Living things share a set of features. They grow. They need food, water and air. They respond to what happens around them, like a plant bending toward a window or a cat running from a loud noise. They make more of their own kind. No single feature settles it. A toy car moves, and a fire grows, but neither does all of these things.

Virginia’s kindergarten standard uses two groups. **Living** means anything that is alive now or was ever alive, so a dry leaf or a log counts. **Nonliving** means anything that never was, like a rock or a spoon. Older children can split "living" into "alive now" and "once alive."

## What to do

1. **Gather.** Put 10 to 12 things in a basket, plus the dry beans. Mix things that are alive now (a houseplant, an apple, a fresh leaf), things that were once alive (a dry leaf, a stick, a shell), and things that never were (a rock, a spoon, a toy car).
2. **First guess.** Ask your child to sort everything into two piles, "alive" and "not alive," with no help from you. Then ask, "How did you decide?" Write down their rule in their words. Don’t correct it yet.
3. **Build a checklist.** Look at something everyone agrees is alive, such as your child, a pet or a houseplant. Ask, "What does it do that the rock doesn’t?" Help them land on four questions: Does it grow? Does it need food, water and air? Does it make more of its own kind? Does it react when things change around it?
4. **Test each object.** Go through the basket one thing at a time and check it against the list. Let your child move things between piles as they go.
5. **Try the tricky ones.** Spend the most time on the seed, the toy car and the dry leaf. A seed doesn’t seem to do anything. Ask, "What would happen if we planted it?" The car moves. Ask, "Does it grow? Can it make a baby car?" The dry leaf isn’t growing now. Ask, "Was it ever part of something alive?"
6. **Final sort.** Use Virginia’s two groups: living (alive now or ever alive) and nonliving (never alive). For ages 8 to 10, use three piles instead: alive now, once alive, never alive.
7. **Walk outside (optional).** With an adult, spend 10 minutes in your own yard or on a park path finding three living things and three nonliving things. Stay on the path or short grass, away from tall grass, roads and water. Look, don’t pick, unless it is a fallen leaf or stick that you are sure is not poison ivy (leaves in groups of three).

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder if this seed is alive. What could we do to find out?"
- "The car moves. Does it grow? Does it eat?"
- "Does this rock need anything to keep going? What happens to a plant with no water?"
- "Was this stick ever alive? How can you tell?"
- "What do you need to stay alive? Does a dog need the same things? Does a tree?"
- "Can you find something that breaks our rule?"

## Why it works

Scientists have no one-line test for life either. They look at features together: being made of cells, taking in energy from food or light, growing, responding to surroundings, and reproducing. Your child’s checklist is a simple version of that list.

The seed is the best puzzle. A dry seed is alive but resting. It uses very little of its stored food, and many seeds can wait months or years until water and warmth let them sprout. The toy car and the fire each meet one or two items on the checklist, which is why the full list matters more than any single clue.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use only two piles and six to eight objects, with clear examples (a plant, an apple, a rock, a spoon) and one puzzle (a seed). Ask just three questions: Does it grow? Does it eat or drink? Can it have babies? Your child can draw instead of write.

### Ages 8 to 10
Use three piles (alive now, once alive, never alive) and add harder items: a cotton sock, a sheet of paper, a wooden spoon, an unlit beeswax candle (never light anything for this activity). Each was made from something, so ask what. Have your child write a rule, then hunt for an object that breaks it and change the rule to fit.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If your child gets stuck, go back to themselves: "Do you grow? Do you eat? Did you start as a baby?" Then compare an object to that. If they want a yes-or-no answer for fire or the car, it’s fine to leave it open and say, "Scientists use the whole list. Let’s check it again." Leaving a question open is part of science.
