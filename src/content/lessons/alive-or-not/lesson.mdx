## The big idea

Use this 25-minute sort to practice looking closely. Your child names a rule and checks it against tricky objects. See why it takes more than one clue to decide whether something is alive.

**Say it together:**
> "Living things grow. They need water, air, and food—animals eat food, and plants use light to make their own. They react to what happens around them, and their kind can make more like them."

Living things share these features together. A plant bends toward a window, and a cat runs from a loud noise. A kind of living thing can make more of its own kind even if some individuals never have young. A kitten is already alive before it can have kittens. No single feature decides it. A toy car moves, and a fire grows, yet neither does all of these things.

<details>
<summary>For teachers: Virginia standards</summary>

Virginia’s kindergarten standard uses two groups. **Living** means anything that is alive now or was ever alive, so a dry leaf or a fallen log counts. **Nonliving** means anything that never was, like a rock or a metal spoon. Older children (ages 8 to 10) can split "living" into "alive now" and "once alive."

</details>

<SafetyNote>
- **Adult close by:** Stay with your child the whole time. An adult gathers and checks basket items, handles any heavy or breakable pot, and picks up any outdoor leaf or stick.
- **Choking and small items:** Dry beans, small shells, and pebbles can be swallowed, breathed in, or pushed into a nose or ear. Use large beans so they are easy to see and count. Even a large bean can choke a child. Watch closely to keep beans out of mouths. Count them out and back into a lidded jar. Keep all small objects away from children under 4, who are most likely to choke.
- **No tasting and emergency help:** Nothing in the basket is for eating or chewing—raw beans can make people sick and many houseplants are poisonous. **If a child is choking or struggling to breathe, call 911.** If a child swallows a bean or part of a plant, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222.
- **Allergies and handwashing:** Check for allergies to pets, plants or pollen, beans, apples or other basket foods, and bee or wasp stings. Wash hands with soap and water after handling soil, leaves, a pet, or anything from outside.
- **Water and outdoors:** On the walk, check the weather, dress for sun and weather, and stay in your yard, a public park, or another place where you have permission. **Never leave a child alone near water of any depth**, and stay within arm’s reach near roads or water.

## What to do

1. **Gather (adult step).** Put 10 to 12 everyday things in a basket, plus 1 or 2 large dry kitchen beans. Mix things that are alive now (houseplant, apple, fresh leaf), things that were alive or came from living things (dry leaf, stick, smooth shell), and things that never lived (rock, metal spoon, toy car).
2. **First guess.** Say, *"Sort these into two piles: alive and not alive."* Do not correct yet. Ask, *"How did you decide?"* Write down their rule in their words. Let your child make the first sort on their own. If they put the car in "alive" or the bean or plant in "not alive," ask how movement figured into their rule.
3. **Build a checklist, one clue at a time.** Point to your child, a pet, or the houseplant beside the rock. Ask, *"What does it do that the rock does not?"* Build four questions:
   - *Does it grow?*
   - *Does it need water, air, and food?* (A plant uses light to make its own food.)
   - *Do things like it make more of their own kind?* (Your child is alive before having babies; this is about the whole kind, like cats having kittens.)
   - *Does it react when things change around it?*
4. **Work one easy case first, then test.** Check the rock together against all four questions so your child sees how the list works. Then let them test the rest one by one and move items between piles. Remind them that a resting seed will not show every clue today.
5. **Predict, then check the tricky ones.** Spend extra time on the dry bean, toy car, and dry leaf. Ask your child to predict first:
   - **Dry bean:** *"What do you think will happen if we put a dry bean in a damp paper towel?"* Write down the prediction. In the next activity, Wake Up a Seed, put a bean in a damp paper towel and watch it over a few days. Compare what you see with the prediction.
   - **Toy car:** *"It moves. Does it grow? Can it make a baby car?"*
   - **Dry leaf:** *"It is not growing now. Was it ever part of something alive?"*
   - **Apple and fresh leaf:** Each is part of a plant, not a whole plant. Its cells stay alive for a while after picking, then slowly die. Either way, it belongs in the living group.
6. **Final sort and debrief.** Sort into two piles: **living** (alive now or ever alive) and **nonliving** (never alive). For ages 8 to 10, use three piles: alive now, once alive, never alive. Close with two debrief questions:
   - *"Which object surprised you most, and which clue changed your mind?"*
   - *"If someone hands you something new tomorrow, what will you check?"*
7. **Walk outside (optional).** Check the weather, dress for sun and weather, and stay in your yard, a public park, or another place where you have permission. Spend 10 minutes finding three living and three nonliving things. Stay on the path or short grass, away from tall grass, roads, and water; never leave your child alone near water of any depth. Look, do not pick. An adult picks up any fallen leaf or stick only where no poison ivy grows nearby (dry leaves and hairy winter vines still cause a rash). Wash hands afterward.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder if this seed is alive. What could we do to find out?"
- "The car moves. Does it grow? Does it eat?"
- "Does this rock need anything to keep going? What happens to a plant with no water?"
- "Was this stick ever alive? How can you tell?"
- "What do you need to stay alive? Does a dog need the same things? Does a tree?"
- "Can you find something that breaks our rule?"

## Why it works

Scientists have no one-line test for life either. They look at features together: being made of cells, taking in energy from food or light, growing, responding to surroundings, and reproducing. Your child’s checklist is a simple version of that list.

The seed is the best puzzle. A dry seed is alive but resting. It uses very little of its stored food, and many seeds can wait months or years until water and warmth let them sprout. The toy car and fire each seem alive in one or two ways, like moving or growing. That is why the full list matters more than any single clue.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use two piles and six to eight objects: clear examples (a plant, an apple, a rock, a metal spoon) and one puzzle (a seed). Ask just three questions: *Does it grow? Does it eat or drink? Can things like it have babies?* Your child can draw instead of write.

### Ages 8 to 10
Use three piles (alive now, once alive, never alive) and add harder items: a cotton sock, a sheet of paper, a wooden spoon, and an unlit beeswax candle (never light anything for this activity). Ask what each was made from. Paper and cotton come from plant parts that were once alive. Beeswax, like a shell, was made by a living animal but was never made of living cells, so your child may add a pile called "made by a living thing." Have them write a rule, hunt for an object that breaks it, and revise the rule to fit.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If your child gets stuck, come back to themselves: *"Do you grow? Do you eat? Did you start as a baby?"* Then compare the object to that. If they want a simple yes or no for fire or the car, leave it open and say, *"Scientists use the whole list. Let’s check it again."* Leaving a question open is part of science.
