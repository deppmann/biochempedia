summary: >-
  The vestibular nerve reports each head movement on two overlapping channels, whoever caused it: a
  steady, precise firing rate and a higher-gain, well-timed signal.
  People with severe loss in both balance organs can see the world blur or bounce as they walk,
  because the reflex that holds the eyes steady has lost its input. Yet a different reflex, the one
  that steadies the head, would fight every turn you make yourself if it took the turn for a
  disturbance. In the
  canals, regular afferents follow how fast the head is turning in a steady, precise firing rate;
  irregular afferents, many from the central zone where calyx endings wrap type I hair cells, have
  higher gain, respond more to how quickly the motion is changing and time their spikes precisely.
  Otolith afferents show the same contrast for linear acceleration and tilt. In the head-posture
  neurons tested in monkeys, the response to your own turn is largely cancelled when neck feedback
  matches the motor command's prediction: they pass on the surprise, which may help the head reflex
  spare intended turns. You will resolve why the
  low-gain fibre detects small rotations better, see why a type I hair cell can drive its calyx
  without vesicles, predict when central neurons cancel your own head turns, and weigh what a
  vestibular implant restores and what it costs. Going deeper holds the numbers, the separate
  cerebellar story of reflex-gain learning and the implant's statistics.

techniques:
  - name: "Dual patch clamp of hair cell and calyx"
    blurb: >-
      One pipette records the hair cell and another records the calyx, so presynaptic current and
      postsynaptic response are measured at once. It lets an experimenter separate a slow potassium
      accumulation from a fast resistive coupling.
  - name: "Membrane-capacitance assay of exocytosis"
    blurb: >-
      When vesicles fuse, the cell membrane gains area and its capacitance rises. Dividing a
      capacitance jump by a per-vesicle value converts it to a vesicle count, with the assumption
      that the conversion holds for that cell. In type I hair cells a fast transient capacitance
      change comes from potassium-channel gating, not vesicle fusion, so only the sustained
      component counts as release.
  - name: "Single-unit recording in alert macaques"
    blurb: >-
      A fine electrode records one neuron while the animal makes or receives head movements, with
      search coils tracking head and gaze. It shows what a neuron did in that task, and it is
      correlational unless paired with a manipulation.
  - name: "Finite-element electrodiffusion modelling"
    blurb: >-
      The cleft is divided into small elements and the electrical potential and ion concentrations
      are computed in each. It predicts variables that cannot be measured in a space this narrow,
      and its outputs are predictions until a measurement tests them.

practiceQuestions:
- stem: A vestibular afferent fires at evenly spaced intervals at rest, has low gain, and responds to slow head
    rotation with a firing rate that rises and falls with head velocity. Which description fits it best?
  choices:
  - An irregular afferent that codes rapid motion in spike timing
  - A regular afferent that codes head velocity in firing rate
  - An irregular afferent from the central zone with calyx endings
  - A regular afferent that stays silent until the head moves
  answer: 1
  rationale: Evenly spaced resting intervals, low gain and a rate that follows slow rotation mark a regular afferent,
    which carries most of its information in firing rate and tracks how fast the head is moving. Irregular afferents
    have variable resting intervals, higher gain and more information in spike timing, so both irregular choices
    describe the other channel. Regular and irregular afferents both fire at a high resting rate, which lets them
    signal motion in either direction, so an afferent that is silent until the head moves is wrong.
- stem: Which pairing of vestibular hair-cell type and afferent ending is correct?
  choices:
  - 'Type II: flask-shaped, with small bouton endings'
  - 'Type I: flask-shaped, enclosed by one calyx'
  - 'Type I: cylindrical, with small bouton endings'
  - 'Type II: flask-shaped, enclosed by one calyx'
  answer: 1
  rationale: Type I hair cells are flask-shaped and their afferent calyx almost completely encloses the basolateral
    membrane. The cylindrical shape with several small bouton endings belongs to type II cells, so calling a type
    I cell cylindrical with boutons swaps the two types. Placing a calyx on a type II cell gives it the wrong ending,
    and a flask-shaped type II cell with boutons mixes a type I shape with a type II ending.
- stem: In the mature mouse utricle, type I hair cells lacking otoferlin or CaV1.3 could still drive calyx action
    potentials. What does this support?
  choices:
  - Type I cells release glutamate only through gap junctions
  - Type I cells have no ribbon synapses or calcium channels
  - A route needing no vesicle fusion can drive the calyx
  - The calyx fires without any input from the hair cell
  answer: 2
  rationale: Both proteins serve calcium-dependent vesicle release, so calyx spikes that survive their loss support
    a transmission route that does not need vesicle fusion, in this assay. Gap junctions are not the proposed route,
    since non-quantal transmission is defined as lacking them. Type I cells do have ribbon synapses and calcium-dependent
    quantal release, so the claim of none is wrong. Calyx spikes here still followed the hair cell, so they did
    not arise independently of it.
- stem: Which statement defines non-quantal transmission at the hair cell to calyx synapse?
  choices:
  - Glutamate released by the calyx back onto the hair cell
  - Direct current flow through connexin channels joining the two cells
  - A double quantum of glutamate released by vesicle fusion
  - A change in calyx potential with no vesicle fusion and no gap junctions
  answer: 3
  rationale: The definition is a change in calyx potential without vesicular exocytosis and without gap junctions.
    A double quantum is still vesicular and so still quantal. Current through connexin channels is conventional
    electrical transmission, which is exactly what this mechanism is said not to be. Glutamate released by the calyx
    would still be vesicular, so it is not this mechanism, although the non-quantal coupling itself can run in both
    directions.
- stem: In alert macaques, vestibular afferents respond to active and passive head turns, while recorded vestibular-only
    neurons respond mainly to passive turns, except when neck feedback differs from what the motor command predicts.
    What does this indicate?
  choices:
  - Afferents switch off during self-generated head motion
  - Central cancellation depends on matched expected feedback
  - Neck proprioception alone cancels any head motion signal
  - Hair cells adapt faster during voluntary head turns
  answer: 1
  rationale: 'The afferents keep responding, so the cancellation is central, and it appears only when the neck feedback
    matches the prediction from the motor command, in the neurons tested. A peripheral switch-off is ruled out by
    the afferent responses. Faster hair-cell adaptation is not what the experiment manipulated. Neck proprioception
    without a matching prediction does not cancel: the neurons responded when the feedback did not match.'

    a smooth rise and fall in firing rate. Afferent B has highly variable resting intervals and higher gain, and

  choices:
  - B fits the fast channel, which carries rapid motion in spike timing
  - A fits the fast channel, since its resting spikes are the most even
  - B's calyx by itself proves that its discharge is irregular
  - A must lack type II hair-cell input, since it fires regularly
  answer: 0
  rationale: 'Variable resting intervals, high gain and spikes locked to a fast vibration mark an irregular afferent,
    the channel that carries rapid motion in the timing of its spikes, and irregular afferents mostly arise in the

    sensitivity fell, and it rose again on catch trials when the load was removed unexpectedly. Time constants were
    34 plus or minus 7 trials for head velocity and 24 plus or minus 8 for neuronal sensitivity. Which reading is

  - Sensitivity follows a mismatch between expected and actual feedback
  - The neurons caused the behavioural change, since 24 is less than 34
  - The load changed the hair cells so they gave a smaller signal
  - The load fatigued peripheral afferents, which lowered sensitivity
  answer: 0
  rationale: A sensitivity that drops as a prediction improves and rebounds when the prediction is violated is consistent

**People with severe loss in both balance organs, for example from an antibiotic that damages hair cells, can find that the world blurs or jumps as they walk, and in the dark they often become unsteady and can fall. Their vestibular input to the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) is greatly reduced; when gaze is held on a target, this reflex turns the eyes against head rotation to hold the image still. Healthy balance organs report each head movement fast enough for the eyes and neck to counter it. Yet a different reflex, the one that steadies the head on the neck, would fight every turn you make yourself if it took the turn for a disturbance. How does one nerve report motion both precisely and quickly, and how does the brain set your own turns aside?**

This lesson answers the two puzzles in turn. The first is about speed and precision, and the nerve answers it with two overlapping kinds of signal. The second is about authorship: the nerve reports a movement, but something must decide whether you made it.

[Hearing and balance](/lessons/hearing-and-balance/) gave you the canals, which sense rotation and over ordinary movements report roughly the head's rotational velocity, the otoliths, which sense linear acceleration and tilt, and the three-neuron reflex arc. This lesson starts one step later, at the nerve fibres (**afferents**) that leave those organs, and one step further in, at the first central neurons that read them.

## Two channels for one head movement

The afferents carry head motion to the brain, where it drives the reflexes that keep vision and posture steady and feeds the sense of heading (Eatock, 2025). They can be split two ways. The split by organ tells you *what* a fibre reports. The split by **regularity**, which this lesson is about, tells you *how* it reports it, and every organ sends both kinds.

In mammals both kinds fire at a high rate with the head still, so each can signal motion in either direction by speeding up or slowing down. Regularity at rest is measured by the coefficient of variation (CV) of the intervals between spikes: roughly a few per cent for the most regular fibres, tens of per cent for the most irregular. Regularity is a continuum, and "regular" and "irregular" name its two ends. One more term: a fibre's **gain** is how much its rate changes for a given motion.

| | What the organ senses | Regular afferents | Irregular afferents |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Canals** | rotation of the head | rate follows angular velocity, closely over everyday turns | higher gain; respond more to how quickly the rotation changes; precisely timed spikes |
| **Otoliths** | linear acceleration and tilt | rate follows linear acceleration, including gravity's pull in a held tilt | higher gain; respond more to rapid changes; adapt faster to a held tilt |

These are overlapping tendencies of two populations, not two exact channels.

### The signature: two spike trains

Picture one canal fibre of each kind side by side through four conditions.

| | Regular afferent | Irregular afferent |
|---|---|---|
| Head still | fires like a clock | a similar high rate, with uneven gaps |
| Slow head turn | rate rises and falls smoothly with head velocity | rate changes more for the same turn (higher gain) |
| Fast change in motion | keeps following velocity | responds more strongly, with spikes that lock to the fast event |
| Long turn at steady speed | signal fades, with the canal | signal fades, and it adapts faster |

Read down the columns. Most of a regular canal fibre's information is in its rate. An irregular fibre favours higher frequencies: as movements get faster it responds more and more to *how quickly* the velocity is changing, roughly the head's acceleration, and it carries more information in the precise timing of its spikes (Eatock, 2018; Lacquaniti and colleagues, 2023).

The fading in a long turn belongs to the canals, as Hearing and balance showed. A held tilt is different: it changes how gravity pulls along an otolith fibre's preferred direction, so regular otolith fibres keep reporting it for as long as it lasts.

### A paradox: lower gain, better detection

Here is something that should puzzle you. In the canals, regular fibres have *lower* gain, yet recordings show they detect *small* rotations better.

**Predict.** Take two made-up fibres, each at rest near 100 spikes per second. The regular one has a gain of 0.5 spikes per second per degree per second, and a CV of 0.05. The irregular one has three times the gain, 1.5, but ten times the CV, 0.5. Which detects a slow, small head turn first? One hint: a rate measured over a stretch of time wobbles, and the wobble is bigger for a fibre with a bigger CV.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A change in rate is useful only if it stands out from the rate's own wobble. For a simple made-up decoder that counts spikes in a window, call the **detection threshold** the turn that shifts the mean rate by one standard deviation of that wobble: threshold = noise / gain. A rough rule for long windows puts the rate's standard deviation at √(CV² × rate / time).

- Regular, over 1 second, by the rough rule: √(0.05² × 100 / 1) = 0.5 spikes per second of noise, so the threshold is 0.5 / 0.5 = 1 degree per second.
- Irregular, over 1 second: √(0.5² × 100 / 1) = 5 spikes per second of noise, so the threshold is 5 / 1.5 = about 3.3 degrees per second.

The quiet, low-gain fibre wins, because its noise is far smaller and its gain only three times smaller. For so regular a fibre the rough rule is already off at 1 second: counted exactly, for model spike trains and a window placed at a random moment, the noise is about 0.65 against 5.0 spikes per second, and the thresholds are **about 1.3 against 3.3 degrees per second**. The ranking holds, with the regular fibre ahead about 2.6-fold.

Now suppose our counting decoder must report a brief change within 0.1 second, so its window shrinks to 0.1 second and holds only about ten spikes. Counted exactly, the thresholds rise to **about 7.1 and 10.9 degrees per second**, and the regular fibre's lead shrinks to about 1.5-fold. In this toy, a fast local rate estimate is much less reliable, which leaves room for the irregular fibre's other strength; other decoding strategies, and real afferents' frequency-dependent gains, can shift the trade-off.

</details>

### Irregular at rest, precise when it matters

"Irregular" describes a fibre with nothing to report. Give it something fast and the picture changes. Researchers played the same noisy head motion, roughly like natural movement, again and again, and compared the spike trains from each repeat. A fibre whose spikes are locked to the motion fires at nearly the same moments on every repeat; one whose spikes are pure noise does not. Here **information** means how well a spike train tells one motion from another.

**Predict.** If an irregular fibre's spikes were just noise, could their timing tell one motion waveform from another?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It could not: their timing would carry nothing about the motion. Instead, an irregular afferent's precisely timed spikes told different waveforms apart, while a regular afferent carried more of its information in its rate (Lacquaniti and colleagues, 2023).

</details>

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** The vestibular nerve reports head motion on two overlapping channels, whoever caused it. In the canals, regular afferents follow how fast the head is turning in a steady, precise rate, and irregular afferents add higher gain, more response to how quickly the motion is changing, and precisely timed spikes; otolith afferents show the same contrast for linear acceleration and tilt. Centrally, in the head-posture neurons tested in monkeys, the response to a turn you make yourself is largely cancelled when neck feedback matches what the motor command predicts. They pass on the surprise.

The idea covers mammalian afferents and the vestibular-only neurons recorded in monkeys later in this lesson; not every central vestibular neuron cancels self-generated motion. The next sections take it apart: where each channel comes from, how the fast one may be made fast, and what the brain subtracts.

## Where each channel comes from

Each channel has a home in the organ, as a population pattern. Mammalian vestibular organs contain two hair-cell types. A **type I** cell is flask-shaped, and its afferent ending, a cup-shaped **calyx**, almost completely encloses the basolateral membrane. A **type II** cell is cylindrical and receives several small bouton endings. Both types occur across each organ, in proportions that vary by organ and species. Each organ also has a **central zone**, the striola of the otolith maculae and the centre of each canal crista, and a **peripheral zone** around it.

One afferent can carry more than one kind of ending, which gives three kinds of fibre. The table maps populations, not single cells: regular discharge can come from fibres with calyx endings, irregular discharge from fibres with only boutons, and there is no compelling evidence that the calyx alone sets a fibre's regularity (Contini and colleagues, 2022).

| Fibre | Its endings | Mostly found in | Usual discharge |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Calyx-only** | calyces on type I cells | central zone | irregular |
| **Dimorphic** | calyces on type I cells and boutons on type II cells | both zones | irregular in the central zone, regular in the peripheral zone |
| **Bouton-only** | boutons on type II cells | peripheral zone | regular |

How was this map made? For decades, researchers recorded single afferents, filled them with dye, reconstructed their endings, and matched each fibre's firing to the zone and endings it came from, in chinchillas, turtles and other species (reviewed by Contini and colleagues, 2022).

What is each channel for? In the canals, the rate channel tracks angular velocity and detects small rotations with lower thresholds; regular otolith afferents also give a sustained signal of orientation relative to gravity. Reviews propose that the timing channel gives the reflexes that steady gaze and posture fast, precisely timed input during rapid movement. But silencing irregular fibres in monkeys left the VOR largely unchanged for the stimuli tested, so how much each reflex needs the fast channel is still open (Minor and Goldberg, 1991).

The fast channel can also be read in the clinic. Sound and bone-conducted vibration mostly activate irregular otolith afferents from the striola. That is why the vestibular-evoked myogenic potential (VEMP), a reflex response of neck or eye muscles to sound or vibration, reports on this pathway (Pastras and colleagues, 2023).

## A synapse that need not wait for vesicles

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Many irregular fibres start at central-zone type I cells and their calyces, so the synapse there is one place to look for their speed, though not the only one. Begin with what the two cell types share. Both hair-cell types release glutamate by calcium-dependent **quantal** release, in packets of one vesicle each, at ribbon synapses, release sites where a protein ribbon tethers vesicles (Spaiardi and colleagues, 2022). The tempting shortcut, type I as the non-vesicular cell and type II as the vesicular one, is wrong.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/vestibular-system/type-i-vs-type-ii-hair-cells.webp" width={1200} height={776} alt="AI-generated illustration: two vestibular hair cells side by side, a flask-shaped type I cell wrapped by a cup-like calyx and a cylindrical type II cell touching several small boutons. What differs is how much each cell releases, and whether it has another route. Release can be counted from membrane capacitance, because each fusing vesicle adds membrane. In mature mouse utricles, sustained calcium-evoked exocytosis measured this way was about tenfold smaller in type I than in type II cells. For type II cells, the large vesicle pools would favour sustained, tonic or low-frequency transmission. Type I cells release fewer vesicles, and they may have a second route that needs none.

In **non-quantal transmission** the hair cell changes its calyx's voltage directly, through what it does to the cleft they share. Formally, it is a change in calyx potential without vesicular exocytosis and without gap junctions (Govindaraju and colleagues, 2023). Its appeal is speed. At a chemical synapse a vesicle has to fuse, glutamate has to cross the cleft, and receptors have to open, and each step adds delay. The fast electrical part of a non-quantal route skips those steps, while its potassium part builds more slowly, and reviews propose that it evolved to give the reflex pathways fast, reliable input (Eatock, 2018).

**Predict.** Otoferlin and CaV1.3, a calcium channel, are two proteins that vesicle release in hair cells needs. Remove either one in mature mice. If vesicle release were a type I cell's only way to drive its calyx, what would the calyx do?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It would stop responding to the hair cell: the spikes the hair cell evokes would disappear, whatever firing the calyx makes on its own. Instead, in the mature mouse utricle, type I cells lacking otoferlin or CaV1.3 could still drive calyx action potentials (Spaiardi and colleagues, 2022). The authors took that to support a functional role for non-quantal transmission. The test was whether spikes survive, in vitro, not whether the signal is normal.

</details>

### How the cleft can move the calyx

The calyx wraps the type I cell so closely that the cleft between them is very narrow, and ions leave it only slowly. When the hair cell depolarizes, more potassium current flows out of it into that thin space. Potassium accumulates there, and the current, crossing the cleft's resistance on its way out, makes the cleft electrically positive. Two consequences follow. The [action potential](/lessons/action-potential/) lesson's balance-point rule explains the first: for a compartment with fixed conductances, one common outside voltage and no other net current, the steady voltage is the average of its reversal potentials, weighted by each conductance. The calyx breaks the common-outside condition, because its inner face sees the cleft and its outer face the bath, so here the rule gives the direction of a change, not the absolute voltage. The second consequence needs only a subtraction.

**Predict.** The calyx's inner membrane, the one facing the cleft, has potassium channels, and potassium's reversal potential there (E_K, the voltage at which potassium stops flowing) depends on the potassium concentration in the cleft. With illustrative values of 140 mM potassium inside the calyx and 37 °C, a cleft at 7 mM gives an E_K of about −80 mV. Raise the cleft to 20 mM and E_K is about −52 mV. Which way does the calyx's voltage move?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Up. Raising cleft potassium moves E_K toward positive, so, with the other conductances fixed, the weighted average moves up and the calyx tends to depolarize. The 7 mM is the model's resting cleft value; the 20 mM is made up to show the direction.

The second consequence is faster. Follow it in three steps.

1. Current leaving the hair cell makes the cleft itself positive.
2. The potassium current across the calyx's inner face is g_K × (V_calyx − V_cleft − E_K), so a more positive cleft, like a higher E_K, shrinks the driving force: less potassium leaves the calyx there, or some enters.
3. The calyx depolarizes as the cleft potential changes, with no vesicle to fuse.

</details>

In the model of Govindaraju and colleagues, the electrical potential in the cleft changes first and gives the initial change in driving force on the calyx; potassium accumulation contributes later. That order is a model output, not a measurement in the cleft.

### Three kinds of evidence, three animals

The evidence comes from different animals and methods.

| Preparation | What was done | What it showed | Measured or modelled |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mature mouse utricle, in vitro | capacitance; otoferlin and CaV1.3 knockouts | type I exocytosis about tenfold smaller; calyx spikes survive the knockouts | measured |
| Neonatal rat saccule, in vitro | hair cell and calyx recorded separately | maturing channels shorten the receptor potential's rise; some immature calyces show non-quantal transmission with little delay | measured |
| Isolated turtle posterior-canal epithelium | dual voltage clamp of hair cell and calyx together | a slow component and a much faster one, each working in both directions | measured, in turtle |
| Finite-element model with adult rat geometry | electrodiffusion computed in the cleft | cleft potential first, potassium accumulation later | predicted |

In the turtle, the slow component fits potassium building up in the cleft. For the fast one, which accumulation cannot explain, Contini, Holstein and Art proposed potassium-sensitive resistive coupling: the two cells behave as if joined through a resistance, so current in one changes the other's voltage almost at once. In both directions means current or potassium from either cell changed the other. These are turtle recordings, not mammalian latencies.

The fast channel is built during development. In mice, as low-voltage-activated potassium channels of the KV1 and KV7 families are acquired over the first postnatal weeks, hair-cell receptor potentials become faster, transmission from type I cells gains a non-quantal component, and afferent firing becomes more irregular (Eatock, 2025).

## Passing on the surprise

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Now the second puzzle: how can the head reflex spare your own turns? Does the ear itself tell a self-made turn from an imposed one? It does not. Semicircular-canal afferents in alert monkeys respond to head motion whether the animal causes it or something else does (Cullen and Minor, 2002). So any cancellation of your own movement must happen centrally.

Roy and Cullen (2004) recorded **vestibular-only neurons**, neurons of the vestibular nuclei that respond to head motion but not to eye movements, in alert macaques. These neurons are thought to drive the vestibulocollic reflex, which turns the neck against an imposed head movement to steady the head. For a voluntary turn the brain has two signals to compare. It has an internal copy of the motor command sent to the neck muscles, called an efference copy, from which it can predict the neck's feedback. And it has the neck's actual sense of stretch and position (**neck proprioception**).

**Predict.** Compare one head turn in three conditions: the monkey is turned passively; the monkey turns its own head and the neck feedback matches the motor command; the monkey turns its own head but the neck feedback is made not to match. In which conditions does a vestibular-only neuron respond strongly?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It responded strongly to the passive turn and to the active turn with mismatched feedback, and weakly to the active turn with matched feedback. In the neurons tested, cancellation of the response to active motion required neck proprioceptive activation that matched the feedback expected from the motor command. Neither the motor command nor neck feedback alone was enough.

</details>

*Illustration with made-up numbers.* Suppose a passive turn peaking at 20 degrees per second evokes a 40 spikes per second modulation, a sensitivity of 2.0 spikes per second per degree per second. An active turn of the same size evokes 4 spikes per second, so sensitivity is 0.2 and the response is 1 - 0.2/2.0 = 90 percent cancelled. If neck feedback is made to mismatch the prediction, the sensitivity is expected to climb back toward 2.0.

Two points keep this accurate. The periphery did not switch off; the afferents kept firing. And the muting may help the head-steadying reflex spare a voluntary turn; it does not mean you stop sensing your own head movements. ## Keeping the prediction calibrated

A prediction is only useful if it stays right, so it must be updated when the head or the world changes. Brooks, Carriot and Cullen (2015) recorded rostral fastigial neurons, neurons of a cerebellar output nucleus that also carry head-motion signals, in rhesus macaques turning their heads voluntarily against a resistive load. As the animals adapted, neuronal vestibular sensitivity fell. On catch trials, when the load was unexpectedly removed, sensitivity rose again.

That is what a prediction-error signal should do: shrink as the new head becomes predictable, and return the moment the prediction is wrong, so the pattern is consistent with updating of an internal model. Neural and behavioural learning ran at statistically similar rates, and recordings alone cannot show that these neurons cause the learning.

Reflexes need calibration too. The VOR's gain, the size of the eye movement made for a given vestibular input, must be well calibrated to keep images stable on the retina, and visual error can drive it up or down through the cerebellum (Kimpo and colleagues, 2014). That is a different circuit with a different learning problem. Purkinje-cell circuitry belongs to [the cerebellum](/lessons/cerebellum/), and the reflex arcs to [motor units and reflexes](/lessons/motor-units-reflexes/).

## Rebuilding the signal: a vestibular implant

A vestibular implant tries to restore the motion signal when both ears have lost it. Chow and colleagues (2021, New England Journal of Medicine) implanted one prosthesis in each of eight adults with adult-onset bilateral vestibular hypofunction, meaning weak or absent function in both balance organs. The device electrically stimulated the three semicircular-canal branches of the vestibular nerve, varying its pulse rate and amplitude to represent rotational head motion. Measured against the one idea, it supplies a motion-modulated signal for rotation that does not reproduce the nerve's natural regular and irregular code, and it encodes no otolith signal for linear acceleration or tilt.

At six months, two of five posture and gait measures had within-participant change intervals that excluded zero, though the intervals were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Self-reported dizziness handicap fell. Hearing in the implanted ear was reduced in all but one of the eight, severely in three.

A cohort this small, in one age band and without an independent implantation control, establishes feasibility and a trade-off, not population efficacy.

### Design problem

Reviews propose that the type I calyx's non-quantal route helps the fast, irregular channel stay fast. Design an experiment in mature mice that tests this idea, and say which result would count against it.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Record single afferents, classify each as regular or irregular from its resting intervals, and fill it with dye so you know whether it ends in a calyx. Measure what the fast channel is for: latency and spike-timing precision to rapid stimuli such as vibration, with responses to slow rotation as a comparison. Then remove the non-quantal route while leaving quantal release intact. That is the hard part. The low-voltage-activated potassium channels that come with non-quantal transmission also speed the hair cell's own receptor potential, so a knockout could slow the signal before it reaches the synapse. Check quantal release by capacitance and record the hair cell's receptor potential, so that any change can be pinned on the synapse.

Choose the control with care. Regular afferents are not a clean control, because some carry calyx endings, and removing the calyx's route would reach them too. Use dye-identified bouton-only fibres, which have no calyx, as the control that should change little, and compare calyx-bearing irregular fibres with calyx-bearing regular ones. If irregular, calyx-bearing afferents keep their latency and timing precision after the non-quantal route is gone, the idea is in trouble. Judge the result on latency and timing, not on regularity, which the calyx does not set alone. A reflex readout, such as a vibration-evoked VEMP, would show whether any change matters for behaviour.

</details>

## Going deeper

This section keeps the numbers, preparations and methods behind the main line.

### Thresholds and the averaging window

The rough rule in the paradox treats a spike train as a renewal process, with independent, identically distributed intervals, in which the count variance approaches CV² times the mean count as the window grows. It needs a window long enough for edge effects to be negligible, and a large mean count is not enough for a very regular train. For made-up gamma-process spike trains with the lesson's rates and CVs, and a window placed at a random moment, the exact count variance gives the 1 s noise quoted in the paradox and, over 0.1 s, about 3.6 and 16 spikes per second. For the regular fibre at 1 s, with 100 spikes in the window, the count variance is about 0.42 against the rule's 0.25, because the long-window expansion carries an extra edge term of about (1 − CV⁴)/6, about 0.17 here. It comes from where the window edges fall between evenly spaced spikes, and at 0.1 s it supplies most of the regular fibre's noise. A simulation of 200,000 such windows agrees with these values to within about 1 percent. These are toy numbers: real thresholds also depend on the stimulus, on correlations between spikes and on how downstream neurons read the train. With real afferents, Sadeghi, Chacron, Taylor and Cullen (2007) reported lower angular-velocity detection thresholds for regular than for irregular canal afferents, despite the regular fibres' significantly lower gains.

### Does the vestibulo-ocular reflex need the fast channel?

In a classic functional-ablation study, Minor and Goldberg (1991) applied large inhibitory galvanic currents to the inner ear of alert squirrel monkeys, which silenced irregular afferents selectively. The eye movements evoked by the head rotations tested were largely unchanged, so the regular channel can carry the VOR, at least for those stimuli.

### Counting vesicles from capacitance

Spaiardi and colleagues worked in acutely dissected mouse utricles from postnatal days 18 to 28, in vitro, and measured exocytosis as a rise in membrane capacitance. They converted capacitance to vesicle number with 37 aF per vesicle. A later study from the same group showed that a large transient capacitance change in type I cells comes from the gating of a potassium conductance, not from vesicle fusion (Spaiardi and colleagues, 2025), so only the sustained component counts as release.

**Worked problem: from capacitance to vesicles.** Suppose, in a made-up recording, a depolarizing step raises a type II cell's capacitance by 74 fF.

*Step 1: units.* 74 fF is 74,000 aF.

*Step 2: divide.* 74,000 aF / 37 aF per vesicle = 2,000 vesicles.

*Step 3: scale.* A cell with about tenfold smaller exocytosis would show about 7.4 fF, or about 200 vesicles. The conversion is an assumption borrowed from the paper, so the count is an estimate, not a head count.

### The three preparations, in numbers

Songer and Eatock used excised rat saccules at postnatal days 1 to 9, deflected single bundles, and recorded hair cells and, separately, calyces at room and at body temperature. The shortening of the receptor potential's rise time by low-voltage-activated channels was seen by days 7 to 9.

In the turtle recordings, a representative fast time constant, at a holding potential of minus 70 millivolts, was 32 microseconds; that is one illustrated response, not a population latency. The mean resistive coupling across 17 pairs was 522 plus or minus 577 pS, a spread larger than the mean.

**Worked problem: what does 522 pS buy?** Take the mean coupling and an assumed 10 mV change in hair-cell voltage while the calyx voltage is held fixed. Treating the coupling as a simple transfer conductance, the calyx current changes by 522 pS x 10 mV = 5.2 pA. The 10 mV is a teaching assumption, not a measurement, and the real spread across pairs means individual pairs would differ widely.

The Govindaraju model used adult rat morphology and channel parameters from mouse and rat recordings at room temperature, and its output was compared with turtle recordings. In it, the cleft-base potential rose from 1.9 to 6 mV in 0.43 ms after excitatory stimulation, and taller modelled calyces reduced spike latency. None of these model numbers was measured in the cleft.

The field is unsettled in an instructive way. A direct measurement of cleft potential or potassium in a mature mammalian calyx would test the model, and so would a manipulation that changes calyx height or potassium-channel density and then reads latency. Neither experiment has been reported in the work described here.

### The cancellation experiment

Roy and Cullen's study appeared in the Journal of Neuroscience in 2004. They recorded in four alert macaques, with magnetic search coils tracking head and gaze, and varied conditions such as head restraint and neck torque. The comparator circuit was not located.

### Learning rates in the load experiment

The study (Nature Neuroscience, 2015) used two adult male rhesus macaques. The learning constants were 34 plus or minus 7 trials for peak head velocity and 24 plus or minus 8 trials for neuronal sensitivity. For an exponential course, a constant is the number of trials to cover about 63 percent of the change. The two did not differ significantly (Wilcoxon test, p > 0.05). Time constants measure rate, not onset, so they cannot say whether neurons or behaviour changed first.

### A different circuit: how the VOR's gain is taught

This is the floccular circuit, not the rostral fastigial circuit of the load experiment.

What teaches the cerebellum? Masao Ito proposed in 1972 that climbing-fibre signals carry visual error to the flocculus (as described in Lisberger's 2021 review). The modern picture is conditional. Kimpo and colleagues (2014, eLife) recorded floccular Purkinje cells in two rhesus monkeys, where complex spikes are a one-to-one readout of climbing-fibre spikes. During training for increased gain, climbing-fibre activity predicted a lower Purkinje output on the next trial. During training for decreased gain, the same climbing fibres responded just as strongly, yet their activity had no trial-by-trial effect on Purkinje output. The authors read these correlations as gating: a climbing-fibre error signal does not always trigger plasticity. In adult male mice, the same group paired climbing-fibre photostimulation with 1 Hz, plus or minus 10 degrees per second rotation in darkness. Appropriately timed pairing induced a gain increase, but the protocols tested did not produce associative gain decrease beyond vestibular-only habituation. So stimulation sufficed for one direction under those conditions; it did not prove climbing fibres are required in natural learning, nor that they are irrelevant to a decrease.

Rowan and colleagues (2018, Neuron) added that inhibition shapes the teaching signal. In cerebellar slices from mice aged 6 to 15 weeks, coincident activation of molecular-layer interneurons reduced climbing-fibre-evoked dendritic calcium, to 61.3 plus or minus 3.2 percent of control, and strong activation could reverse the sign of the plasticity that climbing-fibre activity induces at parallel-fibre synapses. In awake mice, co-activating climbing fibres with those interneurons, timed to the end of ipsiversive head turns, produced a decrease in VOR gain instead of an increase.

### Reading the implant cohort

The eight participants were aged 50 to 66. Beyond the two posture and gait measures whose intervals excluded zero, a third had a lower bound of exactly zero; with five endpoints and two positive, the unadjusted intervals invite a correction for multiple comparisons.

Pure-tone thresholds in the implanted ear rose by 3 to 16 dB in five participants and by 74 to 104 dB in three, a severe loss in that ear. That is a rise in all eight, though the authors counted hearing as reduced in all but one; read the threshold changes rather than the count.

**Worked problem: group medians are not paired improvements.** The Dizziness Handicap Inventory median fell from 79 before implantation to 19 at six months, and the paper reports a median within-participant improvement of 50 points.

*Step 1: difference of group medians.* 79 - 19 = 60 points.

*Step 2: the paired statistic.* Pair each participant first, subtract, then take the median of the eight differences. The paper gives 50.

*Step 3: why they differ.* A median is not a linear summary, so the median of paired changes can differ from the difference of group medians, even when one person supplies both group medians. The 60 is not a correction of the paper's 50; it answers a different question.

**The missing control.** The design is single-arm and within-participant, so in the baseline-to-six-month comparison surgery, device use and time are confounded. The blinded, randomised same-day comparison with a constant-stimulation mode helps: it holds the surgery and the elapsed time fixed and addresses expectancy and spontaneous recovery, so it isolates an immediate effect of motion-modulated stimulation. It cannot estimate the long-term effect of the operation or of months of adaptation, so a control that separates those from the stimulation is still missing.
