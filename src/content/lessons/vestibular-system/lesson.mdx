techniques:
  - name: "Dual patch clamp of hair cell and calyx"
    blurb: >-
      One pipette records the hair cell and another records the calyx, so presynaptic current and
      postsynaptic response are measured at once. It lets an experimenter separate a slow potassium
      accumulation from a fast resistive coupling.
  - name: "Membrane-capacitance assay of exocytosis"
    blurb: >-
      When vesicles fuse, the cell membrane gains area and its capacitance rises. Dividing a
      capacitance jump by a per-vesicle value converts it to a vesicle count, with the assumption
      that the conversion holds for that cell. In type I hair cells a fast transient capacitance
      change comes from potassium-channel gating, not vesicle fusion, so only the sustained
      component counts as release.
  - name: "Single-unit recording in alert macaques"
    blurb: >-
      A fine electrode records one neuron while the animal makes or receives head movements, with
      search coils tracking head and gaze. It shows what a neuron did in that task, and it is
      correlational unless paired with a manipulation.
  - name: "Finite-element electrodiffusion modelling"
    blurb: >-
      The cleft is divided into small elements and the electrical potential and ion concentrations
      are computed in each. It predicts variables that cannot be measured in a space this narrow,
      and its outputs are predictions until a measurement tests them.

**Your balance organs report each head movement on two kinds of afferent at once. Regular afferents fire like a clock and carry sustained motion in their firing rate. Irregular afferents fire unevenly and carry rapid changes in the timing of their spikes. This lesson is about how that split is built, down to an unusual synapse that can work without vesicles, and how the brain reads the result.**

[Hearing and balance](/lessons/hearing-and-balance/) gave you the canals, the otoliths and the three-neuron reflex arc. This lesson starts one step later, at the afferents and the hair cell's output, and one step further in, at the central comparison of expected with actual head motion. A **calyx** is the cup-shaped afferent ending that wraps a type I cell; the rest of the vocabulary arrives as it is needed.

## Two channels for one head movement

A head movement has parts that last and parts that change fast. The vestibular organs split it into angular and linear components with different time courses and frequencies, and their afferents carry the result to the brain, where it drives the reflexes that keep vision and posture steady and feeds the sense of heading (Eatock, 2025). Both kinds of afferent fire at a high resting rate, so each can signal motion in either direction by speeding up or slowing down.

**Regular afferents** fire at evenly spaced intervals at rest. They respond to slow head rotation with tonic discharge, have lower gain, carry most of their information in firing rate, and detect small motions well. **Irregular afferents** have highly variable intervals at rest. They have higher gain, adapt faster to a sustained head position, favour higher frequencies, and carry more information in the precise timing of their spikes (Eatock, 2018; Lacquaniti and colleagues, 2023). One head movement is sent twice: once as a rate code and once as a timing code.

Each channel has a home in the organ. Mammalian vestibular organs contain two hair-cell types. A **type I** cell is flask-shaped, and its afferent calyx almost completely encloses the basolateral membrane. A **type II** cell is cylindrical and receives several small bouton endings. Type I cells are most plentiful in the central zone of each organ: the striola of the otolith maculae and the centre of each canal crista. Irregular afferents mostly arise there, with calyx endings on type I cells and boutons on type II cells. Regular afferents mostly arise in the peripheral zone, many of them through bouton endings on type II cells.

How was this map made? For decades, researchers recorded single afferents, filled them with dye, reconstructed their endings, and matched each fibre's firing to the zone and endings it came from, in chinchillas, turtles and other species (reviewed by Contini and colleagues, 2022). The coding difference was then shown directly. When the same noisy head motion, roughly like natural movement, was played again and again, an irregular afferent's precisely timed spikes told different waveforms apart, while a regular afferent carried more of its information in rate (Lacquaniti and colleagues, 2023).

The map describes populations, not single cells. Regular discharge can come from fibres with calyx endings, irregular discharge from fibres with only boutons, and there is no compelling evidence that the calyx alone sets a fibre's regularity (Contini and colleagues, 2022).

Why two channels? The rate channel tracks sustained motion and small movements. The timing channel gives the reflexes that steady gaze and posture fast, precisely timed input during rapid movement. The fast channel can also be read in the clinic: sound and bone-conducted vibration mostly activate irregular otolith afferents from the striola, which is why vibration-evoked tests such as the vestibular-evoked myogenic potential (VEMP) report on this pathway (Pastras and colleagues, 2023).

The split is built during development. In mice, as low-voltage-activated potassium channels of the KV1 and KV7 families are acquired over the first postnatal weeks, hair-cell receptor potentials become faster, transmission from type I cells becomes non-quantal, and afferent firing becomes more irregular (Eatock, 2025). That brings us to the synapse.

## The type I calyx: release with and without vesicles

Both hair-cell types release glutamate by calcium-dependent, quantal release at ribbon synapses (Spaiardi and colleagues, 2022). The tempting shortcut, type I as the non-vesicular cell and type II as the vesicular one, is wrong.

What differs is how much, and what else. In mature mouse utricles, sustained calcium-evoked exocytosis, measured as a rise in membrane capacitance, was about tenfold smaller in type I than in type II cells. For type II cells, the large vesicle pools would favour sustained, tonic or low-frequency transmission.

The striking result came from knockout mice. Type I cells could still drive calyx action potentials when otoferlin or CaV1.3, two proteins needed for vesicle release, was lost. The authors took that to support a functional role for non-quantal transmission. The test was whether spikes survive, in vitro, not whether the signal is normal.

**Non-quantal transmission** is a change in calyx potential without vesicular exocytosis and without gap junctions (Govindaraju and colleagues, 2023). Its appeal is speed. It adds little synaptic delay, and reviews propose that it evolved to give the reflex pathways fast, reliable input (Eatock, 2018). Three kinds of evidence bear on it, and they come from three different animals.

In neonatal rat saccules, Songer and Eatock found that as type I cells matured, low-voltage-activated channels shortened the rise time of the receptor potential, and some immature calyces showed non-quantal transmission with little synaptic delay.

In isolated turtle posterior-canal epithelium, Contini, Holstein and Art recorded a hair cell and its calyx together with dual voltage clamp. They saw a slow component, which fits potassium building up in the cleft, and a much faster one that accumulation cannot explain, for which they proposed potassium-sensitive resistive coupling. Both routes worked in both directions: current or potassium from either cell changed the other. These are turtle recordings, not mammalian latencies.

The third kind of evidence is a model. Govindaraju and colleagues built a finite-element electrodiffusion model of a type I cell and its calyx. In it, the electrical potential in the cleft changes first and gives the initial change in driving force on the calyx; potassium accumulation contributes later. These are model outputs, not measurements in the cleft.

## Cancelling your own head movement, but only conditionally

Semicircular-canal afferents in alert monkeys respond to head motion whether the animal causes it or something else does (Cullen and Minor, 2002). Yet the reflexes that steady the head would fight a turn you make yourself if they treated it as a disturbance. Any cancellation must therefore be central.

Roy and Cullen (2004) recorded vestibular-only neurons, neurons of the vestibular nuclei that respond to head motion but not to eye movements, in alert macaques. These neurons are thought to drive the neck reflex that steadies the head (the vestibulocollic reflex). In the neurons tested, cancellation of the response to active motion required neck proprioceptive activation that matched the feedback expected from the motor command. When the match failed, the cancellation failed.

*Illustration with made-up numbers.* Suppose a passive turn peaking at 20 degrees per second evokes a 40 spikes per second modulation, a sensitivity of 2.0 spikes per second per degree per second. An active turn of the same size evokes 4 spikes per second, so sensitivity is 0.2 and the response is 1 - 0.2/2.0 = 90 percent cancelled. If neck feedback is made to mismatch the prediction, the sensitivity is expected to climb back toward 2.0.

And the result explains why the head-steadying reflex does not resist a voluntary turn, not how a turn feels; you still sense your own head movements. ## A prediction that is relearned

Predictions and reflexes both need calibration. The gain of the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR), the size of the eye movement made for a given vestibular input, must be well calibrated to keep images stable on the retina, and visual error can drive it up or down through the cerebellum (Kimpo and colleagues, 2014).

If the brain predicts the consequences of head movements, the prediction must also be updated when the world changes. Brooks, Carriot and Cullen (2015) recorded rostral fastigial neurons, neurons of a cerebellar output nucleus, in rhesus macaques turning their heads voluntarily against a resistive load. As the animals adapted, neuronal vestibular sensitivity fell. On catch trials, when the load was unexpectedly removed, sensitivity rose again. This pattern is consistent with updating of an internal model and with a sensory prediction error. Neural and behavioural learning ran at statistically similar rates, and recordings alone cannot show that these neurons cause the learning.

What teaches the cerebellum? Masao Ito proposed in 1972 that climbing-fibre signals carry visual error to the flocculus (as described in Lisberger's 2021 review). The modern picture is conditional. Kimpo and colleagues (2014) recorded floccular Purkinje cells in monkeys. During training for increased gain, climbing-fibre activity predicted a lower Purkinje output on the next trial. During training for decreased gain, the same climbing fibres responded just as strongly, yet their activity had no trial-by-trial effect on Purkinje output. The authors read these correlations as gating: a climbing-fibre error signal does not always trigger plasticity. In mice, the same group found that timed climbing-fibre stimulation paired with head rotation sufficed to induce a gain increase, but the protocols tested did not produce a learned decrease.

Rowan and colleagues (2018) added that inhibition shapes the teaching signal. In mouse cerebellar slices, coincident activation of molecular-layer interneurons reduced climbing-fibre-evoked dendritic calcium, and strong activation could reverse the sign of the plasticity that climbing-fibre activity induces at parallel-fibre synapses. In awake mice, co-activating climbing fibres with those interneurons, timed to the end of ipsiversive head turns, produced a decrease in VOR gain instead of an increase. Purkinje-cell circuitry belongs to [the cerebellum](/lessons/cerebellum/), and the reflex arcs to [motor units and reflexes](/lessons/motor-units-reflexes/).

## Replacing a vestibular organ, and what it costs

A vestibular implant tries to restore the motion signal when both ears have lost it. Chow and colleagues (2021, New England Journal of Medicine) implanted one prosthesis in each of eight adults with adult-onset bilateral vestibular hypofunction. The device electrically stimulated the three semicircular-canal branches of the vestibular nerve. At six months, two of five posture and gait measures had within-participant change intervals that excluded zero, though the intervals were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. The median Dizziness Handicap Inventory score fell from 79 to 19, and the median improvement within participants was 50 points (not 79 minus 19; Going deeper explains why). Hearing in the implanted ear was reduced in seven of the eight, severely in three.

A cohort this small, in one age band and without an independent implantation control, establishes feasibility and a trade-off, not population efficacy.

### Design problem

Reviews propose that the type I calyx's non-quantal route helps the fast, irregular channel stay fast. Design an experiment in mature mice that tests this idea, and say which result would count against it.

<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Record single afferents, classify each as regular or irregular from its resting intervals, and fill it with dye so you know whether it ends in a calyx. Measure what the fast channel is for: latency and spike-timing precision to rapid stimuli such as vibration, with responses to slow rotation as a comparison. Then remove the non-quantal route while leaving quantal release intact. That is the hard part. The low-voltage-activated potassium channels that come with non-quantal transmission also speed the hair cell's own receptor potential, so a knockout could slow the signal before it reaches the synapse. Check quantal release by capacitance and record the hair cell's receptor potential, so that any change can be pinned on the synapse.

Regular afferents, which mostly carry bouton input from type II cells, are a built-in control and should change little. If irregular, calyx-bearing afferents keep their latency and timing precision after the non-quantal route is gone, the idea is in trouble. Because regularity is not set by the calyx alone, judge the result on latency and timing, not on regularity. A reflex readout, such as a vibration-evoked VEMP, would show whether any change matters for behaviour.

## Going deeper

The main line above holds the ideas. This section keeps the numbers, preparations and methods behind them, for readers who want to check or reuse them.

### Counting vesicles from capacitance

Spaiardi and colleagues worked in acutely dissected mouse utricles from postnatal days 18 to 28, in vitro, and measured exocytosis as a rise in membrane capacitance, because vesicle fusion adds membrane area. They converted capacitance to vesicle number with 37 aF per vesicle. A later study from the same group showed that a large transient capacitance change in type I cells comes from the gating of a potassium conductance, not from vesicle fusion (Spaiardi and colleagues, 2025), so only the sustained component counts as release.

**Worked problem: from capacitance to vesicles.** Suppose, in a made-up recording, a depolarizing step raises a type II cell's capacitance by 74 fF.

*Step 1: units.* 74 fF is 74,000 aF.

*Step 2: divide.* 74,000 aF / 37 aF per vesicle = 2,000 vesicles.

*Step 3: scale.* A cell with about tenfold smaller exocytosis would show about 7.4 fF, or about 200 vesicles. The conversion is an assumption borrowed from the paper, so the count is an estimate, not a head count.

### The three preparations, in numbers

Songer and Eatock worked with excised saccules from rats at postnatal days 1 to 9. They deflected single bundles and recorded hair cells and, separately, calyces, at room and at body temperature. The shortening of the receptor potential's rise time by low-voltage-activated channels was seen by days 7 to 9. That is neonatal rat tissue.

In the turtle recordings, a representative fast time constant, at a holding potential of minus 70 millivolts, was 32 microseconds. The mean resistive coupling across 17 pairs was 522 plus or minus 577 pS, a spread larger than the mean.

**Worked problem: what does 522 pS buy?** Take the mean coupling and an assumed 10 mV change in hair-cell voltage while the calyx voltage is held fixed. Treating the coupling as a simple transfer conductance, the calyx current changes by 522 pS x 10 mV = 5.2 pA. The 10 mV is a teaching assumption, not a measurement, and the real spread across pairs means individual pairs would differ widely.

The 32 microseconds is one illustrated response. It is not a population latency, and a chemical synapse adds delay that a fast electrical route does not.

The Govindaraju model used adult rat morphology and channel parameters from mouse and rat recordings at room temperature, and its output was compared with turtle recordings. In it, the cleft-base potential rose from 1.9 to 6 mV in 0.43 ms after excitatory stimulation, with a resting cleft potassium of 7 mM, and taller modelled calyces reduced spike latency. None of these model numbers was measured in the cleft.

The field is unsettled in an instructive way. A direct measurement of cleft potential or potassium in a mature mammalian calyx would test the model, and so would a manipulation that changes calyx height or potassium-channel density and then reads latency. Neither experiment has been reported in the work described here.

### The cancellation experiment

Roy and Cullen's study appeared in the Journal of Neuroscience in 2004. They recorded in four alert macaques, with magnetic search coils tracking head and gaze, and varied conditions such as head restraint and neck torque. Two further limits apply. The comparator circuit was not located. And not every vestibular neuron was shown to cancel.

### Learning rates and teaching signals

Brooks, Carriot and Cullen published in Nature Neuroscience in 2015 and recorded in two adult male rhesus macaques. The learning constants were 34 plus or minus 7 trials for peak head velocity and 24 plus or minus 8 trials for neuronal sensitivity. For an exponential course, a constant is the number of trials to cover about 63 percent of the change. The paper found no significant difference between the two constants (Wilcoxon test, p > 0.05). Time constants measure rate, not onset, so they cannot say whether neurons or behaviour changed first.

Kimpo and colleagues (2014, eLife) recorded floccular Purkinje cells in two rhesus monkeys, where complex spikes are a one-to-one readout of climbing-fibre spikes. In adult male mice, the same group paired climbing-fibre photostimulation with 1 Hz, plus or minus 10 degrees per second rotation in darkness. Appropriately timed pairing induced a gain increase, but the protocols tested did not produce associative gain decrease beyond vestibular-only habituation. So stimulation sufficed for one direction under those conditions; it did not prove climbing fibres are required in natural learning, nor that they are irrelevant to a decrease.

Rowan and colleagues (2018, Neuron) used cerebellar slices from mice aged 6 to 15 weeks. Coincident activation of molecular-layer interneurons reduced climbing-fibre-evoked dendritic calcium to 61.3 plus or minus 3.2 percent of control.

### Reading the implant cohort

The eight participants were aged 50 to 66. Beyond the two posture and gait measures whose intervals excluded zero, a third had a lower bound of exactly zero, and the paper's intervals were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Pure-tone thresholds in the implanted ear rose by 3 to 16 dB in five participants and by 74 to 104 dB in three, a severe loss in that ear.

**Worked problem: group medians are not paired improvements.** The Dizziness Handicap Inventory median fell from 79 before implantation to 19 at six months, and the paper reports a median within-participant improvement of 50 points.

*Step 1: difference of group medians.* 79 - 19 = 60 points.

*Step 2: the paired statistic.* Pair each participant first, subtract, then take the median of the eight differences. The paper gives 50.

*Step 3: why they differ.* Subtraction and taking a median do not commute. A median is not a linear summary, so the median of paired changes can differ from the difference of group medians, even when one person supplies both group medians. The 60 is therefore not a correction of the paper's 50; it answers a different question.

**The missing control.** The design is single-arm and within-participant, so it has no independent implantation control. In the baseline-to-six-month comparison, surgery, device use and time are confounded. The blinded, randomised same-day comparison with a constant-stimulation mode helps: it holds the surgery and the elapsed time fixed and addresses expectancy and spontaneous recovery, so it isolates an immediate effect of motion-modulated stimulation. It cannot estimate the long-term effect of the operation or of months of adaptation, so a control that separates those from the stimulation is still missing.

Five endpoints with two positive invite a correction for multiple comparisons. The hearing cost falls in the implanted ear, is severe in three of the eight, and belongs in any statement of benefit and harm. A small cohort in one age band cannot support population efficacy, and it cannot show restored otolith function.
