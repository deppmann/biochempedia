## Explore: share out life’s jobs

In the model you get five life jobs: take in food, move, sense the surroundings, reproduce and remove extra water. You can give every job to one cell, or build a group of cells and give each cell one or two jobs. Each cell has a limited energy supply, and you can make the organism bigger and see which arrangement keeps up.

## Explain: one cell that does it all

<WordBank terms={['unicellular', 'multicellular', 'cilia', 'contractile vacuole', 'binary fission', 'specialized cell', 'tissue', 'organ', 'organ system', 'gene expression']} />

A **unicellular** organism is a living thing made of just one cell. A **multicellular** organism is made of many cells. Bacteria, many algae and many protists, such as Paramecium, are unicellular.

A Paramecium has no tissues or organs, yet its one cell does every job of a living thing:

- **Moves.** Its surface is covered in **cilia**, short hair-like structures that beat together like oars.
- **Feeds.** Food such as bacteria is swept into a groove and enclosed in food vacuoles, where it is digested.
- **Removes extra water.** Pond water has fewer dissolved particles than the cell, so water keeps moving in by osmosis. A **contractile vacuole** is a sac that fills with this extra water and squeezes it out. You met osmosis in [that lesson](/lessons/crossing-the-membrane/).
- **Reproduces.** It can split into two cells by **binary fission**, a way of reproducing without a partner in which one cell divides into two.

{/* DIAGRAM: a Paramecium with numbered parts: 1 cilia on the surface, 2 oral groove, 3 food vacuole, 4 contractile vacuole, 5 macronucleus and micronucleus */}

Some organisms sit in between. Volvox, a green alga, lives as a hollow ball of many cells. In Volvox carteri, most cells swim and make food, while a few large cells are set aside for reproduction. Colonies like this show a first step toward division of labor.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A unicellular organism carries out every life process inside one cell, using different structures within that cell for different jobs.</Support>

## Explain: specialized cells, from tissues to systems

In a multicellular organism, cells share the work. A **specialized cell** is a cell with a structure that suits it to a particular job. Similar cells working together form a **tissue**, such as muscle tissue. Several tissues working together form an **organ**, such as the heart. Related organs form an **organ system**, such as the circulatory system.

> cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism

A 70-kilogram reference adult man holds about 30 to 36 trillion cells, according to two careful estimates (2016 and 2023); the 2023 model gave about 28 trillion for a reference woman and about 17 trillion for a 10-year-old child. Textbooks often say there are about 200 kinds of cells. A 2022 atlas that sorted cells by the genes they use counted more than 400 types in the tissues it sampled, so the true number depends on how finely you sort.

Each specialized cell's shape fits its job:

| Cell | Structure | How it fits the job |
|---|---|---|
| Neuron (nerve cell) | Long, thin extension called an axon | Carries signals long distances, such as from the spine to the foot |
| Red blood cell | Small, flexible disc dented in the middle; no nucleus when mature | Large surface for oxygen to cross; bends to squeeze through narrow vessels |
| Skeletal muscle cell | Very long cylinder with many nuclei | Packed with protein fibres that shorten together to pull on bone |
| Root hair cell | Long tube growing out from the root surface | Adds surface touching the soil, where water and minerals enter |
| Guard cell | Two curved cells around a pore in a leaf | Change shape to open and close the pore, letting gases in and out |

{/* DIAGRAM: five specialized cells drawn side by side with a scale note: 1 neuron with long axon, 2 red blood cell seen from the side showing the dent, 3 skeletal muscle cell with several nuclei, 4 root hair cell with its tube into soil, 5 pair of guard cells around a leaf pore */}

Think of a restaurant kitchen, where one cook chops, one grills and one washes dishes. The analogy breaks in one place: a specialized cell still does its own basic work, such as taking in food molecules and making ATP. A muscle cell cannot hand all of that to another cell.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** In a multicellular organism, specialized cells with shapes suited to their jobs are organized into tissues, organs and organ systems.</Support>

## Explain: same DNA, different genes

If your nerve cells and muscle cells look and work so differently, do they carry different instructions? Mostly no. Nearly every body cell with a nucleus carries essentially the same DNA, copied from the first cell you began as. You can see how that copying happens in [that lesson](/lessons/cell-cycle-and-mitosis/).

The difference is **gene expression**, the use of a gene's instructions to make a product such as a protein. Each kind of cell uses a different set of its genes. A muscle cell uses the genes for the proteins that shorten; a red blood cell in the making uses the genes for hemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen. Many other genes stay unused in each cell.

There are a few exceptions. A maturing red blood cell pushes out its nucleus, so a mature one has no DNA left. Egg and sperm cells carry half a set of DNA. You will learn how genes are read in [that lesson](/lessons/protein-synthesis/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** The cells of one organism carry the same DNA, but each kind of cell uses a different set of genes, which gives it its own structure and job.</Support>

### Design problem

Do radish seedlings grown on drier paper towels grow longer root hairs than seedlings grown on wetter towels? Plan an investigation using a hand lens or a microscope with a scale. Name your variables, constants, control, sample size and safety steps.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Independent variable:** how wet the paper towel is. Add a set volume of water per towel, such as 5 mL for drier and 15 mL for wetter.
- **Dependent variable:** length of root hairs, measured on the same part of each root with a scale in view.
- **Constants:** seed batch, number of seeds per dish, temperature, light, towel type and the day you measure.
- **Control:** the dishes given the usual amount of water used for sprouting seeds.
- **Sample size and trials:** several dishes per group, ten or more seedlings per dish, and at least ten hairs per seedling. Report the mean and the spread.
- **Fairness:** a partner codes the dishes so the person measuring does not know the group.
- **Limit:** this tests one plant species at one age.
- **Safety:** handle slides and cover slips with care, wash hands after handling seeds, and do not eat them.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

A Paramecium survives alone because its one cell holds every structure it needs: cilia to swim, food vacuoles to digest, a contractile vacuole to pump out water and the ability to divide. Your body works differently. It shares those jobs among specialized cells whose shapes fit their work, all carrying the same DNA but using different genes.

**Science practices you used:** using a model and naming its limits, reading a data table with means and spread, converting units and comparing sizes, and planning a fair test with enough repeated measurements.
