**Every muscle pulls the same way: two proteins, actin and myosin, slide past each other using the energy of ATP, and calcium switches the sliding on.** That machinery is behind every muscle in this lesson, from a heart to a fly's wing. Muscles are strong, but they are attached close to the joints they move, so the skeleton they pull on matters too. In a simple lever model of the elbow, holding a 2 kg bag takes a biceps pull of about seven and a half times that weight, and the lesson works that example out later. This lesson follows a contraction from the nerve to the protein that does the pulling, compares the muscles of hearts, guts, tuna and flies, and then looks at what muscles pull on.

## From nerve to calcium to cross-bridge

A **skeletal muscle fibre** is one long cell with many nuclei, formed when embryonic muscle cells (myoblasts) fuse. It is packed with **myofibrils**, chains of repeating units called **sarcomeres**. Each sarcomere holds two kinds of filament: **thick filaments** of the protein myosin and **thin filaments** of actin. Their regular overlap makes the stripes you see under a microscope, which is why skeletal muscle is called **striated**.

The signal starts at the **neuromuscular junction**, the synapse between a motor neuron and a muscle fibre. The neuron releases **acetylcholine**, which opens channels in the fibre's membrane and depolarizes it ([synaptic transmission](/lessons/synaptic-transmission/) and [the neuromuscular junction](/lessons/neuromuscular-junction/) cover the details). That starts an **action potential**, an electrical signal that travels along the membrane ([action potential](/lessons/action-potential/)). The signal runs down **T tubules**, infoldings of the membrane that reach deep into the fibre.

Next to each T tubule lies the **sarcoplasmic reticulum** (SR), the fibre's internal calcium store. In vertebrate skeletal muscle, a voltage sensor in the T-tubule membrane (a channel called CaV1.1) is linked to the SR's calcium release channel (RyR1). When the voltage changes, the sensor opens the release channel, and calcium floods out. Calcium entering from outside the cell is not required for this step.

Calcium binds **troponin**, a complex of three proteins on the thin filament (calcium binds the part called troponin C). Troponin then shifts **tropomyosin**, a strand that lies along actin and covers the sites where myosin binds. With the sites uncovered, myosin heads attach to actin, swing, and pull the thin filaments toward the middle of the sarcomere. These attached heads are **cross-bridges**.

This is the **sliding filament** model: the filaments do not get shorter; they slide past each other. The A band, which is the length of the thick filaments, keeps its width while the sarcomere shortens. Think of two combs pushed together tooth into tooth. The comb is the same length; the overlap changes.

ATP has two jobs here. First, at the myosin head: ATP binding releases the head from actin, and splitting the ATP re-cocks it for the next stroke. Second, pumps in the SR use ATP to move calcium back into the store, and as calcium falls, tropomyosin covers the sites again and the muscle relaxes. Without ATP, the heads stay stuck to actin, which is why muscles stiffen after death. How the cross-bridge cycle and the 1954 sliding filament papers fit together is told in [molecular motors](/lessons/molecular-motors/); troponin and calcium as a signal are in [calcium signaling](/lessons/calcium-signaling/).

## Three muscles, three triggers

Vertebrates have three kinds of muscle, all built from actin and myosin (each type makes its own versions of these proteins) but switched on differently.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/muscle-and-movement/three-muscle-types.webp" width={1200} height={769} alt="AI-generated illustration: three panels comparing vertebrate muscle; long separate striped skeletal fibres, short branched striped cardiac cells joined end to end by gap junctions, and unstriped spindle-shaped smooth muscle cells with a gap junction between two neighbours." caption="Skeletal fibres are long, striped and unconnected; cardiac cells are short, branched, striped and joined by gap junctions; smooth muscle cells are unstriped, with gap junctions coupling neighbours in many smooth muscles, such as the gut wall. Skeletal and cardiac muscle use a troponin switch, smooth muscle calcium-calmodulin." />

**Skeletal muscle** is striated and voluntary. Each fibre contracts when its own motor neuron fires, and fibres are not electrically connected to each other. Graded force comes from how many motor units fire and how often ([motor units and reflexes](/lessons/motor-units-reflexes/)).

**Cardiac muscle** is also striated, but its cells are short, branched and joined end to end by **gap junctions**, channels that let electrical current pass from cell to cell, so a wave of excitation spreads through the heart. No motor neuron starts the beat: pacemaker cells generate action potentials on their own, and nerves adjust their rate and the force of each beat (see [circulation and the heart](/lessons/circulation-and-heart/)). The calcium trigger differs too. In mammalian heart cells, calcium entering through membrane channels during the action potential triggers a much larger release from the SR. Frog hearts and many fish hearts rely mostly on the entering calcium itself.

**Smooth muscle** lines the gut, blood vessels, airways and ducts. Its actin and myosin are not organized into sarcomeres, so it has no stripes. It is not under voluntary control; autonomic nerves, hormones and stretch act on it. In many smooth muscles, such as those of the gut wall, gap junctions couple neighbouring cells. Smooth muscle has no troponin switch. Instead calcium binds **calmodulin**, and calcium-calmodulin activates **myosin light-chain kinase**, an enzyme that adds a phosphate to the myosin light chain and turns myosin on.

## Fast and slow: fibres, tuna and chickens

Skeletal fibres differ in speed and in how they make ATP. **Slow oxidative** fibres contract slowly, make most of their ATP by aerobic respiration, are rich in mitochondria and in **myoglobin** (an oxygen-binding protein, see [oxygen transport](/lessons/oxygen-transport/)), look dark red, and resist fatigue. **Fast oxidative** fibres contract quickly but still rely mainly on aerobic respiration. **Fast glycolytic** fibres contract quickly, rely on glycolysis ([glycolysis](/lessons/glycolysis/)), look pale, and fatigue sooner.

In humans the three main types are I, IIa and IIx; older papers use other labels (see Going deeper).

Animals show the same split at the scale of whole muscles. In many fishes a thin band of dark red muscle runs along each side of the body under the skin and powers steady swimming, while the bulk of white muscle is used in bursts. In tunas the red muscle lies deeper, close to the backbone, and stays warmer than the water ([thermoregulation](/lessons/thermoregulation/)). In domestic chickens, the leg muscles used in standing and walking are darker, and the breast muscles that power brief bursts of flapping are pale. In each of these animals, the muscles used for steady work are red and oxidative and those used for bursts are pale and glycolytic, although species also differ in which fibre types they have.

## A fly's wings outrun its nerves

In a locust or a dragonfly, each contraction of a flight muscle follows its own nerve impulse. That is **synchronous** muscle, the same rule as vertebrate skeletal muscle. Flies beat their wings much faster, and their main power muscles work differently: the beat is set by the muscle and the springy thorax it pulls on, and nerve impulses keep the muscle switched on. This is **asynchronous** muscle, found in flies, bees and wasps, beetles and some other insects. J. W. S. Pringle proposed it in 1949 from recordings of the flight muscles of tethered blue-bottle flies (Calliphora) in flight. If each contraction needed its own impulse, there should be one large spike per wingbeat. He saw a small electrical signal with each beat, but the large spikes, which he linked to nerve impulses, came far less often than the wingbeats and were not timed to particular beats. The recordings left open whether those spikes were true propagated action potentials and did not show how the muscle oscillates (see Going deeper).

<LessonFigure src="/figures/muscle-and-movement/pringle-tethered-fly-recording.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a blue-bottle fly held by a rod on its back, wings blurred in motion, with a fine electrode in its thorax connected by a wire to a plain recording box; an inset shows a springy thorax box pressed by two flight muscles with a nerve keeping them switched on." caption="Pringle recorded from the flight muscles of tethered blue-bottle flies (Calliphora) as they beat their wings. In flies the asynchronous muscle drives the main power muscles, while the small steering muscles at the wing base are synchronous. Asynchronous does not mean nerveless; without impulses the muscle stops. Later work on isolated beetle flight muscle by Machin and Pringle (1959) reported that the muscle is activated by being stretched: as one muscle pulls, it stretches its antagonist, which then contracts.

## Skeletons: water, armour and bone, with levers

Muscles only pull, so every muscle works against something that stretches it back. A **hydrostatic skeleton** is fluid under pressure in a closed cavity. An earthworm contracts the muscles of its body wall against fluid in its body cavity, so each segment can lengthen or shorten ([animal body plans](/lessons/animal-body-plans/)). A **muscular hydrostat** has no fluid cavity: octopus arms and mammalian tongues are mostly muscle at constant volume, so muscle in one direction acts against muscle in another.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/muscle-and-movement/skeletons-without-bone.webp" width={1200} height={797} alt="AI-generated illustration: three panels comparing skeletons; a cut-open earthworm segment with fluid in its body cavity, an octopus arm with solid muscle and no cavity, and a crab leg whose hard outer case holds muscle attached inside." caption="Muscles only pull, so each needs something to pull against: fluid under pressure in an earthworm's body cavity, other muscle at constant volume in an octopus arm or a mammalian tongue, or a hard outer case, as in crabs and insects." />

An **exoskeleton** is a hard outer case with muscles attached inside, as in insects and crabs. It cannot grow, so arthropods shed it and make a larger one at each moult ([endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/)). An **endoskeleton** lies inside the soft tissues, as in vertebrates.

Bone is living tissue. Its matrix combines collagen fibres with a calcium phosphate mineral, hydroxyapatite. **Compact bone** forms the dense outer wall and is built of **osteons**, cylinders of matrix around a central canal. **Spongy bone**, inside, is a lattice of thin struts called trabeculae. **Osteoblasts** lay down new bone, **osteoclasts** dissolve it, and **osteocytes** are former osteoblasts trapped in the matrix. Because bone is resorbed and rebuilt throughout life, its shape changes with the loads it carries (on how bone thickness scales with body size, see [size and scaling](/lessons/size-and-scaling/)).

Bones meet at **joints**. In humans, joints are grouped by structure as **fibrous** (bones joined by connective tissue, as in skull sutures), **cartilaginous** (joined by cartilage) or **synovial** (a lubricated, fluid-filled cavity, as at the elbow and knee). At a synovial joint muscles work in **antagonistic** groups: the biceps and other flexors, such as the brachialis, bend the elbow and the triceps straightens it. Tendons carry the pull to bone, and the bone acts as a lever.

**Worked problem.** These numbers are invented for an idealized model. A forearm is held horizontal and treated as weightless, and the biceps is treated as the only muscle bending the elbow; in a real arm the brachialis and brachioradialis share the load. A 20 N load (about 2 kg) is held in the hand 0.30 m from the elbow. The biceps pulls straight up with a moment arm of 0.040 m. Assume static equilibrium, with the elbow as the pivot.

> **muscle force x muscle moment arm = load x load arm**

1. *Torque from the load.* 20 N x 0.30 m = 6.0 N m.
2. *Muscle force.* F x 0.040 m = 6.0 N m, so F = 6.0 / 0.040 = 150 N, which is 7.5 times the load.
3. *Check.* 150 N x 0.040 m = 6.0 N m, matching the load's torque.
4. *What the trade buys.* The same geometry means the hand moves 0.30 / 0.040 = 7.5 times as far, and as fast, as the point where the biceps attaches. If the biceps shortens by 1.0 cm, the hand moves about 7.5 cm (for a small angle). This is a third-class lever: the muscle pulls between the pivot and the load, and gives up force for distance and speed.

### Design problem

Does a muscle require calcium from outside the cell to contract? You have frog skeletal muscle fibres and isolated cardiac cells, a calcium-free bathing solution with a calcium binder, and a drug that blocks the L-type calcium channels in the cell membrane. Design an experiment that tells the two triggers apart.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Stimulate each preparation electrically and record force, first in normal solution and then after switching to the calcium-free solution, and in a separate run with the channel blocker added to normal solution.

The prediction for skeletal fibres: twitches continue for a time without calcium entry, because the voltage sensor is linked mechanically to the SR release channel. For mammalian ventricular cells: force falls quickly once the calcium-free solution reaches the cells and drops with the blocker, because entering calcium triggers SR release, as the Fabiatos' work on skinned rat ventricular cells suggested (see Going deeper). The calcium-free bath is the cleaner test (see Going deeper for why skeletal twitches eventually fade and for a caution about the blocker in skeletal fibres).

Controls matter. Record action potentials in every condition, so that a loss of force cannot be blamed on a membrane that no longer fires. Return each preparation to normal solution to show that the effect reverses and the cells were not damaged. Keep the calcium-free period short, because heart cells can be injured when calcium is restored after a long time without it. Keep the temperature the same, and take the cardiac cells from a mammal such as the rat: frog ventricle cells rely almost entirely on the entering calcium itself and have little SR release, so they would not test calcium-triggered release. Name the species in your conclusion: the result is about frog skeletal muscle and the heart cells you used. The older story of Ringer's frog heart and bath calcium is told in [calcium signaling](/lessons/calcium-signaling/).

## Going deeper

### Discovery details

The Fabiatos showed this calcium-triggered release in skinned cells from adult rat ventricles in 1975.

Chacko, Conti and Adelstein found in purified guinea-pig smooth muscle myosin that this phosphorylation raised its actin-activated ATPase activity.

The names you read depend on the species. In humans the three main types are I, IIa and IIx. Human fibres long labelled IIb by staining turned out, in the 1994 study by Smerdu and colleagues, to express mostly IIx myosin, not the rodent IIb isoform.

### What Pringle's recordings did not settle

Pringle left open whether the large spikes were true propagated action potentials. The recordings did not show how the muscle oscillates.

### Reading the calcium-free test with care

Twitches may still fade after some minutes, but not because the store has run down: the voltage sensor itself needs calcium bound to the outside of the membrane. Read the blocker run with care for skeletal fibres: there the L-type channel (CaV1.1) is itself the voltage sensor, so some blockers of this kind reduce skeletal force without any need for calcium entry. The calcium-free bath is the cleaner test.
