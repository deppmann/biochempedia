techniques:
  - name: "Linkage mapping in experimental crosses"
    blurb: >-
      Two populations that differ in a trait are crossed, the hybrids are bred together, and
      hundreds of offspring are scored for the trait and genotyped at markers across the genome.
      A region whose genotype co-segregates with the trait is linked to a cause, but it can hold
      many genes.
  - name: "Whole-mount in situ hybridization"
    blurb: >-
      A labelled antisense RNA probe that pairs with a gene's transcript is applied to a whole
      embryo or larva, showing where the transcript is present. A sense probe is the negative
      control; mixing the two up was the error the 2006 stickleback correction fixed.
  - name: "Allele-specific expression in F1 hybrids"
    blurb: >-
      In a hybrid, both parental alleles share one nucleus and one set of transcription factors.
      Measuring how much RNA comes from each allele, tissue by tissue, tells you whether an
      expression difference is built into the allele's own DNA (cis) or comes from the cell's
      other factors (trans).
  - name: "Reporter assays and engineered deletions"
    blurb: >-
      A candidate enhancer is placed beside a minimal promoter and a reporter such as GFP or lacZ
      to ask where it can drive expression. Deleting the enhancer at its native locus with
      CRISPR/Cas9 asks a different question: does the organism's form depend on it?

**Marine threespine sticklebacks carry a pelvis armed with two spines. In Paxton Lake, British Columbia, the bottom-feeding benthic sticklebacks have a greatly reduced pelvis and most have no pelvic bones at all, yet the protein made by Pitx1, the gene most strongly tied to the difference, is unchanged in the fish examined. How can a body part shrink while its gene still makes a normal protein?**

The answer is that a gene's protein and a gene's pattern of use are separate things, and evolution can change one without the other. This lesson follows the stickleback evidence step by step, because each method answers its own question, and the order in which they were applied is what turns a hunch into a case.

## The puzzle: a lost pelvis with a working gene

The **threespine stickleback**, *Gasterosteus aculeatus*, lives in the sea along northern coasts and in many lakes that marine fish colonised after the last ice sheets retreated. Marine fish have a pelvic girdle with a pair of long spines. In several lakes, the populations have a reduced pelvis or almost none. The stickleback pelvic fin is **homologous** to your hind limb: the two structures are inherited, in modified form, from a common ancestor (see [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/)).

**Pitx1** encodes a homeodomain transcription factor, a protein that binds DNA and switches other genes on or off. Mice lacking Pitx1 protein have malformed hind limbs, among other defects. If stickleback Pitx1 were broken outright, you would expect damage wherever the gene works, including the jaw, where it is also expressed.

The way out is the **enhancer**: a short stretch of noncoding DNA that binds transcription factors and raises a gene's expression in particular tissues at particular times. A gene can have several, one for the jaw and another for the pelvis, like separate light switches for separate rooms wired to the same bulb. Break the pelvic switch and the jaw light stays on. How enhancers work is taught in [eukaryotic gene regulation](/lessons/eukaryotic-regulation/); here the question is how you would show that evolution used one.

## Mapping and expression: one major region, four minor

Shapiro, Kingsley and colleagues reported in 2004 that pelvic reduction in their stickleback crosses mapped to **one major and four minor chromosome regions**. The major region sat near Pitx1. The method is [linkage mapping](/lessons/linkage-and-mapping/): cross the two forms, breed the hybrids together, and look for chromosome regions whose genotype travels with the trait in the offspring.

**Worked problem, part 1.** These data are invented, in the style of a stickleback cross. A marine fish (pelvis complete) is crossed with a Paxton benthic fish (pelvis reduced). The F1 fish are intercrossed, giving 400 F2 fish. Each is scored for pelvic reduction and genotyped at a marker beside Pitx1: M is the marine allele, P the Paxton allele. Assume all fish are raised together and scored on the same scale.

1. *Frequency of reduction in each genotype.* MM: 2 reduced of 100, or 2%. MP: 6 of 200, or 3%. PP: 32 of 100, or 32%.
2. *Read dominance.* One Paxton copy (MP) barely changes the frequency from MM, while two copies raise it about tenfold. The region acts close to recessively.
3. *How much does the region explain?* Overall, 2 + 6 + 32 = 40 of 400 fish, or 10%, are reduced. Yet 68 of the 100 PP fish (68%) are not reduced, and 8 reduced fish carry a marine allele. So other loci or the environment also matter, as the four minor regions in the real study suggest.

A mapped region shows that something in it is linked to the trait. It can hold many genes, and it says nothing about whether the change lies in the protein or its regulation. The 2004 paper went further on both points: the Pitx1 protein sequence was unchanged in the pelvic-reduced fish examined, and Pitx1 transcript, detected by **in situ hybridization** (a labelled antisense RNA probe that pairs with the transcript in a whole larva), was missing from the pelvic region of Paxton larvae and present in the jaw. The original figures had used a sense probe by mistake. The 2006 correction, with an antisense probe, reported retained mouth and lower-jaw expression, lost pelvic expression, and no tail expression in either population. Cite the correction for the expression pattern.

## Cis or trans: the F1 test

Lower expression in the pelvis could come from a change near Pitx1, acting **in cis** (on the same DNA molecule), or from a change in some other gene that supplies Pitx1's regulators, acting **in trans** (through a diffusible product). Mapping cannot separate these, because a trans regulator could sit in the same region.

The F1 hybrid can. Each hybrid cell holds one marine and one Paxton allele in the same nucleus, bathed by the same transcription factors. If the regulators were the problem, both alleles would be affected alike. If the difference is built into one allele's DNA, that allele alone is down.

**Worked problem, part 2.** Same invented cross. In F1 pelvic buds, 90% of Pitx1 transcripts come from the marine allele and 10% from the Paxton allele. In jaw tissue the split is 50:50.

4. *Cis or trans?* The two alleles share one trans environment, so a 90:10 ratio means the Paxton allele itself is less active. The change is cis-acting.
5. *Tissue-specific?* The jaw ratio is 50:50, so the Paxton allele works normally there. The defect is specific to the pelvis.
6. *What has improved since part 1?* Mapping alone could not exclude another gene in the region. The allele-specific result points to a cis-regulatory change at Pitx1 itself.

## Enhancers on trial: reporters, rescue and a mouse deletion

The same 2010 study identified **Pel** (later called PelA), a noncoding sequence upstream of Pitx1 that can drive expression in the developing pelvic fin. In a **reporter assay**, the candidate sequence is joined to a minimal promoter and a visible marker, and placed in embryos. The marine version drove pelvic expression; the Paxton version, which carried a deletion, gave pelvic expression in **0 of 8** transgenic fish tested. A reporter answers one question: can this sequence drive expression here? It does not say the sequence matters for form.

**Rescue** comes closer. Chan's group put a Pitx1 minigene driven by marine Pel into fry from Bear Paw Lake, Alaska, another pelvic-reduced population, and pelvic spine development was variably enhanced compared with uninjected siblings. "Variably" is the paper's hedge and stays.

In 2018 Thompson, Capellini, Kingsley and colleagues found a second hind-limb enhancer, **PelB**, downstream of Pitx1. They deleted 9,425 base pairs containing it at its native place in the mouse genome using CRISPR/Cas9 (methods in [CRISPR and functional genomics](/lessons/crispr-and-functional-genomics/)). Homozygous mice were viable and fertile, and several hind-limb bones were shorter. Their hind-limb Pitx1 RNA, measured at embryonic day 12.5:

> **relative decrease = (control - mutant) / control = (1.00 - 0.85) / 1.00 = 0.15, or 15%**

About 85% of the RNA remained. Patellae were absent only in mice carrying the PelB deletion opposite a Pitx1 null allele, a sensitized background. This enhancer tunes how much Pitx1 the limb makes; it is not an on-off switch for the whole limb.

In sticklebacks, the Paxton benthic PelB carried a 125-base-pair deletion and a 341-base-pair insertion, and its reporter drove weaker, more restricted pelvic expression than the marine one while keeping strong jaw expression. Paxton fish therefore carry changes in both PelA and PelB.

Why might Pel deletions recur? Xie and colleagues (2019) found that yeast artificial chromosomes carrying marine Pel broke about 25 to 50 times as often as an empty control, and the reverse-complement orientation broke 10 to 20 times less often than the forward one. That is a yeast assay, not a fish mutation rate, and it speaks to how deletions arise, not why they spread.

## What stays open

The evidence ladder, in order: mapping localises, in situ hybridization shows where transcript is, the F1 test separates cis from trans, reporters show what a sequence can drive, and rescue or a native deletion tests whether form depends on it. Four questions remain:

- **PelA versus PelB.** The fish reporters do not split the natural contributions of the two enhancers; the 2018 authors proposed engineered fish to do so.
- **Other loci.** The four minor regions, and background, are not explained.
- **The agent of selection.** Why reduction spread in particular lakes is not established by this work, and this lesson does not name a cause.
- **Generality.** A 2023 study of sticklebacks from two small subarctic Norwegian lakes and a nearby marine site found no obvious association between its tested PelA and PelB variants and pelvic status in the lake where spineless fish occur, so the Pel story should not be assumed for every pelvic-reduced population. How form evolves at the gene level is compared across lineages in [molecular evolution](/lessons/molecular-evolution/), and how ancient developmental toolkits were reused in [how signaling evolved](/lessons/how-signaling-evolved/); the [Hox](/lessons/hox-and-hindbrain/) genes are another case of shared developmental control.

### Design problem

Paxton fish carry changes in both PelA (upstream) and PelB (downstream). Design an experiment to measure each one's contribution to pelvic size.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Work at the native locus, not with reporters, because a reporter shows what a sequence can drive in a construct, not its effect on bone. Start with marine (pelvis-complete) fish and use genome editing to install the Paxton-type change at PelA alone, at PelB alone, and at both. Make several independent lines of each so that an off-target edit in one line cannot explain the result, and keep unedited siblings from the same families as controls.

Raise all genotypes together in the same tanks and diet, so that the environment is shared, and score pelvic structures blind to genotype on a stated ordinal scale (spine length and girdle extent, summed across both sides), with a sample size chosen in advance. Measure pelvic Pitx1 RNA in the same lines to link expression to form.

Predictions: if the effects add, the double edit should be about the sum of the single edits. If they interact, the double edit will be larger or smaller than the sum; for example, each alone may do little if the other enhancer compensates. Finally, one marine background may not represent others, so repeat in a second marine population, and keep the result to the backgrounds tested.

</details>
