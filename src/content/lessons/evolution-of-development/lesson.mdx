techniques:
  - name: "Linkage mapping in experimental crosses"
    blurb: >-
      Two populations that differ in a trait are crossed, the hybrids are bred together, and
      hundreds of offspring are scored for the trait and genotyped at markers across the genome.
      A region whose genotype co-segregates with the trait is linked to a cause, but it can hold
      many genes.
  - name: "Whole-mount in situ hybridization"
    blurb: >-
      A labelled antisense RNA probe that pairs with a gene's transcript is applied to a whole
      embryo or larva, showing where the transcript is present. A sense probe is the negative
      control; mixing the two up was the error the 2006 stickleback correction fixed.
  - name: "Allele-specific expression in F1 hybrids"
    blurb: >-
      In a hybrid, both parental alleles share one nucleus and one set of transcription factors.
      Measuring how much RNA comes from each allele, tissue by tissue, tells you whether an
      expression difference is built into the allele's own DNA (cis). Comparing that ratio with the difference between the parent fish shows whether the cell's
      other factors (trans) add to it.
  - name: "Reporter assays and engineered deletions"
    blurb: >-
      A candidate enhancer is placed beside a minimal promoter and a reporter such as GFP or lacZ
      to ask where it can drive expression. Deleting the enhancer at its native locus with
      CRISPR/Cas9 asks a different question: does the organism's form depend on it?

anecdotes:
  - title: "The probe that was the wrong way round"
    source: "Shapiro et al., Correction, Nature 439:1014 (2006)."
    text: >-
      In situ hybridization uses an antisense RNA probe, which pairs with a gene's transcript; the
      matching sense probe should give little specific signal and serves as a control. In 2006 the
      authors of the 2004 stickleback paper published a correction stating that the original Pitx1
      expression figures had been made with a sense probe. With the correct antisense probe, they
      reported Pitx1 expression in the mouth and lower jaw of Paxton benthic larvae but none in
      the pelvic region, and no expression in the tail of either marine or Paxton larvae.
      The sense probe had not been blank: it stained in a pattern not seen with sense probes for other genes, which the authors suggested might reflect natural antisense transcripts from the Pitx1 region. That signal was also lost from the Paxton pelvis, so the central conclusion, a cis-acting regulatory change at Pitx1, survived; the published pictures did not.
  - title: "Eighty-five per cent of what?"
    source: "Thompson et al., eLife 7:e38555 (2018), qRT-PCR of E12.5 mouse hind limbs."
    text: >-
      The mouse PelB result is easy to misquote. Thompson and colleagues measured Pitx1 RNA in
      whole hind limbs at embryonic day 12.5, normalised to the housekeeping gene Pgk1, in seven
      wild-type and eight homozygous mutant samples. The mutant limbs kept about 85% of the control
      level. That is a 15% drop in a bulk average. It does not say every cell lost 15%, and it does
      not translate into a 15% shorter bone. The skeletal effect was a set of modest reductions in
      the lengths of several hind-limb bones.
  - title: "A fragile stretch of DNA, measured in yeast"
    source: "Xie et al., Science 363:81-84 (2019)."
    text: >-
      To ask whether Pel breaks easily, Xie and colleagues did not count mutations in fish
      families. They moved the sequence into budding yeast on an
      artificial chromosome, where breakage can be scored over millions of cell divisions. An empty
      chromosome broke at 3.37 per million divisions, and chromosomes carrying marine Pel broke
      roughly 25 to 50 times as often, and the reverse-complement orientation broke 10 to 20 times less often than the forward one. The result
      describes that yeast assay, not the rate at which wild sticklebacks gain deletions.

**The first film set the puzzle: Paxton Lake (British Columbia) benthic sticklebacks, most with no pelvic bones at all, make an unchanged Pitx1 protein. How was a lost switch proved, and where does the proof stop?**

> **The one idea.** A gene's protein and its pattern of use can change separately. Pitx1's expression is set by several enhancers (stretches of noncoding DNA that switch a gene on in particular tissues). In Paxton fish, a deletion in the pelvic enhancer PelA, with changes in a second enhancer, PelB, goes with the loss of Pitx1 from the developing pelvis, while the protein and its jaw expression are kept. Losing the protein outright would remove Pitx1 wherever it works; losing pelvic enhancer activity can shrink one body part and spare the gene's other jobs.
>
> *Scope:* "Switch" is shorthand: several enhancers can serve one tissue, and one can act in more than one. The case is Pitx1 in threespine sticklebacks: the best-studied route, found in many pelvic-reduced populations but not all, and explaining much of the trait, not all. It is not a claim that form mostly evolves this way.

## The puzzle: a lost pelvis with a working gene

The **threespine stickleback**, *Gasterosteus aculeatus*, lives in the sea along northern coasts and in many lakes colonised after the last ice sheets retreated. Its **pelvis** is **homologous** to the tetrapod hip girdle and hind limb: inherited, modified, from a common ancestor (see [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/)).

The first film showed why a broken protein is a poor route: **Pitx1**, a transcription factor, also works in the jaw, and mice with no working Pitx1 have malformed hind limbs among other defects and die at or shortly after birth. Enhancers are taught in [eukaryotic gene regulation](/lessons/eukaryotic-regulation/); the question here is how to show evolution used one.

## Mapping: one major region, four minor

Shapiro, Kingsley and colleagues reported in 2004 that pelvic reduction in their stickleback crosses mapped to **one major and four minor chromosome regions**, the major one near Pitx1. The method is [linkage mapping](/lessons/linkage-and-mapping/): cross the two forms to make **F1** hybrids, breed them together to make an **F2** generation in which the parents' chromosomes are shuffled, and find regions whose genotype travels with the trait.

**Worked problem, part 1.** These data are invented. A marine fish (pelvis complete) is crossed with a Paxton benthic fish (pelvis reduced). The F1 fish are intercrossed, giving 400 F2 fish. Each is scored for pelvic reduction and genotyped at a marker beside Pitx1: M is the marine allele, P the Paxton allele. All fish are raised together and scored on the same scale. Of 100 MM fish, 2 are reduced; of 200 MP fish, 6; of 100 PP fish, 32.

The fish and counts in the worked problem are invented." />

**Predict.** Does one Paxton copy do much? (Dominant means one copy shows the effect; recessive means it takes two.) And if you knew only a fish's marker genotype, how well could you guess whether its pelvis is reduced?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

1. *Frequency of reduction in each genotype.* MM: 2 of 100, or 2%. MP: 6 of 200, or 3%. PP: 32 of 100, or 32%.
2. *Read dominance.* One Paxton copy (MP) barely changes the frequency; two raise it from 2% to 32%. The region acts close to recessively.
3. *How well does the marker predict reduction?* Of the 40 reduced fish, 32 (80%) are PP. But 68 of the 100 PP fish are not reduced, so other loci or the environment matter too. And 8 reduced fish carry a marine allele; those other factors, or a crossover between the marker and the causal change, could explain them. (How much variation the region explains needs a statistical model, not a head count.)

</details>

In the real 2004 cross the link was tighter: all 70 F2 fish with no pelvic spines on either side carried two Paxton alleles at Pitx1.

A mapped region tells you where to look, not what changed: it can hold many genes. No coding mutation changed the encoded Pitx1 protein sequence in the pelvic-reduced fish they examined. **In situ hybridization**, shown in the first film, gave the pattern in the figure (the first published pictures used the wrong probe; see the anecdote "The probe that was the wrong way round").

Two possibilities remain. The Paxton copy's own pelvic control could be broken: a **cis** change, travelling with that copy of the DNA, like a broken switch wired to one lamp. Or another gene that supplies Pitx1's regulators could be broken: a **trans** change, acting through a diffusible product on every Pitx1 copy in the cell, like a power cut that dims every lamp in the house. Mapping cannot tell these apart, since the regulator's gene could sit in the same region. One kind of fish can.

## Cis or trans: the F1 test

An F1 hybrid cell holds one marine and one Paxton allele in one nucleus, bathed by the same regulators. Measure how much Pitx1 RNA comes from each allele (allele-specific expression), tissue by tissue.

**Predict.** Suppose the Paxton fish's problem were trans: a shortage of some regulator in the pelvis. In an F1 pelvic bud, what share of Pitx1 RNA would come from each allele?

About half each. The F1 cell's regulators act on both alleles alike, so a trans change cannot make one allele lower than the other. Only a difference in one allele's own DNA can, so an unequal share in one tissue only points to a tissue-specific cis change.

## Enhancers on trial: a second switch, and why the first keeps breaking

The second film's tests came from Chan's 2010 study. It left out a second enhancer, and why PelA is lost so often.

In 2018 Thompson, Capellini, Kingsley and colleagues found a second hind-limb enhancer, **PelB**, downstream of Pitx1. Deleting it in mice trimmed hind-limb Pitx1 expression rather than abolishing it, so PelB supplies part of the limb's Pitx1 expression and other enhancers the rest; it is not an on-off switch. Paxton PelB also drove weaker pelvic reporter expression, so Paxton fish carry changes in both enhancers (Going deeper).

**Predict.** The second film found the same switch lost in lake after lake. Why that one? What evidence would point to selection, what to mutation, and could both be true?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Both can contribute: mutation may explain why Pel deletions arise again and again, selection why they spread (with the limits under "The agent of selection" below).

- *Selection.* Chan's 2010 study found signs of positive selection (a variant spreading because it helps its carriers) around Pel in pelvic-reduced populations: an excess of derived alleles (newer variants, not the ancestral ones) and reduced genetic diversity not seen in pelvis-complete lake populations.
- *Mutation.* Xie and colleagues (2019) found that the Pel sequence breaks easily in a yeast chromosome assay, a clue to why deletions recur. That is a yeast result, not a fish mutation rate (anecdote: "A fragile stretch of DNA, measured in yeast").

## What stays open

Each method asks its own question: mapping localises, in situ hybridization shows where transcript is, the F1 test detects a cis difference, a reporter shows what a sequence can drive, rescue asks whether adding it back restores form, and a native-locus edit tests whether form depends on the sequence (done for mouse PelB; in sticklebacks, Xie and colleagues reported that CRISPR breaks at Pel could give local deletions and pelvic loss in crosses). Together they support one claim, a lost pelvic switch rather than a lost protein. The open questions ask how far it reaches:

- **PelA versus PelB.** Swapping the natural Paxton PelA and PelB variants into fish, separately and together, remains to be done; reporters cannot split their contributions (see the Design problem).
- **Other loci.** The four minor regions, and background, are not explained.
- **The agent of selection.** The signals support positive selection on Pel-region variants in pelvic-reduced populations, consistent with a smaller pelvis being favoured. Because a sweep marks a linked region, they do not show which variant or trait was the direct target, or which ecological pressure acted in particular lakes; this lesson names no cause.
- **Generality.** Pel deletions are common but not universal among pelvic-reduced populations, and a 2023 study of two small Norwegian lakes found no obvious association (details under Going deeper). For gene-level change across lineages and reused developmental toolkits, see [molecular evolution](/lessons/molecular-evolution/), [how signaling evolved](/lessons/how-signaling-evolved/) and the [Hox](/lessons/hox-and-hindbrain/) genes.

### Design problem

Paxton fish carry changes in both PelA (upstream) and PelB (downstream). Design an experiment to measure each one's contribution to pelvic size.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Work at the native locus, not with reporters. In marine (pelvis-complete) fish, use genome editing to install the Paxton-type change at PelA alone, PelB alone, and both. Make several independent lines of each (so one off-target edit cannot explain the result), with unedited siblings as controls.

Raise all genotypes together and, blind to genotype, measure spine length and girdle length on each side, with the left-right summary, sample size and statistical model chosen in advance. Measure pelvic Pitx1 RNA in the same lines to link expression to form.

Measure each edit as its loss relative to controls. If effects add on that length scale, the double edit's loss is about the sum of the single losses (double = PelA alone + PelB alone − control); if they interact, larger or smaller (each alone may do little if the other compensates). Ordered categories cannot be added like this; for them, fit an ordinal model chosen in advance and define interaction on its scale. A fish with no pelvis left cannot lose more, and that floor can mimic an interaction. One marine background may not represent others, so repeat in a second marine population and keep the result to the backgrounds tested.

</details>

## Going deeper

### What the F1 test does and does not separate

The F1 logic assumes regulators act alike on both copies; one that reads one allele's sequence differently is itself evidence of a cis difference. To ask whether trans factors add to it, compare the parents (invented numbers, every ratio Paxton over marine, like tissues compared). The F1 allele ratio, 0.11, estimates the cis effect. If cis and trans effects multiply, parents' ratio = cis × trans, so a parents' ratio of 0.11 gives trans = 0.11 / 0.11 = 1.0 (none), and 0.05 gives 0.05 / 0.11, about 0.45.

### PelB in mice and in sticklebacks

Because PelA is not conserved in mammals, Thompson and colleagues tested PelB in mice, deleting 9,425 base pairs containing it at its native mouse locus using CRISPR/Cas9 (methods in [CRISPR and functional genomics](/lessons/crispr-and-functional-genomics/)). Homozygous mice were viable and fertile, and several hind-limb bones were shorter. Their hind-limb Pitx1 RNA, measured at embryonic day 12.5, kept about 85% of the control level, a 15% drop. Patellae were absent only in mice carrying the PelB deletion on one chromosome and a Pitx1 null allele on the other, a sensitized background. PelB's reporter is active mainly in the posterior part of the developing foot (the posterior autopod), so its share may be regional rather than an even trim everywhere.

In sticklebacks, the Paxton benthic PelB carried a 125-base-pair deletion and a 341-base-pair insertion, and its reporter drove weaker, more restricted pelvic expression than the marine one while keeping strong jaw expression.

### The 2023 Norwegian lakes

A 2023 study of sticklebacks from two small subarctic Norwegian lakes and a nearby marine site found no obvious association between PelA variants and pelvic status in the lake where spineless fish occur, and found no variation in PelB.
