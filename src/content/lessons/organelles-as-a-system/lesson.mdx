    story: >-
      George Palade trained as a physician at the University of Bucharest before he turned to the
      study of cells. With James Jamieson he asked a plain question: after a cell builds a protein
      for export, where does that protein go? They worked with thin slices of guinea pig pancreas,
      from the cells that make digestive enzymes. The slices got a 3-minute pulse of a radioactive
      protein building block, so only proteins made in those 3 minutes carried the label. Then
      they followed the label over time with two separate methods: spinning broken cells apart
      into fractions, and electron microscope pictures that show where radioactive grains sit. The
      label showed up first in the rough ER, then near the Golgi, then in storage granules. A 1968
      follow-up found that blocking the cell's energy supply stopped the first step, from the rough ER to the Golgi. Their
      experiments gave direct evidence for the order of stops that textbooks still draw: rough ER, then Golgi, then storage granules.

techniques:
  - name: "Pulse-chase labelling"
    blurb: >-
      Cells get a short pulse of a radioactive building block, then plenty of the ordinary one, so
      only proteins made during the pulse are marked. Sampling at set times afterwards shows where
      that marked group of proteins has moved.
  - name: "Cell fractionation"
    blurb: >-
      Broken cells are spun in a centrifuge so their parts settle into separate layers, such as
      ER pieces and Golgi pieces. Measuring the label in each layer shows which part held the
      marked protein at that time.
  - name: "Electron microscopy and volume measurement"
    blurb: >-
      An electron microscope shows organelles far smaller than a light microscope can. Measuring
      many thin slices, or rebuilding a cell in 3D, gives how much of a cell's volume mitochondria
      fill.

## Explain: the parts of a cell and their jobs

<WordBank terms={['organelle', 'cell membrane', 'cell wall', 'cytoplasm', 'nucleus', 'ribosome', 'endoplasmic reticulum', 'Golgi apparatus', 'vesicle', 'mitochondria', 'chloroplast', 'lysosome', 'vacuole']} />

An **organelle** is a structure inside a cell that does a particular job; most are wrapped in their own membrane. The Virginia list names eleven cell structures. The table gives each one's job for a typical plant cell and a typical animal cell.

| Structure | Main job | Plant cells | Animal cells |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cell membrane** | Thin outer layer that controls what enters and leaves the cell | Yes | Yes |
| **Cell wall** | Stiff layer outside the membrane that supports the cell | Yes | No |
| **Chloroplast** | Carries out photosynthesis, storing light energy in sugar | In green cells, such as leaf cells | No |
| **Cytoplasm** | Everything inside the membrane but outside the nucleus; many reactions happen here | Yes | Yes |
| **Nucleus** | Holds most of the cell's DNA, its instructions | Yes | Yes |
| **Ribosome** | Builds proteins | Yes | Yes |
| **Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)** | Folded membranes; rough ER, dotted with ribosomes, folds new proteins for export, and smooth ER makes lipids | Yes | Yes |
| **Golgi apparatus** | Sorts and packs proteins and sends them on | Yes | Yes |
| **Lysosome** | Sac of digestive enzymes that breaks down worn-out cell parts | Usually not; the vacuole does similar digesting | Yes |
| **Mitochondria** | Release energy from sugar and store it in ATP | Yes | Yes |
| **Vacuole** | Sac that stores water and other materials | One large central vacuole that presses on the wall and keeps the cell firm | Small ones, in some cells |

Two cautions. Plant cells have mitochondria as well as chloroplasts. And not every plant cell has chloroplasts: a root cell underground is not green.

## Explain: following insulin through the cell

A list of parts does not show how they work together. Follow insulin instead.

1. **Nucleus.** The gene for insulin is a stretch of DNA. The cell copies it into messenger RNA, which leaves the nucleus. You can read how in that lesson.
2. **Ribosome on the rough ER.** A ribosome reads the messenger RNA and builds a protein chain. The chain threads into the ER, where it folds. In a beta cell this chain is an insulin precursor, a longer protein that is cut into insulin later.
3. **To the Golgi.** A **vesicle**, a small bubble of membrane that carries materials, buds off the ER and delivers the protein to the Golgi apparatus.
4. **Golgi and storage.** The Golgi sorts the protein and packs it into new vesicles. Inside these vesicles the precursor is cut into insulin, which is stored until it is needed.
5. **Out through the membrane.** When blood sugar rises, the beta cell's mitochondria break down more sugar and make more ATP. The rise in ATP sets off a signal, carried by calcium, that makes more of these vesicles fuse with the cell membrane and release insulin outside the cell. Beta cells also release a smaller amount between meals.

Building a protein chain and moving vesicles both use energy. **ATP** is a small molecule that carries energy in a form cell parts can use; [that lesson](/lessons/respiration-and-atp/) shows how mitochondria make it. In a 1968 experiment on guinea pig pancreas cells that make digestive enzymes, not beta cells, chemicals that blocked the mitochondria's ATP making also stopped proteins from moving from the rough ER to the Golgi.

A factory is a useful model. The nucleus is the office that keeps the blueprints, ribosomes are the assembly line, the ER and Golgi are finishing and shipping, vesicles are delivery trucks and mitochondria are the power plant. The model breaks in three places. No manager gives orders; each step follows from chemistry. A power plant does not create energy from nothing, and neither do mitochondria; they move energy from sugar into ATP. And a cell's parts move around and touch one another all the time.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A secreted protein goes from gene to messenger RNA to ribosome on the rough ER, then by vesicle to the Golgi and out through the membrane, with mitochondria supplying ATP.</Support>

## Explain: cells with and without a nucleus

A **prokaryotic** cell has no nucleus and none of the membrane-bound organelles in this lesson; bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes. A **eukaryotic** cell has a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; plants, animals and fungi are eukaryotes.

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
|---|---|---|
| DNA | In an open region called the nucleoid | Mostly inside a nucleus; mitochondria and chloroplasts hold a little |
| Membrane-bound organelles such as ER, Golgi and mitochondria | None of these | Yes |
| Cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes | Yes | Yes |
| Cell wall | Often | Plants and fungi, not animals |

A single bacterium still carries out every life process with this simpler set of parts.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/organelles-as-a-system/prokaryote-and-eukaryote.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a small rod-shaped bacterium with loose DNA in an open region and scattered ribosomes beside a larger eukaryotic cell whose DNA sits inside a nucleus, with mitochondria and other organelles." caption="A bacterium keeps its DNA in an open region with no membrane around it; a eukaryotic cell keeps most of it inside a nucleus. Not drawn to scale." />

Eukaryotic cells with different jobs hold different amounts of each organelle. In one study of mouse muscles, mitochondrial content differed nearly tenfold between muscle types. In one study, normal Arabidopsis mesophyll cells, the inner leaf cells that do most of the photosynthesis, held 121 chloroplasts on average.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Prokaryotes have no nucleus, and in eukaryotes the amount of each organelle tends to fit the cell's work.</Support>

### Design problem

Do leaves grown in brighter light have more chloroplasts per cell? Plan an investigation with a light microscope. Name your variables, constants, control, sample size and safety steps.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Independent variable:** light level. Grow plants of one species from the same seed batch, some in bright light and some in dim light.
- **Dependent variable:** chloroplasts counted per cell in a thin leaf sample under the microscope, focusing slowly up and down through each cell so no layer is missed.
- **Constants:** species, plant age, leaf position, watering, temperature, magnification and the counting rule.
- **Control:** the dim-light plants are the comparison group for the bright-light plants.
- **Sample size and trials:** several plants per group, several leaves per plant, and at least 20 cells per leaf. Report the mean and the spread.
- **Fairness:** a partner labels the slides with codes, so the counter does not know the group.
- **Limit:** chloroplasts can sit on top of one another, so some may be missed or counted twice; use the same counting rule for every cell.
- **Safety:** handle slides and cover slips carefully and report broken glass.

Insulin's instructions stay in the nucleus as DNA, and a messenger RNA copy carries them to ribosomes on the rough ER. The new protein folds in the ER, travels by vesicle to the Golgi, is packed into storage vesicles, and leaves when a vesicle fuses with the cell membrane. Mitochondria supply the ATP that powers the work along the way.
