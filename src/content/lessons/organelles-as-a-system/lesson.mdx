> **The one idea.** Each cell part on a route can only work on what the part before it hands over. When a eukaryotic cell (one with a nucleus) sends out a protein such as insulin, a messenger RNA copy of the gene leaves the nucleus first. Then the protein travels: from a ribosome on the rough ER, by vesicle (a small bubble of membrane) to the Golgi apparatus, and out when another vesicle fuses with the cell membrane. Many steps use energy, much of it as **ATP** (a small molecule that carries energy cell parts can use) made by mitochondria. So if one step is blocked, the protein cannot reach the stops after it.
>
> *Scope:* the usual route for exported proteins; a few take shortcuts (Going deeper). If a handoff, such as leaving the ER or the Golgi, is blocked, protein already on the route may build up before the block at first. Some may be broken down, and over hours the cell may make less. Blocking the copy or the ribosome instead stops new protein being made. Bacteria, with no nucleus, ER or Golgi, do it differently (below).

## What the first film left out

**Finding the ER.** The first stretch of a new insulin chain is an address tag. A helper particle reads the tag and brings the ribosome and its growing chain to a channel in the ER membrane, and the chain grows through it into the ER.

**The cut.** That chain is an insulin precursor, one chain longer than insulin. In the storage vesicles that leave the Golgi, cuts remove a middle stretch, leaving two short chains held together: insulin, stored until needed.

**The trigger.** When blood sugar rises, more sugar enters a beta cell, and its mitochondria break the sugar down and make more ATP ([Respiration and ATP](/lessons/respiration-and-atp/) shows how).

**Predict.** ATP pays for work. How else could a beta cell use a rise in its ATP?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

As a signal. The rise sets off a chain of events, passed on by calcium acting as a messenger, that makes more storage vesicles fuse with the cell membrane and release insulin. Beta cells also release a smaller amount between meals.

</details>

## Parts on the route, and parts off it

<WordBank terms={['organelle', 'cell membrane', 'cell wall', 'cytoplasm', 'nucleus', 'ribosome', 'endoplasmic reticulum', 'Golgi apparatus', 'vesicle', 'mitochondria', 'chloroplast', 'lysosome', 'vacuole']} />

An **organelle** is a structure inside a cell that does a particular job; most are wrapped in their own membrane. Of the eleven structures on the Virginia list, five are on insulin's route (nucleus, ribosome, ER, Golgi apparatus, cell membrane). Mitochondria supply much of its energy, and the vesicles travel through the cytoplasm. The other four do other jobs (cell wall, chloroplast, lysosome, vacuole).

| Structure | Main job | Plant cells | Animal cells |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cell membrane** | Thin outer layer that controls what enters and leaves the cell | Yes | Yes |
| **Cell wall** | Stiff layer outside the membrane that supports the cell | Yes | No |
| **Chloroplast** | Carries out photosynthesis, storing light energy in sugar | In green cells, such as leaf cells | No |
| **Cytoplasm** | Everything inside the membrane but outside the nucleus; many reactions happen here | Yes | Yes |
| **Nucleus** | Holds most of the cell's DNA, its instructions | Yes | Yes |
| **Ribosome** | Builds proteins | Yes | Yes |
| **Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)** | Folded membranes; rough ER, dotted with ribosomes, folds new proteins for export or for membranes, and smooth ER makes lipids | Yes | Yes |
| **Golgi apparatus** | Sorts and packs proteins and sends them on | Yes | Yes |
| **Lysosome** | Sac of digestive enzymes that breaks down worn-out cell parts | Usually not; the vacuole does similar digesting | Yes |
| **Mitochondria** | Release energy from sugar and store it in ATP | Yes | Yes |
| **Vacuole** | Sac that stores water and other materials | One large central vacuole that presses on the wall and keeps the cell firm | Small ones, in some cells |

## A factory model, and where it breaks

In a factory model, the nucleus is the office that keeps blueprints and sends out copies, ribosomes the assembly line, the ER and Golgi finishing and shipping, vesicles delivery trucks, and mitochondria the power plant. It breaks in four places.

- No single manager gives orders; signals such as calcium, and chemistry at each step, set what happens.
- Mitochondria do not create energy; they move energy from sugar into ATP.
- A cell's parts move around and touch one another all the time.
- Real proteins do not travel as one neat shipment.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A messenger RNA copy of the gene leaves the nucleus; the protein is built at a ribosome on the rough ER, carried by vesicle to the Golgi and out through the cell membrane, with mitochondria supplying much of the ATP.</Support>

## Same jobs, simpler parts: prokaryotic cells

A **prokaryotic** cell has no nucleus and none of the membrane-bound organelles on this route; bacteria and archaea (another group of single-celled microbes) are prokaryotes. A **eukaryotic** cell has a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; plants, animals and fungi are eukaryotes.

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
|---|---|---|
| DNA | In an open region with no membrane, the **nucleoid** | Mostly inside a nucleus; mitochondria and chloroplasts hold a little |
| ER, Golgi and mitochondria | None | Yes |
| Cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes | Yes | Yes |
| Cell wall | Often | Plants and fungi, not animals |

A single bacterium still carries out every life process with this simpler set of parts.

**Predict.** A bacterium also sends proteins out, yet it has no ER or Golgi. How do you think it does the job? Hint: think about the membrane, or membranes, around the cell.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

With channels in its membranes. Its ribosomes build the protein in the cytoplasm, and a channel in the cell membrane moves it across. Many bacteria also have a second, outer membrane, which needs another channel or a system that spans both membranes. In bacteria that use oxygen, the cell membrane also makes much of the ATP, the job mitochondria do in your cells. Others make most of theirs in the cytoplasm.

</details>

## Cells built for their work

Cells that need a large, steady supply of ATP made using oxygen often hold more mitochondria. In one study of mouse muscles, mitochondrial content differed nearly tenfold between muscle types. In normal mouse hearts, which beat without rest, mitochondria filled about 30% of muscle cell volume. These are single studies, and disease can change the amount too (Going deeper).

Chloroplasts follow the work too. Inner leaf cells (mesophyll cells) of the small plant *Arabidopsis*, which do most of its photosynthesis, held 121 chloroplasts each on average; root cells underground are not green and have none.

**Predict.** Plants with a change in a nuclear gene called *arc5* had only 13 chloroplasts per leaf cell. Does that mean their leaf cells have only a small fraction of the normal amount of chloroplast?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not necessarily: each fully grown *arc5* chloroplast was about six times larger, so the count alone understates how much chloroplast is there. Count the parts, and measure their size too. The gene also shows one organelle depending on another: it sits in the nucleus, yet changes how chloroplasts divide.

</details>

### Design problem

Do leaves grown in brighter light have more chloroplasts per cell? Plan an investigation with a light microscope. Name your variables, constants, control, sample size and safety steps.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Independent variable:** light level. Grow plants of one species from one seed batch, some in bright and some in dim light.
- **Dependent variable:** chloroplasts per cell in a thin leaf sample, focusing slowly up and down so no layer is missed.
- **Constants:** species, plant age, leaf position, watering, temperature, magnification and the counting rule.
- **Control:** the dim-light plants are the comparison group.
- **Sample size:** several plants per group, several leaves per plant, at least 20 cells per leaf. Report the mean and the spread.
- **Fairness:** a partner codes the slides, so the counter does not know the group.
- **Limit:** chloroplasts can overlap and be missed or counted twice; use one counting rule for every cell.
- **Safety:** handle slides and cover slips carefully and report broken glass.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Two more mitochondria numbers, and a fair test

In the same heart study, mitochondria filled 41.1 ± 2.7% of heart muscle cell volume in GENA348 disease-model mice. That does not show amounts fit a cell's work; it may be a response to the disease. In 5 normal rats, liver-cell mitochondria made up 18% of the whole liver's volume (Weibel and colleagues, Journal of Cell Biology, 1969). Why is comparing 30.2% in heart cells with 18% in liver not a fair, matched comparison?

<details>
<summary>One answer</summary>

Two things differ: the species (mice, rats) and the measure (share of one cell type's volume, share of the whole organ's volume).

</details>

### Shortcuts and other energy carriers

A few exported proteins skip the ER and Golgi. And ATP is not the only energy carrier on the route: ribosomes and the machinery that buds vesicles also use a close chemical relative of ATP.
