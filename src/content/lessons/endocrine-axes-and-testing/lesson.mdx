techniques:
  - name: "Frequent blood sampling with sleep staging"
    blurb: >-
      Place an indwelling venous catheter so blood can be drawn every 10 to 30 minutes with little disturbance to
      the subject, and record EEG and eye movements to stage sleep. The paired records separate
      short secretory pulses from the daily rhythm and link release to a state such as sleep onset.
  - name: "Competitive and two-site (sandwich) immunoassays"
    blurb: >-
      In a competitive assay, sample hormone and a labelled hormone compete for a limited antibody,
      so bound label falls as hormone rises. In a sandwich assay, a capture antibody and a signal
      antibody bind two sites on one hormone molecule, so signal rises with hormone. Both report
      what an antibody recognizes, which is not always what a receptor responds to.
  - name: "Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) with equilibrium dialysis"
    blurb: >-
      Chromatography separates the hormone from related molecules, and two stages of mass
      spectrometry identify it by its mass and fragments and quantify it. Dialysing serum across a
      membrane first, until free hormone equilibrates, lets the method measure the free fraction
      directly rather than estimate it.
  - name: "Gene knockout mice, global and cell-specific"
    blurb: >-
      Delete a receptor gene in every cell, or only in one cell type with a Cre recombinase driven
      by that cell's promoter, and ask which feedback arrow disappears. Lifelong deletion can let
      other parts of the axis adapt, so a phenotype is evidence about a loop, not a direct reading
      of its strength in adults.

    rationale: >-
      With a limited number of antibody sites, more unlabelled hormone from the sample occupies

**In 1968, seven of eight young adults sampled through the night showed a large surge of growth hormone as they entered deep sleep, and when sleep was delayed by about three hours in four of the eight, the surge was delayed with it in the three who showed one.**

A hormone level is one point on a moving curve. This lesson labels the feedback loops of four anterior-pituitary axes and asks what one sample, a pair of samples, a challenge test or the assay itself can tell you about where an axis has failed.

## Five axes, one wiring plan: a releasing signal, a pituitary hormone, a target

[The endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/) introduced the hypothalamus, portal vessels and anterior pituitary, and [hypothalamus and autonomic control](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/) tells how the portal link was shown. The plan is a relay, like a chain of managers: a hypothalamic signal in portal blood, a pituitary hormone, a target tissue.

- **Growth hormone (GH).** GHRH (growth hormone-releasing hormone) stimulates somatotrophs; somatostatin, in the same portal blood, inhibits them. GH makes the liver secrete IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1), and GH also acts directly through its own receptors, for example promoting breakdown of stored fat in adipose tissue, so IGF-1 does not carry every GH effect. GH and prolactin receptors signal through JAK2 and STAT5 ([JAK-STAT](/lessons/jak-stat/)).
- **Prolactin.** This axis runs backwards. Hypothalamic dopamine tonically inhibits prolactin release through lactotroph D2 receptors. Several factors, TRH among them, can stimulate prolactin in experiments, but no releasing hormone dominates. Milk production belongs to [pregnancy, birth and lactation](/lessons/pregnancy-birth-lactation/).
- **ACTH.** CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone), helped by vasopressin from the same hypothalamic neurons, drives ACTH, and ACTH drives the adrenal cortex to make cortisol ([the adrenal glands](/lessons/adrenal-glands/)).
- **TSH.** TRH drives TSH, and TSH stimulation is required for the thyroid to make enough T4 and T3 ([thyroid hormone](/lessons/thyroid-hormone/)).
- **LH and FSH.** Pulsatile GnRH drives both ([animal reproduction](/lessons/animal-reproduction/)).

For the MCAT headings: releasing hormones, pituitary hormones and dopamine are peptides or amines acting mostly on G protein-coupled receptors (GH and prolactin are exceptions: their receptors use JAK2) and second messengers such as cyclic AMP and calcium ([GPCRs and G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/), [cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/)). Cortisol and thyroid hormone travel mostly on carrier proteins and act on [nuclear receptors](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/). Target specificity is receptor expression: every cell sees ACTH, and only cells with ACTH receptors respond.

## Feedback comes in long, short and ultra-short loops

A feedback arrow is named by its origin. **Long-loop** feedback runs from the target-gland hormone back to the hypothalamus and pituitary. **Short-loop** feedback runs from a pituitary hormone back to the hypothalamus. **Ultra-short** feedback is a hormone acting back on the cells that make it, classically a hypothalamic releasing hormone acting on its own neurons. The term is sometimes also used for a pituitary hormone acting on its own cells.

| Arrow | Type | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Cortisol on CRH and ACTH | Long | Strong, at both levels |
| T4 and T3 on TRH and TSH | Long | Strong, at both levels |
| IGF-1 on GH | Long | Strong, human infusion |
| Prolactin on dopamine neurons | Short | Causal, knockout mice |
| GH on GHRH neurons | Short | Causal, cell-specific knockout mice |
| TSH or ACTH on hypothalamus | Short | Less established |
| Prolactin on lactotrophs | Ultra-short | Suggested by cell culture, of proliferation not secretion |

The GH long loop was tested in people. Bermann, Barkan and colleagues (1994) gave six normal fed men a saline infusion followed, in the same admission, by a 48-hour infusion of recombinant IGF-I, sampling GH every 10 minutes for 24 hours in each period. Overall GH fell by 85 plus or minus 3 percent and pulse amplitude by 77 plus or minus 4 percent (mean plus or minus SE). The authors favoured increased hypothalamic somatostatin, an inference from hormone responses; nobody sampled portal blood.

The modern experiment tests a short loop: prolactin is thought to stimulate the dopamine neurons that inhibit it. Schuff, Low and colleagues (2002) compared mice lacking D2 receptors, mice lacking prolactin receptors and compound mutants, used D2 agonist and antagonist challenges, and cultured pituitary cells. If prolactin acted only by driving dopamine, the prolactin receptor knockout should look like the D2 knockout. Females lacking either receptor developed hyperprolactinemia and large prolactinomas, but prolactin receptor knockouts had more hyperprolactinemia than age-matched D2 knockouts and had lost much of their dopamine tone. Prolactin also slowed proliferation of normal female lactotrophs in culture. So the loop runs through the hypothalamus and also acts at the pituitary independently of dopamine, which fits an ultra-short action on lactotroph proliferation, shown in cultured cells. Global, lifelong deletions cannot assign each phenotype to one cell population, and the results are mainly in female mice.

For GH, deleting GH receptors only in GHRH-expressing mouse cells raised pulsatile GH secretion and pulse amplitude, yet acute GH or IGF-1 did not change GHRH-neuron firing in recordings (Gusmao and colleagues, 2025). Short-loop feedback need not mean fast electrical inhibition. TSH and ACTH short loops rest on older, thinner evidence, and their role in humans is not settled.

## Hormones keep time: pulses, daily rhythms and sleep

The classic experiment asked what times the night-time GH rise. Takahashi, Kipnis and Daughaday (1968) studied eight young adults over 38 nights, drawing repeated blood samples through an indwelling catheter while EEG and eye-movement recordings staged sleep. If a clock or the overnight fall in glucose set the rise, delaying sleep should leave the peak in place. In seven of the eight, a major GH peak appeared at the onset of deep (slow-wave) sleep and lasted 1.5 to 3.5 hours. Delaying sleep onset by about three hours in four subjects delayed the peak in the three who had one, three subjects woken for two to three hours showed another peak on falling asleep again, and the peak did not correlate with concurrent changes in glucose, insulin or cortisol. That ties the surge to sleep onset in these young adults and argues against a simple glucose or insulin trigger. It does not show the hypothalamic mechanism (more GHRH or less somatostatin), that the pattern holds at every age, or that glucose never regulates GH. Sleep staging is in [circadian rhythms and sleep](/lessons/sleep-and-rhythms/).

The HPA axis keeps a daily rhythm with pulses on top. In humans under normal conditions on a usual sleep-wake schedule, it peaks between 06:00 and 10:00 and reaches its nadir between 22:00 and 02:00, with ACTH and cortisol pulses on that curve; the rodent pulse experiments are in [emotion and stress](/lessons/emotion-and-stress/).

Pulses matter for measurement because of clearance. Parker, Utiger and Daughaday (1962) injected 5 or 10 mg of human GH intravenously into five people and found that plasma levels fell with an average half-life of 27 minutes (individual values 23 to 38). That is one small study's mean after a large dose, not a constant; later deconvolution of naturally secreted GH in healthy adults gave shorter values, about 15 minutes. Either way the scale is the same: a pulse decays to a quarter or less within an hour, and a sample drawn just before a pulse can read many-fold lower than one drawn on its peak. A random GH value is weak evidence, and in adults a single random GH or IGF-1 value is not considered enough, in most patients, to establish GH deficiency. IGF-1 serves as an integrated readout because it circulates mostly bound to binding proteins and is considered to have a much longer half-life than GH, so it averages many pulses, though it also depends on nutrition, liver function and age.

## Paired levels locate the lesion; dynamic tests ask about reserve

A **primary** disorder starts in the target gland, a **secondary** one in the pituitary, a **tertiary** one in the hypothalamus. Treat the trophic hormone as the pituitary's report on its target: a low target hormone should make an intact pituitary drive hard.

**Worked problem: reading three pairs.** In this made-up example, three adults on a normal sleep-wake schedule, taking no glucocorticoid and with normal cortisol-binding globulin, have blood drawn at 08:00. The numbers are illustrative, the ACTH values are invented, and reference intervals differ by laboratory and assay.

| Person | Cortisol (µg per dL) | ACTH (pg per mL) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 3 | 300 |
| B | 3 | 5 |
| C | 14 | 30 |

*Step 1, convert.* Cortisol's molar mass is about 362.5 g per mol, so 1 µg per dL = 10 µg per L = (10 x 10^-6 g per L) / (362.5 g per mol) = 2.76 x 10^-8 mol per L, or 27.6 nmol per L.

> **cortisol (nmol per L) = cortisol (µg per dL) x 27.6**

A and B: 3 x 27.6 = 82.8 nmol per L. C: 14 x 27.6 = 386.4 nmol per L.

*Step 2, read each pair.* A: low cortisol, high ACTH. The pituitary senses low cortisol and drives hard; the adrenal does not answer: primary. B: low cortisol, low ACTH. The drive is missing: secondary or tertiary, and the pair cannot say which, because either lesion lowers ACTH. C: morning cortisol in the expected direction with mid-range ACTH; no failure shown at rest, though a value of 14 µg per dL does not by itself prove normal reserve if adrenal insufficiency is suspected.

*Step 3, stimulate.* B receives a dose of synthetic ACTH (cosyntropin, ACTH 1-24), and cortisol 30 minutes later is 16 µg per dL, or 16 x 27.6 = 441.6 nmol per L. Whether that is an adequate response depends on the assay: criteria derived in 2021 for newer, more specific cortisol assays sit below the historical criterion from older immunoassays.

*Step 4, interpret.* An adequate response shows only that the adrenal can answer ACTH. After a recent loss of central drive the adrenal cortex may not yet have atrophied and can respond normally, so a normal result does not exclude a recent pituitary or hypothalamic defect. Answers: A primary; B secondary or tertiary, unresolved; C no failure shown; B's 441.6 nmol per L takes its label from the assay.

The thyroid reads the same way: high TSH with low free T4 is the classical primary pattern, while low free T4 with low or inappropriately normal TSH points to central disease.

Dynamic tests change one input and watch the output. **Suppression tests** probe autonomy. A night-time dose of dexamethasone, a synthetic glucocorticoid, normally lowers ACTH and next-morning cortisol; failure to suppress suggests secretion escaping feedback but does not locate the source, and faster dexamethasone clearance or more binding globulin can confound it. Oral glucose normally suppresses GH, and failure to suppress helps when GH excess is in question. **Stimulation tests** probe reserve: synthetic ACTH tests the adrenal, and GH stimulation tests (insulin-induced hypoglycemia, glucagon, or macimorelin, a ghrelin-receptor agonist) test whether the GH axis can release GH; several act through the hypothalamus rather than on somatotrophs directly. A test that stimulates a gland directly cannot say whether a weak response reflects the gland itself or a long-standing loss of drive from above, and a normal response does not exclude a recent upstream defect.

**Test the wiring: a compressed stalk.** A non-secreting mass presses on the pituitary stalk, and portal delivery falls. Hormones driven by releasing hormones tend to fall (ACTH, TSH, GH, LH and FSH), in combinations that vary from patient to patient, and with them cortisol, free T4, IGF-1 and sex steroids; TSH may be low or inappropriately normal rather than high. Prolactin rises, usually modestly, because the signal it loses is inhibitory dopamine. A drug that blocks D2 receptors, as many antipsychotics do, also raises prolactin, with no lesion at all, and a D2 agonist lowers prolactin whether the cause is a stalk lesion or a prolactinoma. So a drug response does not locate the problem. What points to the stalk is a modest prolactin rise together with low levels of other pituitary hormones.

## A number is only as good as its assay

Competitive radioimmunoassay is explained in [detecting a signal](/lessons/detecting-a-signal/). In a **competitive** assay, labelled and sample hormone compete for a limited antibody, so bound signal falls as hormone rises. In a **two-site (sandwich)** assay, a capture antibody and a signal antibody bind two sites on one molecule, so signal rises with hormone. Sandwich assays suit large peptides such as TSH; small molecules such as free T4 are usually measured competitively.

Both measure **immunoreactivity**, what an antibody recognizes, not **bioactivity**, what a receptor responds to. Macroprolactin, a large prolactin-IgG complex, is detected by immunoassays but has low biological activity, so a high reported prolactin can mean little at the receptor. Antibodies also cross-react with related molecules, a problem for steroids. **LC-MS/MS** (liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry) separates the hormone from its relatives and identifies it by mass and fragments, so it reports a defined chemical species. Better specificity changes the numbers, so a criterion from an older assay can mislabel a newer result.

Then there is binding. Roughly 99.97 percent of circulating T4 is protein-bound in commonly quoted figures, and published values vary a little with method. **Free** hormone is the unbound fraction, considered the part available to tissues ([binding and dose-response](/lessons/binding-and-dose-response/)). Total T4 moves with binding protein: more thyroxine-binding globulin, as in pregnancy or with oral estrogen, raises total T4 while free T4 can stay near its usual range. Physical separation by equilibrium dialysis followed by isotope-dilution LC-MS/MS is a reference approach for free thyroid hormones; routine free-hormone immunoassays are method-dependent estimates.



### Design problem

In a 2020 study, ten healthy adults took a biotin supplement daily for a week, at a lower and later a higher amount, and their thyroid tests were run on three platforms. Two hours after the last dose of the first regimen, one platform reported lower TSH and higher free T4 than baseline, a pattern that looks hyperthyroid, while the tested assays on another showed no significant change. A healthy volunteer taking biotin shows that pattern. Design tests that separate a real change in thyroid function from an artefact of measurement.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with the mechanism. The standard explanation is that some immunoassays capture the antibody complex with biotin and streptavidin. Excess sample biotin occupies the streptavidin, less complex is captured, and signal falls. In a sandwich assay (TSH) less signal reads as less hormone; in a competitive assay (free T4) it reads as more. Both errors point toward "hyperthyroid".

Rerun the same sample on a platform without a biotin capture step. A biological change should appear on both; an artefact only on the susceptible one.

Repeat after stopping biotin. In this study the interference lasted about one to two days after the higher regimen on the susceptible systems, so a repeat after that should return to baseline if the change was analytical. This small study does not set a universal washout.

Look for physiological agreement: a real hyperthyroid state usually also moves heart rate and symptoms, though older adults may show few, and T3 measured on a platform without a biotin step, because T3 assays on the susceptible platform can be biased too.

The study did not test larger doses, impaired kidney function, other analytes or current versions of every assay.
