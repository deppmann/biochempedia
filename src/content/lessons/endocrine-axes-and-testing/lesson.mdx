techniques:
  - name: "Frequent blood sampling with sleep staging"
    blurb: >-
      Place an indwelling venous catheter so blood can be drawn every 10 to 30 minutes with little disturbance to
      the subject, and record EEG and eye movements to stage sleep. The paired records separate
      short secretory pulses from the daily rhythm and link release to a state such as sleep onset.
  - name: "Competitive and two-site (sandwich) immunoassays"
    blurb: >-
      In a competitive assay, sample hormone and a labelled hormone compete for a limited antibody,
      so bound label falls as hormone rises. In a sandwich assay, a capture antibody and a signal
      antibody bind two sites on one hormone molecule, so signal rises with hormone. Both report
      what an antibody recognizes, which is not always what a receptor responds to.
  - name: "Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) with equilibrium dialysis"
    blurb: >-
      Chromatography separates the hormone from related molecules, and two stages of mass
      spectrometry identify it by its mass and fragments and quantify it. Dialysing serum across a
      membrane first, until free hormone equilibrates, lets the method measure the free fraction
      directly rather than estimate it.
  - name: "Gene knockout mice, global and cell-specific"
    blurb: >-
      Delete a receptor gene in every cell, or only in one cell type with a Cre recombinase driven
      by that cell's promoter, and ask which feedback arrow disappears. Lifelong deletion can let
      other parts of the axis adapt, so a phenotype is evidence about a loop, not a direct reading
      of its strength in adults.

    choices:
      - "Tonic inhibition by dopamine acting on lactotroph D2 receptors"
      - "Stimulation by a prolactin-releasing hormone in portal blood"
      - "Stimulation by somatostatin released along with each GH pulse"
      - "Inhibition by IGF-1 returning from the liver to the pituitary"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Hypothalamic dopamine reaches the lactotrophs through portal blood and inhibits prolactin
      release through D2 receptors, tonically, so removing the hypothalamic signal raises
      prolactin. A dominant prolactin-releasing hormone is the mistake of assuming every pituitary
      hormone is driven like ACTH or TSH; several factors can stimulate prolactin in experiments
      but none dominates. Somatostatin inhibits growth hormone and does not stimulate anything
      here. IGF-1 is the long-loop signal of the growth hormone axis, not of prolactin.
  - stem: "In mice, growth hormone receptors were deleted only in GHRH-expressing cells, and pulsatile GH secretion increased. Which kind of feedback does this experiment test?"
    choices:
      - "Long-loop feedback, from a target-gland hormone"
      - "Ultra-short feedback, a hormone acting on its own source cells"
      - "Feedforward drive from the liver to the hypothalamus"
      - "Short-loop feedback, from a pituitary hormone"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Growth hormone is the pituitary hormone, and GHRH neurons are in the hypothalamus, so a loop
      from GH back to GHRH neurons is short-loop feedback by definition. Long-loop feedback would
      come from the target product, IGF-1 from the liver, not from GH itself. Ultra-short feedback
      acts back on the cells that made the hormone, but here a pituitary hormone acts on hypothalamic cells. A
      feedforward signal would raise output ahead of a change, and removing it would lower GH,
      the opposite of what was seen.
  - stem: "An adult not taking thyroid medicine has a low free T4, and a TSH that sits inside the laboratory's reference interval. Where does this pair point?"
    choices:
      - "To the thyroid gland, because free T4 is the hormone that is low"
      - "Nowhere, because a TSH in the reference interval rules out disease"
      - "To the pituitary or hypothalamus, because TSH failed to rise"
      - "To the thyroid gland, because TSH is not suppressed"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      With free T4 low, an intact pituitary should push TSH well up. A TSH that stays in the
      reference interval has failed to rise: it is inappropriately normal, and it reads the same as
      low, a same-direction pattern that points to central disease. Blaming the thyroid because free
      T4 is low ignores the trophic hormone, which is the whole point of reading a pair; primary
      failure would come with a high TSH. Treating an in-range TSH as reassuring judges the trophic
      hormone against the population instead of against its own target. Pointing to the thyroid
      because TSH is not suppressed confuses this with an overactive gland, where free T4 would be
      high, not low.
  - stem: "A patient's pituitary stopped making ACTH many months ago. The patient is given synthetic ACTH, and cortisol is measured 30 minutes later. What response is expected, and why?"
    choices:
      - "A normal rise, because the adrenal gland itself is not diseased"
      - "A weak rise, because the adrenal cortex has shrunk without ACTH"
      - "No rise, because synthetic ACTH acts on the pituitary, not the adrenal"
      - "An exaggerated rise, because the adrenal has been waiting for signal"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      ACTH is trophic: besides driving cortisol secretion, it keeps the cortisol-making layers of the
      adrenal cortex from shrinking. After months without it the cortex has shrunk, so one dose
      usually cannot produce a full response, and that weak rise is how the test detects long-standing
      central insufficiency. A normal rise is what a recent loss of drive can give, before the cortex
      has had time to shrink, not a loss months old. Synthetic ACTH acts directly on the adrenal,
      which is why the test probes the gland. Nothing in the axis stores up response while drive is
      missing, so an exaggerated rise has no basis.
  - stem: "In a competitive radioimmunoassay, a fixed amount of radiolabelled hormone competes with hormone from the sample for a limited amount of antibody. As the sample's hormone concentration rises, what happens to the antibody-bound radioactivity?"
    choices:
      - "It rises, because more hormone is bound to antibody"
      - "It falls, because sample hormone displaces the tracer"
      - "It stays constant, because antibody is in excess"
      - "It rises, because the tracer binds sample hormone"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      With a limited number of antibody sites, more unlabelled hormone from the sample occupies
      more of them, leaving fewer for the tracer, so bound radioactivity falls. More sample hormone is
      bound, but the counter sees only the label, which is the reversed arrow behind the
      rising choice. If antibody were in excess there would be no competition and the assay would
      not work, so the constant choice describes a broken design. Tracer and sample hormone are the
      same molecule competing for antibody; they do not bind each other.
  - stem: "Why is plasma IGF-1 often used as a readout of growth hormone secretion integrated over many hours, rather than a single GH sample?"
    choices:
      - "IGF-1 is measured by mass spectrometry, unlike GH"
      - "IGF-1 is secreted by the pituitary in the same pulses"
      - "IGF-1 mediates every action that GH has on tissues"
      - "IGF-1 lasts longer in plasma, smoothing out GH pulses"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      IGF-1 is made mainly by the liver in response to GH, circulates largely bound to binding
      proteins, and is considered to persist far longer in plasma than GH, whose average half-life
      after intravenous injection was 27 minutes in a 1962 human study; a slow readout averages the
      pulses. The pituitary is not the source of circulating IGF-1, and if IGF-1 pulsed with GH it would not help.
      GH also acts directly on tissues such as fat, so IGF-1 does not mediate every action. Both
      hormones are usually measured by immunoassay, so the assay is not the reason.
  - stem: "A non-secreting mass compresses the pituitary stalk and reduces portal blood flow from the hypothalamus to the anterior pituitary. Which pattern of plasma hormones best fits the wiring of the axes?"
    choices:
      - "Every anterior pituitary hormone falls to the same degree"
      - "ACTH, TSH and prolactin all rise as feedback weakens"
      - "ACTH and GH fall while prolactin modestly rises"
      - "Prolactin falls while ACTH, TSH and GH all rise"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      ACTH, GH, TSH, LH and FSH depend on stimulating releasing hormones that arrive in portal
      blood, so they tend to fall when portal delivery falls, though not every axis fails in every patient. Prolactin is the exception because its
      dominant hypothalamic signal is inhibitory dopamine, so losing that signal lets prolactin
      rise, usually modestly. Having everything rise reverses the sign of the releasing hormones.
      Having prolactin fall and the others rise swaps the two kinds of control. A uniform fall
      ignores that prolactin is held down, not driven up, by the hypothalamus.

**Two people have the same low morning cortisol. In one, the adrenal gland has failed. In the other, the adrenal is healthy, but nothing is telling it to work.**

One number cannot tell them apart. Two numbers can: cortisol, and ACTH, the pituitary hormone that drives the adrenal, read together. Two terms first. A **target hormone** is made by a gland the pituitary drives, such as cortisol from the adrenal cortex. A **trophic hormone** is the pituitary hormone that drives that gland, such as ACTH.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Read the target hormone and its trophic hormone together. If the fault is in the target gland, feedback still works, so the pituitary pushes against it and the two hormones move in **opposite directions**. If the fault is in the drive from above (the pituitary or the hypothalamus), they move in the **same direction**.

The rule holds once the axis has settled, for free hormone, in a sample timed to the hormone's rhythm, on an assay whose number means what you think. It does not apply to prolactin. Going deeper lists where else it is silent or fooled.

## One chain, three links, one feedback loop

[The endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/) introduced the hypothalamus, portal vessels and anterior pituitary, and [hypothalamus and autonomic control](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/) tells how the portal link was shown. The plan is a relay, like a chain of managers: a hypothalamic signal in portal blood, a pituitary hormone, a target tissue. Five axes follow it.

- **ACTH.** CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone), helped by vasopressin from the same hypothalamic neurons, drives ACTH, and ACTH drives the adrenal cortex to make cortisol ([the adrenal glands](/lessons/adrenal-glands/)).
- **TSH.** TRH drives TSH, and TSH stimulation is required for the thyroid to make enough T4 and T3 ([thyroid hormone](/lessons/thyroid-hormone/)).
- **Growth hormone (GH).** GHRH (growth hormone-releasing hormone) stimulates somatotrophs; somatostatin, in the same portal blood, inhibits them. GH makes the liver secrete IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1). GH also acts directly through its own receptors, for example promoting breakdown of stored fat in adipose tissue, so IGF-1 does not carry every GH effect.
- **LH and FSH.** Pulsatile GnRH drives both ([animal reproduction](/lessons/animal-reproduction/)).
- **Prolactin, the axis that runs on a brake.** Hypothalamic dopamine tonically inhibits prolactin release through lactotroph D2 receptors. Several factors, TRH among them, can stimulate prolactin in experiments, but no releasing hormone dominates. Milk production belongs to [pregnancy, birth and lactation](/lessons/pregnancy-birth-lactation/).

For the MCAT: releasing hormones, pituitary hormones and dopamine are peptides or amines acting mostly on G protein-coupled receptors and second messengers such as cyclic AMP and calcium ([GPCRs and G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/), [cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/)). GH and prolactin are the exceptions: their receptors signal through JAK2 and STAT5 ([JAK-STAT](/lessons/jak-stat/)). Cortisol and thyroid hormone travel mostly on carrier proteins and act on [nuclear receptors](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/). Target specificity is receptor expression: every cell sees ACTH, and only cells with ACTH receptors respond.

**Trophic means two jobs.** A trophic hormone tells its gland to secrete now. Over weeks it also keeps the gland's secreting cells from shrinking: take ACTH away for weeks and the cortisol-making layers of the adrenal cortex shrink, which will matter for the stimulation tests.

**Long-loop feedback closes the chain.** The target hormone travels back and restrains the links above it: cortisol restrains CRH and ACTH, T4 and T3 restrain TRH and TSH, and IGF-1 restrains GH. The evidence for all three loops is strong, at both the hypothalamus and the pituitary for cortisol and thyroid hormone, and from a human infusion experiment for IGF-1. Shorter loops, from a pituitary hormone back to the hypothalamus or onto the cells that made it, have causal evidence in mice for prolactin and GH and weaker evidence elsewhere; how much they matter in humans is open (Going deeper).

Feedback is why the rule works: an intact pituitary keeps reading the target hormone, so the trophic hormone is a running report on its target. If the drive from above fails or runs too hard, the gland simply follows it.

## Which link failed? Read the pair

A disorder is named by the link that fails. In a **primary** disorder the target gland itself fails. In a **secondary** disorder the pituitary fails. In a **tertiary** disorder the hypothalamus fails. Secondary and tertiary together are called **central**.

The film at the top of this page walks through persons A and B below. It opens with hormone pulses, which the section on timing takes up.

### Three mornings

In this made-up example, three adults on a normal sleep-wake schedule have blood drawn at 08:00, near cortisol's daily peak. None takes a glucocorticoid medicine, and all have normal cortisol-binding globulin. The numbers are illustrative, the ACTH values are invented, and reference intervals differ by laboratory and assay.

| Person | Cortisol (µg per dL) | ACTH (pg per mL) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 3 | 300 |
| B | 3 | 5 |
| C | 14 | 30 |

Cortisol is reported in two units. Its molar mass is about 362.5 g per mol, so 1 µg per dL = 10 µg per L = (10 x 10^-6 g per L) / (362.5 g per mol) = 2.76 x 10^-8 mol per L, or 27.6 nmol per L.

> **cortisol (nmol per L) = cortisol (µg per dL) x 27.6**

A and B: 3 x 27.6 = 82.8 nmol per L. C: 14 x 27.6 = 386.4 nmol per L.

**Predict.** A and B have the same low cortisol. Where has each chain failed?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

**A: low cortisol, very high ACTH. The two move in opposite directions.** The pituitary senses too little cortisol and drives hard, and the adrenal does not answer. The fault is in the gland: **primary** adrenal insufficiency.

**B: low cortisol, low ACTH. The two move in the same direction.** The adrenal is getting almost no signal. The fault is above it: **central**. The pair cannot say whether it is the pituitary (secondary) or the hypothalamus (tertiary), because either lesion lowers ACTH.

</details>

Left: the adrenal is damaged, the pituitary sends a thick ACTH arrow, and the gauges show ACTH high and cortisol low, moving in opposite directions. Right: the pituitary is damaged, a thin ACTH arrow reaches a healthy adrenal, and the gauges show ACTH low and cortisol low, moving in the same direction." caption="Same low cortisol, two different faults: when the gland fails, feedback drives the trophic hormone up and the pair moves in opposite directions; when the drive fails, both fall together, which points above the gland but cannot say whether to the pituitary or the hypothalamus." />

C has cortisol and ACTH both in the usual morning range, so nothing has failed at rest. A resting pair cannot show how the axis would cope with stress, and a value of 14 µg per dL does not by itself prove normal reserve if adrenal insufficiency is suspected. That is a question for the tests in the next section.

### "Inappropriately normal"

Suppose B's ACTH had been 25 pg per mL, inside a typical reference interval. Would that clear B's pituitary? No. With cortisol at 3 µg per dL, an intact pituitary should be driving hard, as A's is. An ACTH that sits in the normal range while cortisol is low has failed to rise. Endocrinologists call it **inappropriately normal**, and it reads the same as low. A reference interval comes from people whose target hormone is normal, so judge a trophic hormone against its own target, not against the population.

### The thyroid reads the same way

High TSH with low free T4 is the classical primary pattern. Low free T4 with low or inappropriately normal TSH points to central disease. The pituitary is very sensitive to thyroid hormone: a small fall in free T4 brings a much larger proportional rise in TSH. That is why TSH alone is usually the first screen for primary thyroid disease. It is also why a TSH-only screen misses central hypothyroidism; when pituitary disease is suspected, measure free T4 too.

### Excess reads the same way

The rule does not care whether the target hormone is too low or too high.

| | Trophic hormone high | Trophic hormone low |
|---|---|---|
| **Target hormone high** | Same direction: excess drive. Examples: an ACTH-secreting pituitary tumour (Cushing disease); a TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma. | Opposite: the gland is working on its own. Examples: a cortisol-secreting adrenal adenoma; Graves disease, where an antibody stimulates the thyroid's TSH receptor. |
| **Target hormone low** | Opposite: the gland has failed. Examples: primary adrenal insufficiency (A); primary hypothyroidism. | Same direction: missing drive. Example: secondary or tertiary insufficiency (B). |

**Predict.** A patient has high cortisol and an ACTH so low the assay can barely detect it. Is the problem in the pituitary or the adrenal?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The adrenal. Cortisol is high and ACTH is suppressed, so the two move in opposite directions. The pituitary is obeying feedback and shutting down. If no glucocorticoid medicine is involved, the cortisol is coming from the adrenal itself, for example an adenoma.

</details>

## Change one input: stimulation and suppression tests

A dynamic test changes one input and watches the output. **Stimulation tests** ask about reserve: can this gland respond? **Suppression tests** ask about autonomy: does secretion still fall when the signals that normally turn it down are applied?

Take B. B receives a dose of synthetic ACTH (cosyntropin, ACTH 1-24), and cortisol 30 minutes later is 16 µg per dL, or 16 x 27.6 = 441.6 nmol per L.

**Predict.** Suppose B's pituitary stopped making ACTH months ago. What should the adrenal do when given ACTH? What if it stopped last week?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

**Months ago:** without its trophic support the cortex has shrunk, so one dose of ACTH usually gives a subnormal response. That is how the test detects long-standing central insufficiency, even though the adrenal itself is not diseased. It misses some cases, especially partial deficiency, where enough ACTH remains to keep the cortex from shrinking.

**Last week:** the cortex has not had time to shrink, so it can answer normally.

So a normal response shows only that the adrenal can answer ACTH today. It does not exclude a recent pituitary or hypothalamic defect.

</details>

Is 16 µg per dL a normal response? That depends on the assay that made the number. The historical criterion, 18 µg per dL (about 500 nmol per L), was carried over from older, less specific immunoassays. Newer, more specific assays read lower on the same blood, and the criteria derived for them in 2021 are lower, about 14 to 15 µg per dL. Measured on a newer assay, B's 16 passes. Judged against the historical number, it would fail.

So: A is primary. B is central, with an adrenal that, on a newer assay, can still answer ACTH. That fits a loss of drive too recent for the cortex to have shrunk, or a partial one, and the test does not clear B. C shows no failure at rest.

### The other dynamic tests

- **Dexamethasone suppression.** A night-time dose of dexamethasone, a synthetic glucocorticoid, normally lowers ACTH and next-morning cortisol. Failure to suppress suggests secretion escaping feedback, but it does not locate the source. Faster dexamethasone clearance or more binding globulin can confound it.
- **Glucose suppression of GH.** Oral glucose normally suppresses GH. Failure to suppress helps when GH excess is in question.
- **GH stimulation.** Insulin-induced hypoglycemia, glucagon, or macimorelin (a ghrelin-receptor agonist) test whether the GH axis can release GH. Several act through the hypothalamus rather than on somatotrophs directly.

Like cosyntropin, any test that stimulates a gland directly cannot say whether a weak response reflects the gland itself or a long-standing loss of drive from above.

### Test the wiring: a compressed stalk

A non-secreting mass presses on the pituitary stalk, and portal delivery from the hypothalamus falls.

**Predict.** What happens to ACTH, TSH and GH? What happens to prolactin?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Hormones driven by releasing hormones tend to fall (ACTH, TSH, GH, LH and FSH), in combinations that vary from patient to patient, and with them cortisol, free T4, IGF-1 and sex steroids. TSH may be low or inappropriately normal rather than high. Prolactin rises, usually modestly, because the signal it loses is inhibitory dopamine.

</details>

This is why prolactin sits outside the pair rule: it has no target hormone to pair with, and its hypothalamic control is a brake. A drug that blocks D2 receptors, as many antipsychotics do, also raises prolactin, with no lesion at all, and a D2 agonist lowers prolactin whether the cause is a stalk lesion or a prolactinoma. So a drug response does not locate the problem. What points to the stalk is a modest prolactin rise together with low levels of other pituitary hormones.

## Condition 1: catch the right moment

Many pituitary hormones are released in pulses, and several follow a daily rhythm, so when the blood was drawn can matter as much as what is wrong.

**Rhythms.** In young adults the night's large GH surge comes at the onset of deep (slow-wave) sleep. Takahashi, Kipnis and Daughaday (1968) delayed sleep by about three hours in four young adults, and in the three who had a surge it moved with sleep: the surge was tied to sleep onset, not clock time (sleep staging is in [circadian rhythms and sleep](/lessons/sleep-and-rhythms/)). The HPA axis keeps a daily rhythm with pulses on top. In humans under normal conditions on a usual sleep-wake schedule, it peaks between 06:00 and 10:00 and reaches its nadir between 22:00 and 02:00, with ACTH and cortisol pulses on that curve; the rodent pulse experiments are in [emotion and stress](/lessons/emotion-and-stress/). That is why the three mornings were drawn at 08:00.

**Pulses and clearance.** Pulses matter for measurement because GH is cleared fast: a 1962 study measured an average half-life of 27 minutes after large intravenous doses, and later estimates are shorter (Going deeper). Either way the scale is the same: a pulse decays to a quarter or less within an hour, and a sample drawn just before a pulse can read many-fold lower than one drawn on its peak.

**Predict.** A single GH sample, drawn at a random time in an adult, comes back low. Does it show GH deficiency?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The sample may simply have fallen between pulses. A pulse that peaked at 12 ng per mL and then stopped would be down to about 2.6 ng per mL an hour later, at a 27-minute half-life (12 x 2^(-60/27)). A random GH value is weak evidence, and in adults a single random GH or IGF-1 value is not considered enough, in most patients, to establish GH deficiency. That is why the stimulation tests above exist.

</details>

<PulsatileHormoneSampler />

**An integrated readout.** IGF-1 serves as a readout of GH secretion over many hours because it circulates mostly bound to binding proteins and is considered to have a much longer half-life than GH, so it averages many pulses. It also depends on nutrition, liver function and age.

So time the sample to the rhythm, and where a single value swings too much, read an integrated measure or run a dynamic test.

## Condition 2: know what the assay measured

**Two antibody designs.** Competitive radioimmunoassay is explained in [detecting a signal](/lessons/detecting-a-signal/). In a **competitive** assay, labelled and sample hormone compete for a limited antibody, so bound signal falls as hormone rises. In a **two-site (sandwich)** assay, a capture antibody and a signal antibody bind two sites on one molecule, so signal rises with hormone. Sandwich assays suit large peptides such as TSH. Small molecules such as free T4 are usually measured competitively, because a small molecule is too small for two antibodies to bind it at once.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/endocrine-axes-and-testing/competitive-vs-sandwich.webp" width={1200} height={806} alt="AI-generated illustration: Two side-by-side assay schematics. Left, competitive: a few antibodies on a surface are mostly occupied by unlabelled sample hormone, displacing gold-tagged tracer, so more sample means less bound signal. Right, sandwich: a capture antibody and a gold-tagged signal antibody bind two sites on each hormone molecule, so more sample means more signal." caption="In a competitive assay sample hormone displaces labelled tracer, so signal falls as hormone rises; in a sandwich assay two antibodies grip one molecule, so signal rises with hormone, and that opposite wiring is why one interference can push TSH and free T4 in opposite directions." />

**What the antibody sees.** Both designs measure **immunoreactivity**, what an antibody recognizes, not **bioactivity**, what a receptor responds to. Macroprolactin, a large prolactin-IgG complex, is detected by immunoassays but has low biological activity, so a high reported prolactin can mean little at the receptor. Antibodies also cross-react with related molecules, a problem for steroids. **LC-MS/MS** (liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry) separates the hormone from its relatives and identifies it by mass and fragments, so it reports a defined chemical species. Better specificity changes the numbers, so a criterion from an older assay can mislabel a newer result. That is what happened to B's cosyntropin result: measured on a newer specific assay, 16 µg per dL passes that assay's criterion, yet it would be called a failure if read against the historical 18.

**Free and total.** Roughly 99.97 percent of circulating T4 is protein-bound in commonly quoted figures, and published values vary a little with method. **Free** hormone is the unbound fraction, considered the part available to tissues ([binding and dose-response](/lessons/binding-and-dose-response/)). Total T4 moves with binding protein: more thyroxine-binding globulin, as with oral estrogen, raises total T4 while free T4 stays near its usual range and TSH stays normal. (Pregnancy raises the binding globulin too, but adds its own effects: hCG lowers TSH in the first trimester.) Read the pair with total T4 and the rule seems broken; read it with free T4 and it holds. Physical separation by equilibrium dialysis followed by isotope-dilution LC-MS/MS is a reference approach for free thyroid hormones; routine free-hormone immunoassays are method-dependent estimates.

**When the assay fakes a pattern.** Some immunoassays capture the antibody complex with biotin and streptavidin, and a biotin supplement can block that step. On a susceptible platform TSH then reads falsely low and free T4 falsely high: the opposite-direction pattern of an overactive thyroid, in a healthy person. Back to the rule: before reading a pair, ask what the assay measured.

### Design problem

A patient had pituitary surgery 10 days ago. Morning cortisol is low, ACTH is low-normal, and a cosyntropin test is normal. Lay out the reasoning, and say what each result can and cannot show about whether the axis is intact.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Read the pair first. Low cortisol with an ACTH that has not risen is a same-direction pattern: the low-normal ACTH is inappropriately normal, so the drive from above looks inadequate.

Then weigh the cosyntropin result against the time since surgery. Ten days is too short for the adrenal cortex to have shrunk, so a normal response shows only that the adrenal works today. It cannot clear the pituitary.

Check the conditions: a morning sample, no glucocorticoid medicine suppressing ACTH, and a cortisol criterion that matches the assay.

So the low morning pair is the stronger evidence now. A stimulation test repeated weeks later, once the cortex would have had time to shrink if drive is truly missing, can detect the loss, though it can still miss a partial deficiency. Until then, the normal cosyntropin result is not reassurance.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Short and ultra-short loops

Loops are named by how far the signal travels back: **long-loop** from the target-gland hormone, as above; **short-loop** from a pituitary hormone back to the hypothalamus. **Ultra-short** feedback is a hormone acting back on the very cells that make it, classically a hypothalamic releasing hormone acting on its own neurons. The term is sometimes also used for a pituitary hormone acting on its own cells.

| Arrow | Type | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Cortisol on CRH and ACTH | Long | Strong, at both levels |
| T4 and T3 on TRH and TSH | Long | Strong, at both levels |
| IGF-1 on GH | Long | Strong, human infusion |
| Prolactin on dopamine neurons | Short | Causal, knockout mice |
| GH on GHRH neurons | Short | Causal, cell-specific knockout mice |
| TSH or ACTH on hypothalamus | Short | Less established |
| Prolactin on lactotrophs | Ultra-short | Suggested by cell culture, of proliferation not secretion |

**Prolactin.** Prolactin is thought to stimulate the dopamine neurons that inhibit it. Schuff, Low and colleagues (2002) compared mice lacking D2 receptors, mice lacking prolactin receptors and compound mutants, used D2 agonist and antagonist challenges, and cultured pituitary cells. Females lacking either receptor developed hyperprolactinemia and large prolactinomas, but prolactin receptor knockouts had more hyperprolactinemia than age-matched D2 knockouts and had lost much of their dopamine tone. Here is the step that matters. If prolactin restrained lactotrophs only by driving dopamine, losing its receptor could at most remove dopamine's brake, which the D2 knockout already does. Doing worse than the D2 knockout therefore points to a second restraint that does not run through dopamine, though the two knockouts also differ in other ways (prolactin receptor-null females are infertile, with an altered steroid milieu). The culture result supports the direct action: prolactin slowed proliferation of normal female lactotrophs. So the loop runs through the hypothalamus and also acts at the pituitary independently of dopamine, which fits an ultra-short action on lactotroph proliferation, shown in cultured cells. Global, lifelong deletions cannot assign each phenotype to one cell population, and the results are mainly in female mice.

**GH.** Deleting GH receptors only in GHRH-expressing mouse cells raised pulsatile GH secretion and pulse amplitude, yet acute GH or IGF-1 did not change GHRH-neuron firing in recordings (Gusmao and colleagues, 2025): short-loop feedback, since GH is a pituitary hormone acting on hypothalamic neurons, and it need not mean fast electrical inhibition. TSH and ACTH short loops rest on older, thinner evidence, and their role in humans is not settled.

### The IGF-I infusion experiment

The GH long loop was tested in people. Bermann, Barkan and colleagues (1994) gave six normal fed men a saline infusion followed, in the same admission, by a 48-hour infusion of recombinant IGF-I, sampling GH every 10 minutes for 24 hours in each period. Overall GH fell by 85 plus or minus 3 percent and pulse amplitude by 77 plus or minus 4 percent (mean plus or minus SE). The authors favoured increased hypothalamic somatostatin, an inference from hormone responses; nobody sampled portal blood.

### The 1968 sleep study

Takahashi, Kipnis and Daughaday (1968) asked what times the night-time GH rise. They studied eight young adults over 38 nights, drawing repeated blood samples through an indwelling catheter while EEG and eye-movement recordings staged sleep. If a clock or the overnight fall in glucose set the rise, delaying sleep should leave the peak in place. In seven of the eight, a major GH peak appeared at the onset of deep (slow-wave) sleep and lasted 1.5 to 3.5 hours. Besides the delay result above, three subjects woken for two to three hours showed another peak on falling asleep again, and the peak did not correlate with concurrent changes in glucose, insulin or cortisol. That argues against a simple glucose or insulin trigger. It does not show the hypothalamic mechanism (more GHRH or less somatostatin), that the pattern holds at every age, or that glucose never regulates GH.

### How fast GH clears

Parker, Utiger and Daughaday (1962) injected 5 or 10 mg of human GH intravenously into five people and found that plasma levels fell with an average half-life of 27 minutes (individual values 23 to 38). That is one small study's mean after a large dose, not a constant. Later deconvolution of naturally secreted GH in healthy adults gave shorter values, about 15 minutes.

### The biotin study

In a 2020 study, ten healthy adults took a biotin supplement daily for a week, at a lower and later a higher amount, and their thyroid tests were run on three platforms. Two hours after the last dose of the first regimen, one platform reported lower TSH and higher free T4 than baseline, a pattern that looks hyperthyroid, while the tested assays on another showed no significant change.

The standard explanation: excess sample biotin occupies the streptavidin, less antibody complex is captured, and signal falls. In a sandwich assay (TSH) less signal reads as less hormone; in a competitive assay (free T4) it reads as more. Both errors point toward "hyperthyroid".

Ways to separate a real change from an artefact follow from that. Rerun the same sample on a platform without a biotin capture step: a biological change should appear on both, an artefact only on the susceptible one. Repeat after stopping biotin: in this study the interference lasted about one to two days after the higher regimen on the susceptible systems, so a repeat after that should return to baseline if the change was analytical, though this small study does not set a universal washout. Look for physiological agreement: a real hyperthyroid state usually also moves heart rate and symptoms, though older adults may show few, and T3 measured on a platform without a biotin step, because T3 assays on the susceptible platform can be biased too. The study did not test larger doses, impaired kidney function, other analytes or current versions of every assay.

### When the pair rule is silent or fooled

- **During transitions.** The rule describes a settled axis. After a hyperthyroid patient is treated, TSH can stay suppressed for weeks to months while free T4 is already normal or low. The cosyntropin limit above is the same problem.
- **Bioinactive TSH.** The exception to the central thyroid pattern: in hypothalamic hypothyroidism, immunoreactive TSH can be mildly high but poorly bioactive, which mimics the opposite-direction pattern of thyroid failure. The assay reports the antibody's view, not the receptor's.
- **Where, not just which way.** "Same direction" says the drive is at fault, not where. It cannot split pituitary from hypothalamus, or pituitary ACTH from an ectopic ACTH-secreting tumour elsewhere in the body.
- **A raised set point.** If the pituitary becomes less sensitive to feedback, it holds both hormones higher. That reads as "same direction, drive", which correctly places the fault in the regulator rather than the gland. It cannot say whether the regulator is a tumour or a receptor that resists the hormone, as in resistance to thyroid hormone, so hormone-resistance syndromes and hormones taken as medicines need their own reading.
- **The receptor needs a partner.** Strictly, the ACTH receptor (MC2R) also needs an accessory protein, MRAP, to reach the cell surface. So "the gland cannot answer ACTH" includes the receptor machinery, and a fault there reads as primary: high ACTH, low cortisol.
- **A model check.** Suppose gland output is proportional to the trophic hormone, and pituitary output falls as the target hormone to the power 1.6 (an assumed feedback strength; real axes, especially the thyroid, are steeper). A partial loss of drive that lowers the target to 0.82 times normal then leaves the trophic hormone at 0.82 times normal, possibly inside a reference range. An intact pituitary facing the same target level would sit at 0.82^-1.6, about 1.37 times normal. That is "inappropriately normal" in numbers.
