techniques:
  - name: "Mark-release-recapture with manipulated frequencies"
    blurb: >-
      Marked animals are released, often with the composition of the group set by the
      experimenter, and later counted again. A difference in recapture between types is read as a
      difference in survival only if loss means death rather than emigration and both types are equally likely to be found, assumptions the
      design must state and, where possible, test.
  - name: "Phenotype manipulation in the field"
    blurb: >-
      The trait or its frequency is changed (a tail lengthened or shortened, a prey made rare or common) in individuals assigned at random, so
      that it no longer travels with age, health or territory. A change in mating success or
      survival then points to the trait as the cause in that population.
  - name: "Selection gradient and fitness-curve analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Relative fitness, or a stated proxy for it, is regressed on measured traits. Linear terms
      estimate directional selection and quadratic terms estimate curvature; partial regression
      separates correlated traits that were measured (unmeasured ones can still confound it), and a fitted spline shows whether a minimum or maximum actually
      falls inside the sampled range.
  - name: "Paired density manipulation in enclosures"
    blurb: >-
      Enclosures in the same lake or field are stocked at different densities, so that place, season
      and source population are shared and only crowding differs. A difference in the selection
      pattern between paired enclosures can then be attributed to density.

**Natural selection is a difference in reproductive success among variants that already exist, caused by something in their environment: a predator, a parasitoid, a competitor, a choosy mate, a drought.** Change the environment and a different variant can be favoured. Selection sorts within one generation. It becomes evolution only to the extent that the sorted variation is inherited. This lesson builds that argument, then reads it against field experiments on peppered moths, gall flies, sticklebacks, guppies and widowbirds.

## The argument: a named agent sorts inherited variation

Darwin and Wallace made this argument in 1858. It still has the same parts, and a claim that selection is changing a population needs all four.

1. **Variation in a trait.** Individuals differ in beak depth, tail length, colour or gall size.
2. **Inheritance.** Some of that variation passes from parents to offspring.
3. **A measured difference in fitness.** **Fitness** is reproductive success relative to other variants in a given environment. You rarely measure lifetime reproduction directly, so you measure a **fitness component**: survival over a stated period, mating success, or fecundity (number of eggs or young).
4. **A named agent in a stated environment.** A predator, a parasitoid, a competitor, a choosy female, a drought. The same trait can be favoured in one place or year and disfavoured in another.

Think of selection as a sieve. The sieve does not make the grains; it sorts what is poured in. A drought does not create deep beaks. It removes more of the birds whose beaks are shallow, among the variants already present.

Selection also need not keep pointing one way. On Daphne Major in the Galápagos, Peter and Rosemary Grant measured selection on the beaks and bodies of two finch species from 1972 to 2001, and it ranged from consistently one-directional to oscillating ([testing evolutionary explanations](/lessons/testing-evolutionary-explanations/)). There was no single permanently favoured beak.

## Selection is not yet evolution: peppered moths and birds

Keep two steps apart. **Selection** is a difference in fitness among phenotypes within one generation. **Evolutionary response** is the change across generations, and it happens only to the extent that the selected variation is inherited. A trait can be strongly selected and not change at all if its variation is environmental. How large the response is, and how allele frequencies move under a given fitness difference, belong to [population genetics](/lessons/population-genetics/) and [complex traits](/lessons/complex-traits-and-gwas/). Here the job is to show that the selection is real and to name its cause.

The peppered moth, *Biston betularia*, shows all four parts in one case. It has a pale form, typica, and a dark form, carbonaria, and the colour difference is inherited. Birds hunt the moths by sight on tree trunks. Where air pollution killed lichens and soot coated the bark, dark moths matched the bark, and carbonaria became common around British industrial cities in the 19th century. After air pollution fell in the late 20th century and lichens recovered, pale moths matched the trunks better and carbonaria declined. Walton and Stevens (2018) measured the match with models of bird vision: typica is a close match to lichen, while carbonaria is poorly matched to lichen and closer to plain bark in lightness. Same moth, same birds; a different environment favoured a different colour.

Older predation experiments had been criticised for how moths were released, so Michael Majerus repeated the work near Cambridge over six years, releasing live moths and recording which disappeared, which he took to mean eaten by birds (the assumption is examined in Going deeper). Cook, Grant, Saccheri and Mallet (2012) analysed his data. Each day, a dark moth survived at about 0.91 times the rate of a pale one, a selection coefficient of **s = 0.091 per day** against carbonaria. That number is relative and per day, at one site over those years. It measures selection: a difference in one fitness component, survival, with a named agent, birds. Whether the next generation is paler depends on inheritance, and turning a change in morph share into a change in allele frequency needs the dominance and inheritance model in [population genetics](/lessons/population-genetics/). The worked arithmetic and the details of the measurement are in Going deeper.

## Directional and stabilizing: gall flies caught between wasps and birds

Plot relative fitness against a trait and the shape of the curve names the mode. **Directional selection** favours one end of the trait distribution. **Stabilizing selection** favours intermediates over both extremes.

The goldenrod gall fly, *Eurosta solidaginis*, lays an egg in a bud of tall goldenrod, and the plant grows a round gall around the larva. In central Pennsylvania, Abrahamson and colleagues (1989) followed what killed larvae in galls of different sizes. Larvae in small galls were more often attacked by the parasitoid wasp *Eurytoma gigantea*. Large galls were more often opened by birds. Larvae in intermediate galls had the highest survival: stabilizing selection, with two named agents pressing from opposite sides.

The same data also held a directional component favouring larger galls. Parasitoid attack was more frequent than bird attack, so the two did not balance. One trait, in one set of fields, carried both components at once. The modes are descriptions of a fitness curve, not boxes a trait must fit into.

## Disruptive: crowded sticklebacks

**Disruptive selection** favours both extremes over intermediates. Theory predicts it when individuals compete for the commonest resource and those that use something different do better. Bolnick (2004) tested this with threespine sticklebacks, *Gasterosteus aculeatus*, in paired enclosures in each of five lakes, one at low and one at high density. Because he lacked lifetime reproduction, he used body size and relative gonad mass as proxies for potential fecundity. Disruptive selection inferred from those proxies was consistently stronger at high density. Manipulating density made crowding a tested cause, within the limits of the proxies.

Reading the curve takes care. Martin and Pfennig (2012), working on Mexican spadefoot tadpoles, *Spea multiplicata*, found that disruptive selection was the commonest form of curvature across the populations they studied, and strongest where competition within the species was most intense. They also applied an established caution: a significant positive quadratic term alone does not show a fitness minimum among the trait values actually sampled. If the low point lies outside the data, the curve is directional selection that accelerates. Look at the fitted curve, not only the coefficient.

## Frequency-dependent: rare guppies

**Negative frequency-dependent selection** favours a phenotype when it is rare, and so it can keep several variants in a population. To test it you must separate rarity from the phenotype itself, because a colour could do well for reasons that have nothing to do with how common it is. Olendorf and colleagues (2006) did this with male guppies, *Poecilia reticulata*, in three natural populations in Trinidad streams. They set male colour-pattern types at about 3 common to 1 rare, and reversed which morph was rare between replicate pools. After about two weeks, rare males had higher estimated survival, whichever pattern was rare. Recapture stands in for survival only if missing fish died rather than moved. Predators forming a search image for the common type is one possible explanation, not a demonstrated one. Like the other studies here, this shows selection within a generation, not an evolutionary response; the next generation was not measured.

### Design problem

Students propose that birds take rare colour morphs of a prey less often than common ones. Design a field test.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use several comparable pools or plots. Make morph A rare in some replicates and common in others, for example at 1 A to 3 B in half and 3 A to 1 B in the rest, so that each morph is rare somewhere. Keep total density, habitat and season the same across replicates, so that only frequency differs.

Mark every individual, release, and recapture after a set interval, such as two weeks. State the assumption that an individual not recaptured has died rather than emigrated, and reduce emigration with barriers. Detect it by searching neighbouring habitat, and search until you have caught every fish you can, so that the morphs are equally likely to be found.

The prediction is that whichever morph is rare survives better, in both sets of replicates. If one colour does better everywhere, that shows directional selection on colour. It does not rule out frequency dependence as well, so compare each colour's survival when rare with its survival when common. To test a mechanism, watch predators and record whether they take the common morph more than its share, the pattern a search image would produce. Other predator behaviours could produce it too, so this supports a search image without proving one.

Finally, keep the conclusion to its scope. One season of survival in these sites shows frequency-dependent selection on survival. It does not show an evolutionary response; for that you would need evidence that the colour is inherited and data on the next generation.

</details>

## Sexual selection: the widowbird tail

**Sexual selection** is selection through mating success, by competition for mates or by mate choice. Male long-tailed widowbirds, *Euplectes progne*, carry very long breeding tails. Comparing males that happened to differ in tail length could not separate the tail from age or territory quality, so Andersson (1982) changed the tails of males in Kenya. Males given longer tails attracted more females to nest on their territories than males with normal or shortened tails, and males with shortened tails held their territories as long as the others. The tail affected mating, not territory holding, which points to female choice. What the experiment does not show is why females prefer long tails, or the net lifetime fitness of a long-tailed male.

### The moth numbers, worked

The moth estimate s = 0.091 per day means a relative daily survival for carbonaria of 1 - s = 0.909.

> **relative daily survival of carbonaria = 1 - s = 0.909**

**Worked problem.** What does 0.909 per day do to the make-up of a resting population? The starting numbers here are invented for the exercise. Suppose 1,000 typica and 1,000 carbonaria moths, typica daily survival 0.80, and no migration, reproduction or change in rates.

1. *Carbonaria daily survival.* 0.80 x 0.909 = 0.7272.
2. *Expected survivors after one day.* Typica: 1,000 x 0.80 = 800. Carbonaria: 1,000 x 0.7272 = 727.2.
3. *Carbonaria share among survivors.* 727.2 / (800 + 727.2) = 727.2 / 1,527.2 = 0.476, or 47.6%, down from 50%.
4. *Check by cancellation.* The 0.80 and the 1,000s cancel: 0.909 / (1 + 0.909) = 0.909 / 1.909 = 0.476.
5. *Five days.* The ratio compounds: 0.909^5 = 0.621, so the share is 0.621 / (1 + 0.621) = 0.621 / 1.621 = 0.383, about 38%.

> **carbonaria share after t days = 0.909^t / (1 + 0.909^t)   (equal starting numbers)**

Two cautions about this arithmetic. A fractional survivor such as 727.2 is an expectation, not a moth. And the 0.091 is not an annual decline, not an absolute mortality, and not a figure for all of Britain. A change in morph share among survivors is also not a change in allele frequency: carbonaria is dominant, so a dark moth can carry one or two copies.

### How the moth estimate was made

Majerus released 4,864 moths near Coton, Cambridgeshire, over six years. He checked each release four hours after sunrise and presumed that moths which had gone were eaten. Only about 26% of disappearances were witnessed bird attacks; the rest were inferred. The likelihood 95% interval on s was 0.028 to 0.157. The inheritance of colour has a known locus: van't Hof and colleagues (2016) traced carbonaria to a transposable-element insertion in the *cortex* region. Later work in butterflies suggests that non-coding RNAs at the same locus, rather than cortex itself, control melanism there.

### Field-study details

The gall fly study, by Abrahamson, Sattler, McCrea and Weis (1989), covered 20 fields in central Pennsylvania over two years. Two other enemies, a second parasitoid and a beetle, attacked galls of all sizes. The mean directional selection intensity favoured larger galls by 0.21 phenotypic standard deviations, a standardized shift in the trait, not a death rate. Martin and Pfennig's spadefoot data came from 15 ponds in Arizona and New Mexico. In the guppy study, survival was estimated after 15 or 17 days, depending on the site.
