techniques:
  - name: "Mark-release-recapture with manipulated frequencies"
    blurb: >-
      Marked animals are released, often with the composition of the group set by the
      experimenter, and later counted again. A difference in recapture between types is read as a
      difference in survival only if loss means death rather than emigration and both types are equally likely to be found, assumptions the
      design must state and, where possible, test.
  - name: "Phenotype manipulation in the field"
    blurb: >-
      The trait or its frequency is changed (a tail lengthened or shortened, a prey made rare or common) in individuals assigned at random, so
      that it no longer travels with age, health or territory. A change in mating success or
      survival then points to the trait as the cause in that population.
  - name: "Selection gradient and fitness-curve analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Relative fitness, or a stated proxy for it, is regressed on measured traits. Linear terms
      estimate directional selection and quadratic terms estimate curvature; partial regression
      separates correlated traits that were measured (unmeasured ones can still confound it), and a fitted spline shows whether a minimum or maximum actually
      falls inside the sampled range.
  - name: "Paired density manipulation in enclosures"
    blurb: >-
      Enclosures in the same lake or field are stocked at different densities, so that place, season
      and source population are shared and only crowding differs. A difference in the selection
      pattern between paired enclosures can then be attributed to density.

      - "Reproductive success relative to other variants in a given environment"

    rationale: >-
      Fitness is relative reproductive success, and it belongs to an environment: the same variant
      can be favoured in one place or year and disfavoured in another. Strength and speed matter

      but a long-lived individual that does not reproduce has low fitness. Health is likewise only

  - stem: "Dark peppered moths became common around British industrial cities in the 19th century and declined after air pollution fell in the late 20th. Which statement best explains this pattern?"

      - "Dark colour has higher fitness than pale colour in every British wood"
      - "Pollution made individual moths darken their wings to match the bark"
      - "Bird predation favoured whichever colour matched the bark in each place"
      - "Soot raised the rate of new mutations to the dark form near the cities"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "Fitness belongs to an environment, and the agent here is birds hunting by sight. Where pollution killed lichens and soot coated the bark, dark moths were the better match and survived better; once lichens recovered, pale moths matched better and the dark form declined. A form that is favoured everywhere could not rise and then fall, so the first answer contradicts the pattern.   - stem: "Goldenrod gall-fly larvae in small galls were often killed by parasitoid wasps and those in large galls by birds, and larvae in intermediate galls survived best. Which mode of selection does this pattern show most directly?"
    choices:
      - "Stabilizing selection favouring intermediate sizes"
      - "Negative frequency-dependent selection on size"
      - "Disruptive selection favouring both extreme sizes"
      - "Directional selection favouring the smallest galls"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      When intermediate phenotypes survive best and both extremes do worse, the pattern is
      stabilizing selection. Directional selection toward small galls would need small galls to
      survive best, but wasps took them. Disruptive selection would need both extremes to beat
      the intermediates, the reverse of what was seen. Frequency dependence would require survival

  - stem: "A study fits a regression of relative fitness on a trait and finds a significant positive quadratic term. What should be checked before calling the selection disruptive?"
    choices:
      - "Whether the trait is measured on a logarithmic or linear scale"
      - "Whether the linear term is also significant and also positive"
      - "Whether the sample of individuals contained more than one sex"
      - "Whether a fitness minimum lies within the range of trait values"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      A positive quadratic term means the curve bends upward, but the low point can sit outside the
      measured range, in which case the data show accelerating directional selection rather than a
      dip between two favoured extremes. A significant linear term would add a directional

Birds taking more dark moths than pale ones is not yet evolution: the first film ended on the four parts a selection argument needs (variation, inheritance, a fitness difference, and an agent in an environment). This text adds how to measure the fitness difference, what it does to a population, and how to read selection on a trait that comes in a range of sizes.

> **The one idea.** Natural selection is a difference in reproductive success among variants that already exist, caused by an agent in an environment; inheritance carries the sorting forward. You measure selection as a fitness ratio between variants, and the way that ratio changes along a trait names the mode of selection.

## Measuring the fitness difference

**Fitness** is reproductive success relative to other variants in a given environment. **Relative fitness** is one variant's fitness divided by the average, or by a reference variant's. You rarely measure lifetime reproduction directly, so you measure a **fitness component**: survival over a stated period, mating success, or fecundity (number of eggs or young).

**Selection** is a difference in fitness among phenotypes (the observable forms of a trait) within one generation. **Evolutionary response** is the change across generations, and it happens only to the extent that the selected variation is inherited. A trait can be strongly selected and not change at all if its variation is environmental. The size of the response belongs to [population genetics](/lessons/population-genetics/) and [complex traits](/lessons/complex-traits-and-gwas/).

## Peppered moths and birds

The first film introduced the peppered moth, *Biston betularia*, and its pale typica and dark carbonaria morphs. (Intermediate insularia morphs also exist; the experiment below used only typica and carbonaria.) It did not show how differently birds see them: Walton and Stevens (2018) measured the match with models of bird vision: typica is a close match to lichen, while carbonaria is poorly matched to lichen and closer to plain bark in lightness.

### How big is the difference, and what does it do?

Earlier experiments had been criticised for how moths were released, so Michael Majerus repeated the work near Cambridge over six years. He released live moths of both morphs and counted a moth as eaten if it was gone four hours after sunrise; only about a quarter of those disappearances were seen as bird attacks. Cook, Grant, Saccheri and Mallet (2012) analysed his records.

On each day, a dark moth's chance of surviving was about 0.91 times a pale moth's: the first film's dial, now with a number. The **selection coefficient**, *s*, states it as a shortfall from 1: s = 1 − 0.909 = **0.091 per day** against carbonaria. It compares the two morphs; it is not the share that died, not an annual decline and not a figure for all of Britain. It holds for one site, over those years, with birds as the agent.

**Predict.** Start with equal numbers of each morph on lichen-covered trunks. After five days of this selection, are dark moths about 45%, 38% or 5% of the survivors?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

About 38%. Work in **odds**, the number of dark moths per pale moth. Equal numbers give odds of 1 : 1. One day multiplies them by 0.909, to 0.909 : 1. Five days give 0.909^5 = 0.62 : 1. To turn odds into a share, divide by the total: 0.62 / 1.62 = 0.38. Only the ratio mattered, not the number of moths or a pale moth's survival. A common slip is 0.5 × 0.62 = 0.31, which treats the pale moths as if all survived and mixes survivors with starting numbers; the share must be taken among the survivors.

</details>

In general:

> **odds of dark : pale after t days = starting odds × (1 − s)^t**

Each day multiplies the odds by the ratio of the two morphs' fitnesses, w_dark / w_pale = 1 − s. The rule assumes two morphs, a ratio steady over the interval, and no births or migration, and it gives shares among one generation's survivors: still selection, not evolution. Turning the 38% into a change in allele frequency needs the dominance model in [population genetics](/lessons/population-genetics/), because carbonaria is dominant and a dark moth can carry one or two copies.

The moth ratio pointed one way, at one site. Most traits come in a range of sizes, and then the ratio changes along the trait: the shape of the curve of relative fitness against trait size names the mode. The second film builds that curve, in a simple model, for a gall caught between two enemies.

## Gall flies: two modes in one trait

Abrahamson, Sattler, McCrea and Weis (1989) found the second film's pattern in the field. They collected galls of the goldenrod gall fly, *Eurosta solidaginis*, from 20 fields in central Pennsylvania over two years, and recorded what had killed each larva. The film's parasitoid wasp was *Eurytoma gigantea*. Larvae in intermediate galls survived best, **stabilizing selection**. Because parasitoid attack was more frequent than bird attack in those fields and years, the same data held a **directional** component favouring larger galls. Its mean intensity was 0.21 phenotypic standard deviations: the surviving galls' mean sat about a fifth of the usual spread in gall size above the mean of all galls, a shift in the trait, not a death rate.

**Predict.** Two other enemies, a second parasitoid and a beetle, attacked galls of all sizes. Suppose one of them killed the same share of larvae at every gall size. Would it change the shape of the fitness curve?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No, as long as that enemy acts on its own, multiplying survival at every size by the same factor. Relative fitness divides that factor out, just as the pale moths' own survival dropped out of the moth calculation. An enemy changes the curve's shape only if it takes some sizes more than others, or if its effect depends on what the other enemies do.

</details>

## Disruptive: crowded sticklebacks

The second film ended on the dip, **disruptive selection**, predicted when individuals crowd onto the commonest resource. Bolnick (2004) tested that by making crowding the only difference. In each of five lakes he set a pair of enclosures of threespine sticklebacks, *Gasterosteus aculeatus*, one stocked at low density and one at high. The trait was **gill raker length**; gill rakers are bony projections on the gill arches that help sieve food, so their length relates to what a fish eats. Consistently across the lakes, the curve dipped more in the middle at high density. Because only crowding differed within each pair, crowding became a tested cause, within the limits of the fitness measure: body size and relative gonad mass, stand-ins for how many offspring a fish could produce, not lifetime reproduction.

When reading such a curve, check that the dip lies inside the measured data, not past its edge (see Going deeper).

## Frequency-dependent: rare guppies

**Negative frequency-dependent selection** favours a phenotype when it is rare. Because the fitness ratio changes with the share, it can keep several variants in a population. A test must separate rarity from the phenotype. Olendorf and colleagues (2006) did this with male guppies, *Poecilia reticulata*, in three natural populations in Trinidad streams. They set male colour-pattern morphs at about 3 common to 1 rare, and reversed which morph was rare between replicate pools. After about two weeks (15 or 17 days, depending on the site), rare males had higher estimated survival, whichever pattern was rare. Recapture stands in for survival only if missing fish died rather than moved. Predators forming a search image for the common morph is one possible explanation, not a demonstrated one. The next generation was not measured, so this is selection, not an evolutionary response.

### Design problem

Students propose that birds take rare colour morphs of a prey less often than common ones. Design a field test.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use several comparable pools or plots, with morph A rare in some and common in others (for example 1 A to 3 B in half and 3 A to 1 B in the rest), so each morph is rare somewhere. Keep total density, habitat and season the same.

Mark every individual, release, and recapture after a set interval, such as two weeks. State the assumption that an individual not recaptured has died rather than emigrated. Reduce emigration with barriers, and detect it by searching neighbouring habitat. Search each pool until you have caught every individual you can, so both morphs are equally likely to be found.

The prediction is that whichever morph is rare survives better, in both sets of replicates. One colour doing better everywhere shows directional selection on colour but does not rule out frequency dependence too, so compare each colour's survival when rare and when common. To test a mechanism, record whether predators take the common morph more than its share. A search image would produce that pattern, but so could other predator behaviours, so it supports a search image without proving one.

Keep the conclusion to its scope: one season of survival in these sites shows frequency-dependent selection, not an evolutionary response, which would need evidence that colour is inherited and data on the next generation.

</details>

## Sexual selection: the widowbird tail

**Sexual selection** is selection through mating success, by competition for mates or by mate choice. Mating success is the fitness component here: a male with more females nesting on his territory can father more young. Male long-tailed widowbirds, *Euplectes progne*, carry very long breeding tails. Males that happen to differ in tail length may also differ in age or territory quality, so Andersson (1982) changed the tails of males in Kenya. Males given longer tails attracted more females to nest on their territories than males with normal or shortened tails.

**Predict.** Suppose the long-tailed males had also held their territories longer. What could you no longer conclude?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

That the tail acted through female choice. A territory held longer could gather more nests by itself, tangling the two effects.

</details>

In fact, males with shortened tails held their territories as long as the others, so the tail affected mating, not territory holding, which points to female choice. The experiment does not show why females prefer long tails, or a long-tailed male's net lifetime fitness.

## Going deeper

### The moth numbers, worked

**Worked problem.** With invented numbers (1,000 moths of each morph, typica daily survival 0.80, no migration, reproduction or change in rates), carbonaria survives at 0.80 x 0.909 = 0.7272. One day leaves 800 typica and 727.2 carbonaria, so the dark share is 727.2 / 1,527.2 = 47.6%, down from 50%. The 0.80 and the 1,000s cancel: 0.909 / 1.909 gives the same 0.476.

> **carbonaria share after t days = 0.909^t / (1 + 0.909^t)   (equal starting numbers)**

The odds form works from any start: from 10% dark (odds 1 : 9), five days give 0.62 : 9, about 6.5% dark. A fractional survivor such as 727.2 is an expectation, not a moth.

### How the moth estimate was made

Majerus released 4,864 moths near Coton, Cambridgeshire, over six years. Moths gone at the four-hour check were presumed eaten, although a missing moth may have flown away or been taken by a predator other than a bird; only about 26% of disappearances were witnessed bird attacks, and the rest were inferred. The likelihood 95% interval on s was 0.028 to 0.157; at its ends, five days from equal numbers leave about 46% or 30% dark, not 38%. The inheritance of colour has a known locus: van't Hof and colleagues (2016) traced carbonaria to a transposable-element insertion in the *cortex* region. Later work in butterflies suggests that non-coding RNAs at the same locus, rather than cortex itself, control melanism there.

### Reading a fitness curve

Studies usually fit relative fitness to a trait by regression. A significant positive quadratic term means the curve bends upward, but alone it does not show a fitness minimum among the trait values sampled. Picture a curve still falling, or just flattening, when the data run out: its low point lies outside the data, which show accelerating directional selection, not a dip. Martin and Pfennig (2012), working on Mexican spadefoot tadpoles, *Spea multiplicata*, from 15 ponds in Arizona and New Mexico, found that disruptive selection was the commonest form of curvature across the populations they studied, and strongest where competition within the species was most intense. They applied the established caution: look at the fitted curve, not only the coefficient.

### Selection over time

The fitness ratio need not keep pointing one way. On Daphne Major in the Galápagos, Peter and Rosemary Grant measured selection on the beaks and bodies of two finch species from 1972 to 2001, and it ranged from consistently one-directional to oscillating ([testing evolutionary explanations](/lessons/testing-evolutionary-explanations/)).
