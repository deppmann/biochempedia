      A multi-electrode catheter in the left ventricle reads blood conductance, converted to volume
      after subtracting parallel conductance (found with a hypertonic saline bolus) and scaling to an
      independent volume or flow measurement; a pressure sensor gives the other axis. The result is
      a loop for every beat.
  - name: "Transient vena caval occlusion"
    blurb: >-
      A balloon briefly narrows the inferior vena cava, so filling falls over a few beats and the

      corners the EDPVR; only the first beats are used, before reflexes change contractility.

**Plot the pressure inside your left ventricle against its volume, and each heartbeat draws a closed loop: four corners, four valve events, and an enclosed area equal to the work of the beat.**

Filling, arterial load and contraction strength each move a different part of the loop, predictably, and the predictions show why ejection fraction is a poor measure of contractility. The four phases (filling, contraction with both valves shut, ejection, relaxation with both valves shut) and the stroke-volume arithmetic are in [circulation and the heart](/lessons/circulation-and-heart/); here they get numbers.

When the arteries tighten, which corner of the loop moves, and which way? Hold your guess; once the lesson has built two lines, you can work it out.

The film above uses this lesson's model ventricle. Its 120 mmHg at aortic closure is the model's rounded end-systolic pressure, explained under the valve table.

## One beat, four valve events

A **Wiggers diagram** plots left ventricular (LV), left atrial (LA) and aortic pressure, LV volume, the heart sounds and the electrocardiogram (ECG) on one time axis, with the valve events marked.

You need one rule to read it. A valve opens when the pressure behind it exceeds the pressure ahead, and shuts when that reverses. For the adult human left heart at rest, in round textbook values, the four valve events are:

| Valve event | Pressure crossing | Sound | Phase that follows |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mitral closes | LV rises above LA (about 10 mmHg) | S1 | Isovolumic (constant-volume) contraction |
| Aortic opens | LV rises above aortic (about 80 mmHg) | none | Ejection |
| Aortic closes | LV falls below aortic (about 100 to 110 mmHg) | S2 | Isovolumic relaxation |
| Mitral opens | LV falls below LA (low, the atrial pressure at that moment) | none | Filling |

The 80 mmHg is the aorta's diastolic pressure, not the ventricle's. During ejection, ventricular and aortic pressure rise together to a peak of about 120 mmHg, and then fall a little before the aortic valve shuts at about 100 to 110 mmHg. The worked model in this lesson flattens that hump into a straight climb and rounds end-systolic pressure up to 120 mmHg, so the arithmetic stays simple. (Echo-Doppler studies estimate end-systolic pressure as about 0.9 times systolic pressure.)

Diastole runs from aortic closure to mitral closure. It has three main parts: isovolumic relaxation, early filling and atrial contraction. The ECG's P wave comes just before atrial contraction, the QRS complex just before mitral closure, and the T wave around the end of ejection ([cardiac electrophysiology and the ECG](/lessons/cardiac-electrophysiology-ecg/)).

## Fold time away and the beat becomes a loop

Now drop the time axis. Plot LV pressure up the vertical axis against LV volume along the bottom, and one beat traces a closed loop, run counterclockwise, with one valve event at each corner.

**Predict.** Two sides of the loop are vertical lines. Which two, and why must they be vertical?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The right and left sides. On each, both valves are shut, so no blood can enter or leave and the volume cannot change while the pressure rises or falls. These are the two isovolumic phases.

</details>

With the valve table as the key: bottom right is mitral closure at end-diastolic volume (EDV); the right side is isovolumic contraction; top right is aortic opening; the top is ejection. Top left is aortic closure at end-systolic volume (ESV) and pressure (Pes); the left side is isovolumic relaxation; the bottom, after mitral opening, is filling.

Four short formulas read the loop, each answering one question:

- *How much does one beat eject?* Stroke volume, **SV = EDV - ESV**: the loop's width.
- *What fraction of the filled ventricle is that?* Ejection fraction, **EF = SV / EDV**.
- *How much flows per minute?* Cardiac output, **CO = SV x HR**, with HR the heart rate in beats per min.
- *How much work does one beat do?* **Stroke work**, **SW ≈ SV x mean ejection pressure**: the loop's area, approximated as a rectangle.

EDV 125 mL and ESV 45 mL is a common teaching example, not a reference value. Biplane echocardiography in healthy adults gave mean EDV of 106 mL in men and 76 mL in women, mean ESV of 41 and 28 mL, and mean EF of 62 percent (2-SD range 52 to 72) in men and 64 percent (54 to 74) in women.

### Worked problem, part 1: read one loop

In this made-up example, a model adult left ventricle at rest has EDV 125 mL, ESV 45 mL, Pes 120 mmHg and heart rate 70 per min, at steady state with competent valves. The aortic valve opens at 80 mmHg; take mean ejection pressure as the average of opening and end-systolic pressure.

1. *Stroke volume.* 125 - 45 = 80 mL.
2. *Ejection fraction.* 80 / 125 = 64 percent.
3. *Cardiac output.* 80 mL x 70 per min = 5.6 L per min.
4. *Stroke work.* Mean ejection pressure (80 + 120) / 2 = 100 mmHg; 80 mL x 100 mmHg = 8,000 mmHg mL. To turn that into joules: 1 mmHg = 133.3 Pa and 1 mL = 10^-6 m^3 (a millionth of a cubic metre), and a pascal times a cubic metre is a joule, so 1 mmHg mL = 133.3 x 10^-6 J = 1.333 x 10^-4 J. Then 8,000 x 1.333 x 10^-4 J = 1.07 J per beat ([powers of ten](/math-toolkit/#powers)).
5. *Power.* 1.07 J x 70 / 60 beats per s = 1.24 W, the external work rate of this one ventricle.

Why did this beat stop at 45 mL and 120 mmHg? The rest of the lesson answers that question.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Every beat ends where two lines cross. One line belongs to the ventricle: the end-systolic pressure-volume relation, the most pressure the contracted ventricle can hold at each volume. It rises to the right from a volume intercept, V0, with slope Ees, which indexes **contractility** (how hard the muscle contracts at a given stretch).
>
> The other line belongs to the arteries. It starts at EDV on the volume axis and climbs to the left with steepness Ea, which measures **afterload**, and it gives the end-systolic pressure the arteries return for each millilitre ejected.
>
> Preload moves where the arterial line starts, afterload tilts it, and contractility tilts the ventricle's line. The crossing fixes end-systolic volume and pressure, so SV = (EDV − V0) / (1 + Ea/Ees). Ejection fraction then comes out as (1 − V0/EDV) / (1 + Ea/Ees): it reads the ratio of the two slopes, not contractility.

The rule covers steady ejecting beats of a left ventricle with competent valves, at a fixed heart rate, where the ventricle's line is close to straight, before reflexes or remodelling change Ees. EDV is an input: it comes from the filling side, which the rule does not explain. When the ventricle has a second outlet (mitral regurgitation) or a narrowed valve (aortic stenosis), an Ea calculated from LV pressure includes the leak or the valve. The rule still locates the corner, but Ea no longer describes the arteries alone.

## The ventricle's line: a stiff line at end-systole, a soft curve at end-diastole

Start with a picture. A balloon briefly narrows the inferior vena cava (a transient **inferior vena caval occlusion**), so over a few beats the ventricle fills less and the loops shrink and move left. Their top-left corners fall on one nearly straight line rising to the right; their bottom-right corners fall on a curve along the floor. Suga, Sagawa and colleagues reported in canine hearts that the end-systolic points of differently loaded beats fall on one near-linear line, relatively insensitive to loading.

Tracing the lines in a living heart needs volume beat by beat, which the **conductance catheter** supplies. Tissue around the cavity adds a **parallel conductance** to its reading, a roughly constant extra volume, found by briefly changing blood conductivity with a bolus.

The straight line is the **end-systolic pressure-volume relation (ESPVR)**. It says how much pressure the fully contracted ventricle holds at each volume. Its slope, Ees, is an **elastance**, a stiffness (pressure change per volume change), and it tracks contractility:

Ees rises with positive inotropes (drugs that strengthen contraction) and sympathetic activation and falls with ischaemia and infarction. It is a chamber-level index, though, so wall geometry and passive stiffness affect it too: in a stiff, thick-walled chamber Ees can rise without stronger muscle.

The curve along the floor is the **end-diastolic pressure-volume relation (EDPVR)**, the passive filling curve. Once relaxation is complete, the bottom of the loop lies on it. It is non-linear; its slope indexes chamber stiffness, and the inverse is **compliance**, volume change per unit pressure. How fast the ventricle relaxes is summarized by **tau**, the time constant of the isovolumic pressure fall.

The ventricle's line says what the ventricle *can* do. It does not say where on that line a beat will stop.

## The arteries draw the second line

Where a beat stops depends on what the ventricle pumps into. Look at the arteries over many steady beats. Whatever the ventricle ejects each beat must run off through the arterioles before the next beat, and the pressure that drives that runoff rises with the amount pumped. So, at a fixed heart rate and resistance, the more each beat ejects, the higher the arterial pressure, and with it the pressure at the end of ejection, roughly in proportion:

> **Pes ≈ Ea x SV**

**Ea**, the **effective arterial elastance**, is that proportion: the end-systolic pressure per millilitre ejected, once beats are steady, for this arterial tree at this heart rate. It is a single number standing for the whole load, the lesson's measure of **afterload**, the load the ventricle ejects against. It rises mostly with arteriolar resistance ([resistance, compliance and local flow](/lessons/resistance-compliance-local-flow/)), and also with heart rate, because a faster heart leaves less time for blood to run off between beats (a common approximation is Ea ≈ R / T, total peripheral resistance over the duration of one beat). In the model, Ea is 1.5 mmHg per mL: eject 80 mL each beat and the arteries return an end-systolic pressure of 1.5 x 80 = 120 mmHg.

Now put the arteries on the loop. Ejection starts at end-diastolic volume. That is the **preload**, the end-diastolic stretch, read at the loop's bottom-right corner. Every millilitre ejected moves the beat one millilitre left, so measure stroke volume leftward from EDV.

- Eject nothing, beat after beat: the arteries drain toward zero pressure, so the line starts at EDV, 125 mL, on the volume axis.
- Eject 40 mL each beat: 85 mL, at 1.5 x 40 = 60 mmHg.
- Eject 60 mL each beat: 65 mL, at 1.5 x 60 = 90 mmHg.

Join the points: a straight line that starts at EDV on the volume axis and climbs to the left with steepness Ea. Each point is a steady state, not one beat; a single beat that ejected nothing would leave aortic pressure near its diastolic 80 mmHg. Every point on it is an ending the arteries would accept, a small ejection at low pressure or a large one at high pressure.

**Predict.** The ventricle's line climbs to the right from V0. The arteries' line climbs to the left from EDV. How many beats can satisfy both?

</details>

## Ejection fraction is not contractility

Divide stroke volume by EDV:

> **EF = (1 − V0/EDV) / (1 + Ea/Ees) ≈ 1 / (1 + Ea/Ees)**

The short form holds only when V0 is small next to EDV: in the model it gives 66.7 percent against the exact 64 percent. In a dilated heart with V0 = 40 mL, Ees 1 and Ea 2 mmHg per mL, it gives 33 percent, but the exact form gives 27 to 28 percent at end-diastolic volumes of 200 to 250 mL.

At fixed Ea and Ees, preload barely touches EF: in the model, at Ea/Ees = 0.5, EF goes 63.3, 64.0, 64.3 and 64.8 percent as EDV rises from 100 to 125, 140 and 180 mL. Afterload and contractility move it: raising Ea lowers EF, and raising Ees raises it.

Neither. Both have Ea/Ees = 0.5, so both eject 80 mL and have an EF of 64 percent, though heart A ends systole at 120 mmHg and heart B at 60 mmHg. The loops are equally wide; heart B's is half as tall. EF saw the ratio, not the muscle.

Now the opening question. When the arteries tighten, Ea rises and the arteries' line tilts steeper. It meets the unchanged ventricle's line higher up, so **the top-left corner moves up and to the right** (the top-right corner rises too, as the aortic valve now opens at a higher diastolic pressure): higher end-systolic pressure, larger end-systolic volume, smaller stroke volume and a lower EF, with no change in the muscle.

## Loops in disease: which line has moved?

In **heart failure with reduced EF**, remodelling shifts the ESPVR, the EDPVR and the loops to the right, and the ESPVR is usually flatter (lower Ees): a large chamber ejecting a small fraction of its volume.

In **heart failure with preserved EF**, the floor moves. Catheter studies found the diastolic relation shifted up and to the left, with higher passive stiffness and a longer tau. Ees was raised too in a small catheter study, and Ea and Ees tend to rise together. Here Ees meets its limit: in an echo-Doppler community sample, chamber and myocardial contractility were depressed in this group although Ees was raised.

**The modern experiment.** Is the problem with a normal EF a stiff chamber? Westermann and colleagues (Circulation, 2008) recorded conductance-catheter loops in 70 people with heart failure and normal EF and 20 without heart-failure symptoms, at baseline, during isometric handgrip (raising afterload) and during atrial pacing at 120 beats per min. Diastolic stiffness was raised at baseline; with pacing, stroke volume fell and the loops shifted left; with handgrip, end-diastolic pressures rose more. Ruled in: a stiff chamber, with filling pressure rising more during handgrip and stroke volume falling during pacing. Not shown: cause, that lowering stiffness would help (the authors' hypothesis), or that systolic and vascular changes found in other groups are absent.

In **aortic stenosis** the valve adds to afterload: LV systolic pressure rises and the loop's top domes upward. Ea taken from LV pressure includes the valve, and Ea/Ees rises. EDV stays near normal. ESV is often larger, but in compensated stenosis a thickened wall can keep ESV and EF near normal until the ventricle starts to fail. The same thickening often stiffens the chamber, so the EDPVR can shift up and end-diastolic pressure rises, as in the stiff chambers above. In **mitral stenosis** preload falls and pulmonary venous pressure rises, while the ESPVR is usually preserved (relaxation can be slowed too).

**Pathophysiology: chronic mitral regurgitation.** A leaking mitral valve gives the systolic ventricle two outlets, the aorta and the low-pressure left atrium, so Pes / SV includes the leak.

**Predict.** The ventricle's muscle has not changed, but it can now eject into the low-pressure atrium as well as the aorta. What happens to EF, and does that mean the muscle is stronger?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

EF stays normal or rises, and the muscle is no stronger. The low-pressure outlet helps the ventricle empty, so ESV stays normal or small early on. Over time the chamber dilates and the EDPVR and loops shift right; by the Frank-Starling mechanism (a fuller ventricle ejects more) the large EDV keeps total stroke volume high. So EF can stay normal or supernormal while the myocardium is damaged and forward output is normal or reduced.

</details>

The end-systolic corners still lie on the ESPVR, so Ees still tracks the muscle better than EF does, within its usual geometry and loading limits.

## What a beat costs

The loop also says what a beat costs in oxygen. The **pressure-volume area (PVA)** is stroke work plus the end-systolic **potential energy**: elastic energy held in the contracted wall at end-systole, which the beat pays for but never delivers to the blood. On the plot it is the roughly triangular region between the ESPVR, the EDPVR and the loop's left side.

In excised, cross-circulated dog hearts, Khalafbeigui, Suga and Sagawa (1979) found that oxygen use per beat rose linearly with PVA. The fit is a straight line, oxygen cost = slope x PVA + intercept, where MVO2 is myocardial oxygen consumption per beat:

> **MVO2 (mL O2 per beat) ≈ 1.5 x 10^-5 x PVA (mmHg mL per beat) + 0.019**

Read the slope as a plain number: 1.5 x 10^-5 is 0.000015, so every 10,000 mmHg mL of PVA costs about 0.15 mL O2, on top of a fixed 0.019 mL O2 per beat. The fit comes from dog hearts at one inotropic state, so use it to see the form, not to predict a human heart.

So an isovolumic beat, which does no external work, still costs oxygen in proportion to its PVA; stroke work alone is not the cost.

Coupling decides how much of that oxygen becomes external work. In the linear model, stroke work (taken as Pes x SV) is greatest when Ea equals Ees. Efficiency, work per unit of oxygen, peaks at a lower ratio than that; exactly where depends on how much oxygen a beat costs regardless of load, and the peak is broad. The resting ratio of about 0.6 lies in the broad high-efficiency range measured in dog hearts, so a modest change in load costs little.

### Design problem

An anaesthetized dog (or pig) has a left-ventricular conductance catheter and a balloon catheter in the inferior vena cava. A drug is said to be a positive inotrope; it also dilates arterioles. A student reports that EF rose from 55 to 65 percent and concludes that the drug raises contractility. Design the experiment that tests the claim.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Afterload reduction alone raises EF (part 2 run backwards), so EF cannot separate the two effects.

1. *Fix the rate* by atrial pacing, since Ea and Ees both depend on heart rate.
2. *Calibrate volume:* parallel conductance by a hypertonic saline bolus, scale by an independent volume or flow measurement.
3. *Record baseline* steady loops, then brief caval occlusions, and fit Ees and V0 to the end-systolic corners. Use only the first few beats, before reflexes act, or block the reflexes.
4. *Give vehicle, then drug,* repeating loops and occlusions after each.
5. *Separate the effects:* Ea = Pes / SV for afterload, Ees and V0 for contractility. A shift of V0 alone can mimic a change in slope if V0 is fixed or a single beat is used, so fit both.

If the end-systolic points before and after the drug lie on one ESPVR while Ea falls, the EF rise reflects loading, not contractility (afterload, plus any change in EDV). A steeper ESPVR after drug than after vehicle would support the claim.

Limits: anaesthesia, open or closed chest, one species, and an Ees that also responds to geometry and passive stiffness.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The Wiggers diagram in more detail

The Wiggers diagram rests on descriptions Wiggers published from 1915 and has been taught for more than 90 years. Some texts add a slow-filling phase, diastasis, between early filling and atrial contraction. In one illustrative subject, the ventricle held about 80 percent of its end-diastolic volume by the end of early filling, and atrial contraction added the last 20 percent. S3 falls in rapid early filling; S4, when present, with atrial contraction.

### End-systole and aortic closure

This lesson puts aortic closure and end-systole at one corner. Strictly, end-systole is the moment of greatest elastance, the highest ratio of pressure to (volume − V0), which usually comes slightly before the aortic valve shuts.

### How the lines were measured

The canine findings came from Suga, Sagawa and Shoukas (1973), on the instantaneous pressure-volume ratio, and from Sagawa and colleagues (1977), on the end-systolic ratio in isolated ventricles. The eight-electrode conductance catheter had been used for stroke volume in dogs by Baan and colleagues, who in 1984 calibrated it for absolute volume in dogs and patients. The parallel-conductance bolus is hypertonic saline in modern practice; Baan used cold glucose solution in patients.

### The classic experiment: what a beat costs in oxygen

What sets the oxygen cost of a beat? Khalafbeigui, Suga and Sagawa (American Journal of Physiology, 1979) used 13 excised dog hearts, each cross-circulated by a support dog that supplied the coronary blood. A volume servo pump set the load, beats were isovolumic or ejecting, and oxygen consumption per beat was measured. Oxygen use per beat correlated linearly with PVA (mean r = 0.92).

The intercept is oxygen use that does not depend on PVA. It includes basal metabolism and the cost of exciting and activating the muscle: in later canine experiments, epinephrine or calcium raised it by more than 50 percent while basal metabolism stayed unchanged. Ruled in: the PVA cost of an isovolumic beat. Ruled out: stroke work alone as the cost. Not shown: that the line holds when contractility changes (the authors noted a new inotropic state shifts it), or anything in intact humans.

*Worked problem, part 3: the oxygen cost of the part 1 beat.* For the part 1 loop with V0 = 5 mL, treating potential energy as a triangle, 0.5 x (45 - 5) mL x 120 mmHg = 2,400 mmHg mL, so PVA = 8,000 + 2,400 = 10,400 mmHg mL. The dog fit, used only to show its form, gives 1.5 x 10^-5 x 10,400 + 0.019 = 0.175 mL O2 per beat; that is not a human prediction.

### Where the efficiency peak falls

Published optima differ. A review places mechanical efficiency at its best near Ea/Ees = 0.5 and stroke work at its maximum near 1, but in isolated blood-perfused dog hearts the measured peaks were nearer 0.7 and 0.8, and both stayed within 10 percent of their best from about 0.3 to 1.3.

The lesson's own pieces show why. Take stroke work as Pes x SV (the rectangle up to end-systolic pressure, a little larger than the part 1 estimate), add the potential-energy triangle for PVA, and cost it with the dog fit. Without the intercept, work per PVA only falls as Ea/Ees rises; the intercept, oxygen spent regardless of load, creates the peak. Applied unchanged to this lesson's human-sized model, the dog fit puts the peak near 0.24. A heavier human ventricle has a larger fixed cost, though, and that moves the peak up (about 0.3 to 0.4 if it is 1.5 to 3 times larger); for smaller, dog-sized ventricles the peak sits near the ratios measured in dog hearts. The number shows only the direction: the optimum depends on a beat's fixed oxygen cost relative to its PVA, and is not a constant.

### Heart failure with preserved EF: the studies

Tau is about 20 to 40 ms in invasive human recordings and about 60 to 100 ms when relaxation is impaired, depending on how it is fitted. The longer tau in this group was 59 versus 35 ms in Zile, Baicu and Gaasch's 2004 comparison. In a small 2003 catheter study of 10 patients, Ees was 4.7 mmHg per mL, against 2.1 in normotensive and 3.3 in matched hypertensive controls. The community sample with depressed chamber and myocardial contractility despite a raised Ees was Borlaug and colleagues' echo-Doppler study (2009).

### Chronic mitral regurgitation: the loop in detail

Ejection into the atrium starts before the aortic valve opens, so isovolumic contraction shortens and the loop's right side leans left. ESV stays normal or small early on, but grows later as the chamber dilates and wall stress rises. Left atrial pressure rises, with a large v wave (the atrial pressure rise during ventricular systole) when the atrium is stiff or the leak is sudden, while a large, compliant atrium can keep it small.
