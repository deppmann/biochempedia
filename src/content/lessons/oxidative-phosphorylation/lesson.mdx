Electrons fall from NADH down a five-story redox ladder to oxygen, and the three big drops pump protons; ATP synthase then spends that gradient to mint most of the ~30-32 ATP per glucose. Get the gradient as the currency and the famous misconceptions dissolve: why FADH2 yields less ATP, why uncouplers make heat instead of ATP, and why cyanide kills in minutes."

    story: "Ill health forced Mitchell to resign from Edinburgh in 1963. techniques:
  - name: "Difference spectroscopy of cytochromes"
    blurb: "Keilin's microspectroscope read respiration in real time: oxidized and reduced cytochromes absorb light at different wavelengths, so watching absorption bands appear and vanish as oxygen is added or consumed tells you which carrier is reduced and which is oxidized. Add an inhibitor and the chain freezes in a 'crossover' pattern (reduced upstream, oxidized downstream), and that pattern is one of the main ways the order of the carriers was deduced before any structure existed."
  - name: "Inhibitor crossover analysis"
    blurb: "Specific blockers cut the chain at known points: rotenone at Complex I, antimycin A at the Complex III Qi site, cyanide and CO at Complex IV. Apply one, and every carrier upstream of the block piles up reduced while everything downstream goes oxidized. Mapping that boundary for each inhibitor pins down where each component sits in the sequence and is the classic logic for ordering an electron-transfer pathway."
  - name: "Submitochondrial particles and the pH-electrode pulse"
    blurb: "Tear mitochondria apart by sonication and the inner membrane reseals into closed, inside-out vesicles that still pump and still make ATP, proving OxPhos lives on the membrane while the matrix runs the soluble reactions. Mitchell and Moyle put a fast glass pH electrode in a mitochondrial suspension, pulsed in oxygen, and watched the medium acidify, measuring the protons ejected per oxygen atom consumed (and, in separate ATP-hydrolysis pulses, per ATP). That turned chemiosmosis from hypothesis into numbers; the early ratios have since been revised upward."
  - name: "Cryo-EM of supercomplexes"
    blurb: "Single-particle cryo-electron microscopy resolved respiratory supercomplexes, including the 'respirasome' (Complex I + III2 + IV), which may shorten the diffusion path for CoQ and cytochrome c and reduce electron leak. It also solved mammalian Complex I, the L-shaped 45-subunit machine, at near-atomic resolution. The first structures of the bc1 complex and cytochrome c oxidase came from X-ray crystallography in the 1990s."

  - stem: "In Mitchell's chemiosmotic model, where is the energy from electron transport stored before it is used to make ATP?"
    choices:
      - "In a high-energy phosphorylated chemical intermediate that hands its bond directly to ADP"
      - "In the reduced cytochrome c pool waiting at Complex IV"
      - "As heat released by the inner membrane that ATP synthase later recaptures"
      - "As a proton gradient (proton-motive force) across the inner mitochondrial membrane"
    answer: 3
    rationale: "Chemiosmotic coupling stores the energy as an electrochemical proton gradient across the impermeable inner membrane; ATP synthase then discharges that gradient to make ATP. The high-energy chemical intermediate was the rejected chemical-coupling hypothesis (the intermediate was never found). Reduced cytochrome c carries electrons, not the stored coupling energy, and the gradient is not stored as heat (heat is what an uncoupler wastes it as)."
  - stem: "Why does FADH2 yield approximately 1.5 ATP while NADH yields approximately 2.5 ATP?"
    choices:
      - "FADH2 carries only one electron, so it can reduce only half as much oxygen"
      - "FADH2 has a more negative reduction potential than NADH, releasing less energy"
      - "ATP synthase cannot use protons pumped by FADH2-derived electrons"
      - "FADH2 enters at Complex II, which pumps no protons, so it drives fewer pumps than NADH"
    answer: 3
    rationale: "FADH2 feeds electrons in at Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase), which lacks proton-pumping machinery, so its electrons skip the first pump and bank only ~6 H+ versus ~10 for NADH. Both carriers deliver two electrons. FADH2 actually sits at a more positive potential than NADH, and ATP synthase uses all pumped protons equally regardless of their source."
  - stem: "A researcher adds antimycin A to actively respiring mitochondria and monitors the redox state of the carriers by spectroscopy. What pattern is expected?"
    choices:

      - "Only cytochrome c oxidase changes state; the rest are unaffected"
      - "Carriers upstream of Complex III become reduced and those downstream become oxidized"
      - "All carriers oscillate rapidly between oxidized and reduced states"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "Antimycin A blocks Complex III. Electrons entering from NADH/FADH2 still flow in, so the carriers upstream of the block (Complexes I and II, CoQ) pile up reduced, while everything downstream (cyt c, Complex IV) loses its electron supply and goes oxidized. This crossover pattern is exactly the inhibitor logic used to establish the order of the chain."
  - stem: "Complex IV holds O2 at its heme a3/CuB binuclear center until it is fully reduced to water. What is the primary functional consequence of this 'vise-grip' mechanism?"
    choices:
      - "It prevents the release of partially reduced, ROS-generating oxygen intermediates"
      - "It allows Complex IV to pump more protons than any other complex"
      - "It lets O2 be reduced without consuming any protons from the matrix"
      - "It enables Complex IV to accept two electrons from cytochrome c at once"
    answer: 0
    rationale: "By holding O2 and its partly reduced forms until O2 is fully reduced to water, the binuclear center avoids releasing peroxide or superoxide (partially reduced O2), which is why Complex IV almost never leaks ROS. It does not pump the most protons (per electron pair it pumps 2 and uses 2 more to make water), it consumes matrix protons to make water, and cytochrome c delivers electrons strictly one at a time."
  - stem: "An uncoupler such as 2,4-dinitrophenol makes the inner mitochondrial membrane leaky to protons. What is the immediate effect on electron transport and ATP synthesis?"
    choices:
      - "Electron transport stops and ATP synthesis continues from stored gradient"
      - "Both electron transport and ATP synthesis stop simultaneously"
      - "Electron transport speeds up while ATP synthesis falls and heat rises"
      - "ATP synthase reverses and begins pumping protons out of the matrix"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "An uncoupler dissipates the proton gradient, so protons return without passing through ATP synthase and ATP output collapses while the energy is released as heat. Because the gradient no longer builds up to oppose pumping, electron transport actually accelerates (respiration increases). This is how brown fat generates warmth, and how DNP was dangerously used for weight loss."

  - stem: "A patient is exposed to cyanide. Given that cyanide binds the heme a3/CuB center of Complex IV, which downstream consequence best explains death within minutes?"
    choices:
      - "Pyruvate can no longer enter the citric acid cycle, halting acetyl-CoA production"

    answer: 1
    rationale: "Blocking the terminal oxidase prevents electrons from reaching O2, so every upstream carrier saturates reduced, no further protons are pumped, the proton-motive force dissipates, and ATP synthase loses its driving gradient. Cells dependent on oxidative ATP fail almost immediately. Cyanide does not directly block pyruvate entry or glycolysis, and the lethal effect is loss of the gradient, not reversal of ATP synthase as the primary event. In intact cells ATP synthase can run backward and hold some gradient by spending glycolytic ATP, but the loss of oxidative ATP synthesis is the lethal event."

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm 5"
    text: "Imagine tiny balls rolling down a five-story slide inside your cells. Every time a ball drops past certain spots, it pushes a little pump that squirts water up to a high tank. When lots of water is up in the tank, it pours back down and spins a waterwheel, and the spinning wheel makes the cell's energy money called ATP. The slide and the waterwheel are two different machines, and the water in the tank is what connects them. Air (oxygen) waits at the bottom to catch the balls so the slide never gets jammed. If something blocks the bottom, every ball gets stuck, the wheel stops, and that is why a poison like cyanide is so fast and deadly."
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: "Oxidative phosphorylation is how your cells make most of their ATP, and it happens on the folded inner membrane of the mitochondrion. Electrons from NADH and FADH2 (made earlier in glucose breakdown) flow through a chain of protein complexes (NADH's enter at Complex I, FADH2's at Complex II), ending at oxygen, which becomes water. As electrons fall, three of the complexes (I, III, IV) use the energy to pump protons (H+) from the inside of the mitochondrion to the space between its membranes. That builds up a proton gradient, like water behind a dam. Then the protons flow back through a turbine called ATP synthase, and that flow powers ATP production. The key trick: electron flow and ATP-making are separate steps connected only by the proton gradient. NADH makes about 2.5 ATP and FADH2 about 1.5, because FADH2 enters at Complex II, which doesn't pump. Poisons like cyanide block the last step, the chain jams, and the cell can't make most of its ATP."
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: "The ETC is a series of redox carriers in the inner mitochondrial membrane arranged by increasing reduction potential (E0'), from NADH (−0.32 V) to O2 (+0.82 V). Electrons flow spontaneously down this gradient (ΔG°' = −nFΔE°'). Complex I (NADH→FMN→7 Fe-S→Q) pumps ~4 H+; Complex II (succinate→FAD→Fe-S→Q) pumps none and is the structural reason FADH2 yields less ATP; Complex III runs the Q cycle (resolving the 2e−/1e− mismatch via the Rieske Fe-S and b hemes, generating a semiquinone radical) and releases ~4 H+; Complex IV reduces O2 to water at the heme a3/CuB binuclear center, holding O2 and its partly reduced forms until O2 is fully reduced, so ROS almost never escape, and pumps 2 H+ per electron pair while using 2 more to make water (8 per O2 in all). All these counts are per electron pair unless stated. CoQ (lipophilic, in the bilayer) and cytochrome c (water-soluble, on the IMS face) are the mobile carriers. The pumped protons create the proton-motive force; ATP synthase discharges it at ~4 H+/ATP, giving ~2.5 ATP/NADH and ~1.5 ATP/FADH2, for ~30-32 ATP/glucose. This is Mitchell's chemiosmotic coupling: the energy is stored as a gradient, not a chemical intermediate. Inhibitor crossover (rotenone/I, antimycin A/III, cyanide/IV) reveals chain order; uncouplers dissipate the gradient as heat."
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: "OxPhos is the canonical case of energy transduction by vectorial chemistry: the inner membrane's ion impermeability converts scalar redox free energy into a transmembrane electrochemical proton potential (Δp = Δψ − 2.3RT/F·ΔpH), dominated in mitochondria by the membrane potential term. Carrier organization follows thermodynamics (ascending E0') but the kinetically interesting features are mechanistic: Complex I couples NADH:Q oxidoreduction to long-range conformational proton pumping across antiporter-like subunits remote from the redox chemistry; Complex III's bifurcated Q cycle at the Qo site is an electron-bifurcation event whose obligatory semiquinone is a major site of mitochondrial superoxide, gated by the Rieske head's conformational swing; Complex IV achieves four-electron O2 reduction through the Pm/F/Oh intermediates of the binuclear center, coupling each electron to proton uptake while avoiding partially reduced oxygen release. The H+/2e− stoichiometries (4/0/4/2 released per 2e− across I/II/III/IV, III's 4 including 2 carried across on QH₂, with IV's 2 'chemical' matrix protons per 2e− adding charge separation) and the ATP synthase c-ring stoichiometry (which sets H+/ATP, typically ~8 c-subunits → ~2.7 H+/ATP plus ~1 for ANT/Pi) jointly set the theoretical yields (about 2.7 and 1.6 ATP with a c8 ring); measured values of ~2.5/1.5 sit a little lower because of proton leak and slip, and neither need be an integer because coupling is chemiosmotic rather than through a shared chemical intermediate. Respiratory supercomplex assembly (respirasome) is proposed to channel CoQ/cyt c and limit ROS, though substrate channeling vs. random-diffusion remains contested. **Cyanide kills in minutes, and the reason is one enzyme at the very bottom of the electron-transport chain.** Block that last step and electrons have nowhere to fall, the chain backs up, the proton gradient runs down, and a cell suffocates while surrounded by air.

Oxidative phosphorylation is two machines, not one. The first drops electrons down a ladder and uses the energy to pump protons out of the **matrix**, the mitochondrion's innermost compartment, into the **intermembrane space**, like pumping water up behind a dam. The second, ATP synthase, lets them flow back like water through a turbine and uses *that* to make ATP. The two are linked by exactly one thing: the proton gradient. Treat the gradient as the currency and the famous misconceptions dissolve.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Count protons per pair of electrons. Each pair pushes a fixed number of protons out of the matrix, set by which of the three pumps (Complexes I, III and IV) it passes: from NADH, 4 + 4 + 2 = 10; from FADH₂, which joins below Complex I, 4 + 2 = 6. Each ATP spends about 4 protons coming back in. So the yield is protons ÷ 4: 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per FADH₂. And anything that changes the gradient changes both rates: electrons can push protons out only as fast as ATP synthase, or a leak, lets them back in.

The yield arithmetic assumes an intact, tightly coupled membrane and uses rounded textbook values (Going deeper, "Why 2.5 and not 2.7"). With a leaky membrane (an uncoupler, below) it fails, though the rate half still holds. Neither half covers electrons pushed backward through Complex I (reperfusion).

## The energy ladder

Electrons fall the way water falls. The height is the reduction potential, E°′: a more negative E°′ means a molecule donates electrons eagerly; a more positive one means it grabs them hard. Electrons flow spontaneously from negative toward positive, downstairs (what the explainer film calls "from low to high potential").

Picture the chain as a five-story building, its landmarks from top to bottom: NADH in the penthouse at −0.32 V, succinate at about +0.03 V, coenzyme Q just below it near +0.04 V, cytochrome c at +0.25 V, and oxygen in the basement at +0.82 V. Succinate and Q sit almost level. Succinate's electrons reach Q as FADH₂ inside Complex II, whose FAD sits close to that level too: a relay on that short hop, not a floor of its own. The complexes are the stairways between floors (table below).

| Complex IV: cytochrome c → O₂ | 0.57 V | about 110 kJ/mol |

The three steep drops make up the whole fall (70 + 40 + 110 = 220), and their energy pumps protons uphill, out of the matrix.

## The chain, in order

Everything happens on the **inner mitochondrial membrane**, folded into cristae and impermeable to ions. That is the whole foundation: a gradient is only useful if it cannot leak away. The matrix inside runs the citric acid cycle and fatty-acid oxidation.

Two mobile carriers link the complexes: lipid-soluble **coenzyme Q**, moving within the membrane (reduced, it is QH₂), and water-soluble **cytochrome c**, on the inner membrane's intermembrane-space face, carrying one electron at a time.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/oxidative-phosphorylation/chain-proton-count.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: the inner mitochondrial membrane with Complexes I to IV and ATP synthase. Electrons from NADH pass through Complex I, coenzyme Q, Complex III, cytochrome c and Complex IV to oxygen; proton beads are pumped out above Complexes I (four), III (four) and IV (two), none above Complex II, and flow back through ATP synthase. A pair from NADH passes Complexes I, III and IV and moves 4 + 4 + 2 = 10 protons out of the matrix; a pair from FADH2 joins at Q, skips Complex I and moves 6. ATP synthase, a separate machine, spends about 4 protons per ATP." />

**Complex I** (NADH-Q oxidoreductase) is an L-shaped giant. NADH hands two electrons to FMN (a flavin, like FAD), which passes them through a relay of Fe-S clusters (small iron-sulfur groups that carry one electron at a time) to Q. It moves about 4 protons out per NADH.

**Complex II** (succinate dehydrogenase), also a citric acid cycle enzyme, oxidizes succinate to fumarate and passes the electrons through its FAD and Fe-S clusters to Q. It is small, only four subunits, and, as you predicted, pumps nothing.

**Complex III** (cytochrome bc1) passes electrons from QH₂ to cytochrome c. QH₂ carries two electrons but cytochrome c takes one, so the complex splits each pair by the **Q cycle**. Net per pair: one QH₂ in, two cytochrome c reduced, four protons out. The cost is a short-lived Q radical (a Q holding a single unpaired electron) that makes superoxide, O₂ with one extra electron, if it meets O₂: elegant and dangerous.

**Complex IV** (cytochrome c oxidase) is the terminal step, and it deals with oxygen, whose partly reduced forms are among the most damaging molecules a cell can make. Electrons pass through copper and heme centers to the binuclear center (a heme iron and a copper side by side), where O₂ binds. It holds oxygen and every partly reduced form of it until all of it is water, so partly reduced oxygen almost never escapes. Four electrons plus four protons plus O₂ give two waters. Cyanide and CO jam this site, and the chain dies here.

## The proton ledger

One NADH, FADH₂ or QH₂ carries one **pair of electrons**; one oxygen atom (half an O₂) accepts one. Count per O₂ or per Q-cycle turn instead and the numbers will not add up.

| Step | Protons moved out per pair |
| --- | --- |
| Complex I: NADH → Q | 4 |
| Complex II: succinate → Q | 0 |
| Complex III: QH₂ → cytochrome c | 4 |
| Complex IV: cytochrome c → ½ O₂ | 2 |

- **Complex IV** pumps 2 protons out per pair and uses 2 more from the matrix to make water. Only the pumped ones go in this tally, but the water-making ones also move charge across the membrane, and that work takes part of the biggest drop. Per whole O₂ that is 4 pumped plus 4 used, the "8 per O₂" you may see elsewhere.
- **Complex III** releases 4, but two of them came from the matrix earlier: when Complex I or II loaded electrons onto Q, Q picked up two matrix protons with them, and QH₂ carried them across. That is how it can release 4 when its own 40 kJ pays to move only about 2.

The column gives the rule's totals: **10** from NADH, **6** from FADH₂, whose pair joins at Q and never passes the first pump. Those four protons are the entire difference: both carriers bring two electrons, and every proton they move is identical.

### The exchange rate: about 4 protons per ATP

The pumped protons store energy as the **proton-motive force**, the gradient's push on protons to flow back in, which ATP synthase discharges. Each ATP made in the matrix and delivered to the cytosol costs about 4 protons back in:

- **about 3 through ATP synthase itself** (in mammals more exactly 2.7, set by the size of its rotor, which differs between species);
- **about 1 more to move the goods**: the carriers that bring ADP and phosphate in and send ATP out run on the same gradient.

So one NADH buys 10 ÷ 4 = **2.5** ATP and one FADH₂ 6 ÷ 4 = **1.5**. Neither has to be a whole number, because no chemical step ties one electron pair to a fixed number of ATP: the protons go into one shared gradient, and ATP synthase draws from it.

Now one glucose. Glycolysis, pyruvate dehydrogenase and the citric acid cycle make 10 NADH and 2 FADH₂, plus 4 ATP or GTP made directly: 10 × 2.5 + 2 × 1.5 + 4 = **32 ATP**. That counts the 2 cytosolic NADH fully, but they cannot cross the inner membrane, so a shuttle passes on their electrons. The malate-aspartate shuttle (heart, liver) delivers them into the matrix as NADH, giving the 32. The glycerol 3-phosphate shuttle (skeletal muscle, brain) hands them straight to Q from the intermembrane-space side, as if they were FADH₂, worth 1.5 ATP each instead of 2.5, which gives **30**. So roughly **30 to 32 ATP per glucose**, an order of magnitude beyond the 2 from glycolysis alone.

## Two machines that keep each other in step

The proton-motive force has two parts: a voltage, with the intermembrane side positive, and a pH difference, with that side more acidic. In mitochondria the voltage does most of the work, and both parts push protons back toward the matrix.

That push also works against the pumps. As the gradient builds, each proton costs more to push out. When a pair's protons would cost nearly as much as its fall releases, electron flow stalls. That is the rule's second half, and the return route is ATP synthase or a small leak. With little ADP to make into ATP, the synthase slows and the chain burns less oxygen.

**Predict.** Oligomycin blocks ATP synthase. What happens to oxygen use? Now add instead an **uncoupler**, a molecule that makes the membrane leaky to protons. What happens to oxygen use, ATP and heat?

Brown fat uncouples on purpose, for warmth, through a regulated proton channel, UCP1. The diet drug DNP (dinitrophenol) did it with no control, and lethally.

Now cyanide makes sense. It stops the chain, so the pumps stop, while protons keep leaking back in. The gradient runs down and ATP synthase can no longer make ATP; it may even run backward, spending glycolytic ATP to prop the gradient up. Either way, the cell's ATP supply falls fast.

## Reading the order of the chain

**Inhibitors** freeze the chain at one point, and the freeze is diagnostic. Rotenone blocks Complex I, antimycin A Complex III, cyanide Complex IV. Every carrier upstream of the block piles up reduced, because electrons cannot leave; everything downstream goes oxidized, because none arrive.

**Predict.** Add rotenone to mitochondria fed an NADH-linked fuel. Which carriers end up reduced? Now feed them succinate instead. Does the chain still run?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

With NADH-linked fuel, NADH stays reduced, everything past Complex I goes oxidized, and the chain stops. With succinate it runs, because those electrons enter at Q, below the block, and each pair still moves 6 protons.

</details>

That crossover pattern, read by spectroscopy, is one of the main ways the order of the chain was worked out before anyone had a structure.

## The price of running on oxygen

**Reactive oxygen species** are that price. Mitochondria leak a small fraction of the O₂ they handle as superoxide, and the leak is lower when they are busy making ATP. Complex I and the Complex III Qo site are among the main sources, and which sites dominate depends on the fuels present.

In **Friedreich's ataxia**, a GAA-repeat expansion lowers levels of frataxin, a protein that helps build Fe-S clusters; cluster assembly is impaired, and Fe-S enzymes of the chain and the citric acid cycle lose activity, with sensory neurons and the heart hit hardest.

In **ischemia-reperfusion injury**, succinate builds up while the heart or brain is starved of oxygen. When blood returns, Complex II oxidizes it fast and over-reduces the Q pool. Complex I's drop only just covers its four protons on standard values (about 70 kJ against 4 × 17), so it runs near balance, and shifts in the NADH and Q pool ratios easily tip it. With the gradient high and Q crammed with electrons, it tips and electrons are pushed backward through it, from QH₂ toward NADH, making a burst of superoxide. With calcium overload and opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, that burst adds damage on top of the ischemia itself.

## Going deeper

### The full Q cycle

At the Qo site, facing the intermembrane space, QH₂ sends one electron through the Rieske Fe-S cluster and cytochrome c1 to cytochrome c. The other loops back through the b hemes to reduce a Q at the Qi site, facing the matrix, which antimycin A blocks. Two rounds oxidize two QH₂ and regenerate one, giving the net per pair above. The semiquinone radical (Q•⁻) forms fleetingly at Qo.

### How Complex IV reduces oxygen

Electrons go from cytochrome c to CuA, heme a and the heme a3/CuB center, which binds O₂ once it holds two electrons, then breaks the O–O bond in one step, drawing four electrons from its own metals and a nearby tyrosine (the heme a3 iron goes to the ferryl state). Electrons from cytochrome c then reset those groups and complete the two waters.

### Protons released versus charges moved

The ledger counts protons released, not charges each complex moves across the membrane. Complex III moves only 2 charges itself, and Complex IV moves 4 (its 2 pumped protons plus 2 matrix protons consumed with electrons arriving from the intermembrane-space side); with Complex I's 4, both tallies total 10. Check energy on the whole chain: 10 protons at about 17 kJ is about 174 of the 220 kJ an NADH pair releases, and 6 is about 104 of the roughly 150 kJ from succinate.

### Why 2.5 and not 2.7

The mammalian ATP synthase rotor has 8 c-subunits and makes 3 ATP per turn, about 2.7 protons per ATP. Add about 1 for the carriers and the cost is about 3.7, not 4, giving theoretical yields of about 2.7 and 1.6. Proton leak and slip bring measured values down to about 2.5 and 1.5.

### Supercomplexes

Cryo-EM shows the complexes assembling into **supercomplexes** (the respirasome, I + III₂ + IV), which may shorten the diffusion path for the mobile carriers and reduce leak. Whether that matters for normal function is still debated.

### Carriers between the landmark floors

The potentials here are standard values, E°′. Other carriers sit between the landmark floors: FMN at −0.30 V, the iron-sulfur clusters across a range (Complex I's mostly negative, Complex III's Rieske cluster strongly positive), and cytochrome a at +0.29 V. In Complex I, a machine of about 45 subunits, the relay from FMN to coenzyme Q passes through about seven Fe-S clusters.

### The porous outer membrane

The gradient depends on the inner membrane being impermeable to ions; the outer membrane is the opposite. Its VDAC channels let most small molecules and ions, up to roughly 5000 Da, through.

### Reactive oxygen species: numbers and cleanup

The old estimate that 1 to 2 percent of the O₂ mitochondria handle leaks as superoxide is now thought to be too high. Eleven leak sites have been mapped; Complex I's main ones are its flavin and its quinone site. Superoxide dismutase converts two superoxides to H₂O₂ and O₂, then peroxiredoxins and glutathione peroxidases (and catalase, mostly in peroxisomes) finish the cleanup.

### Low oxygen at ecosystem scale

Every aerobic animal needs O₂ waiting at the bottom of the ladder; without it the chain backs up just as it does under cyanide. The Gulf of Mexico **dead zone** is this chemistry at ecosystem scale: nutrient runoff fuels an algal bloom, bacteria decomposing the dead algae use up the dissolved oxygen, and the terminal electron acceptor that fish and other aerobic animals depend on runs too low to support them.
