**A newborn mouse that lacks the muscle kinase MuSK cannot breathe. Its motor axons reach the diaphragm, but no neuromuscular junction forms.**

The axon arrived. The muscle was there. Both cells were healthy, and still no synapse. So a synapse is not something that happens whenever an axon touches a target. It is built, by signals passing in both directions, and MuSK sits at the centre of that exchange. This lesson asks how the junction between a motor neuron and a muscle fibre is assembled, and which partner decides where it goes.

## Chemical synapses: three signs of a synaptic specialization

Look at any chemical synapse in an electron micrograph and you will find three signs that the two membranes are specialized for each other:

1. **Small vesicles** packed into the presynaptic terminal, holding transmitter.
2. **A narrow cleft** between the two cells.
3. **A thickened postsynaptic membrane**, where receptors and the proteins that hold them are concentrated.

The neuromuscular junction (NMJ), where a motor axon meets a skeletal muscle fibre, is where these were first worked out, and for good reasons. It is large, it lies outside the brain where you can reach it, and it is simple: one transmitter, acetylcholine, and one major receptor, the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (AChR), a channel that opens when acetylcholine binds and lets cations into the muscle. (You met this receptor as a ligand-gated channel in [that lesson](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/).) The NMJ cleft also holds a sheet of basal lamina, the thin layer of extracellular matrix that wraps each muscle fibre. Keep that sheet in mind.

At a mature junction the receptors are packed under the nerve ending and sparse everywhere else on the fibre. Building the junction means solving that sorting problem: where do the receptors go, and who tells them?

The first contact is fast and physical. In culture, a growth cone from a frog spinal neuron adheres to a muscle cell rapidly after it touches; this is "the sticky synapse". Adhesion holds the partners together. It does not by itself make a postsynaptic specialization.

## The muscle was waiting

You might expect the nerve to choose where receptors gather. The first evidence against that came from culture: muscle cells grown with no neurons at all make small, spontaneous AChR clusters. When a motor axon is added, it does not seek out those clusters, but where it touches, far more clustering follows. And AChR activation is not the inducer: receptors still form synapses in mice that cannot make acetylcholine (Misgeld et al., PNAS 2005).

Culture leaves an objection. In an embryo, perhaps a nerve had marked the spot before anyone looked. In 2001 two groups answered this in the mouse. Xia Yang, Steven Burden and colleagues used mice whose motor neurons were eliminated genetically with diphtheria toxin, so that no motor axons reached the muscle. Weichun Lin, Joshua Sanes, Kuo-Fen Lee and colleagues also used mouse genetics. In both studies, the diaphragm still built a band of AChR clusters across its middle, and in the first study RNA for receptor subunits was also concentrated in that band. The muscle had laid down a **prepattern**.

The prepattern had limits. It needed MuSK: embryos lacking both motor axons and MuSK made receptors but no central band. It did not need agrin. And the nerve changed it: without innervation the band was wider. So the nerve does not need to choose the first position of the clusters, but contact makes far more clustering, narrows the band and keeps only the receptors beneath the terminal. Keep two statements apart. "AChR clusters can form without innervation" is true. "The nerve is not needed for a synapse" is false.

## The agrin relay: from an empty sheath to a kinase

The search for the nerve's signal began with a strange experiment. In 1978 Sanes, Lynn Marshall and U. J. McMahan damaged frog muscle so that the fibres and the nerve terminals degenerated, leaving only the sheaths of basal lamina. Regenerating axons returned to the old synaptic sites on the empty sheaths. In later work from the same laboratory, regenerating muscle fibres clustered receptors at the old sites even with the nerve kept out. The basal lamina remembered where the synapse had been.

Think of a building demolished down to its foundation, with a mark on the slab showing where the front door stood. The new builders read the mark. McMahan's group went looking for the ink. They needed tissue denser in cholinergic synapses than muscle, and found it in the electric organ of the ray *Torpedo*. Fractions of electric organ were tested on cultured chick muscle cells, counting receptor clusters stained with fluorescent alpha-bungarotoxin, a snake toxin that binds the AChR. In 1987 they named the active protein **agrin**.

Motor neurons make agrin and send it into the basal lamina, but muscle makes agrin too. The difference is splicing. Neural agrin carries a short insert of a few amino acids at one splice site, and in culture assays on chick muscle cells that insert makes it more than a thousand times more potent at clustering receptors than the form without it, which muscle mainly makes. Mice lacking agrin had profoundly disrupted junctions.

Agrin had to be received. In 1996 the Regeneron group of David Glass and George Yancopoulos, with Burden, reported that mice lacking **MuSK**, a muscle-specific receptor tyrosine kinase, make no junctions, and that agrin acts through a complex containing MuSK. Like other receptor tyrosine kinases, MuSK signals when two kinase domains are brought together and phosphorylate each other ([that lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)). Yet agrin did not bind MuSK directly. Something was missing.

The missing link was **Lrp4**, a large membrane protein of the LDL receptor family. Mice lacking Lrp4 were reported in 2006 to have NMJ defects resembling those of MuSK mutants. In 2008 two laboratories showed that Lrp4 is the agrin receptor. Natalie Kim and colleagues in Burden's laboratory found that nonmuscle cells given Lrp4 bound neural agrin and that Lrp4 forms a complex with MuSK. Bin Zhang and colleagues in Lin Mei's laboratory showed that purified neural agrin binds purified Lrp4, and that Lrp4 also binds MuSK.

Two more proteins finish the chain. **Dok7**, an adaptor inside the muscle, binds phosphorylated MuSK and boosts its activity; mice lacking Dok7 form no junctions. **Rapsyn**, a scaffold on the inner face of the membrane, holds the receptors together ([that lesson](/lessons/adaptors-and-scaffolds/)). Mice lacking rapsyn make receptors but do not cluster them, though nuclei beneath the synapse still transcribe receptor genes.

> **neural agrin → Lrp4 → MuSK (with Dok7) → rapsyn → clustered AChR**

## Two forces: agrin protects, acetylcholine disperses

The prepattern raised a puzzle. If the muscle can cluster receptors with no agrin, why do mice lacking agrin lose most of their clusters? The answer is that the nerve brings a second signal. Acetylcholine, released at every terminal, disperses receptor clusters that agrin does not protect. The rule fits in one line: "Receptor clustering is a competition between two forces: facilitation by Agrin-MuSK and inhibition by ACh-AChRs."

Activity also works through the nuclei. A muscle fibre is one long cell with many nuclei. When the nerve drives the fibre, receptor transcription is suppressed in nuclei away from the synapse, while the few nuclei beneath the terminal keep transcribing receptor genes, a pattern Burden's group showed with transgenic mice in 1992. Cut the nerve and the suppression lifts: receptors appear along the whole fibre, and the denervated muscle becomes "supersensitive" to acetylcholine applied anywhere on it.

### Design problem

You have three mouse lines: one lacking agrin, one lacking choline acetyltransferase (ChAT, the enzyme that makes acetylcholine, so its motor neurons release none), and the double knockout. Predict what AChR clusters look like at birth in each, and say what the double tells you about agrin's job.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use the two forces. Without agrin, MuSK still runs at its own lower level, but acetylcholine keeps dispersing clusters with nothing to protect them, so most clusters are lost. Without acetylcholine, nothing disperses clusters, so the muscle keeps more of them, spread over a wider band, and the nerve branches widely. In the double knockout, both forces are gone, and clusters return, built by the muscle's own MuSK-dependent program.

The double is the telling line. If agrin's job were to start clusters, removing acetylcholine could not bring them back. Clusters come back, so in the animal agrin's main job is to protect clusters from dispersal by acetylcholine. Two mouse studies published in 2005, one from Sanes and Lichtman's laboratories and one from Kuo-Fen Lee's laboratory with Weichun Lin, reported this result.

</details>

## NMJ vs. central synapses, and a junction that fails

The NMJ is a good guide to central synapses, but not a template. A central neuron receives thousands of inputs that use different transmitters, its synapses have no basal lamina in the cleft, and receptor clustering there uses other molecules, including neurexins, neuroligins and the scaffold PSD-95, taken up in [that lesson](/lessons/cns-synapses/). What carries over is the logic: each partner sends signals that make the other specialize, and stable synapses need both sides. How a muscle fibre that begins with several inputs ends with one is the subject of [that lesson](/lessons/synapse-elimination/).

The NMJ also fails in a known way. In **myasthenia gravis**, the immune system makes antibodies against the junction, and muscles tire and weaken with use. Most patients have antibodies against the AChR. Some of the rest have antibodies against MuSK or Lrp4, two links from the chain above. Inherited mutations in several of the same genes, Dok7 among them, cause congenital myasthenic syndromes. The disease reads like a list of the knockouts.
