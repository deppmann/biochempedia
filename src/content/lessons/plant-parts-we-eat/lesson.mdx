## The big idea

Every food on a dinner plate came from some part of a plant, and that part had a job. A carrot is a root. Lettuce is a leaf. Broccoli is a bunch of flower buds that have not opened yet. Sorting groceries onto a drawing of a plant ties each food to its part and to that part's job.

Roots hold the plant in the ground and take in water and nutrients. Stems hold the plant up and carry water and nutrients. Leaves make the plant's food using light. Flowers make seeds. A **fruit** is the part that grows from a flower and holds the seeds. That is why a scientist calls a tomato or a cucumber a fruit, even if the kitchen calls it a vegetable.

Plants give us more than food: cotton for clothes, wood for houses and paper, and rubber. They feed animals too. Virginia's grade 2 standard covers both ideas.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time food is out. An adult does all the cutting and puts the knife away right after.
- Check for food allergies before anyone tastes anything, and follow your school's food rules. With a class, sort without tasting unless families have been asked first. A child who reacts to a food should not taste or handle it. Raw apple, carrot and celery can make the mouth itch in some children with hay fever; if anyone's mouth itches or swells, or they have trouble breathing, stop and get help right away.
- Whole grapes, cherry tomatoes, whole peas and beans, corn kernels, nuts and seeds, hard raw carrot and raw apple chunks are choking hazards. Keep them away from children under 4, and take care with older children who gulp their food. For tasting, the adult cuts everything small, no bigger than half an inch, with grapes and cherry tomatoes cut lengthwise into quarters, and children sit down and chew well.
- No tasting raw potato. Use a firm potato with no green skin and no sprouts: green skin and sprouts can hold natural toxins (solanine) that make people sick. If your potato has either, use a different one.
- Wash your hands and the produce under running water (no soap on the food) before tasting. Taste from a clean plate, not from the drawing sheet, and give each child their own small piece. Use canned or cooked beans; dry beans from a bag are not for eating raw.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Draw the plant.** On a big sheet, help your child draw a plant: roots under a soil line, a stem, leaves, a flower, and a fruit with seeds inside. Label each part. With a class, give each group of four one drawing and one bag of foods.
2. **Wash and cut.** Wash the produce. You cut the apple and the tomato in half across the middle so the seeds show. Leave everything else whole.
3. **Predict.** Hold up one food at a time and ask, "Which part of the plant do you think this is?" Let your child put it on the drawing. Don't correct anything yet.
4. **Look for clues.** Go back through the pile. Seeds inside means fruit. Look along a carrot for thin side roots, or the small marks where they were trimmed. Look closely at broccoli: each small green bump is a flower bud. If broccoli is left to grow, the buds open into small yellow flowers. Lettuce is flat and green, with veins like other leaves.
5. **Play the trick cards.** Hand over the potato. Ask, "It grew underground. Is it a root?" Then look at the eyes. Each eye is a bud that can sprout a new shoot, set in neat spots like the buds along a stem. A potato is an underground stem, called a **tuber**. Next, hold up the celery. Point out that the leaves grow from the top of the stalk. The celery we buy is mostly long leaf stalks, so it belongs with the leaves.
6. **Taste (optional).** If allergies are checked and rules allow, taste one food from each part, in small pieces the adult has cut, from a clean plate. Ask which part your child likes best.
7. **Talk about jobs.** Point to each part on the drawing and ask what it does for the plant. Then ask which animals eat it: rabbits eat leaves, squirrels eat seeds and nuts, birds eat fruit.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "Which part of the plant do you think this came from? What is your clue?"
- "I wonder if everything we eat grows above the ground. Can you find one that doesn't?"
- "What do you think we will find inside the tomato?"
- "Do you think a fruit always tastes sweet? What about a cucumber?"
- "What do you think would happen to a carrot plant if we ate all its leaves?"
- "I wonder which part of a plant your shirt or this paper came from."

## Why it works

Scientists sort plant parts by how they grow and what they do, while cooks sort foods by taste. The two systems often disagree. "Vegetable" has no exact meaning in science. "Fruit" does: the part that develops from the flower and holds the seeds. So tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, green bean pods and apples are all fruits.

Storage parts are the most puzzling. Many plants pack starch away underground to regrow later. A carrot stores it in a root. A potato stores it in a stem tip that swells into a tuber, and its eyes are buds that can sprout. A sweet potato stores it in a swollen root, and it is only a distant relative of the potato.

Two more puzzles are worth knowing. Celery stalks are leaf stalks, which carry water like a stem. And a corn kernel or an unshelled sunflower "seed" is botanically a tiny fruit with one seed inside, so this activity leaves them out.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use six foods, one for each part, and skip the trick cards at first. Place each food on the drawing, then add the potato last as a puzzle. Ask simple job questions: "Which part drinks? Which part holds it up?" Your child can draw a line from each food to its part instead of writing.

### Ages 8 to 10
Add the trick cards and harder foods: an onion (fleshy leaves), a green bean pod (a fruit with seeds inside) and ginger (an underground stem). Ask your child to write each part's job beside it. Then sort things that are not food: a cotton sock, a paper bag, a wooden spoon. Which plant part did each come from?
