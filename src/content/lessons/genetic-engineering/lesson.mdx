## Build one change, then test it

<WordBank terms={['genetic engineering', 'restriction enzyme', 'DNA ligase', 'plasmid', 'recombinant DNA', 'polymerase chain reaction (PCR)', 'gel electrophoresis', 'CRISPR', 'synthetic biology', 'genetically modified organism']} />

**Genetic engineering** is using laboratory tools to change an organism’s DNA. DNA has the same basic structure in living things, and nearly all of them share one genetic code. So a bacterium can read intron-free DNA for a human protein when bacterial control DNA is present. Intron-free matters: most human genes contain introns, stretches that human cells cut out of the RNA message. Bacteria cannot cut them out, so they generally cannot read a natural human gene correctly. Labs instead use DNA made chemically or copied from the RNA message. Review how DNA is built in [that lesson](/lessons/dna-structure-and-copying/) and how cells read a gene in [the protein lesson](/lessons/protein-synthesis/).

A **restriction enzyme** is a protein from bacteria that cuts DNA wherever it finds one particular short sequence; many leave short single-stranded tails called sticky ends. A **plasmid** is a small, usually circular piece of bacterial DNA that copies separately from the main chromosome. **DNA ligase** is an enzyme that seals paired ends into one continuous strand, making **recombinant DNA**: DNA joined from more than one source.

The tools work like a word processor's cut, paste and copy, with one difference: they are molecules acting by chance in a tube, so each step works on only some molecules or cells. As the first film showed, the plasmid usually also carries an antibiotic-resistance gene, so growing the bacteria with that antibiotic, called **selection**, leaves mostly the ones that took up a plasmid.

Now test the DNA. The **polymerase chain reaction (PCR)** uses short DNA pieces called primers, which mark where copying starts, and a DNA-copying enzyme to make many copies of one chosen DNA stretch. In the ideal model, the amount doubles each cycle.

**Predict 2.** Start with one copy. How many copies would the ideal model give after ten cycles?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Copies = 1 × 2<sup>10</sup> = 1,024. Real reactions copy a bit less than perfectly.

</details>

> ideal PCR: copies after n cycles = starting copies × 2 × 2 × ... (n times)

**Gel electrophoresis** sorts DNA pieces by size. DNA has a negative electric charge, so current pulls it toward the positive end of a gel. Shorter pieces slip through the gel faster and travel farther. Comparing a band with a ladder of known sizes estimates the piece's size, but not its DNA sequence.

**Predict 3.** A ladder has bands at 500 and 1,000 base pairs. Where should a piece about 700 base pairs long stop?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Between the 500 and 1,000 base-pair bands. That size fits the target, but sequencing would confirm that it is the intended DNA.

</details>

## From one product to many uses

The second film left out a newer way to aim the change itself. **CRISPR** comes from a bacterial defense system. In a common version, a guide RNA helps aim Cas9 at a matching DNA region, and Cas9 cuts both strands. A cell then repairs the cut. The final change must be tested, because unintended sites may also be changed.

A bacterium or crop engineered to carry a DNA change is a **genetically modified organism**. Casgevy is different: it modifies a patient's cells outside the body, not a whole organism. Uses come from several fields:

| Field | Example | What was changed |
|---|---|---|
| Medicine | Human insulin; human growth hormone | Bacteria or yeast carry a human gene |
| Medicine | Casgevy, approved in 2023 for sickle cell disease | A patient’s own blood stem cells are edited with CRISPR |
| Agriculture | Bt corn and cotton | A bacterial gene makes a protein that kills certain insect pests |
| Agriculture | Herbicide-tolerant soybeans | Plants survive certain weed-killers |
| Industry | Cheese-making enzyme chymosin | Microbes carry the gene for an enzyme once taken from calf stomachs |
| Research | Edited cells grown in a lab | Scientists switch off one gene to learn its job |

**Synthetic biology** goes a step further: it designs and builds new biological parts, devices and systems. For example, researchers built a new chemical pathway in baker’s yeast to make a malaria medicine's starting material.

Each use raises questions that data alone cannot settle.

- **Safety:** Could an edit land in the wrong place? Could a crop affect insects it was not meant for? Can relying on one weed-killer select for resistant weeds, as in that lesson?
- **Equity:** Who can pay for a new treatment? Who can buy the seed?
- **Limits:** Should anyone edit sperm, eggs or embryos, when future people cannot agree to the change?

To the second film's source questions (who wrote it, what evidence, checked by other scientists?), add one more: does the writer gain from your belief? In short: change, test, judge.

### Design problem

You put an insulin plasmid into bacteria. Plan how to show that your bacteria took up the plasmid and are making insulin, and which controls you need.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Groups:** bacteria given the insulin plasmid; bacteria given the same plasmid with no insulin DNA (the control); bacteria given nothing.
- **Selection:** grow all three on antibiotic plates. Bacteria given nothing should not grow, showing the antibiotic works. On a plate without antibiotic they should grow, showing the cells were alive.
- **Check the DNA:** extract the plasmid from grown colonies, cut on both sides of the insert and run a gel. The insulin plasmid should give a backbone band plus an insert-sized band that the empty plasmid lacks.
- **Check the product:** test each group for insulin with an antibody test. The empty-plasmid group should give no signal, so a signal in the insulin group points to the added DNA. A known insulin sample shows the test works. A signal shows insulin is present; purity and activity need further tests.
- **Constants:** same strain, temperature and growth time. Give the two plasmid groups equal molar amounts of plasmid (the same number of plasmid molecules), and give the no-DNA group the same volume of plasmid buffer without DNA.
- **Repeats:** test at least three colonies from each group that grows, on three different days.
- **Safety:** use a harmless lab strain, wear gloves and destroy all cultures afterwards.

</details>

## Going deeper

From the 1920s on, people who needed insulin usually used insulin from cattle and pig pancreases. The FDA approved human insulin made by bacteria in 1982. In the 1979 experiment behind the simple model, scientists chemically made DNA for insulin's two chains. Bacteria made each chain attached to a bacterial protein; the chains were cut free, purified and joined. Today bacteria or yeast usually make one precursor chain that is trimmed into insulin.
