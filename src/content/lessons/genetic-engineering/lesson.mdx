## Explain: tools that cut, join, copy and sort DNA

<WordBank terms={['genetic engineering', 'restriction enzyme', 'DNA ligase', 'plasmid', 'recombinant DNA', 'polymerase chain reaction (PCR)', 'gel electrophoresis', 'CRISPR', 'synthetic biology', 'genetically modified organism']} />

**Genetic engineering** is using laboratory tools to change an organism’s DNA. DNA is the same kind of molecule in every living thing, and nearly all living things use the same genetic code, so a cell can often read a gene that came from another species. For the cell to use it, the gene also needs nearby control DNA that this kind of cell recognizes. You can review how DNA is built in [that lesson](/lessons/dna-structure-and-copying/).

The tools work like an editing kit. A word-processing analogy helps: cut, paste and copy. The analogy breaks because DNA tools are molecules acting by chance in a tube, and each step works on only some of the DNA.

- A **restriction enzyme** is a protein from bacteria that cuts DNA wherever it finds one particular short sequence. Many leave short single-stranded tails, called sticky ends.
- **DNA ligase** is an enzyme that joins two DNA ends into one continuous strand.
- A **plasmid** is a small, usually circular piece of DNA in a bacterium that copies itself separately from the main chromosome. When it copies, any gene added to it is copied too.
- **Recombinant DNA** is DNA joined together from more than one source, such as a plasmid carrying a human gene.
- The **polymerase chain reaction (PCR)** makes many copies of one chosen stretch of DNA. Short DNA pieces called primers mark where copying starts. In an ideal reaction the amount doubles each cycle.
- **Gel electrophoresis** sorts DNA pieces by size. DNA carries a negative electric charge, so a current pulls it toward the positive end of a gel. Shorter pieces slip through the gel faster and travel farther.
- **CRISPR** is a gene-editing tool taken from a bacterial defense system. In the most common version, a guide RNA matches one DNA sequence, and a protein called Cas9 cuts both strands there.

## Explain: from a human gene to insulin, crops and cures

Here is the textbook path to insulin made by bacteria. It is a simplified model, not a factory recipe.

1. Get DNA that codes for insulin. In an early experiment, reported in 1979, scientists made this DNA chemically, as two pieces, one for each of insulin’s two protein chains.
2. Cut a plasmid open with a restriction enzyme that leaves sticky ends. Cutting the gene with the same enzyme gives it matching sticky ends. Many labs cut both pieces with a pair of enzymes, so the gene can go in only one way round.
3. Mix them so the ends pair, and seal them with ligase. Now you have recombinant DNA.
4. Put the plasmids into bacteria. Only some bacteria take one up. The plasmid usually also carries a gene for resistance to an antibiotic, so growing the bacteria with that antibiotic leaves mostly the ones that took up the plasmid.
5. Grow the bacteria. As they divide, the plasmid is copied. If the gene is switched on, the bacteria make each insulin chain attached to a bacterial protein. The chains are cut free, purified and joined into insulin. Today most insulin is made instead as one precursor chain in bacteria or yeast, which is then trimmed into insulin.

Adding a gene does not guarantee the cell makes its protein, so scientists test for the product. You can follow how cells read a gene into a protein in [that lesson](/lessons/protein-synthesis/).

An organism whose DNA was changed this way is a **genetically modified organism**. Examples come from three fields:

| Field | Example | What was changed |
|---|---|---|
| Medicine | Human insulin; human growth hormone | Bacteria or yeast carry a human gene |
| Medicine | Casgevy, approved in 2023 for sickle cell disease | A patient’s own blood stem cells are edited with CRISPR |
| Agriculture | Bt corn and cotton | A bacterial gene makes a protein that kills certain insect pests |
| Agriculture | Herbicide-tolerant soybeans | Plants survive certain weed-killers |
| Industry | Cheese-making enzyme chymosin | Microbes carry the gene for an enzyme once taken from calf stomachs |
| Research | Edited cells grown in a lab | Scientists switch off one gene to learn its job |

**Synthetic biology** goes a step further: it designs and builds new biological parts, devices and systems. In one example, researchers built a new chemical pathway in baker’s yeast so that it made a starting material for a malaria medicine.

Each use raises questions that data alone cannot settle. People disagree, and that is part of the work.

- **Safety:** Could an edit land in the wrong place? Could a crop affect insects it was not meant for? Can relying on one weed-killer select for resistant weeds, as in that lesson?
- **Equity:** Who can pay for a new treatment? Who can buy the seed?
- **Limits:** Should anyone edit sperm, eggs or embryos, when future people cannot agree to the change?

To judge a claim, ask who wrote it, what evidence it gives, whether it was checked by other scientists, and whether the writer gains from your belief.

### Design problem

You put an insulin plasmid into bacteria. Plan how to show that your bacteria took up the plasmid and are making insulin, and which controls you need.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Groups:** bacteria given the insulin plasmid; bacteria given the same plasmid with no insulin DNA (the control); bacteria given nothing.
- **Selection:** grow all three on plates with the antibiotic. Bacteria given nothing should not grow, which shows the antibiotic works. Also spread some on a plate without the antibiotic, where they should grow, to show the cells were alive.
- **Check the DNA:** grow colonies, extract the plasmid, cut it with the restriction enzyme and run a gel. The insulin plasmid should show an extra band of the expected size that the empty plasmid lacks.
- **Check the product:** test each group for insulin with an antibody test. The empty-plasmid group should give no signal, so a signal in the insulin group points to the added DNA. Run a known insulin sample too, to show the test works.
- **Constants:** same strain, same temperature, same growth time, same amount of DNA.
- **Repeats:** test at least three colonies from each group that grows, on three different days.
- **Safety:** use a harmless lab strain, wear gloves and destroy all cultures afterwards.

</details>
