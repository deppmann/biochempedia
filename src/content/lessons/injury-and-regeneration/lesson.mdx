summary: "A peripheral nerve can regrow after injury, but axons in the adult brain and spinal cord mostly cannot. One idea carries the lesson: one cut, two fates. scientists:
  - name: "Augustus Waller"
    contribution: >-
      Cut nerves of the frog's tongue and showed that the part of each fibre separated from its
      cell body degenerates while the part still connected survives, the process now called
      Wallerian degeneration.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Waller, Phil. Trans. R. Soc. Lond. 140:423 (1850), 'Experiments on the section of the glossopharyngeal and hypoglossal nerves of the frog'."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The physiologist Augustus Waller worked on the nerves that serve the frog's tongue. He cut two of them, the glossopharyngeal and the hypoglossal,
      and examined the fibres over the following days. Beyond the cut, the part of each fibre
      that had been separated from its cell body changed: its contents broke up. The part still joined to the brain or spinal cord looked normal. He reported the
      work in the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society, in a paper published in 1850.
      Waller read the result as evidence that a nerve fibre depends on a centre for its
      nutrition, a centre we now place in the cell body. The method lasted longer than the
      explanation. Because degenerating fibres can be stained and followed, anatomists for
      decades afterwards cut a pathway and traced which fibres degenerated, and in this way
      mapped tracts in the brain and spinal cord. The breakdown of the cut-off stump still
      carries his name.
  - name: "Michael Coleman"
    contribution: >-
      His group found the mutation in the slow Wallerian degeneration (Wlds) mouse, a fusion gene
      joining part of Ube4b to the NAD+-making enzyme Nmnat1, and with Jonathan Gilley identified
      NMNAT2 as the short-lived survival factor that a cut axon loses.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Mack et al., Nat. Neurosci. 4:1199 (2001); Gilley & Coleman, PLoS Biol. 8:e1000300 (2010)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Coleman's work turned a strange mouse into a gene and then into a pathway. The C57BL/Wlds
      mouse had been described in 1989 by Lunn, Perry, Brown, Rosen and Gordon, and crosses
      reported in 1990 showed that a single dominant gene was responsible. In 1998 Coleman, Hugh
      Perry and colleagues found an unusual mutation in the gene's region on mouse chromosome 4:
      an 85-kilobase stretch of DNA present in three tandem copies. In 2001 his group, with
      Till Mack as first author, showed that the rearrangement makes a chimeric gene, joining
      the start of the ubiquitination factor Ube4b to the enzyme Nmnat1, and that adding this
      gene to normal mice delayed Wallerian degeneration in a dose-dependent way. The protein sat
      mostly in the nucleus, which left a puzzle about how it protected axons. In 2010 Jonathan
      Gilley and Coleman proposed an answer. The axon's own isoform, NMNAT2, is short-lived and
      must be supplied constantly by transport from the cell body, and the longer-lived Wlds
      protein can stand in for it after a cut.
  - name: "Marc Freeman"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory found that flies lacking dSarm and mice lacking Sarm1 keep severed axons
      intact for a long time, the first loss-of-function mutation shown to block axon
      self-destruction this strongly.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Osterloh et al., Science 337:481 (2012), 'dSarm/Sarm1 is required for activation of an injury-induced axon death pathway'."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      If a cut axon destroys itself, a mutation in the machinery should leave the severed axon
      intact. Freeman's laboratory looked for such mutations in the fruit fly, cutting sensory
      axons and searching for mutants whose detached axons survived. Loss of dSarm, an adaptor
      protein then known from Toll receptor signalling, did this. Severed axons lacking dSarm
      lasted for weeks, and the protection was cell-autonomous: the mutation had to be in the
      neuron itself, not in the cells around it. The 2012 paper, a collaboration whose authors
      included the groups of Michael Coleman and Marc Tessier-Lavigne, then showed that mouse
      axons lacking Sarm1 also survived long after being cut, both in the living nerve and in
      culture. Wlds is the gain of an unusual fusion protein; this was the loss of an ordinary
      gene. The result gave direct evidence that axons actively promote their own destruction
      after injury, and it handed the field a protein that the laboratories of Aaron DiAntonio
      and Jeffrey Milbrandt went on to show is an enzyme that destroys NAD+.
  - name: "Albert Aguayo"
    contribution: >-
      With Samuel David, showed that axons of adult central neurons grow for long distances into
      grafts of peripheral nerve, so the central environment, and not only the neurons, limits
      regeneration.
    profileSourceAnchor: "David & Aguayo, Science 214:931 (1981), 'Axonal elongation into peripheral nervous system \"bridges\" after central nervous system injury in adult rats'."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Aguayo's laboratory in Montreal asked a plain question about the adult brain and spinal
      cord. Have their neurons lost the ability to regrow an axon, or does something around them
      stop it? Peripheral nerve offered a test, because it holds Schwann cells and none of the
      glia of the central nervous system. In 1981 Samuel David and Aguayo took segments of
      peripheral nerve in adult rats and placed them as bridges, with one end in the medulla and
      the other in the spinal cord. Axons grew along the grafts for about 30 millimetres, and
      their cell bodies were traced to neurons at both levels of the central nervous system.
      Where axons left the graft and entered central tissue again, they grew only a short way.
      The experiment separated the two explanations. Adult central neurons can extend long axons
      when their glial surroundings are peripheral. That made the central environment itself a
      target, and it set up the search for the myelin and scar molecules that other laboratories
      identified in the years that followed.

  - name: "Compartmented (microfluidic) neuron culture"
    blurb: >-
      Neurons are grown so that their cell bodies sit in one chamber and their axons grow through
      narrow channels into another. Axons can then be cut, deprived of growth factor or treated
      with a drug on their own, which shows whether a process runs locally in the axon.
  - name: "Forward genetic screen in Drosophila"
    blurb: >-
      Flies are mutagenized at random, and their offspring are screened for a phenotype, here
      severed axons that fail to degenerate. Because the screen assumes nothing about which gene is
      involved, it found dSarm, a protein no one had linked to axon death.
  - name: "Peripheral nerve bridge graft with tracing"
    blurb: >-
      A segment of peripheral nerve is grafted into the central nervous system, and a tracer taken
      up by axons in the graft is carried back to their cell bodies. Labelled cell bodies show
      which neurons grew into the graft, which tests whether central neurons can regrow in a
      peripheral environment.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: In adult mice, deleting PTEN from retinal ganglion cells lets far more of their axons than normal regenerate
      through a crushed optic nerve, which is central tissue. What does this result add to the peripheral nerve graft experiments of
      David and Aguayo?
    choices:
    - The neuron's own growth state limits regrowth, not only its surroundings
    - Central myelin is the only barrier, since PTEN deletion removes myelin
    - Retinal ganglion cells are peripheral neurons, so they regrow anyway
    - PTEN deletion makes new neurons that replace those whose axons were cut
    answer: 0
    rationale: 'The graft experiments showed that central neurons can regrow when their surroundings are peripheral.
      The PTEN result is the other half: with the central surroundings left in place, removing PTEN, a brake on the
      mTOR pathway inside the neuron, let far more axons than normal regrow. So both the environment and the neuron''s intrinsic growth
      state limit regrowth. PTEN deletion acts in the neurons and does not remove myelin, and myelin is one barrier
      among several. Retinal ganglion cells are central neurons whose axons form the optic nerve. Mature neurons do
      not divide; the regrowing axons come from the injured neurons themselves.'
  - stem: A crushed nerve in the forearm can regrow, but a crushed tract in the spinal cord usually does not. Which
      difference in the surroundings does most to explain this?
    choices:
    - Central axons lack myelin, so they have no tube to regrow along
    - Repair Schwann cells and macrophages clear debris and guide regrowth
    - Peripheral axons lack SARM1, so their stumps never degenerate at all
    - Peripheral neurons divide to replace lost axons, but central ones cannot
    answer: 1
    rationale: In peripheral nerve, Schwann cells convert to a repair state, recruit macrophages, clear myelin debris
      and line up into tubes that guide regrowing axons; central tissue clears debris slowly and contains myelin inhibitors
      and a scar. Mature neurons do not divide in either system, and an axon regrows from its own cell body. Peripheral
      axons have SARM1, and their distal stumps degenerate on schedule. Many central axons are myelinated, by oligodendrocytes,
      and their myelin hinders regrowth rather than guiding it. The surroundings are only half the story, though; the neuron's
      own growth state also differs, as the conditioning lesion shows.
  - stem: After an axon is cut, what does activated SARM1 do that commits the stump to degeneration?
    choices:
    - It makes extra NAD+ from NMN, which overloads the mitochondria
    - It cleaves caspase-3, which then cuts the cytoskeleton of the axon
    - It cleaves NAD+, so the axon runs short of NAD+ and then of ATP
    - It phosphorylates JNK, which enters the nucleus to stop transcription
    answer: 2
    rationale: The TIR domain of SARM1 is an enzyme that cleaves NAD+; as NAD+ falls, ATP falls, calcium rises and
      calpains break down the axon. Making NAD+ from NMN is what NMNAT does, and it protects axons rather than harming
      them. SARM1 is not a kinase, and a distal stump has no nucleus to receive a signal. Injury-induced degeneration
      proceeds when caspases are blocked, so cleavage of caspase-3 is not the committing step.
  - stem: In mice with spinal cord injury, the border of reactive astrocytes around the lesion is prevented from forming.
      Based on the lesson, what is the most likely result?
    choices:
    - Cut axons regrow across the lesion, because the border was the main wall
    - Cut axons still fail to regrow, and regrowth that was stimulated falls
    - Damage spreads less, because the border normally widens the injury
    - The lesion loses its proteoglycans, so axons regrow as in a nerve
    answer: 1
    rationale: In mice, preventing or removing the astrocyte border did not let cut axons regrow, and it reduced regrowth
      that had been stimulated, so the border is not simply a wall. The scar also limits the spread of damage, so
      losing it would not make damage spread less. Chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans at the lesion are made by astrocytes
      and by other cells such as NG2 glia, and it was digesting their sugar chains with chondroitinase ABC, not removing
      the border, that improved axon growth in rats. The aim is to change the scar, not remove it.
  - stem: A drug that blocks SARM1 is given right after a nerve is completely severed, and the distal stump stays
      intact for weeks. Why would this alone not restore function?
    choices:
    - The intact stump is cut off from its cell body and cannot rejoin it
    - Axons need SARM1 to make NAD+, so the stump soon runs out of energy
    - The preserved stump regrows back toward the cell body on its own
    - Peripheral axons lack SARM1, so the drug has no target in the nerve
    answer: 0
    rationale: However long it survives, a fully severed distal stump cannot rejoin its cell body on its own. Function
      returns only if the part still joined to the cell body regrows, reaches the right target and forms working connections.
      That is why the strongest case for SARM1 inhibitors is slower damage, such as some peripheral neuropathies,
      where failing axons are still connected. SARM1 destroys NAD+; NMNAT makes it. Regrowth comes from the side joined
      to the cell body, not from the stump. Peripheral axons have SARM1, and their stumps degenerate on schedule without
      a drug.

  - level: "highschool"

    text: >-
      A neuron's axon can be a metre long, and it depends on proteins made in the cell body and
      carried down it. When the axon is cut, the part beyond the cut breaks up after a delay, about a day and a half in mouse nerve.
      For a long time scientists thought it simply starved. Then they found mice in which a single
      gene change keeps the cut piece alive for weeks, which means the breakup is a program the
      axon runs. The program starts when a short-lived protein, NMNAT2, stops arriving. An enzyme
      called SARM1 then switches on and destroys NAD+, a molecule the axon needs to make energy,
      and the axon falls apart. Nerves in the body can regrow afterwards because their support
      cells clear the debris and guide the new axon. In the brain and spinal cord, proteins in
      myelin, growth-blocking molecules at the injury site and the neurons' own low drive to grow all hold regrowth
      back.

    text: >-
      SARM1 is a metabolic sensor built as an octamer. Its ARM domain binds NAD+ in the
      autoinhibited state, and NMN, the NMNAT2 substrate that rises when NMNAT2 is degraded,
      competes for an allosteric site; the ratio of NMN to NAD+ is the variable most models now

  - title: "Spheroids before the end"
    source: "Yong, Gamage, Cushman, Spano & Deppmann, Sci. Rep. 10:15472 (2020) [Deppmann lab]."
    text: >-
      Many cut axons swell into beads called spheroids before they fall apart. Working with cultured
      sympathetic neurons from newborn mice, the Deppmann laboratory reported that after injury
      these spheroids fill with calcium, grow by a Rho- and actin-dependent process, and rupture,
      releasing their contents before catastrophic degeneration. Wlds neurons, which have extra
      NMNAT activity, and neurons given added NAD+ suppressed spheroid formation. Sarm1-null and
      DR6-null neurons still formed spheroids that ruptured. The authors proposed that an early fall in NAD+ acts
      upstream of spheroids and that SARM1 and DR6 act after rupture, so rupture alone does not doom an axon.

**Cut a nerve in your arm and its axons can grow back. Injure your spinal cord and they mostly do not, which is why such an injury usually leaves a lasting loss of movement or feeling. Is the fault in the neurons, or in what surrounds them?**

The axon is the long process that carries a neuron's output; the cell body holds the nucleus and makes most of the cell's proteins.

## One cut, two pieces

A cut splits each axon in two. The **distal** piece, beyond the cut, is separated from its cell body. The **proximal** piece is still joined to it. The distal piece breaks down, in a limb nerve and in the spinal cord alike. The proximal piece may grow a new tip.

These responses overlap, and they run on different clocks.

| When | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| The first day and a half to two days (mouse sciatic nerve) | the distal piece looks normal (the latent phase) |
| Then | the distal piece falls to pieces |
| Days to weeks in a peripheral nerve; far longer, and incompletely, in the brain and spinal cord | the debris is cleared |
| After a delay, overlapping the clearing, for weeks to months or longer | the proximal piece may regrow, often at around a millimetre a day in humans (the rate depends on the nerve and the injury), or it stalls, as many central axons do |

So the lesson has two questions. Why does the distal piece die? And when can the proximal piece regrow?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** One cut, two fates. The piece cut off from its cell body destroys itself by a program it carries. The piece still attached tends to regrow where the neuron's drive to grow outweighs the resistance of the tissue around it.

**What the rule covers.** It is written for mature mammalian axons broken by a cut or a severe crush, with the cell body surviving. It is not about the death of whole neurons, or about developmental pruning in general (see [the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/)). The first half also holds in flies, and it runs in peripheral nerve and in the brain and spinal cord alike, though the timing differs. The second half is a rule of thumb: drive and resistance are not measured quantities. Fish regrow many central axons; in the rule's terms, the balance there favours growth.

## The cut-off piece destroys itself

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

In most mice, cut sciatic nerve axons fragment within two days. In one strain they stay intact for two weeks or more.

In a paper published in 1850, the physiologist Augustus Waller described cutting nerves that serve the frog's tongue and following the fibres under the microscope. Over the following days the fibres beyond the cut broke up, while the parts still joined to the brain or spinal cord stayed normal. Breakdown of the distal stump is still called Wallerian degeneration. Waller read it as a matter of nutrition: a fibre depends on a centre for its upkeep. That is true, and it suggests a passive death, the way a severed branch withers. Starve the stump and it fails.

### Design problem

After a nerve is cut, is the degeneration of the distal stump passive starvation, because it has lost its supply from the cell body, or an active program of self-destruction? Design a genetic test, and say what result would decide it.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Starvation and a program make different predictions. If the stump is starving, no change to a single gene of the neuron should save it for long, short of replacing what it lacks. A program has parts, and removing a part should stop it.

So make mouse lines that each lack one candidate gene, cut the sciatic nerve, and examine the distal stump at one, three, seven and fourteen days, by electron microscopy and by testing whether the nerve still conducts. If deleting a single gene keeps cut distal axons intact for weeks, while normal axons fragment in a day or two, the stump was not simply starving. It was executing a program, and the gene you deleted drives it. To show that the gene acts in the neuron and not in the cells around it, cut the axons of mutant neurons in culture, away from glia and immune cells, or delete the gene only in neurons.

</details>

### The slow mouse, and a knockout that copies it

The first such mouse came by accident. In 1989 a substrain of C57BL mice was described in which cut sciatic nerve axons degenerated very slowly. It became known as Wlds, for slow Wallerian degeneration. In 2001 Michael Coleman's group showed that the mutation fuses the start of a ubiquitination factor, Ube4b, to NMNAT1, an enzyme that makes NAD+ from nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN). Keep that enzyme in mind: it is the clue to the whole program. Adding the fusion gene to normal mice protected cut axons, and more of it protected them more.

Then came a loss-of-function result of the kind the design problem asks for. In 2012 Marc Freeman's laboratory and its collaborators reported that flies lacking a protein called dSarm keep severed axons for weeks, and that mice lacking its counterpart, Sarm1, keep cut axons intact for a long time, in the nerve and in culture. Deleting one ordinary gene gave protection like that of the odd fusion. The stump is not simply starving. Losing its supply sets off a program it carries, and the program dismantles it.

### The program: a switch that waits for the deliveries to stop

Start with the problem the axon has. In an adult human, the axon of a motor neuron that moves a toe runs roughly a metre from the spinal cord. An axon that long and about 10 micrometres across can hold hundreds of times the volume of its cell body (about 1,200 times for a body 50 micrometres across, about 150 times for one 100 micrometres across). Many of its essential proteins are made in the cell body and carried down, though axons make some locally. The far end depends on a steady flow of deliveries, and the program is how an axon responds when that flow stops.

The current model, built by several laboratories, has four steps.

1. **A delivery that must keep coming.** NMNAT2 is the NMNAT isoform that works in axons. Like every NMNAT, it joins NMN to the adenylyl (AMP) part of ATP to make NAD+, a coenzyme the axon needs to make ATP. In 2010 Jonathan Gilley and Coleman showed that NMNAT2 is the least stable NMNAT isoform and must be replenished constantly by transport from the cell body ([long-distance signaling](/lessons/long-distance-signaling-in-neurons/)). After a cut the deliveries stop, and NMNAT2 in the stump falls below a threshold before the axon breaks up. Blocking the proteasome, the cell's protein-disposal machine, slows its loss ([ubiquitin and proteolysis](/lessons/ubiquitin-and-proteolysis/)).
2. **A gauge that reads the gap.** As NMNAT2 disappears, its substrate, NMN, piles up and its product, NAD+, starts to fall. So a rising ratio of NMN to NAD+ signals that NMNAT2 is failing, though NMN supply and NAD+ use also move it. SARM1 senses that ratio. It waits in the axon as a ring of eight, held shut by its ARM domain, the sensor part, while NAD+ is bound there. In the leading model, NMN competes for the same site, and a rise in NMN relative to NAD+ switches SARM1 on.
3. **An executioner.** The TIR domain of SARM1, its enzyme part, cleaves NAD+. In 2015 the laboratories of Aaron DiAntonio and Jeffrey Milbrandt showed that forcing SARM1 TIR domains together is enough to destroy axonal NAD+ and trigger degeneration, and in 2017 that the TIR domain itself cleaves NAD+. Each NAD+ it destroys pushes the NMN-to-NAD+ ratio higher, so in this model the switch may feed itself, a proposed positive feedback. The slow drift becomes a crash: NAD+ falls fast, and ATP falls after it.
4. **Collapse.** As energy fails, calcium rises inside the axon. Calpains, proteases switched on by calcium, cut the cytoskeleton, and the axon fragments, often after swelling into beads called spheroids.

Think of a dead-man's switch, the kind that stops a train when the driver's hand leaves the handle. The cell body is the hand, and every delivery of NMNAT2 says "still attached." When the deliveries stop, the switch trips. The Wlds protein works because it is a longer-lived source of NMNAT activity that keeps the handle pressed after the cut. Deleting Sarm1 removes what the switch sets off. The analogy breaks in one place: a real switch trips at once, while this gauge drifts for hours, and how long the latent phase lasts in a living nerve depends on more than how fast NMNAT2 disappears.

This is not apoptosis, the death program of a whole cell (see [Bcl-2 and caspases](/lessons/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases/)). Injury-induced degeneration of the stump proceeds when caspases are blocked or missing, while the developmental [pruning](/lessons/axon-pruning/) of axons deprived of trophic factor needs them. The program also runs locally, in a stump with no nucleus. Stress kinases such as DLK and JNK take part after injury ([stress signals](/lessons/stress-signals/)), and where they fit in the sequence is still being worked out.

## Clearing the way

As the distal piece falls apart, its debris is cleared. Clearing helps a new axon use the path, though regrowth need not wait for it to finish. Here peripheral nerve and the central nervous system part company.

In a peripheral nerve, Schwann cells, the glia of peripheral nerve, become repair cells (a switch run by the transcription factor c-Jun). They do three jobs: they help break down myelin, they recruit macrophages from the blood to clear the rest, and they line up into tubes that guide regrowing axons back toward their targets. In the central nervous system, myelin debris is cleared far more slowly and incompletely.

## The attached piece: drive against resistance

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

The proximal stump forms a growth cone, the motile tip that leads a growing axon.

Nature has already run one comparison. A sensory neuron in a spinal (dorsal root) ganglion has one cell body, one nucleus and two branches. One branch runs out in a peripheral nerve to the skin or muscle. The other enters the spinal cord. Injure the peripheral branch and it regrows; injure the central branch inside the spinal cord and it mostly does not. Same neuron, same nucleus, different outcomes. The difference could lie in the tissue around each branch, or in what each injury tells the cell body to do. The conditioning lesion tests the second: cutting the peripheral branch before the central branch is injured raises the central branch's regrowth. So the neuron's state matters, and the tissue may too. To weigh each one, change mainly one side at a time.

### Change mainly the tissue

In 1981 Samuel David and Albert Aguayo used peripheral nerve, which holds Schwann cells and none of the glia of the central nervous system, as a test path. They grafted segments of it into adult rats as bridges, with one end in the medulla and the other in the spinal cord.

</details>

### Change mainly the neuron

Now leave the central surroundings in place and change mainly the neuron. In 2008 Zhigang He's laboratory deleted PTEN in mice from retinal ganglion cells, the central neurons whose axons form the optic nerve. PTEN is a brake on the mTOR pathway, the kinase pathway that drives protein synthesis and cell growth. Then they crushed the optic nerve, which is central tissue.

**Predict.** If the tissue alone decides, what happens to these axons at the crush? If the neuron's drive counts too?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Axons regenerated through the crushed optic nerve, with the central surroundings left in place. They came from a minority of the neurons, but far more than normal. So the surroundings are not the whole answer.

</details>

Put the two experiments side by side and the opening question has its answer. Two things hold central axons back: the tissue around them blocks regrowth, and adult central neurons turn their own growth program down. Neither is a gate that must open first, since each experiment got growth while changing mainly one side, though a graft also sends signals to the neurons and PTEN loss also helps them survive. It is a balance.

### What resists

Two features of the central lesion have been studied most as resistance: proteins in central myelin, and the scar that forms around the injury.

**Myelin inhibitors.** In 1988 Pico Caroni and Martin Schwab reported membrane proteins from central myelin that block neurite growth, and an antibody, IN-1, that neutralized them. The larger antigen was later cloned as Nogo-A. Nogo-A's Nogo-66 domain, myelin-associated glycoprotein (MAG) and oligodendrocyte myelin glycoprotein (OMgp) all bind the Nogo receptor, NgR, on the growth cone, which signals through partner proteins such as p75 ([p75](/lessons/trk-and-p75/)) to activate RhoA and collapse the growth cone.

**The glial scar.** Reactive astrocytes form a border around the lesion, and the lesion is rich in chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans, matrix proteins with long sugar chains, made by these astrocytes and by other cells such as NG2 glia. Digesting their sugar chains with the bacterial enzyme chondroitinase ABC improved axon growth and recovery in rats with spinal cord injury. The astrocyte border itself is not the wall it was once thought to be: in mice, preventing or removing it did not let cut axons regrow, and it reduced regrowth that had been stimulated with growth-promoting treatments. The scar also limits the spread of damage, so the aim is to change it, not remove it.

### Why each fix is only partial

**Predict.** Myelin inhibitors really do collapse growth cones. Yet mice lacking all three, Nogo, MAG and OMgp, show little or no extra regeneration of cut axons, though spared fibres may sprout more. How can a real inhibitor matter so little when it is removed?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

One way to picture it is as a threshold. Spread the neurons out by how strongly each drives growth, and let the tissue set a line; only neurons whose drive passes the line regrow. If most adult central neurons sit far below the line, removing one part of the resistance lowers it a little and moves few of them across. Myelin inhibitors are one barrier among several, and the others remain.

</details>

That is because the line sits in the thin tail of the bell curve, where each step toward the middle passes more neurons than the last. With a different spread, or with changes large enough to reach most neurons, the gains might only add, or add up to less. So the model is a reason to test combinations, not a promise that they work better. Nor do the gains compare the two kinds of change: here the resistance step is about a quarter of the spread of drive and the drive step about one spread.

## Regrowth is not recovery

Useful function returns only when a regrowing axon reaches the right target and forms working connections, and regrowth is slow. Recall that the axon to a toe runs about a metre.

Even in peripheral nerve, recovery is often incomplete: the repair state of Schwann cells does not last as long as slow regrowth over a long distance needs, and after severe injuries some axons never reinnervate (reconnect to) their targets.

## Two problems, two fixes

The two fates need different fixes. The first is to keep axons from degenerating. Small molecules that inhibit SARM1 have protected axons in cultured neurons and in animal models, and raising NAD+ with precursors such as nicotinamide riboside or NMN is being explored. The model contains a catch. Both precursors pass through NMN on the way to NAD+ (nicotinamide riboside is first converted to NMN), so in a cut stump a precursor can raise NAD+ only while NMNAT is still there to turn NMN into NAD+. Once the stump has lost NMNAT2, extra NMN could add to the signal that switches SARM1 on. Which precursor reaches the axon, and in what amount, matters. A fully severed stump cannot rejoin its cell body on its own however long it survives, so the strongest case for these drugs is slower damage, such as some peripheral neuropathies, where failing axons are still connected.

The second problem is regrowth after the axon is lost, and no single fix is enough. Blocking Nogo signalling, digesting the scar, raising intrinsic growth and supplying bridges each help in rodents, usually partly, and none yet restores function reliably in people.

For now, acute care for spinal cord injury centres on surgery to relieve pressure on the cord and stabilize the spine, supportive care, and rehabilitation. High-dose methylprednisolone, a steroid, was once widely given in the first hours, but many current guidelines no longer recommend it routinely, because its benefit was small and uncertain and it carries risks.

## Going deeper

### Who found the slow mouse

Waller's 1850 paper appeared in the *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society*, and the two nerves he cut were the glossopharyngeal and the hypoglossal. The slow mouse was described by Lunn, Perry, Brown, Rosen and Gordon, and crosses reported in 1990 traced it to a single dominant gene. In 1998 Michael Coleman, Hugh Perry and colleagues found that the gene's region on mouse chromosome 4 holds an 85-kilobase stretch of DNA in three tandem copies, the rearrangement that makes the fusion gene.

### Receptors for myelin inhibitors

NgR has no cytoplasmic domain of its own. Its partners are LINGO-1 with p75, or with TROY in neurons that lack p75. A second receptor, PirB, also binds Nogo-66, MAG and OMgp.

### Spheroids [Deppmann lab]

Working with sympathetic neurons from newborn mice, the Deppmann laboratory first studied spheroids in axons after withdrawal of nerve growth factor, a setting in which, unlike injury, caspases are needed. The axons stayed intact for about 18 hours (the latent phase) and then came apart over about 3 hours (the catastrophic phase). Toward the end of latency, calcium rose inside the axons, and calcium-rich spheroids grew and ruptured, releasing their contents. Medium from such axons hastened the catastrophic phase in others (Yong et al., *J Neurosci* 2019). After injury, the same group reported that spheroid growth depends on Rho and actin, that Wlds neurons and added NAD+ suppress it, and that Sarm1-null and DR6-null neurons still form spheroids that rupture (Yong et al., *Sci Rep* 2020). DR6 is a member of the TNF receptor family; the group had found that cut sciatic nerve axons in mice lacking DR6 were preserved up to four weeks after injury (Gamage et al., *Curr Biol* 2017). The authors propose that spheroid rupture marks the transition from latency to catastrophic degeneration, and they fit it to the model above by suggesting that an early fall in NAD+, not caused by SARM1, drives spheroid formation, and that SARM1 then drives NAD+ down further. This is one laboratory's culture work and a proposed ordering, and rupture alone does not commit an axon, since Sarm1-null axons rupture spheroids and still resist degeneration. Spheroids and calcium appear in both injury and developmental loss of axons, which raises the question of whether the two share mechanisms.
