**Cut a nerve in your arm and its axons can grow back. Injure your spinal cord and they mostly do not, which is why such an injury usually leaves a lasting loss of movement or feeling. Two things hold central axons back: the tissue around them blocks regrowth, and adult central neurons turn their own growth program down.**

The axon is the long process that carries a neuron's output; the cell body holds the nucleus and makes most of the cell's proteins. A cut splits each axon in two, and the two parts have different fates. The part beyond the cut, separated from its cell body, breaks down, in a limb nerve and in the spinal cord alike. The part still joined to the cell body can grow a new tip, and whether it does depends on the neuron and on what surrounds it. This lesson starts with the experiments that separated those two causes. Then it follows the part that breaks down, which destroys itself by a program of its own, and ends with what all this means for repair.

## Why a peripheral nerve regrows and the brain does not

In a peripheral nerve, losing the stump is not the end. The proximal stump, still attached to its cell body, forms a growth cone and regrows, often at around a millimetre a day in humans, depending on the nerve and the injury. Schwann cells, the glia of peripheral nerve, convert into repair cells under the control of the transcription factor c-Jun. They help break down myelin, recruit macrophages from the blood, and line up into tubes that guide regrowing axons back toward their targets. In the central nervous system, myelin debris is cleared far more slowly and incompletely.

Is the difference in the neurons or in their surroundings? In 1981 Samuel David and Albert Aguayo tested this with peripheral nerve, which holds Schwann cells and none of the glia of the central nervous system. They grafted segments of it into adult rats as bridges between the medulla and the spinal cord. Axons from central neurons at both levels grew about 30 millimetres along the grafts. Where they re-entered central tissue, they stopped within a short distance. Central neurons can regrow; their surroundings do much to stop them.

The surroundings are not the whole answer. Adult central neurons also turn their growth program down. In 2008 Zhigang He's laboratory reported that deleting PTEN, a brake on the mTOR pathway, from retinal ganglion cells in mice let many of their axons regenerate through a crushed optic nerve, with the central surroundings left in place. In sensory neurons, cutting the peripheral branch before the central branch is injured raises the central branch's regrowth, an effect called the conditioning lesion. Change the surroundings, or change the neuron, and some central axons regrow. Regrowth needs a neuron that is ready to grow and surroundings that let it.

## The part beyond the cut destroys itself

Whatever happens at the cell body's end, the part beyond the cut is lost. Cut the sciatic nerve of a mouse and the part of each axon beyond the cut looks normal for a day and a half to two days, then falls to pieces. In one strain of mouse the same cut axons stay intact for two weeks or more. A cut axon was long assumed to die the way a severed branch withers: cut off from its supply, it runs down. If one mutation can stop the process, something is running it.

In a paper published in 1850, the physiologist Augustus Waller described cutting nerves that serve the frog's tongue and following the fibres under the microscope. Over the following days the fibres beyond the cut broke up. The parts still joined to the brain or spinal cord stayed normal. Breakdown of the distal stump, the part cut off from its cell body, is still called Wallerian degeneration.

Waller read his result as a matter of nutrition: a fibre depends on a centre for its upkeep. That is true, and it suggests a passive death. Starve the stump and it fails.

### Design problem

After a nerve is cut, is the degeneration of the distal stump passive starvation, because it has lost its supply from the cell body, or an active program of self-destruction? Design a genetic test, and say what result would decide it.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Starvation and a program make different predictions. If the stump is starving, no change to a single gene of the neuron should save it for long, short of replacing what it lacks. A program has parts, and removing a part should stop it.

So make mouse lines that each lack one candidate gene, cut the sciatic nerve, and examine the distal stump at one, three, seven and fourteen days, by electron microscopy and by testing whether the nerve still conducts. If deleting a single gene keeps cut distal axons intact for weeks, while normal axons fragment in a day or two, the stump was not simply starving. It was executing a program, and the gene you deleted drives it. To show that the gene acts in the neuron and not in the cells around it, cut the axons of mutant neurons in culture, away from glia and immune cells, or delete the gene only in neurons.

</details>

### The slow mouse, and a knockout that copies it

The first such mouse came by accident. In 1989 a substrain of C57BL mice was described in which cut sciatic nerve axons degenerated very slowly. It became known as Wlds, for slow Wallerian degeneration, and a single dominant gene was responsible. In 2001 Michael Coleman's group showed that the mutation fuses the start of a ubiquitination factor, Ube4b, to NMNAT1, an enzyme that makes NAD+ from nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN). Adding that fusion gene to normal mice protected cut axons, and more of it protected them more.

Then came a loss-of-function result of the kind the design problem asks for. In 2012 Marc Freeman's laboratory and its collaborators reported that flies lacking a protein called dSarm keep severed axons for weeks, and that mice lacking its counterpart, Sarm1, keep cut axons intact for a long time, in the nerve and in culture. Deleting one ordinary gene gave protection like that of the odd fusion. The stump is not simply starving. Losing its supply sets off a program it carries, and the program dismantles it.

### The program: from latency to catastrophe

The current model, built by several laboratories, has four steps.

1. **A short-lived survival factor.** NMNAT2 is the NMNAT isoform that works in axons. In 2010 Jonathan Gilley and Coleman showed that it is the least stable NMNAT isoform and must be replenished constantly by transport from the cell body ([long-distance signaling](/lessons/long-distance-signaling-in-neurons/)). After a cut the supply stops, and NMNAT2 in the stump falls below a threshold before the axon breaks up. Blocking the proteasome slows its loss ([ubiquitin and proteolysis](/lessons/ubiquitin-and-proteolysis/)).
2. **A sensor.** With NMNAT2 gone, its substrate NMN builds up and NAD+ begins to fall. SARM1 waits in the axon, held shut by its ARM domain. In the leading model the ARM domain binds either NAD+ or NMN, and a rise in NMN relative to NAD+ switches SARM1 on.
3. **An executioner.** The TIR domain of SARM1 is an enzyme. In 2015 the laboratories of Aaron DiAntonio and Jeffrey Milbrandt showed that forcing SARM1 TIR domains together is enough to destroy axonal NAD+ and trigger degeneration, and in 2017 that the TIR domain itself cleaves NAD+. ATP falls after NAD+.
4. **Collapse.** As energy fails, calcium rises inside the axon. Calpains, proteases switched on by calcium, cut the cytoskeleton, and the axon fragments.

Picture a building whose sprinkler tank must be topped up every hour by truck. Cut the road and the tank runs low. A sensor reads the low level and, instead of sounding an alarm, starts the fire. The Wlds protein works because it is a longer-lived source of NMNAT activity that keeps the tank full after the trucks stop.

This is not apoptosis, the death program of a whole cell (see [Bcl-2 and caspases](/lessons/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases/)). Injury-induced degeneration of the stump proceeds when caspases are blocked or missing, while the developmental [pruning](/lessons/axon-pruning/) of axons deprived of trophic factor needs them. The program also runs locally, in a stump with no nucleus. Stress kinases such as DLK and JNK take part after injury ([stress signals](/lessons/stress-signals/)), and where they fit in the sequence is still being worked out.

Why are axons so vulnerable to injury compared to the cell body? Part of the answer is scale. In an adult human, the axon of a motor neuron that moves a toe runs roughly a metre from the spinal cord, and its far end depends on proteins made in the cell body and carried all that way. A survival factor as short-lived as NMNAT2 makes the far end only as healthy as the transport that reaches it.

Many axons swell into beads called spheroids before they break up. What spheroids do is one laboratory's research question, covered under Going deeper below.

## What blocks regrowth at a central lesion

Two features of the central lesion have been studied most as barriers: proteins in central myelin, and the scar that forms around the injury. The third limit, the neuron's own low growth, was described above.

**Myelin inhibitors.** In 1988 Pico Caroni and Martin Schwab reported membrane proteins from central myelin that block neurite growth, and an antibody, IN-1, that neutralized them. The larger antigen was later cloned as Nogo-A. Nogo-A's Nogo-66 domain, myelin-associated glycoprotein (MAG) and oligodendrocyte myelin glycoprotein (OMgp) all bind the Nogo receptor, NgR, which signals through partner proteins such as p75 ([p75](/lessons/trk-and-p75/)) to activate RhoA and collapse the growth cone. Mice lacking all three inhibitors show little or no extra regeneration of cut axons, though spared fibres may sprout more, so they are one barrier among several.

**The glial scar.** Reactive astrocytes form a border around the lesion, and the lesion is rich in chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans, made by these astrocytes and by other cells such as NG2 glia. Digesting their sugar chains with the bacterial enzyme chondroitinase ABC improved axon growth and recovery in rats with spinal cord injury. The astrocyte border itself is not the wall it was once thought to be: in mice, preventing or removing it did not let cut axons regrow, and it reduced regrowth that had been stimulated. The scar also limits the spread of damage, so the aim is to change it, not remove it.

## Fixing it

There are two problems here, and they need different fixes. The first is to keep axons from degenerating. Small molecules that inhibit SARM1 have protected axons in cultured neurons and in animal models, and raising NAD+ with precursors such as nicotinamide riboside or NMN is being explored. The model contains a catch: NMN is itself an activator of SARM1, so which precursor reaches the axon, and in what amount, matters. A fully severed stump cannot rejoin its cell body on its own however long it survives, so the strongest case for these drugs is slower damage, such as some peripheral neuropathies, where failing axons are still connected.

The second problem is regrowth after the axon is lost, and no single fix is enough. Blocking Nogo signalling, digesting the scar, raising intrinsic growth and supplying bridges each help in rodents, usually partly, and none yet restores function reliably in people. Regrowth is also not the same as recovery. Useful function returns only when a regrowing axon reaches the right target and forms working connections. Even in peripheral nerve, recovery is often incomplete: at around a millimetre a day, regrowth over a long distance is slow, the repair state of Schwann cells does not last as long as that slow regrowth needs, and after severe injuries some axons never reinnervate their targets.

Acute care for spinal cord injury today centres on surgery to relieve pressure on the cord and stabilize the spine, supportive care, and rehabilitation. High-dose methylprednisolone, a steroid, was once widely given in the first hours, but many current guidelines no longer recommend it routinely, because its benefit was small and uncertain and it carries risks.

## Going deeper

### Who found the slow mouse

Waller's 1850 paper appeared in the *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society*, and the two nerves he cut were the glossopharyngeal and the hypoglossal. The slow mouse was described in 1989 by Lunn, Perry, Brown, Rosen and Gordon, and crosses reported in 1990 showed that a single dominant gene was responsible. In 1998 Michael Coleman, Hugh Perry and colleagues found that the gene's region on mouse chromosome 4 holds an 85-kilobase stretch of DNA in three tandem copies. In 2001 Coleman's group showed that this rearrangement makes the Ube4b-NMNAT1 fusion gene.

### Receptors for myelin inhibitors

NgR has no cytoplasmic domain of its own. It signals with LINGO-1 and p75, or with TROY in neurons that lack p75, to activate RhoA. A second receptor, PirB, also binds Nogo-66, MAG and OMgp.

### Spheroids [Deppmann lab]

Before they break up, many axons swell into beads called spheroids. Working with sympathetic neurons from newborn mice, the Deppmann laboratory first studied axons after withdrawal of nerve growth factor, a setting in which, unlike injury, caspases are needed. The axons stayed intact for about 18 hours (the latent phase) and then came apart over about 3 hours (the catastrophic phase). Toward the end of latency, calcium rose inside the axons, and calcium-rich spheroids grew and ruptured, releasing their contents. Medium from such axons hastened the catastrophic phase in others (Yong et al., *J Neurosci* 2019). After injury, the same group reported that spheroid growth depends on Rho and actin, that Wlds neurons and added NAD+ suppress it, and that Sarm1-null and DR6-null neurons still form spheroids that rupture (Yong et al., *Sci Rep* 2020). DR6 is a member of the TNF receptor family; the group had found that cut sciatic nerve axons in mice lacking DR6 were preserved up to four weeks after injury (Gamage et al., *Curr Biol* 2017). The authors propose that spheroid rupture marks the transition from latency to catastrophic degeneration, and they fit it to the model above by suggesting that an early fall in NAD+, not caused by SARM1, drives spheroid formation, and that SARM1 then drives NAD+ down further. This is one laboratory's culture work and a proposed ordering, and rupture alone does not commit an axon, since Sarm1-null axons rupture spheroids and still resist degeneration. Spheroids and calcium appear in both injury and developmental loss of axons, which raises the question of whether the two share mechanisms.
