## Explain: why living things need nitrogen, and why air is not enough

<WordBank terms={['nitrogen cycle', 'protein', 'bacteria', 'root nodule', 'decomposer', 'fertilizer', 'biodiversity', 'algal bloom']} />

Every living thing is built partly from nitrogen. A **protein** is a large molecule that builds cells and does much of their work. Proteins contain nitrogen. DNA, the material that carries a living thing’s instructions, contains nitrogen too. So a plant cannot grow new leaves without it, and you cannot grow muscle without it.

Here is the puzzle. The air is about 78 percent nitrogen, but it is nitrogen gas. Most living things cannot use nitrogen in that form. Corn can stand in nitrogen-rich air all summer and still run short.

Nitrogen gas must first be changed into other forms that plants can use. Three things can change it:

- **Bacteria** are tiny living things, each made of one cell. Some kinds that live free in the soil change nitrogen gas into a usable form.
- A **root nodule** is a small bump on the root of a plant such as a bean, pea, soybean or peanut. Bacteria live inside it and change nitrogen gas into a usable form for the plant.
- Lightning changes a small amount of nitrogen gas, and factories that make **fertilizer** change a large amount. Fertilizer is material that farmers add to soil to supply nutrients such as nitrogen.

The soybean and its bacteria are partners. The plant sends sugar it made by photosynthesis to the nodules, where it is turned into food for the bacteria. The bacteria give the plant usable nitrogen. Corn has no root nodules, so it depends on usable nitrogen already in the soil.

## Explain: the nitrogen cycle

The **nitrogen cycle** is the movement of nitrogen from the air, through the soil, into living things and back to the air. At each stage, the nitrogen changes form. No new nitrogen is made, and none is used up.

1. Bacteria, lightning and factories change nitrogen gas from the air into usable forms.
2. Plant roots take up usable nitrogen from the soil.
3. Animals get nitrogen by eating plants or other animals.
4. A **decomposer** is a living thing, such as a fungus or a bacterium, that breaks down dead things and wastes. Decomposers return the nitrogen to the soil in a usable form.
5. Other bacteria in the soil change some usable nitrogen back into nitrogen gas, which goes back into the air.

This cycle is tied to the others. Water moves usable nitrogen through the soil and carries it into streams. Plants use sugar from photosynthesis to build proteins with nitrogen. You met the carbon and water cycles in [The Carbon and Water Cycles](/lessons/carbon-and-water-cycles/).

## Explain: too little or too much nitrogen

The amount of usable nitrogen changes what can live in a place. In many ecosystems, there is not enough of it, so plants grow less. Less plant growth means less food for animals.

Too much nitrogen causes problems too. Rain can wash extra nitrogen from farms, lawns and other places into streams that flow to the Chesapeake Bay. There it can feed an **algal bloom**, a fast growth of algae in the water. When the algae die, decomposers break them down and use up oxygen that fish and crabs need. You will see more in [Big Changes](/lessons/eutrophication-and-big-changes/).

<LessonFigure src="/figures/nitrogen-for-life/extra-nitrogen-to-the-bay.webp" width={1200} height={675} alt="AI-generated illustration: three numbered steps. 1, rain washes extra nitrogen from a fertilized field into a stream. 2, the Bay water near the surface is thick with green algae. 3, dead algae sink to the bottom, where decomposers break them down near a fish and a blue crab." caption="Too much nitrogen: rain carries it to the Bay, it feeds an algal bloom, and decomposers breaking down the dead algae use up oxygen that fish and crabs need." />

Extra nutrients can also change which plants grow. In an experiment in 40 grasslands on six continents, adding a mix of nutrients, including nitrogen, made plants grow more. But fewer kinds of plants grew, mostly because the thicker growth left less light near the ground for small plants. Where grazing animals ate enough plants to let light back in, more kinds of plants survived. **Biodiversity** is the variety of living things in an area. So extra nutrients, such as nitrogen, can lower biodiversity.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Too little nitrogen limits growth, and too much can feed algae or crowd out some plants, so nitrogen shapes which living things an ecosystem holds.</Support>

### Design problem

Do root bacteria help pea plants grow in soil with little usable nitrogen? Plan an investigation.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do pea plants grow more mass when their seeds are coated with root-nodule bacteria?
- **Independent variable:** seeds coated with a garden inoculant (a powder of root-nodule bacteria sold for peas and beans) or not coated.
- **Dependent variable:** dry mass of each plant above the soil, in grams, after five weeks. Also count the nodules on each plant’s roots.
- **Constants:** same pea variety, same pot size, same new, low-nitrogen mix such as sand or vermiculite, the same plant food with every nutrient except nitrogen for all pots, same water, light and temperature.
- **Control:** pots of uncoated seeds.
- **Repeated trials:** six pots of each kind. Find the mean mass.
- **Safety:** wear gloves when handling the powder and soil, wash your hands after, and do not eat any seeds or plants.

If coated plants have nodules and more mass, the data support the idea that the bacteria supplied nitrogen.

</details>
