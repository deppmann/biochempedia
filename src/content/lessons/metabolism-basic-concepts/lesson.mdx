summary: "Metabolism is an energy economy: a cell earns ATP by burning fuel (catabolism) and spends it to build (anabolism), move, and pump. One rule runs it. A reaction goes forward only when its own ΔG at the cell's concentrations is negative, and the cell meets that test for uphill steps in two ways: an enzyme fuses the step with a group transfer from ATP or another activated carrier, or the next step keeps the product scarce. From there come ATP's middle-of-the-ladder transfer potential, the activated carriers that fuel oxidation recharges, convergence on acetyl-CoA, and energy-charge regulation, and every later chapter becomes bookkeeping."

techniques:
  - name: "Phosphoryl-transfer potential (the ΔG°' of hydrolysis)"
    blurb: "Rank a phosphorylated compound by how much free energy its hydrolysis releases under standard conditions. Measured against a common reference, these values build the ladder that tells you which molecule can phosphorylate which: PEP and creatine phosphate sit above ATP and can charge ADP; glucose-6-phosphate sits below and cannot. The whole logic of substrate-level phosphorylation falls out of this one measured quantity."
  - name: "Energy charge"
    blurb: "A single number, ([ATP] + 0.5[ADP]) / ([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP]), that reports how 'charged' the cell's adenylate pool is, normally near 0.85–0.90. Because adenylate kinase interconverts the three nucleotides, a 10% drop in ATP can raise AMP several-fold in a well-charged cell, so energy charge is a sensitive, dimensionless readout of metabolic state — the experimental basis for understanding AMPK signaling."
  - name: "Isotopic and PET tracing of fuel use"
    blurb: "Label a fuel carbon and follow where it lands, or scan a patient with an 18F-glucose analog and watch which tissues take up sugar fastest. Tracing is how convergence on acetyl-CoA was established and how the Warburg effect is read clinically — many tumors light up on a PET scan because they take up glucose fast even when oxygen is plentiful (inflamed tissue can light up too)."

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "A reaction A → B has ΔG°' = +14 kJ/mol and, under standard conditions, will not proceed on its own. In the cell it is coupled to ATP hydrolysis (ΔG°' = −30.5 kJ/mol) in one enzyme-catalyzed reaction (for example by a shared intermediate or direct group transfer). What is the net ΔG°' of the coupled process, and will it now proceed?"
    choices:
      - "+44.5 kJ/mol; it still will not proceed"
      - "−16.5 kJ/mol; it will now proceed"
      - "−30.5 kJ/mol; it proceeds because ATP overrides A → B"
      - "+14 kJ/mol; coupling does not change ΔG°'"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "Coupling works because ΔG°' values are additive when the reactions are joined in one enzyme-catalyzed reaction: +14 + (−30.5) = −16.5 kJ/mol, which is negative, so under standard conditions the coupled process is now spontaneous. (In a real cell the actual ΔG also depends on concentrations.) The +44.5 value comes from subtracting instead of adding and is wrong. ATP does not 'override' the other reaction; the two free energies simply sum. And coupling absolutely does change the net ΔG°' — that is the entire mechanism."
  - stem: "Which statement correctly explains why ATP hydrolysis is exergonic (ΔG°' ≈ −30.5 kJ/mol)?"
    choices:
      - "The terminal phosphate bond physically stores energy that is released when it breaks"
      - "ATP is the most reactive phosphate in the cell and hydrolyzes the fastest"
      - "The bond between adenine and ribose is a high-energy bond"
      - "The products (ADP + Pi) are more stable than ATP due to charge relief, resonance, entropy, and hydration"
      - "Hydrolysis consumes water, which is thermodynamically favorable on its own"
    answer: 3
    rationale: "The energy is not stored in a special bond. Hydrolysis is favorable because the products ADP and Pi are more stable than ATP — relief of electrostatic repulsion, better resonance stabilization of released phosphate, increased entropy, and improved hydration. ATP is actually kinetically stable (slow to hydrolyze without an enzyme), so it is not the fastest-reacting phosphate. The adenine–ribose bond is irrelevant to the energetics of phosphoryl transfer, and water consumption alone does not account for the free-energy change."
  - stem: "On the phosphoryl-transfer ladder, which compound can directly phosphorylate ADP to form ATP?"
    choices:
      - "Glucose-6-phosphate"
      - "AMP"
      - "Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)"
      - "ATP itself"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "Under standard conditions, a phosphoryl transfer is spontaneous only when it runs downhill — from a compound of higher transfer potential to one of lower. PEP sits above ATP on the ladder (more negative hydrolysis ΔG°'), so it can hand a phosphate down to ADP and make ATP. Glucose-6-phosphate and AMP both sit below ATP and cannot charge ADP. ATP phosphorylating ADP to make ATP is meaningless (no net change in transfer potential)."
  - stem: "A cell's adenylate pool is measured as [ATP] = 7.5, [ADP] = 2, [AMP] = 0.5 (relative units). What is its energy charge, and what response would you expect?"
    choices:
      - "≈ 0.85; the cell favors building (anabolism) and AMPK is largely off"
      - "≈ 0.50; the cell is energy-starved and triggers strong AMPK catabolism"
      - "≈ 0.85; AMPK is fully active and shuts down all biosynthesis"
      - "≈ 0.95; the cell halts catabolism entirely"
    answer: 0
    rationale: "Energy charge = ([ATP] + 0.5[ADP]) / ([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP]) = (7.5 + 1) / 10 = 0.85. That is squarely in the normal resting range (~0.85–0.90), where the cell is well-charged, AMPK is largely inactive, and anabolic (building) pathways are favored. The 0.50 value would require far more AMP/ADP. A charge of 0.85 does not represent starvation, and 0.95 with a total catabolic halt does not match these concentrations."

  - stem: "An enzyme couples the synthesis of compound X (X-formation alone: ΔG°' = +22 kJ/mol) to the hydrolysis of ATP to ADP + Pi (ΔG°' = −30.5 kJ/mol). A researcher engineers a mutant enzyme that still catalyzes X-formation and still hydrolyzes ATP, but at two physically separate active sites with no shared intermediate and no conformational link between them. Compared with the wild-type enzyme, the mutant will most likely:"
    choices:
      - "Synthesize X faster, because ATP hydrolysis now proceeds unhindered"
      - "Synthesize X at the same rate, since the summed ΔG°' is unchanged at −8.5 kJ/mol"
      - "Fail to drive X-formation, because the two reactions are no longer coupled"
      - "Reverse the reaction and consume X to make ATP"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "Free-energy additivity only delivers a favorable net ΔG when the two reactions are mechanistically linked: a group passed within one enzyme (directly or via a covalent intermediate), a real intermediate handed from one step to the next, or, in pumps and motors, a conformational link. If ATP hydrolysis and X-formation happen at separate sites with no shared intermediate, the −30.5 kJ/mol of ATP hydrolysis is simply dissipated as heat and cannot pay for the +22 kJ/mol X-formation, which remains endergonic at the same concentrations, so it will not proceed appreciably. The arithmetic sum (−8.5) is irrelevant without the coupling mechanism, so the 'same rate' choice is wrong. ATP hydrolysis being 'unhindered' does not help X-formation, and there is no basis for net reversal to make ATP."

  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: "The chapter's value is its set of unifying thermodynamic and regulatory principles. Free-energy additivity of mechanistically joined reactions is the formal basis of metabolic coupling; chemical coupling works by enzyme-enforced linkage of the uphill step to a downhill group transfer (directly or through a covalent intermediate) or by pulling the product away, so that the summed ΔG is negative, while pumps, motors and ATP synthase couple through protein conformational change and electrochemical gradients. The phosphoryl-transfer potential series is itself the design rationale for adenylate as the central currency: occupying an intermediate rank lets ATP be both regenerated by super-equilibrium donors (PEP, 1,3-BPG, phosphocreatine) and serve as a kinetically competent donor to sub-ATP acceptors, while kinetic stability (high activation barrier to spontaneous hydrolysis) makes covalent phosphorylation a clean, enzyme-gated switch. **Once glucose is inside a cell, the first step attaches a phosphate to it, and written with free phosphate, that attachment runs backward.**

Hexokinase turns glucose into glucose-6-phosphate, which traps the sugar in the cell and starts glycolysis. Yet at a cell's phosphate level, glucose and free phosphate left to reach equilibrium would put a phosphate on only about one glucose in 50,000 (worked out below). How the cell makes this uphill step anyway, without breaking thermodynamics, is the design idea behind all of metabolism.

The frame is an energy economy. A cell earns energy by oxidizing fuel (**catabolism**) and spends it on three jobs: mechanical work (a muscle contracting, a cell crawling), active transport (the Na⁺/K⁺ pump fighting a gradient) and biosynthesis (building proteins, lipids and nucleic acids), the building being **anabolism**. Plants and other phototrophs capture sunlight; we are chemotrophs and oxidize carbon fuels. Energy is conserved; it is transformed from electrons in food's carbons into a currency the cell can spend.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A reaction runs forward only when its own ΔG, at the concentrations in the cell, is negative. The cell runs uphill chemistry in two ways. It fuses the uphill step with a downhill group transfer from ATP (or another activated carrier) into a single enzyme-catalyzed reaction, so the ΔG°' values add. Or the next step keeps the product scarce, so the uphill step's own ΔG turns negative.

The rule says whether a reaction *can* run, not how fast; enzymes decide that, and regulation (the last section) decides which reactions that can run actually do.

## The rule: ΔG, not ΔG°'

Written the simplest way, attaching free phosphate to glucose runs uphill:

> glucose + Pi → glucose-6-phosphate + H₂O    ΔG°' = +13.8 kJ/mol

ΔG°' is the free-energy change under biochemists' standard conditions: everything at 1 M, pH 7, 25 °C. Positive means that, from those conditions, the reaction runs backward. A cell is never at standard conditions; what decides direction there is ΔG, at the concentrations actually present.

## Coupling, the first way: one enzyme, one reaction

Hexokinase never uses free phosphate. It takes ATP's end phosphoryl group, the γ-phosphoryl, and hands it straight to glucose in one active site:

> glucose + ATP → glucose-6-phosphate + ADP

Free energy depends only on where a reaction starts and ends, so add two bookkeeping reactions that together have the same start and end:

| Step | ΔG°' (kJ/mol) |
| --- | --- |
| glucose + Pi → glucose-6-phosphate + H₂O | +13.8 |
| ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi | −30.5 |
| **glucose + ATP → glucose-6-phosphate + ADP** | **−16.7** |

Pi and water appear on both sides and cancel. The cancellation is legitimate because the two halves are one chemical reaction: the phosphoryl group that leaves ATP is the one glucose receives, and the free phosphate in the table is never made. In hexokinase the group passes directly. Other enzymes pass it through a short-lived covalent intermediate, which textbooks call a **shared (common) intermediate**: glutamine synthetase first puts ATP's phosphoryl group on glutamate, then ammonia displaces it.

The payoff: the coupled K'eq is e^(16.7 / 2.48), about 840. Adding −30.5 kJ/mol multiplied the equilibrium constant by e^(30.5 / 2.48), about 2 × 10⁵. And because Q is now [G6P][ADP] / ([glucose][ATP]), the cell's high ATP-to-ADP ratio helps too. With ATP ten times ADP, equilibrium sits at 840 × 10, about 8,400 glucose-6-phosphates per glucose, up from one per 50,000.

Picture two wagons on a hill: a light one that has to go up and a heavy one that wants to roll down. Tie them together and the heavy one pulls both. In hexokinase, the enzyme is the rope.

**Predict.** A mutant enzyme hydrolyzes ATP at one site, and at a second site, with no link to the first, makes glucose-6-phosphate from glucose and free phosphate. The ΔG°' values still add to −16.7 kJ/mol. Does glucose-6-phosphate form?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. ATP is hydrolyzed and its free energy leaves as heat. The glucose reaction still faces +13.8 kJ/mol with the same Q, so it still runs backward. Free energies add only when the chemistry is joined.

</details>

## Coupling, the second way: keep the product scarce

Pathways use this constantly. In the film's example, A → B + C has ΔG°' = +21 kJ/mol, and the next enzyme takes B away, B → D, with ΔG°' = −34 kJ/mol. Here the shared intermediate is B itself, a real molecule passed between enzymes. The sum, −13 kJ/mol, is the pathway's overall bill. What lets the first step itself run is that the second keeps [B] low. With A and C at 1 mM, Q is just [B], so the first step runs forward while +21 + 2.48 ln[B] stays below zero: B below about 0.2 mM. In a pathway carrying flux, **every step's own ΔG must be negative**, even if only slightly.

For chemistry that passes a group between molecules, those two are all coupling is. Pumps, motors and ATP synthase couple through changes of protein shape and through gradients, though the ΔG test still applies to the whole coupled process.

## Why ATP can pay

ATP is adenine plus ribose plus three phosphates, named α, β and γ from the ribose outward; most kinases transfer the γ-phosphoryl. Hydrolysis of that terminal phosphate has ΔG°' ≈ −30.5 kJ/mol. Three properties make ATP good at paying.

**The products are more stable; there is no high-energy bond.** ADP and Pi carry less crowded negative charge (less electrostatic repulsion), the released phosphate gains resonance stabilization, two molecules have more entropy than one, and the products are better hydrated than ATP was. Textbooks add a fifth factor: ionization of the products at pH 7 releases H⁺. When you hear "spending a bond" (the film uses this shorthand), read it as "transferring the γ-phosphoryl group to products that are more stable."

**The cell holds ATP far from equilibrium.** Q for hydrolysis is tiny, so the actual ΔG is well below the standard −30.5. In a red blood cell at 37 °C, with about 2.25 mM ATP, 0.25 mM ADP and 1.65 mM Pi:

> ΔG = −30.5 + 2.58 × ln(0.00025 × 0.00165 / 0.00225) ≈ −30.5 − 22.2 ≈ −52.7 kJ/mol

Across cell types it runs from about −45 to −65 kJ/mol, so about −50 is a fair working number.

**ATP is kinetically stable.** ATP, and the phosphate esters it puts on proteins, are thermodynamically unstable but kinetically stable: they will not hydrolyze at any useful rate without an enzyme. That makes phosphorylation an ideal on/off switch: a kinase adds a phosphate to flip a protein's state (on for some proteins, off for others), a phosphatase removes it to flip it back, and nothing happens in between.

## The phosphoryl-transfer ladder

The **phosphoryl-transfer ladder** ranks phosphorylated compounds by how much free energy is released when each transfers its phosphoryl group to water (its ΔG°' of hydrolysis). It ranks energy released, not speed.

| --- | --- |
| PEP | −31.4 |

Under standard conditions, transfers run downhill, from higher on the ladder to lower. ATP sits in the middle, and that is what makes it good currency: compounds above it recharge ADP, and ATP phosphorylates compounds below, like glucose. ## Earning: how fuels recharge the carriers

The cell recharges ATP by oxidizing fuel, in three stages. Digestion breaks large molecules into small ones: proteins to amino acids, starch to sugars, fats to fatty acids. Those small molecules funnel into a handful of key intermediates, above all **acetyl-CoA**. Then acetyl-CoA is oxidized completely, and that oxidation drives ATP synthesis.

Whether you eat a steak, a potato or olive oil, most of the carbon reaches your mitochondria as acetyl-CoA; some amino acids join at pyruvate or the citric acid cycle itself. Different highways, one city center. So one central pathway, the citric acid cycle (its own lesson later), serves every fuel you eat. The cycle is amphibolic: it oxidizes fuel and also supplies starting materials for biosynthesis.

Burning a fuel means stripping electrons from its carbons and handing them, in the end, to oxygen. A carbon loaded with hydrogens (the long chain of a fatty acid) has many electrons to give; a carbon already bonded to oxygen (every carbon in glucose) has fewer; CO₂ has none left. That is the main reason fat yields about 9 kcal per gram and carbohydrate about 4, and one reason fat is the body's main long-term fuel store. The other is that fat is stored nearly water-free, while glycogen carries about twice its weight in water, which matters more for storage density than 9 versus 4. Fuel's electrons, like ATP's phosphoryl groups, ride on a small set of **activated carriers**, the cell's delivery trucks. Each is ATP's idea again: a molecule that holds one kind of group (phosphoryl, electrons, acyl) at high transfer potential and hands it over when an enzyme asks. They are kinetically stable, few in number, and mostly built from vitamins, which is why a vitamin deficiency hits metabolism hard.

| Carrier | What it carries | Where it is spent | Built from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ATP | phosphoryl groups | kinases, pumps, motors, biosynthesis | (not a vitamin) |
| NADH | electrons from carbon oxidations | the electron transport chain, roughly 2.5 ATP each | niacin (B3) |
| FADH₂ | electrons | the electron transport chain, entering lower (at ubiquinone), about 1.5 ATP | riboflavin (B2) |
| NADPH | electrons for building | reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defense | niacin (B3) |
| Coenzyme A (as acyl-CoA) | acyl groups, through a thioester | acyl transfers, such as acetyl into the citric acid cycle | pantothenate (B5) |

NADPH shares NADH's chemistry. CoA's thioester has high transfer potential because a thioester is less resonance-stabilized than an oxygen ester, so its hydrolysis or transfer releases more free energy. A broken carrier is a disease: inherited loss of pantothenate kinase 2 (PANK2), the first enzyme in making CoA, causes PKAN, with brain iron accumulation and neurodegeneration.

## Regulation: what can run versus what does

Regulation picks which possible reactions run, in three ways on three timescales. The cell changes the **amount** of an enzyme by transcription (hours to days). It changes the **activity** of existing enzymes by allostery and reversible covalent modification (seconds to minutes), which is how hormones like insulin coordinate the whole body fast. And it changes **substrate accessibility** by compartmentation, keeping fatty-acid synthesis in the cytosol and fatty-acid oxidation in the mitochondrion so the two do not run at once.

Most steps of a pathway sit near equilibrium and run either way on the same enzyme: seven of glycolysis's ten steps run backward in gluconeogenesis, which makes glucose. The few steps far from equilibrium cannot simply be reversed, so each direction gets its own, separately regulated enzyme there, and those steps are where most regulation acts.

A key gauge is **energy charge**, the cell's battery meter, normally held near 0.85–0.90:

> energy charge = ([ATP] + 0.5[ADP]) / ([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP])

Adenylate kinase (2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP) holds the three nucleotides near equilibrium, so [AMP] = K × [ADP]² / [ATP]. Because ADP is squared, a small rise in ADP gives a bigger rise in AMP.

It rises about 3.4-fold, from 0.10 to about 0.34, while energy charge falls only from about 0.95 to 0.89. When regulation fails, you get disease: pathophysiology is biochemistry in a clinical context.

- **Diabetes** is a failure of insulin-based regulation: too little insulin (type 1), or tissues that respond poorly to insulin plus beta cells that cannot keep up (type 2).
- **The Warburg effect.** Many cancer cells run aerobic glycolysis, taking up glucose fast and making lactate even with oxygen present. A leading idea is that this flux feeds biosynthesis rather than maximal ATP yield, though the reasons are still debated. The high glucose uptake is why many tumors light up on an FDG-PET scan (inflamed tissue can too).
- **Mitochondrial disease** is oxidative-phosphorylation failure, often presenting as lactic acidosis.

## Going deeper

The main line above does not depend on these details.

### Six reaction types

Beneath the thousands of named reactions, metabolism uses only about six reaction types: redox, ligation (ATP-driven joining), isomerization, group transfer, hydrolysis and lyase reactions. Three have appeared here: group transfer (hexokinase), ligation (glutamine synthetase joining ammonia to glutamate) and hydrolysis (ATP).

### Where FADH₂'s electrons enter

FADH₂'s working part is the isoalloxazine ring. Its electrons reach ubiquinone via Complex II from succinate, or via ETF from fatty acids.

### More vitamin diseases

Niacin deficiency (NADH, NADPH) is pellagra. Thiamine forms the carrier TPP (thiamine pyrophosphate), and thiamine deficiency causes beriberi and Wernicke encephalopathy. Riboflavin deficiency (FADH₂) is ariboflavinosis. Each disease is a missing or broken truck.

### Reciprocal control of fat

Compartmentation works with reciprocal control: malonyl-CoA, made during fatty-acid synthesis, blocks fatty-acid entry into the mitochondrion, so synthesis and oxidation cannot run together as a futile cycle.

### The muscle's energy timeline

On its own, stored ATP would last only seconds of hard effort, and the creatine phosphate buffer only several seconds more. Glycolysis runs from the first seconds and soon carries more of the load, and aerobic respiration, also on from the start, carries the long haul. When muscle is injured, creatine kinase leaks into the blood, which is why elevated serum CK is a clinical marker of muscle damage.

### Creatine kinase in numbers

At 37 °C, K' for creatine phosphate + ADP → creatine + ATP is about 132 (measured values shift with pH and free Mg²⁺). With resting-muscle values of about 8 mM ATP, 13 mM creatine, 25 mM creatine phosphate and 20 µM free ADP, Q = (13 × 8) / (25 × 0.020) ≈ 208. Q is above K', so ΔG = RT ln(Q/K') ≈ +1.2 kJ/mol for the direction that makes ATP. Read the sign loosely: free ADP in muscle is usually calculated by assuming creatine kinase is at equilibrium, so the numbers show only that ΔG is near zero and small swings in ADP set the direction.

### ATP's actual ΔG in other cells

The −45 to −65 kJ/mol range above comes from concentrations like these. Beside the red cell's −52.7 kJ/mol (all at 37 °C), liver-like concentrations (about 3.4 mM ATP, 1.3 mM ADP, 4.8 mM Pi) give about −47, and resting muscle, counting only free ADP (about 8 mM ATP, 20 µM ADP, 3 mM Pi), about −61.

### The film's numbers

Both of the film's numerical examples (the +21/−34 pair above, then a K rising from about 1/1000 to about 267) are separate textbook reactions with rounded, standard-state values. In a cell, ATP pushes harder.

### Where the "high-energy bond" came from

Fritz Lipmann introduced the "energy-rich phosphate bond" and the ~P squiggle. His "group potential" put the emphasis on the free energy of transferring the group, the idea the ladder is built on. The explanation in terms of product stability came largely from later physical-chemical work.
