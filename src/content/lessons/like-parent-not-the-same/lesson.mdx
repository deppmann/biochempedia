## The big idea

Open a pea pod and the peas look like a row of twins. Line them up next to a ruler, though, and they are not the same. One is bigger. One is paler. One is tiny and flat.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/like-parent-not-the-same/like-parent-peas-in-a-row.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a paper plate with an opened pea pod and a row of green peas in pod order next to a plain ruler; one pea is bigger, one paler and one tiny and flat. A small inset shows an apple cut across the middle with a star of seed pockets and five seeds lined up beside it." caption="Peas from one pod, lined up in the order they sat. They look like twins until you set them next to a ruler. An apple cut across its middle shows a star of seed pockets." />

All the peas in a pod came from the same parent plant. They are its **offspring**, the young that a living thing makes. Offspring are like their parents, and like each other, but not identical. Seeds from one apple or one pepper show the same thing. Leaves are parts of a plant, not offspring, but leaves from one plant are never exactly alike either.

Animals work the same way. Puppies in one litter share many features, yet each one is a little different. Some babies look like small versions of their parents from the start. Others, like caterpillars and tadpoles, look very different at first and change as they grow.

Virginia's kindergarten standard asks children to notice that offspring are similar but not identical. Grade 2 adds the idea that some animals go through stages. This activity keeps to plants and animals on purpose. It does not compare children with their own parents.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time. You do all the cutting with the knife, and you put the knife away, out of reach, as soon as you finish.
- Peas and seeds can be swallowed, breathed in or pushed into a nose or ear. Keep them away from babies, toddlers and children under 4, and watch that older children do not hand them to younger ones. Count the peas and seeds and clear the table when you finish.
- Do not chew or eat apple seeds. Swallowing the few in a core is not harmful, but chewed seeds release a natural toxin.
- Use a sweet bell pepper, not a hot pepper. Hot pepper juice and seeds can burn eyes and skin.
- Use peas from the store, not pods from the garden flower called sweet pea, which are poisonous. Pick leaves with an adult, only from a tree or shrub you can name, and never from poison ivy or from a dieffenbachia or philodendron, whose sap irritates skin and eyes. If skin touches poison ivy, rinse with soap and water soon and wash the clothes. If a child is choking or struggling to breathe, call 911. If a child eats any part of a plant, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222.
- Wash the produce, then everyone's hands before and after. The peas, seeds and fruit are science samples, not snacks.
- Peas, apples and peppers are food, so check first for any pea, peanut, apple or pepper allergy and for your school’s rules on food in class, and have a child with such an allergy look but not handle. If children want a snack, give them separate clean fruit, not the samples, and check allergies first.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Predict.** Hold up a closed pea pod. Ask, "What will the peas inside look like? Will they all be the same?" Write down your child's guess.
2. **Open the pods.** Let your child split each pod with their thumbs. Line up the peas from one pod on a paper plate, in the order they sat in the pod. Count them, then count the peas in another pod. Pods from the same bag often hold different numbers.
3. **Measure.** Measure each pea across with a ruler. Without a ruler, wrap string around each pea, mark the length and compare the marks; that length is about three times the pea's width. Most garden peas are about 1 cm across or a little smaller. Ask your child to find the biggest, the smallest, and any that are a different shade or shape.
4. **Cut the apple and pepper.** You cut the apple across its middle to show a star of seed pockets. Cut the pepper in half from top to bottom. Have your child pick out 5 seeds from each and line them up. Ask, "How are they the same? How are they different?"
5. **Compare leaves from one plant.** Lay out 5 or 6 leaves from the same plant. Look at shape, size, color, edges and veins. Measure the longest and the shortest. They all clearly come from one kind of plant, yet no two match exactly.
6. **Look at animal families.** Find a picture of a litter of puppies or kittens with their mother. Ask, "How do the babies look like the mother? How are they different from her and from each other?" Look for coat color, spots, ear shape and size. Keep the talk to the animals. Do not ask a child which parent they look like: many children are adopted, fostered, donor-conceived or live in blended families, and the question can hurt.
7. **Babies that look different.** Show a caterpillar and a butterfly, or a tadpole and a frog. Ask, "Could this baby belong to that grown-up?" Then sort your animal pictures into two groups: babies that look like their parents, and babies that look different at first.

With a class, give each table of four one pod, one apple half and one set of leaves, and share the animal pictures.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder if all the peas in this pod are the same size. Which one do you think is biggest?"
- "What do you think we will find if we open another pod? The same number of peas?"
- "These seeds all came from one apple. Do you think they will look exactly alike?"
- "I wonder what a kitten from this mother cat will look like. What will be the same?"
- "What do you think a tadpole turns into? How could we find out?"
- "If we planted two peas from this pod, do you think the plants would be twins?"

## Why it works

A **trait** is a feature such as color, shape or size. Parents pass many traits on to their offspring, which is why peas make peas and cats make cats. In most plants and animals, each offspring starts from two parents' contributions, and each one gets a different mix. That is why brothers and sisters differ.

Pea plants mostly pollinate themselves, so the peas in a pod share nearly the same inheritance. They still differ. Some differences likely come from how each pea grew, such as how much food and water reached it. Seeing a difference does not tell you whether it was inherited.

Apples show the opposite case. Most apple trees need pollen from a different apple variety. A seed from a Red Delicious apple does not grow into a Red Delicious tree, so growers copy favorite trees by grafting. Animals such as butterflies and frogs go through **metamorphosis**, a big change in body form as they grow.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use one pea pod and the animal pictures only. Skip the ruler: have your child line peas up from biggest to smallest and say what is the same and what is different. Ask for two "same" and two "different" ideas each time. Your child can draw instead of write. Kindergartners do not need to name the stages of a life cycle. Just notice that some babies look different from their parents.

### Ages 8 to 10
Measure every pea from three pods in millimeters and record them in a table. Make a bar graph of how many peas fall in each size group. You cut two more apples of the same kind, and your child compares the seed counts. Then have your child build a simple key: write yes-or-no questions that split five leaves from the same plant into smaller groups until each leaf is on its own. Talk about which differences might be inherited and which might come from growing conditions.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If the pods are flat with tiny peas, try sugar snap peas or a different store. Snow peas hold very small peas, so look closely or use a magnifying glass. If every pea looks the same, measure more carefully or compare color under bright light. If your child steers toward their own family, bring the talk gently back to the peas and the kittens.
