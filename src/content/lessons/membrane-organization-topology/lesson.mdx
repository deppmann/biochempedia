      By the early 1970s several kinds of evidence about membranes were in hand: the bilayer from
      physical studies of lipids, signs that many membrane proteins are buried in it rather than
      spread over it, and the Frye and Edidin result that surface proteins can drift. In 1972 S.

      experiment; the evidence for fluidity came from several laboratories. Its prediction of fixed
      sidedness with lateral freedom is the starting point for both halves of this lesson.

techniques:
  - name: "Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)"
    blurb: >-
      A laser bleaches the fluorescent label in a small spot of membrane, and the spot's brightness
      is followed as unbleached molecules move in. After correction for background and for bleaching
      during imaging, the plateau gives the mobile fraction and, with a stated model of the bleach
      and of diffusion, the half-time gives an approximate diffusion coefficient.
  - name: "Protease protection of sealed microsomes"
    blurb: >-
      Sealed ER vesicles (microsomes) carrying the protein are treated with a protease, with and
      without detergent. Parts in the lumen survive unless the detergent opens the vesicles, so the
      epitope that survives tells you which side each end faces.
  - name: "Glycosylation mapping with endoglycosidase H"
    blurb: >-
      An N-X-S/T site becomes glycosylated only if it reaches the ER lumen, where the
      oligosaccharyl transferase works. A shift on a gel that endoglycosidase H removes identifies the
      added sugar and so marks that segment as lumenal.
  - name: "Leaflet lipidomics by enzyme digestion"
    blurb: >-
      Intact cells are treated with phospholipases that reach only the outer leaflet, and mass
      spectrometry compares the digested and protected lipids. In human erythrocytes this gave the
      unsaturation of each leaflet separately.

    rationale: >-
      Flippases, such as ATP11C working with CDC50A in human cells, use ATP to move
      aminophospholipids to the cytoplasmic leaflet against their gradient. Scramblases, such as

      collapses asymmetry. Swapping the two descriptions is the commonest mix-up. Saying both use ATP,
      one sideways and one across, is wrong twice: flippases do use ATP but scramblases do not, and

      ATP and splitting them by lipid type invents a distinction neither enzyme has.

**A cell adds a sugar to a protein deep inside its endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and later that same sugar sits on the outside of the cell, though no vesicle ever carried it across a membrane.**

The answer is a rule about the two faces of every membrane, which proteins and lipids obey in different ways.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Moving a protein or a phospholipid across a bilayer at a useful speed takes a dedicated machine. For most membrane and secreted proteins the cell uses that machine once, normally at the ER: as the chain is made, the translocon (the channel it threads through) decides which parts go into the lumen, the space inside the organelle. After that, membrane traffic carries it between compartments, and budding and fusion move nothing across a bilayer. So the face that met the cytosol always meets the cytosol, and the lumen of every compartment on the secretory and endocytic routes counts as "outside the cell." Phospholipids the cell moves across all the time, on purpose: flippases pump some inward and scramblases let them mix, so the lipid difference between the faces is *maintained*, not frozen. Sliding sideways within a leaflet needs no machine, and a photobleaching experiment measures it.

The rule covers the ER and nuclear envelope, Golgi, secretory vesicles, plasma membrane, endosomes and lysosomes, not mitochondria, chloroplasts or peroxisomes. The few things that cross a membrane later on these routes each have their own machine (Going deeper, "Exceptions, and why they fit").

## Two faces of every membrane

The bilayer is two sheets of lipid, each sheet called a **leaflet**, with tails meeting in an oily core. Its chemistry and physics are covered in [lipids](/lessons/lipids/) and [membrane structure and function](/lessons/membrane-structure-function/). Here you need one consequence of that core: a polar head group, or a polar stretch of protein, crosses it only rarely. Phospholipids and proteins can slide sideways within their own leaflet, but on their own they cannot easily flip to the other one. (Cholesterol, ceramide and diacylglycerol, with small uncharged head groups, flip quickly on their own, so the lipid half of this lesson's rule is about phospholipids with polar head groups.)

So every membrane has two faces, as the film above draws them: one touches the **cytosol**, the other the outside of the cell or a **lumen**, the space enclosed by an organelle such as the ER or Golgi. That second face is the **exoplasmic face** (or noncytosolic face): the one that never touches cytosol.

## The rule: a lumen counts as outside

*The first film above follows one protein from the ER to the cell surface and back. This section gives the reasoning behind the rule and the cases the film skips.*

Start with the easy case. When a cell takes something in by **endocytosis**, a patch of plasma membrane dimples inward and pinches off as a small sac, an endocytic vesicle, which soon joins an endosome inside the cell.

**Predict.** What is in the fresh vesicle? And which face of its membrane touches the cytosol?

A gulp of extracellular fluid: the sac closed around fluid that was outside the cell a moment earlier. The face that touched the cytosol still touches the cytosol, and the face that met the outside now faces the lumen. Nothing crossed the membrane. It just changed shape.

</details>

Textbooks say the endosome lumen is "topologically equivalent" to the outside of the cell. In plain words: **you can get from one space to the other by budding and fusing alone, without ever passing through a membrane.** The endosome lumen is equivalent to the outside because it *was* the outside.

Now run it backwards. When a sac fuses with the plasma membrane, its lumen opens to the outside and its contents spill out: **exocytosis**, the way cells secrete. Each vesicle step back through the Golgi to the ER works the same way, as the film shows. Within the Golgi, cargo can also stay in one sac while the sac itself matures, which keeps sides too. So the lumens of the ER, Golgi, secretory vesicles, endosomes and lysosomes all sit on the same side as the outside of the cell: in the film's colors they are teal, and the cytosol (with the nucleus, which connects to it through pores) is gold.

Budding and fusion never swap the colors: when a vesicle fuses with its target, the two cytosol-facing leaflets merge, and the two lumens (or a lumen and the outside) join.

So where does a protein's orientation come from? For a protein of the secretory pathway, it is normally set when the protein enters the ER membrane, usually through the **translocon**, the protein channel that threads new chains into or across the membrane and lets each membrane-spanning stretch slip sideways into the lipid.

**Worked problem, part 1: trace one protein.** A protein with one membrane-spanning stretch is made in the ER of a cultured mammalian cell, N terminus in the lumen and C terminus in the cytosol: the arrangement called **type I**. Its N-terminal domain gets an N-linked glycan (a sugar chain attached to an asparagine), added in the lumen because the enzyme that adds it, oligosaccharyl transferase, has its active site on the lumenal side.

1. **ER to Golgi.** Vesicles bud and fuse. The sugar stays lumenal; the C terminus stays in the cytosol.
2. **Golgi to plasma membrane.** A secretory vesicle carrying the protein fuses with the plasma membrane.

**Predict.** Where are the sugar and the C terminus now?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The sugar faces the extracellular fluid, because the vesicle lumen it sat in opened to the outside. The C terminus is still in the cytosol. That solves the opening puzzle: the sugar's part of the chain crossed once, in the ER, as it was made, and never again.

</details>

3. **Back in by endocytosis.** The sugar and N terminus face the endosome lumen; the C terminus faces the cytosol.


A secreted protein, such as insulin, is threaded all the way into the ER lumen, so it ends up outside. Even viruses obey it: an influenza virus buds outward from the plasma membrane, so its spike proteins, made in the ER with their sugar-carrying ends in the lumen, stud the outside of the virus.

## Four arrangements, one rule

A membrane protein's **topology** is which of its parts face the cytosol and which face the other side. Because a lumen counts as outside, "in the lumen or outside" below is one side, not two.

- **Single-pass type I**: one membrane-spanning segment, N terminus in the lumen or outside, C terminus in the cytosol. Glycophorin A of the red blood cell (its structure is on this page) and the EGF receptor are type I.
- **Single-pass type II**: one segment, N terminus in the cytosol, C terminus in the lumen or outside. The transferrin receptor and many Golgi glycosyltransferases, the enzymes that build sugar chains, are type II.
- **Multipass**: several segments, with loops alternating between the two faces. The glucose transporter GLUT1 is one; the bacteriorhodopsin structure on this page shows the alternating loops.
- **GPI-anchored**: no spanning segment; the protein is attached by a glycolipid, glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI), to the lumenal leaflet, because the anchor is attached in the ER lumen. Prion protein and alkaline phosphatase are held this way. Proteins held by myristoyl or prenyl groups on the cytosolic face are a different class, covered in [lipids as anchors and addresses](/lessons/lipid-anchors-and-addresses/).

Which arrangement a chain takes is normally decided at the translocon by signals in the chain itself, the subject of [protein sorting](/lessons/protein-sorting/). Reorientation normally happens during insertion, though a few proteins rearrange later in the ER membrane; a finished protein is not flipped by traffic.

## How we know which way a protein faces

The standard test uses **microsomes**: sealed vesicles of ER membrane, made by breaking cells, with the lumen still inside. Tag the protein's two ends with different epitopes, short tags an antibody recognizes, and add a protease. On the gel, an antibody to one tag still finds a protected fragment, while the other tag is gone. Add detergent before the protease and both tags are gone.

Now read it. A protease cannot enter a sealed vesicle, so the tag that survived was in the lumen. The detergent control shows that tag could be digested once the membrane was opened, so its survival was protection, not a protease-proof tag. A second test uses sugars: a glycosylation site placed in one end receives a sugar only if that end reaches the lumen (step 3 below).

## Lipids: the side the cell has to keep pumping

Proteins keep their sides with no upkeep; phospholipids keep theirs only with constant help: where each is made sets part of the pattern, and pumps keep restoring the rest.

In the red blood cell, the best-measured case, phosphatidylserine (PS) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) sit mainly on the inner, cytoplasmic leaflet, and phosphatidylcholine (PC) and sphingomyelin mainly on the outer leaflet. Other cells and organelles are less completely measured. The leaflets also differ in how unsaturated their tails are.

Left alone, a phospholipid moves between leaflets slowly, on a scale of hours or longer for most species. Slow is not zero: over the months a red blood cell lives, leakage alone would wear away any pattern set up at the start. Three kinds of protein move lipid across the bilayer, and their names differ by a letter: flip is in, flop is out, scramble is both ways.

| Protein | Moves lipid | Uses ATP? | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Flippase** | Outer leaflet to cytoplasmic leaflet, against the gradient; chiefly PS and PE | Yes | Most are P4-type ATPases, a family of lipid pumps; in human cells, for example ATP11A or ATP11C with their partner CDC50A |
| **Floppase** | Cytoplasmic leaflet to outer leaflet | Yes | ABC transporters, a family of ATP-powered transporters, appear to do this job |
| **Scramblase** | Both ways, down the gradients | Generally no | TMEM16F, switched on by calcium |

So plasma-membrane asymmetry is a steady state: pumps push one way, slow leakage and regulated scramblases push the other, and switching on a scramblase collapses it far faster than spontaneous flip-flop would.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/membrane-organization-topology/flippase-scramblase-leaflets.webp" width={1200} height={534} alt="AI-generated illustration: a membrane bilayer with a flippase using ATP to move lipids from the outer to the cytoplasmic leaflet and a scramblase letting lipids cross both ways; an inset shows the two lipid types mixed after the scramblase is on." caption="In the red blood cell, PS and PE sit mainly in the cytoplasmic leaflet and PC and sphingomyelin mainly in the outer one. TMEM16F is the best-studied calcium-activated scramblase. Its activity can expose PS on the surface of activated platelets, where the negatively charged lipid supports blood clotting. A different route exposes PS on dying cells, where it serves as a signal for engulfment; that pathway belongs to [death receptors, BCL-2 and caspases](/lessons/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases/).

**Predict.** Two red blood cells lose their pattern in different ways. In one, ATP runs out and nothing else changes. In the other, calcium floods in while ATP stays normal. Which puts PS on its surface faster, and why?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The calcium-loaded cell, by far. With no ATP the flippases stop, but PS can still only leak outward by spontaneous flip-flop, so it creeps out over hours or longer. Calcium opens a scramblase, which carries PS across within minutes, and high calcium also inhibits the flippases, so nothing pumps it back. Pump off is slow; a hole opened is fast.

</details>

The ER membrane is thought to be much closer to symmetric, because lipids there are largely scrambled between the leaflets. Cholesterol, which flips quickly, is a harder case, and how it divides between the leaflets of the plasma membrane is still argued.

## Along the membrane, freely: FRAP

In 1972 Singer and Nicolson's fluid mosaic model predicted the split this lesson has followed: proteins keep a fixed orientation across the membrane but are free to move within its plane. Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) measures the sideways motion. A laser destroys the fluorescence of labelled molecules in a small spot. If they are mobile, unbleached ones diffuse in and the spot brightens. The bleached molecules are still there and still moving; they are simply dark. The imaging side of the method is covered in [microscopy and quantitative images](/lessons/microscopy-and-images/). The second film worked the mobile fraction and half-time of the data set below; the page adds the diffusion coefficient, its assumptions and its limits.

**Worked problem, part 2: read a FRAP curve.** In this made-up data set, a fluorescent protein in the plasma membrane of a cultured cell is bleached in a circular spot of radius w = 2.0 µm. Assume the bleach is instantaneous and uniform, the membrane is flat, the unbleached reservoir is large, and recovery is by two-dimensional diffusion alone. The values are already corrected for background and for bleaching during imaging.

- Before the bleach, Fi = 100. Immediately after, F0 = 20. At 4.0 s, 50. Final plateau, F∞ = 80, not 100.

Step 1, mobile fraction:

> **mobile fraction = (F∞ - F0) / (Fi - F0) = (80 - 20) / (100 - 20) = 60 / 80 = 0.75**

The denominator is 80, not 100: only what was bleached can recover. The immobile fraction is 1 - 0.75 = 0.25. That number does not say why a quarter stayed dark. Anchoring to the cytoskeleton, bleaching a large share of the cell's total pool, or bleaching during imaging can each lower the plateau.

Step 2, half-time. Half the recoverable increment is 60 / 2 = 30, so half recovery is reached at 20 + 30 = 50. The curve reads 50 at 4.0 s, so t½ = 4.0 s. Half the prebleach value is also 50 here, by coincidence: with a plateau of 70, half recovery would be at 20 + 25 = 45, not 50. Always halve the recovered increase, not the prebleach value.

Sideways freedom has limits. The plasma membrane has **domains**, regions that differ in composition or mobility. Tight junctions separate the **apical** membrane, which faces the free surface or the cavity of an organ such as the gut, from the **basolateral** membrane facing neighbours and underlying tissue in epithelia. An actin meshwork under the membrane can slow some molecules. **Lipid rafts**, proposed ordered domains rich in cholesterol and sphingolipid, are where care matters most: reviews describe their existence, size and composition in living cells as unresolved, not as one settled universal structure (Going deeper).

## Going deeper

### Exceptions, and why they fit

The rule is about a membrane's own parts: small uncharged molecules such as oxygen, carbon dioxide and water cross bilayers without machines. The nuclear envelope is part of the ER, and its lumen is the space between its two membranes; the nucleoplasm is on the cytosol side, joined to it through pores. Mitochondria and chloroplasts import most of their proteins from the cytosol across their own membranes and are not joined to the secretory pathway by the ordinary vesicle routes. Peroxisomes receive some membrane proteins from the ER in vesicles, but they import their matrix proteins, already folded, straight from the cytosol, so the lumen rule does not describe their contents.

On the vesicle routes, whatever crosses a membrane after the ER is moved by a dedicated machine, which fits the rule's first clause. ER-associated degradation (ERAD) pulls misfolded proteins back to the cytosol to be destroyed. Chaperone-mediated autophagy threads cytosolic proteins into lysosomes. Some toxins escape to the cytosol. Hyaluronan synthase pushes its sugar chain out across the plasma membrane. And a few proteins change topology after insertion, while still in the ER membrane, aquaporin-1 being the textbook case. So the first film's "orientation is set once, at the ER" is the usual case, not a law, though vesicle traffic itself still never flips a protein.

Wrapping is not an exception. Autophagosomes, the vesicles that bud into multivesicular bodies, and enveloped viruses budding from the cell each enclose a pocket of cytosol, and the membrane wrapped directly around each pocket keeps its cytosolic face toward it.

Sugars follow the same logic: where a sugar is added decides where it ends up. N-linked and mucin-type O-linked sugars face outside because they are added in the ER or Golgi lumen; O-GlcNAc added in the cytosol and nucleus stays there. So "glycans always face outside" is wrong.

### Other names for single-pass proteins

This lesson sorts single-pass proteins by orientation only. Some textbooks add "type III" (N terminus outside, no cleaved signal sequence) and tail-anchored proteins, which are inserted after translation without the translocon; hence "usually" through the translocon.

### How close the topology decision can be

Positive charges are statistically enriched on the cytosolic side of membrane segments, a bias first described for bacterial inner-membrane proteins; it is a tendency, not a law. Goder, Bieri and Spiess showed in COS-1 cells that engineered chains with competing signals can reorient within the translocon, and that glycosylation can shift the outcome (details on the Martin Spiess card). Marginally hydrophobic segments can also fail to insert or can reorient, which is why "the first segment sets the rest" holds only in the simplest multipass case.

### The red-cell lipid measurements

The leaflet pairs in the main text come from human erythrocytes, in studies summarized in a recent review. Lorent and colleagues then digested away the outer leaflet's lipids of intact human red blood cells and analysed what was left. The two halves of the membrane differed: the outer half's phospholipids carry about 1.6 double bonds each, the inner half's about 3.4. These 2020 erythrocyte numbers are one recent addition to the old finding that lipid composition differs between the leaflets, an active area of work on how that difference changes membrane physical properties.

### How the transporters were found

In a haploid genetic screen in human cells, Segawa and colleagues found ATP11C and its partner CDC50A required for inward movement of lipid. Alvadia and colleagues purified the mouse TMEM16F protein and showed that, in artificial vesicles, it scrambled both labelled PS and labelled PE when calcium was added. That reconstitution shows the protein can scramble lipid on its own. Its role in exposing PS on activated platelets is a separate, physiological finding.

### When the FRAP formula fails

The 0.224 w²/t½ relation holds only under the assumptions of worked problem part 2. A scanning confocal bleach lets molecules move during the bleach and needs a different analysis, and binding-limited recovery breaks the w² scaling, as the spot-size Predict box showed.

Part of that slowness in the plasma membrane is the cell's doing. In a FRAP study of cholera toxin B on cultured cells, Day and Kenworthy found its slow diffusion was impeded by actin- and ATP-dependent processes, as expected if an actin meshwork restrains it (PLoS ONE 2012).

### What is and is not known about rafts

Membrane material that resists cold detergent was long called raft material, but a detergent-resistant fraction is not a native raft; detergent can create or rearrange domains.

The best support comes from particular systems. Stone and colleagues used super-resolution imaging in the CH27 mouse B-cell lymphoma line. A peptide probe preferring ordered membrane gathered near clustered B-cell receptors and one preferring disordered membrane was excluded, in live and fixed cells; fixed primary mouse B cells agreed. That supports phase-like sorting in that system. It does not give a raft's composition, and it does not show that every cell has them.
