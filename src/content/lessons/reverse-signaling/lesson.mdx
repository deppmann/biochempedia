  - name: "Knock-in of a truncated protein"

      control showing that the altered protein reaches the cell surface.
  - name: "Immunoprecipitation and phosphotyrosine blotting"
    blurb: >-
      The protein of interest is pulled out of a cell extract with an antibody, separated on a
      gel, and probed with an antibody that recognizes phosphotyrosine. A band shows that the
      protein carried phosphate on tyrosine when the cells were broken open.
  - name: "Compartmented neuron culture"
    blurb: >-

      the axons apart from the fluid around the cell bodies. A reagent added to one side reaches
      only that part of the neuron, which shows where on the cell a signal is received.

**Epidermal growth factor is 53 amino acids long. The protein it is cut from is 1,207 amino acids long, and it spans the membrane.** Why would a cell build something that large to release something that small? For EGF the answer is still incomplete. For several other ligands it is known, and it changes what the word "ligand" means: a ligand held in a membrane can receive a signal as well as give one.

## A curiosity: the ligand starts out as large as its receptor

The EGF that Stanley Cohen purified (see the lesson on finding a signal) is a small peptide. When its messenger RNA was cloned from the mouse in 1983, the sequence predicted a protein more than twenty times longer. The human version has 1,207 amino acids. It begins with a signal peptide, the short stretch that sends a new protein into the secretory pathway. After that comes a long outer part. One segment crosses the membrane, and a tail lies in the cytosol. The 53 amino acids of EGF sit just outside the membrane, and proteases cut them free.

The precursor is about as large as the EGF receptor. The finished human EGF receptor has 1,186 amino acids, and the EGF precursor has 1,185 once its signal peptide is removed.

EGF is not a special case. Its relative TGF-alpha is 50 amino acids, cut from a membrane-spanning precursor of 160. Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) is made as a membrane protein of 233 amino acids, and a protease releases the outer 157. Fas ligand and the Notch ligand Delta are membrane proteins too. So a "ligand" can have a tail inside its own cell, and a tail in the cytosol is what you expect of a receptor.

## What is Reverse Signaling? Both cells get a message

Picture two cells that touch. One carries a receptor. The other carries that receptor's ligand, held in its membrane. When the two proteins bind, the receptor signals into its cell. Call this the **forward signal**. **Reverse signaling** is a second message, passing into the cell that carries the ligand. When both happen, the exchange is called bidirectional signaling.

A handshake is a fair picture. You cannot grip someone's hand without your own being gripped. In biology the names were fixed by history: the protein with the kinase domain, or the one found first, was called the receptor.

A reverse signal needs two things. The ligand must stay attached to its cell, and it must have a route to the cytosol, through its own tail or through a neighbor in the same membrane. A useful hint when you meet a new ligand: if it stays in the membrane and its tail has changed little between species, suspect that the tail does something.

The processes affected make a long list: cell migration and proliferation, axon guidance, angiogenesis (the growth of new blood vessels), the immune response, apoptosis and development. Notch also needs two touching cells, but in the account you met in the Notch lesson the message runs one way, into the cell that carries Notch.

## Ephrins: a tail that borrows a kinase

Ephrins are the clearest case. Their receptors, the Eph receptors, are the largest family of receptor tyrosine kinases, with 14 members in humans. Every ephrin is made attached to a membrane, in one of two ways. The five A-type ephrins are held by a GPI anchor, a lipid on the outer face of the membrane (see the lesson on lipid anchors). The three B-type ephrins cross the membrane once and end in a tail of 83 to 93 amino acids in the cytosol. The receptors fall into two matching classes, EphA and EphB, named for the ephrins they prefer, though some pairs cross the line: EphA4 also binds B-type ephrins.

The B-ephrin tail contains no enzyme. It has a few tyrosines whose positions are the same in all three B-type ephrins, and its last residues fit PDZ domains (see the lesson on protein interaction modules).

In 1996 and 1997 two laboratories, Tony Pawson's in Toronto and Rüdiger Klein's in Heidelberg, reported the key observation. When cells carrying a B-type ephrin met the outer part of an EphB receptor, the ephrin's tail became phosphorylated on tyrosine. The receptor fragment had no kinase domain and the ephrin has none, so a kinase inside the ephrin's own cell had to be responsible.

In 2002 Klein's laboratory named it. Src-family kinases gather where B-type ephrins cluster, and they act as positive regulators of the tail's phosphorylation. The same study found an off switch: a tyrosine phosphatase, PTP-BL, binds the PDZ-binding end of the tail and removes the phosphates after a delay. Mark Henkemeyer's laboratory found that the adaptor Grb4 binds the phosphorylated tyrosines through its SH2 domain and connects the ephrin to proteins that reshape the actin skeleton.

The take-away fits in one line: "Ephrins use Src-family kinases to carry out reverse signaling."

### "How could you signal via a GPI anchored protein?"

An A-type ephrin has no tail. It sits in the outer leaflet and never touches the cytosol. Try the question before you read on.

The answer the evidence supports is a partner. A-type ephrins collect in small patches of membrane rich in cholesterol, and Src-family kinases sit on the inner face of such patches, held there by their own lipid anchors. In 1999 Davy and colleagues reported that clustering ephrin-A5 with the outer part of an EphA receptor drew one of these kinases, Fyn, into the patches and changed how the cells adhered to their surroundings, a change that needed Fyn. Later work found partners that span the membrane. In the axons of the retina, ephrin-A associates with p75, a receptor for neurotrophins, and the reverse signal there needs p75.

Other GPI-anchored proteins solve the problem the same way. GFR-alpha-1 binds the growth factor GDNF and leaves the signaling to Ret, a kinase that spans the membrane. Which partner an A-type ephrin uses seems to depend on the cell, and how a protein in the outer leaflet switches on a kinase at the inner leaflet is not settled.

## TNF and semaphorins: other ligands that listen

You met TNF as a ligand in the lessons on NF-kappa-B and on cell death. It is made as a membrane protein, three chains together, with a short tail of 35 amino acids in the cytosol. A protease called TACE can cut the outer part free. The part that stays in the membrane can act as a receptor: when a TNF receptor on another cell binds it, a signal passes into the TNF-bearing cell.

The tail has no enzyme activity. What the tails of several TNF-family ligands share is a short sequence of the kind that casein kinase 1 (CK1), a serine/threonine kinase, prefers. In membrane TNF a serine in the tail carries phosphate, and a 1999 study found that the phosphate was lost when a soluble TNF receptor bound. The take-away is worded with care: "CK1 Domain is likely an essential component in Reverse Signaling." Note the word "likely".

The clearest case in neurons comes from Alun Davies's laboratory. Developing sympathetic neurons carry TNF on their axons, and the tissues those axons grow into make TNF receptor 1. In culture, a soluble form of that receptor made the axons grow and branch more, and the effect depended on TNF in the axon's membrane. Mice lacking either TNF or the receptor had sparser sympathetic innervation in several tissues. Here the protein named "receptor" is the signal, and the protein named "ligand" receives it.

Semaphorins make the same point. The first was found in 1992 in the grasshopper embryo, as a membrane protein that guides growing axons. The family is now sorted into eight classes; some are secreted, some span the membrane, and one class is held by a GPI anchor. All share the sema domain, about 500 amino acids at the outer end: "Sema domain is highly conserved between all classes." Their main receptors are the plexins.

In the fruit fly, the membrane-spanning semaphorin Sema-1a was first known as a ligand for plexin A. It also works the other way round. On the axons of the fly's developing eye, Sema-1a receives a signal from plexin A that helps the axons hold to one another, and it needs its cytoplasmic part to do so. In motor axons, the tail of Sema-1a was found to bind two regulators of the small GTPase Rho, a GEF and a GAP (see the lesson on GTPase switches).

## Reading a knockout: which direction did you remove?

Suppose you delete the gene for a B-type ephrin and an organ fails to form. You have removed the forward signal into the receptor's cell. You have also removed the reverse signal into the ephrin's cell, and the plain grip that held the two cells together. The knockout cannot tell you which loss caused the defect. Deleting the receptor has the same problem, because its outer part was a ligand for the other cell.

### Design problem

"How could we disrupt reverse signaling while leaving forward signaling intact?" Design the mouse, or the reagent, for an ephrin of the B class. Then say how you would do the opposite. Last, say what you would conclude if an animal with no ephrin at all had a defect that neither of your two animals had.

<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Remove the reverse signal.** Change the ephrin's gene so that the protein keeps its outer part and its membrane segment and loses its tail. Henkemeyer's laboratory did this for ephrin-B2, putting the enzyme beta-galactosidase where the tail had been. The outer part can still bind and cluster EphB receptors on the next cell, so the forward signal survives. With no tail there is nothing for a Src-family kinase to phosphorylate and nothing for an adaptor to bind, so the reverse signal through the tail is gone. A finer version changes only the tail's tyrosines to phenylalanine, or removes the last residue, which PDZ domains need. One control is required: show that the altered protein reaches the cell surface in normal amount. If it does not, you have weakened the forward signal as well.

**The reagent.** Add the outer part of an EphB receptor, fused to the Fc region of an antibody and clustered, to cells that carry the ephrin. This gives a reverse signal with no receptor cell present. The reagent asks whether a reverse signal is enough to cause an effect, and the mouse asks whether it is needed.

**The opposite.** Alter the receptor instead: keep its outer part and membrane segment, and remove or inactivate its kinase domain. Henkemeyer and colleagues made this mouse for EphB2 in 1996. Mice with no EphB2 had misrouted axons in a brain tract called the anterior commissure. Mice with the kinase-less receptor did not. The axons carried B-type ephrins and the cells beneath them carried the receptor, so the result pointed to a reverse signal into the axons. Since one ephrin binds several EphB receptors, you may need to alter more than one receptor gene.

**The third animal.** If the full knockout has a defect that neither altered animal shows, the defect needs neither the ephrin's tail by itself nor the receptor's kinase by itself. Either direction may be enough alone, or the cells may only need to be held together. A mouse carrying both alterations at once would tell these two apart.
