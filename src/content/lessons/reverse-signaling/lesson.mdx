techniques:
  - name: "Receptor-Fc fusion protein (a receptor body)"
    blurb: >-
      The outer part of a receptor is joined to the Fc region of an antibody and made as a
      soluble protein, which is often clustered further with an antibody against Fc. Added to
      cells that carry the membrane-bound ligand, it binds the ligand with no receptor cell
      present. A response that Fc alone does not cause, and that needs the ligand, is a reverse signal.
  - name: "Knock-in of a truncated protein"
    blurb: >-
      Gene targeting in the mouse replaces the normal gene with a version that lacks one part,
      such as the cytoplasmic tail, sometimes with beta-galactosidase in its place as a marker.
      The animal shows what the rest of the protein can do without the missing part. It needs a
      control showing that the altered protein reaches the cell surface.
  - name: "Immunoprecipitation and phosphotyrosine blotting"
    blurb: >-
      The protein of interest is pulled out of a cell extract with an antibody, separated on a
      gel, and probed with an antibody that recognizes phosphotyrosine. A band shows that the
      protein carried phosphate on tyrosine when the cells were broken open, provided the band is at that protein's size and not a partner pulled down with it.
  - name: "Compartmented neuron culture"
    blurb: >-
      Neurons are grown in a device whose barriers let axons through and keep the fluid around
      the axons apart from the fluid around the cell bodies. A reagent added to one side reaches
      only that part of the neuron, which shows where on the cell a signal is received.

**A ligand held in a cell's membrane can receive a signal as well as give one.** The first film showed how a membrane ligand signals into its own cell; start here with a structural surprise that hinted ligands do more than float away.

## Why a ligand starts out as a large membrane protein

The epidermal growth factor (EGF) that Stanley Cohen purified (see the lesson on finding a signal) is a 53-amino-acid peptide. Yet when mouse EGF mRNA was cloned in 1983, it predicted a membrane-spanning protein more than twenty times longer. Human pro-EGF has 1,207 amino acids: a **signal peptide** (the short stretch directing a new protein into the secretory pathway), a long outer region, one transmembrane segment, and a cytosolic tail. The 53 amino acids of EGF sit just outside the membrane, where proteases cut them free.

For EGF, why a cell builds so large a precursor is still not fully explained. Yet tumor necrosis factor (TNF), Fas ligand, and the Notch ligand Delta are also made as membrane proteins—giving each "ligand" a tail inside its own cell, the feature you expect of a receptor.

## Forward, reverse, and bidirectional signaling

When two touching cells meet, a receptor on one binds a membrane-bound ligand on the other. The message into the receptor's cell is the **forward signal**; a second message into the ligand's cell is **reverse signaling**. Together they form **bidirectional signaling**. Like a handshake, you cannot grip someone's hand without your own being gripped—the names "ligand" and "receptor" record only which role each protein was first seen to play.

<Support>**The one idea.** A membrane ligand can signal inward through a tail or partner; to separate bidirectional signals, disable one inner route while keeping binding. **Scope:** Applies to contact-dependent, membrane-anchored ligands (transmembrane or GPI-anchored). Proteolytic cleavage ends reverse signaling, and inner-route mutations still require controls for surface expression, direct forward signaling, and receptor redundancy.</Support>

A reverse signal requires two things: the ligand must stay attached to its cell and reach the cytosol via its own tail or a membrane neighbour. Reverse signaling acts in cell migration and proliferation, axon guidance, angiogenesis (the growth of new blood vessels), the immune response, apoptosis, and development. Contact alone is not proof, however: Notch also needs two touching cells, yet in the Notch lesson the signal runs one way, into the Notch-bearing cell.

**Predict.** You sequence a newly found membrane-bound ligand whose cytosolic tail is nearly unchanged from fish to mammals. Why does that conservation suggest reverse signaling—and why is membrane anchoring alone not enough?

Membrane anchoring alone is insufficient because contact ligands such as Delta (in the standard Notch account) signal one way, and for pro-EGF why the cell builds so large a precursor is still not explained.

## Ephrins: borrowing a kinase or a partner

Ephrins are the clearest case. Their **Eph receptors** are the largest receptor tyrosine kinase family (14 members in humans). The five **A-type ephrins** sit in the outer leaflet via a **GPI anchor** (see the lesson on lipid anchors); the three **B-type ephrins** cross the membrane once and end in an 83- to 93-amino-acid cytosolic tail. **EphA** and **EphB** receptors match their preferred ligands, though some cross classes: EphA4 also binds B-type ephrins.

### B-type ephrins: two routes on one short tail

The B-ephrin tail holds no enzyme, only conserved tyrosines shared across all three B-type ephrins and a C-terminal motif fitting **PDZ domains** (see the lesson on protein interaction modules). Building on the first film's cluster picture, mid-1990s experiments exposed ephrin-B cells to the outer part of an EphB receptor and saw tyrosine phosphorylation on the tail—proving a kinase inside the ephrin's own cell acted, since neither the receptor fragment nor the ephrin had a kinase domain.

In 2002, Rüdiger Klein's laboratory at the Max Planck Institute of Neurobiology near Munich named both switches: **Src-family kinases** gather at EphB-clustered B-ephrins as positive regulators of tail phosphorylation, and after a delay the tyrosine phosphatase **PTP-BL** binds the PDZ-binding end to remove the phosphates. Mark Henkemeyer's laboratory showed that the adaptor **Grb4** binds those phosphotyrosines via its **SH2 domain**, linking ephrin-B to proteins that reshape the actin cytoskeleton.

**Predict.** In mouse ephrin-B2, suppose one line mutates all five conserved tyrosines to phenylalanine (sparing the PDZ end) and a second deletes only the PDZ-binding end. If lymphatic remodeling relies mainly on the PDZ branch, what phenotypes do you expect?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The PDZ-deleted mouse should show severe lymphatic defects, while the five-tyrosine mutant stays largely intact. Klein's laboratory saw exactly this split in mice: deleting the PDZ-binding end caused major lymphatic malformations, whereas mutating all five conserved tyrosines caused only a mild defect. Src-family phosphorylation is central to B-ephrin reverse signaling, yet tissue context decides which tail route dominates.

</details>

### How a GPI-anchored ephrin signals without a tail

An A-type ephrin has no transmembrane segment and no cytosolic tail: held in the outer leaflet, it never touches the cytosol, so it must couple inward through partners that vary by cell type. They collect in cholesterol-rich patches above lipid-anchored inner-leaflet Src-family kinases: in 1999, Davy and colleagues showed that clustering ephrin-A5 with the outer part of an EphA receptor drew **Fyn** into these patches and altered cell adhesion in a Fyn-dependent way. In retinal axons, ephrin-A pairs with **p75**, a membrane-spanning neurotrophin receptor required there for reverse signaling and Fyn phosphorylation. How an outer-leaflet protein activates an inner-leaflet kinase without a transmembrane partner remains unsettled (for GFR-alpha-1 and Ret, see Going deeper).

**Predict.** If you add clustered EphA outer-domain reagent to retinal axons lacking p75, what happens to outer binding versus intracellular Fyn phosphorylation?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

EphA still binds ephrin-A on the outer leaflet, since ephrin-A is intact, but Fyn phosphorylation and reverse signaling fail because the transmembrane bridge (p75) required in retinal axons is absent.

## Separating forward from reverse signaling

The second film showed why a full knockout confounds forward signaling, reverse signaling, and cell-to-cell grip, and how kinase-less EphB2 and soluble receptor bodies isolate reverse signaling. Applying the second half of the guiding rule—disable one inner route while keeping binding—yields a clean diagnostic table for a single pair:

|Manipulation|Forward|Reverse|Grip|What a defect (or response) shows|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|Full ligand or receptor KO|lost|lost|lost|Ambiguous: forward, reverse, or plain grip|
|Tail-less ligand (at surface)|intact|lost|intact|Defect points to reverse signaling (with controls)|
|Tail-less ligand (trapped inside)|lost|lost|lost|Same as full KO—cannot isolate reverse|
|Kinase-less receptor (at surface)|lost (own kinase)|intact|intact|Normal rules out forward alone; defect points to forward|
|Double mutant (tail-less + kinase-less)|lost|lost|intact|Normal points to plain grip; defect points to redundancy|
|Soluble receptor-Fc (no receptor cell)|absent|on|absent|Ligand-dependent response proves reverse is *sufficient*|

Row 3 is a classic trap: before interpreting a truncated protein, confirm it reaches the cell surface in normal amounts (see the anecdote "Two tail-less mice that disagreed"). Even at the surface, two caveats apply in real tissues:

1. **Tail edits can alter forward signaling.** A later study found that changing the ephrin-B2 tail can alter the forward signal in the receptor's cell—in one case making it stronger—so measure forward signaling directly.
2. **Receptor co-clustering and redundancy.** Eph receptors cluster with one another, so a kinase-less receptor might still help other Eph receptors send a forward signal; and since one ephrin binds several EphB receptors, you may need to alter more than one receptor gene.

**Predict.** A kinase-less EphB2 mouse looks normal in a tissue co-expressing EphB2 and EphB3. Before concluding forward signaling is unneeded there, what alternative explanation do those caveats raise?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Kinase-less EphB2 can co-cluster with intact EphB3—or EphB3 can substitute directly—keeping forward signaling active. Ruling out forward signaling may require disabling multiple EphB genes.

</details>

### Design problem

Turn the second film's EphB2 design around for a B-type ephrin: how would you remove reverse signaling while keeping forward signaling intact, and how would you disable only one branch of the tail? State the essential controls and how soluble EphB-Fc complements the mouse. Last, what would you conclude if a full ephrin knockout had a defect that neither a tail-less ephrin mouse nor a kinase-less receptor mouse showed?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Remove reverse signaling.** Keep the ephrin's outer domain and transmembrane segment while replacing its tail—as Henkemeyer's laboratory did for ephrin-B2 using beta-galactosidase. The outer part still binds and clusters EphB receptors (preserving forward signaling), while Src-family phosphorylation and adaptor/PDZ binding disappear. Finer alleles mutate the five conserved tyrosines to phenylalanine or delete the C-terminal PDZ residue, removing one route at a time. Run two controls: confirm normal surface levels (preventing intracellular-trapping artefacts), and measure forward signaling directly since ephrin-B2 tail edits can alter it (even strengthening it in one case).

**Reagent vs. mouse.** Clustered EphB-Fc (outer receptor fused to antibody Fc) stimulates ephrin-bearing cells with no receptor cell present; a ligand-dependent response (absent with Fc alone) tests *sufficiency*, while the knock-in mouse tests *necessity*.

**Opposite & third animal.** Disabling the receptor's kinase while keeping its outer domain (as Henkemeyer's 1996 EphB2 mouse did in the anterior commissure) tests forward signaling, subject to Eph heteroclustering and multi-EphB redundancy. If a full knockout has a defect that neither single inner-route mutant shows, either direction suffices alone (redundancy) or plain physical grip suffices. A double-altered mouse (tail-less ephrin plus kinase-less receptor) separates them: normal means adhesion suffices; defective means redundant signaling was required.

</details>

## TNF and semaphorins: other ligands that listen

You met **TNF** as a ligand in the lessons on NF-kappa-B and on cell death. It is made as a three-chain membrane protein, each chain ending in a 35-amino-acid cytosolic tail, and the protease **TACE** can cut the outer trimer free. As the second film showed for growing axons, uncut membrane TNF can receive a reverse signal, whereas TACE cleavage breaks that inward link while leaving released TNF free to signal forward.

The TNF tail has no enzyme activity, but several TNF-family tails share a short sequence preferred by **casein kinase 1 (CK1)**, a serine/threonine kinase. In membrane TNF, a tail serine carries phosphate that a 1999 study found was lost when soluble TNF receptor bound. Word the take-away with care: the CK1 site is *likely* an essential part of reverse signaling in this family—noting "likely," because it is a short motif rather than a catalytic domain and the evidence comes mainly from one study of membrane TNF in mouse macrophage cells.

In neurons, Alun Davies's laboratory combined both halves of the dissection toolkit. Developing sympathetic axons carry membrane TNF while target tissues make **TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1)**. Mice lacking either TNF or TNFR1 had sparser sympathetic innervation in several tissues—ambiguous on its own by our table, since forward signaling predicts the same two knockouts. Pairing that result with soluble TNFR1 in culture (which boosted axon growth and branching only when membrane TNF was present) settled the direction: the receptor in the target was the signal, and TNF on the axon received it.

**Semaphorins**—a large family of secreted and membrane-spanning axon-guidance proteins whose main receptors are the **plexins**—make the same point. Fruit fly **Sema-1a**, first known as a ligand for plexin A, receives a signal from plexin A on developing eye axons that helps them hold together, requiring its cytoplasmic part. In motor axons, Sema-1a's tail binds two regulators of the small GTPase Rho, a GEF and a GAP (see the lesson on GTPase switches).

**Predict.** In the fly eye, losing either Sema-1a or plexin A disrupts axon bundling. What single Sema-1a alteration (and control) tests whether Sema-1a acts as a receptor here?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Delete or replace Sema-1a's cytoplasmic tail while confirming its outer domain reaches the axon surface at normal levels. Persistent bundling defects show the inward route through Sema-1a's tail is required, as fly eye experiments found.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How big is the EGF precursor, and which other ligands are made this way?

The finished human EGF receptor has 1,186 amino acids, and the EGF precursor has 1,185 once its signal peptide is removed. EGF's relative TGF-alpha is 50 amino acids cut from a 160-amino-acid membrane precursor; TNF is made as a 233-amino-acid membrane protein from which a protease releases the outer 157.

### Who first saw ephrin tails become phosphorylated

Tony Pawson's laboratory in Toronto (1996) and Rüdiger Klein's laboratory in Heidelberg (1997) first reported tyrosine phosphorylation of B-type ephrin tails upon contact with an EphB outer domain.

### Another GPI-anchored protein that borrows a partner

**GFR-alpha-1** binds the growth factor **GDNF** and passes the signal mainly to **Ret**, a membrane-spanning kinase, yet can also activate Src-family kinases without Ret—raising the same unresolved bilayer-crossing question as ephrin-A.

### The semaphorin family and its sema domain

First found in 1992 in the grasshopper embryo guiding growing axons, semaphorins span eight classes (secreted, membrane-spanning, and one GPI-anchored). All share a ~500-amino-acid outer **sema domain**, highly conserved across classes, and signal primarily with plexins.
