techniques:
  - name: "Static pressure-volume curves of excised lungs"
    blurb: >-
      A degassed lung is inflated in steps with a syringe, each step held until flow stops, while
      a transducer records the distending pressure; deflation is recorded the same way. Repeating
      the curve with the lung filled with saline removes the air-liquid surface, so the gap between
      the two curves estimates how much recoil surface tension supplies.
  - name: "Surface film balance"
    blurb: >-
      Lung extract fills a shallow trough and its surface-active material forms a film at the surface, a movable barrier
      compresses and expands the film, and a thin plate hanging in the surface (a Wilhelmy plate)
      reports the tension. Plotting tension against area shows whether the film reaches low
      tension when compressed.
  - name: "Spirometry, gas dilution and body plethysmography"
    blurb: >-
      A spirometer records volume breathed against time, giving tidal volume, vital capacity, FVC
      and FEV1. Helium dilution, nitrogen washout or a sealed body box (Boyle's law) add the gas
      that never leaves the lung, giving residual volume, FRC and TLC; in obstruction, dilution and washout can underestimate them.
  - name: "Esophageal balloon manometry"
    blurb: >-
      A thin balloon catheter in the lower esophagus records a pressure used as a surrogate for
      pleural pressure. With mouth or alveolar pressure, it gives transpulmonary pressure and lets
      lung compliance be separated from chest-wall compliance; it reports the region around the
      esophagus, not the whole pleural space.

**Fill an excised lung with saline instead of air and it takes far less pressure to inflate it to the same volume.** The tissue is the same both times.

**Predict.** What does the air add that saline does not?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

An air-liquid surface. Saline removes the wet surface that lines every alveolus but leaves the tissue in place; with air, that surface is present, and its tension pulls each alveolus smaller. The gap between the two inflations is the part of the lung's recoil that comes from surface tension, and it is a large part.

</details>

The lung makes **surfactant**, a film that lowers the tension of its wet lining. A baby born before its lungs make enough has a stiff lung whose alveoli tend to collapse at the end of each breath: **respiratory distress syndrome** (RDS). [Gas exchange and breathing](/lessons/gas-exchange/) gave the first look at surfactant and compliance; here they get numbers.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A breath has two pressure drops. Across the lung's wall, transpulmonary pressure (alveolar minus pleural) equals the lung's recoil at its current volume: exactly when no air flows, and very nearly during quiet breathing. That recoil is tissue plus the surface tension of the wet alveolar lining. Along the airways, the gap between atmospheric and alveolar pressure is what moves air, and it grows with flow and with airway resistance. Pleural pressure, set by the breathing muscles and the chest wall, has to pay for both. Surfactant shrinks the surface part of the recoil; disease changes the recoil or the resistance.

The rule is for the lung alone; the chest wall is a second spring (limits in Going deeper, "Limits of the one idea").

## One breath, two pressure drops

The visceral pleura covers each lung and the parietal pleura lines the chest wall, with a thin film of fluid between. The stretched lung recoils inward; the chest wall springs outward. At **functional residual capacity** (FRC), the volume left after a quiet expiration, the two recoils balance. The lung pulls the inner layer in and the chest wall pulls the outer layer out; the sealed fluid film between them cannot expand, so its pressure falls below atmospheric. Picture two springs hooked together, one stretched and one compressed, resting where their forces cancel.

{/* DIAGRAM: chest at FRC, front view: lungs (right */}

Three pressures describe a breath. Each is measured against atmospheric (0), in centimetres of water (cmH2O), the unit lung physiologists use because breathing pressures are small and a water column reads them easily.

- **Alveolar pressure** is the pressure of the gas inside the alveoli.
- **Pleural pressure** is the pressure in the thin fluid film between lung and chest wall, just outside the lung.
- **Transpulmonary pressure** is alveolar minus pleural pressure: inside minus outside, the pressure across the lung's wall.

Why inside minus outside? Think of a balloon. Its rubber stays stretched only while the air inside pushes harder than the air outside, by just the pull of the rubber; let the difference fall and the balloon shrinks until its rubber pulls only that hard. The lung works the same way, which is why transpulmonary pressure tracks recoil: a bigger lung recoils harder, so it needs a bigger transpulmonary pressure.

The second pressure drop runs along the airways: air flows in only while alveolar pressure is below atmospheric, and out only while it is above, and the faster the flow or the narrower the airways, the bigger that drop.

Now follow one quiet breath in a healthy adult at rest. Recoil, the lung's elastic recoil at that moment's volume, equals transpulmonary pressure on every row of this simplified table (to within a small tissue-friction term during flow; row 2: −1 − (−7.5) = 6.5).

| Moment | Air flow | Alveolar | Pleural | Transpulmonary (= recoil) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| End of a quiet expiration (FRC) | none | 0 | about −5 | about 5 |
| Middle of inspiration | in | about −1 | about −7.5 | about 6.5 |
| End of inspiration | none | 0 | about −8 | about 8 |
| Middle of expiration | out | about +1 | about −5.5 | about 6.5 |

With no flow (FRC, end-inspiration), alveolar pressure is 0 and pleural pressure is simply minus the recoil: −5, then −8 once the lung is more stretched. In between, the diaphragm contracts, pleural pressure falls, the lung expands with the chest and its gas spreads into more room, so alveolar pressure dips below atmospheric and air flows in. Expiration at rest needs no muscle: the diaphragm relaxes and recoil squeezes the alveolar gas above atmospheric. In quiet breathing pleural pressure stays below atmospheric throughout; it turns positive only when the expiratory muscles contract, as in a forced expiration or a cough.

So the fall in pleural pressure pays for two things. At mid-breath it has fallen 2.5 cmH2O: 1.5 pays for stretching the lung (recoil 5 to 6.5) and about 1 pays for moving air through the airways. By end-inspiration flow has stopped, and the whole 3 cmH2O fall is elastic. A sicker lung shifts that split: a stiff lung needs more for the first, and narrowed airways need more for the second. The inspiratory fall in pleural pressure also helps blood return to the heart ([cardiac output and venous return](/lessons/cardiac-output-venous-return/)).

**Predict.** A stab wound opens the pleural space to the outside air. What happens to pleural pressure, to transpulmonary pressure, and to the lung?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Pleural pressure rises to 0, atmospheric. Alveolar pressure is also 0 once flow stops, so transpulmonary pressure falls to 0, less than the lung's recoil at any normal volume. The lung shrinks until its recoil is zero, which is close to collapse. The chest wall, no longer pulled inward by the lung, springs outward. This is a **pneumothorax**.

</details>

## What the air adds: surface tension

A static pressure-volume curve is made by inflating an excised lung in steps, holding each until flow stops, then deflating it the same way. The saline curve lies far to the left: at any volume it needs much less pressure. And it shows little **hysteresis**, while the air curve shows a lot: at the same pressure, the air-filled lung holds more volume on deflation than on inflation.

Saline removes the surface and leaves the tissue, so the pressure gap between the curves at one volume is the surface part of recoil. In 1929 Kurt von Neergaard compared an isolated pig lung filled with air and with a gum solution, and concluded that surface tension, not tissue elasticity, accounts for most of the lung's recoil. Mead's laboratory later found the same in excised lungs: surface tension supplies much of the recoil, and the air-saline gap widens as volume rises. The hysteresis also comes mainly from the air-liquid interface, through the surfactant film and through collapsed airspaces and small airways reopening, with a smaller share from tissue; it is larger for breaths starting near residual volume.

An air-water surface acts like a stretched sheet; its **surface tension**, T, is force per length, in mN per m (equal to dynes per cm). Pulling along the curved surface, it pulls the alveolus smaller, adding to the recoil that transpulmonary pressure must match. How much pressure? Laplace's relation:

## Surfactant lowers tension most when the alveolus is small

**What it is.** Surfactant is about 90 percent lipid, mainly phospholipid, and 10 percent protein, secreted by **alveolar type 2 cells**, which store it in **lamellar bodies**. Its main lipid is dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC; see [lipids](/lessons/lipids/)). Phospholipid alone adsorbs too slowly for a breathing lung; surfactant protein B (SP-B) speeds adsorption.

**What it does.** Its tension changes with area. Expanded in inspiration, the film's tension rises. Compressed at end-expiration, it falls close to 0 mN per m on modern surface balances, and in situ estimates at low lung volume are also very low. So tension is lowest when alveoli are smallest, and the smallest airspaces, which a constant tension would close first, stay open. And with a smaller surface part of the recoil, the same breath needs a smaller pleural swing and less work.

**How we know.** In 1957 John Clements, in Maryland, put films from extracts of rat, cat and dog lungs on a modified Wilhelmy balance, where a movable barrier compresses the film and a thin plate hanging in the surface reports the tension. Tension fell from about 46 to about 10 dynes per cm as the film was compressed. He called it a surface-active material; it later became known as **pulmonary surfactant**. Richard Pattle in England had inferred a low-tension lining two years earlier, from lung foam that stayed stable for hours.

**Why it matters: the classic experiment.** Hyaline membrane disease (HMD) is the lung pathology of respiratory distress syndrome in newborns, most often preterm ones. Mary Ellen Avery saw Clements's balance in December 1957; in their 1959 paper she and Jere Mead put extracts of lungs from infants who had died, with or without HMD, on a surface balance and recorded the lowest tension as each film was compressed. If HMD lungs lacked the lining, their extracts should not reach low tension. They did not: a mean of about 30 dynes per cm in extracts from nine infants with HMD, against about 8 in extracts from infants over about 1,200 g who died of other causes.

**Predict.** Extracts from smaller infants who died of other causes, without HMD, also stayed high, about 26 dynes per cm. Does that weaken or strengthen the claim that a lack of surfactant causes HMD?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It weakens it as proof of cause, though not the link. Immature lungs lacked the low-tension lining with or without the disease, so immaturity and disease were entangled. And post-mortem, injured lungs left open whether the deficiency followed from the disease or from death. </details>

The case for cause came later, from animal studies and from treatment. Randomized controlled trials in the 1980s and 1990s, and their meta-analyses, established that surfactant replacement lowers mortality and pneumothorax in preterm infants. Liggins and Howie's 1972 trial showed that antenatal glucocorticoid treatment can speed fetal lung maturation ([pregnancy, birth and lactation](/lessons/pregnancy-birth-lactation/)), and antenatal glucocorticoids lower RDS and neonatal death. Antenatal steroids for women at risk of early preterm birth, and replacement surfactant for preterm infants with RDS, are now standard care.

The pressure balance explains what RDS looks like. A large surface term means a stiff lung, so each breath needs a large negative swing in pleural pressure. That pulls the infant's soft chest wall inward with each breath (retractions), and at low volume the alveoli close.

**The modern experiment.** If surfactant needs correct packaging as well as its proteins, a packaging defect should cause deficiency even at term. After an SP-B mutation in infants (1994) and SP-B knockout mice (1995) tied the protein to survival, Shulenin, Nogee, Whitsett, Dean and colleagues in 2004 sequenced ABCA3, an ATP-binding cassette transporter gene, in infants born at or near term with severe surfactant deficiency of unknown cause, and found mutations in most: the packaging, not only the proteins, is needed in humans (limits in Going deeper, "The genetics").

## Reading the balance in the clinic

### Spirometry measures what moves, not what stays

A spirometer records volume against time. Four **volumes** do not overlap: tidal volume, inspiratory reserve volume (the extra that can be inhaled after a tidal breath), expiratory reserve volume (the extra that can be exhaled) and **residual volume** (RV, what stays after a maximal expiration). **Capacities** are sums: inspiratory capacity (tidal plus inspiratory reserve), FRC (expiratory reserve plus RV), vital capacity (all but RV) and **total lung capacity** (TLC, all four).

Gas that never leaves the lung is invisible to a spirometer, so RV, FRC and TLC need other methods. These are **helium dilution** (a known amount of helium spreads into the lung and is diluted), **nitrogen washout** (collect the nitrogen washed out by pure oxygen) and **body plethysmography** (Boyle's law in a sealed box). In obstructive disease the first two reach only gas that communicates with the airways, so they can underestimate RV, FRC and TLC.

Volumes vary with height, age and sex, so one person's value is judged against reference equations from healthy populations, which give a lower limit of normal.

In the forced test, from TLC, blow out as hard and fast as possible: the total is the **forced vital capacity** (FVC), and the volume in the first second is **FEV1**.

### Forced expiration: the same balance, run hard

Pleural pressure still pays for recoil and airflow together. In a forced expiration the expiratory muscles push pleural pressure above atmospheric. Alveolar pressure is then recoil plus pleural pressure, so blowing harder raises the pressure that drives air out.

But the airways sit in the same pleural space. As air flows out, friction uses up pressure along the airways, so the pressure inside them falls until it equals pleural pressure, and downstream of that point the positive pleural pressure squeezes the deformable airways: **dynamic compression**.

**Predict.** At mid-volume, a healthy person who is already blowing out moderately hard blows harder still. Does expiratory flow rise?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Past a modest effort, no. Extra pleural pressure raises alveolar pressure, but squeezes the airways downstream of the squeeze point by just as much. The airway upstream of that point is driven by the lung's recoil alone. So at middle and low volumes, maximal expiratory flow is set by recoil and the resistance of the airways upstream of the squeeze point, not effort. At high volumes flow still rises with effort.

</details>

So a weak recoil (emphysema) or narrowed airways upstream of the squeeze point (asthma, chronic bronchitis) means a low maximal flow: the obstructive pattern. In health, most airway resistance sits in the larger airways, not the small ones.

### Obstructive and restrictive patterns

An **obstructive** pattern lowers FEV1 more than FVC, so FEV1/FVC falls. A **restrictive** pattern lowers TLC; FEV1 and FVC fall together and the ratio is normal or high. Both are judged against reference limits, and a low FVC alone does not prove restriction: it goes with a low TLC less than half the time.

**Worked problem, part 3: two made-up spirograms with made-up lower limits.** Volunteer 1: FEV1 1.5 L, FVC 3.0 L, ratio 0.50, below a limit of 0.68: obstructive. Volunteer 2: FEV1 2.2 L, FVC 2.6 L, ratio 0.85, within limits, but plethysmographic TLC 3.9 L, below a limit of 4.6 L: restrictive, which the low FVC alone would not have shown.

Three diseases, read through the one idea as changes to the recoil curve:

- **Emphysema: too little recoil.** Destroyed alveolar walls lower elastic recoil, so compliance rises. The resting balance with the chest wall moves to a larger volume, so FRC rises; airways close earlier in expiration, so RV rises; and with less recoil opposing the inspiratory muscles, TLC usually rises. Low recoil means low maximal flow, and without radial traction small airways compress earlier, so FEV1 falls more than FVC and the ratio falls.
- **Fibrosis: too much tissue recoil.** Compliance and TLC fall. High recoil drives high flow for the volume, and stiff tissue holds airways open, so the ratio is normal or high. The elastic work of a breath of a given size rises, as in part 1, so breathing shifts to small, fast breaths.
- **RDS: too much surface recoil.** The main problem is the surface term, not the tissue, until surfactant is supplied.

Breathing costs elastic work (part 1) and resistive work, and the central drive picks a tidal volume and rate that tend to minimise the total ([control of breathing](/lessons/control-of-breathing/); for respiratory muscles competing with the legs for blood, [exercise physiology](/lessons/exercise-physiology/)).

In one line: the pressure across the lung matches its recoil, the wet lining supplies much of it, and surfactant makes each breath cheap and keeps a newborn's lungs open.

## Going deeper

The main line above does not depend on these details.

### Limits of the one idea

During flow, lung tissue adds a little friction, so transpulmonary pressure slightly exceeds pure elastic recoil during inspiration and falls slightly short of it during expiration. "Recoil at its current volume" means the curve for the direction of the breath (hysteresis). Pleural pressure also varies by region. If flow rises and falls smoothly through inspiration, the most negative pleural pressure, about −8.3 cmH2O, comes slightly before end-inspiration, where flow and recoil terms overlap; −8 is the end-inspiration value. Some texts, and most ventilator papers, define transpulmonary pressure with airway-opening pressure in place of alveolar pressure; the two are equal only at zero flow.

### Pressure units and where pleural pressure is read

1 mmHg = 1.36 cmH2O, so −5 cmH2O is about −3.7 mmHg, and the −3 to −4 mmHg often quoted for pleural pressure is the same thing. Pleural pressure is less negative at the base of the upright lung than at the apex. An esophageal balloon in the lower esophagus is the usual way to estimate it, and it reports the region near the esophagus, not the whole pleural space.

### Lung and chest wall together

Lung and chest wall are in series: one breath must stretch both, so their stiffnesses (elastances) add. In compliances, 1/Crs = 1/CL + 1/Ccw, where Crs is the whole respiratory system's compliance, CL the lung's and Ccw the chest wall's. The whole system is less compliant than either part.

Worked problem part 1 counted only the extra elastic work on the lung. Since 1 cmH2O x L = 98.07 Pa x 0.001 m3 = 0.098 J, that is about 0.074 J per breath, and the stiffer lung's about 0.15 J. For a linear curve from the relaxed FRC, elastic work per breath on the whole respiratory system is 0.5 x VT² / Crs; with a chest-wall compliance of 0.2 L per cmH2O, the same breath costs about 1.4 cmH2O x L. Halving the lung's compliance doubles the lung's part (0.75 to 1.5 cmH2O x L) and raises the total by about half, to about 2.1.

### Helium dilution and typical volumes

Rebreathe helium in a closed circuit. The helium is not absorbed, so the amount before, C1 x V1 in the spirometer, equals the amount after, spread through spirometer and lung, C2 x (V1 + V lung). Divide both sides by C2 and subtract V1: V lung = C1 x V1 / C2 − V1. For scale, one open textbook gives a tidal volume of about 500 mL and a TLC of about 6,000 mL in adult men and 4,200 mL in adult women: rough averages, which is why individuals are judged against reference equations instead.

### Small into large

The small-into-large emptying, or "Laplace instability", is a result of theory and of films in vitro. Whether a small alveolus empties into a larger one depends on how steeply tension changes with area, the film's surface dilatational modulus, E\*. In analyses of DPPC and clinical surfactant monolayers in vitro, uniform inflation is predicted to be stable when 2E\* minus T is positive; a tension that falls with area, but not steeply enough, still lets the small one empty. Real alveoli are also tethered: in Wilson and Bachofen's structural model, alveolar walls are platforms for surface tension, and neighbours share walls, so one alveolus cannot shrink alone like a free bubble. How much film instability matters once tethering is counted is an open question.

### Pattle and Clements

Pattle, the physicist who inferred the low-tension lining in 1955, worked at Porton Down. Clements cited him, but Clements's own estimates from lung bubbles put the lowest tension at 10 to 15 dynes per cm, not the under-1 value Pattle had inferred.

### From association to treatment

Nebulized DPPC alone showed no clear benefit in 1960s trials. In the early 1970s Enhorning and Robertson found that natural surfactant improved lung expansion in preterm rabbits. In 1980 Fujiwara and colleagues reported improved oxygenation in ten preterm infants given a modified bovine surfactant, with no untreated controls. The randomized trials that followed supplied the controls.

### The genetics

The 1994 SP-B mutation was a frameshift, found in full-term infants who died of respiratory failure; the 1995 knockout mice, with both copies of the SP-B gene disrupted, died at birth. The 2004 ABCA3 study sequenced 21 infants, found mutations in 16 (76 percent), and reported abnormal lamellar bodies in lung tissue from four. The study does not show what ABCA3 carries, how each mutation acts, or that preterm respiratory distress is genetic. Later, in adult mice whose SP-B could be switched off, high surface tension injured the alveolar epithelium before edema formed.

### Where airway resistance sits

**Airway resistance** is the pressure difference per unit flow. Poiseuille's fourth-power rule assumes steady laminar flow in a rigid tube ([blood and vessels](/lessons/blood-and-vessels/)), and airways are deformable. Small airways are so numerous, in parallel, that classic measurements put all airways under 2 mm in diameter at roughly 10 to 20 percent of airway resistance in health. The squeeze-point account of forced expiration used above is the classic teaching model; wave-speed theory is the fuller account.

### How the airways condition and clean the air

{/* DIAGRAM: bronchus wall, lumen outward: mucus, watery layer */}

Inspired air reaches the alveoli at 37 degrees C, saturated with water vapour (47 mmHg), which [ventilation and perfusion](/lessons/ventilation-perfusion/) subtracts from barometric pressure in the alveolar gas equation. A separate rule, Henry's law, says dissolved gas is proportional to partial pressure (also in [ventilation and perfusion](/lessons/ventilation-perfusion/)). Nasal hairs trap large particles; mucus from goblet cells and glands catches smaller ones, and cilia sweep it toward the pharynx on a watery layer set by epithelial ion transport, including CFTR ([membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/)). Alveolar macrophages take up most particles that reach the alveoli. A panting dog uses its airway surfaces for evaporative heat loss ([thermoregulation](/lessons/thermoregulation/)).
