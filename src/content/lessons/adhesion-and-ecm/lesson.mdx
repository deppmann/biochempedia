    story: >-
      Takeichi's 1977 paper turned on a practical point: how you take cells apart decides what
      they can do afterwards. He dissociated Chinese hamster V79 cells three ways, with EDTA,

      family of molecules that could sort cells.

    story: >-
      Fibronectin is a large matrix protein built from many repeated modules, and cells stick
      to it. By the early 1980s the question was which small part of it the cell actually
      recognises. Michael Pierschbacher and Erkki Ruoslahti answered by making short synthetic
      pieces of the protein's cell-attachment region and testing whether they could do the job
      of the whole. In 1984 they reported in Nature that small synthetic fragments could
      duplicate fibronectin's cell-attachment activity, and that the activity could be
      accounted for by four amino acids: arginine, glycine, aspartate and serine, RGDS. The same
      sequence appeared in at least five other proteins, so they proposed that it might be a
      recognition signal shared by several of them. The short code made matrix binding easy to
      test. A soluble RGD peptide can compete with a matrix protein for its receptor, and the
      Horwitz group used such peptides the following year to block purified receptor from
      binding fibronectin and laminin. Later crystal structures caught an RGD ligand sitting in
      the head of an integrin.

techniques:
  - name: "Dissociation and reaggregation"
    blurb: >-
      Cells are separated with a chosen treatment (EDTA, trypsin with calcium, trypsin with
      EDTA), then shaken together and counted as they form clumps. Which treatments spare
      aggregation, and whether calcium is needed during re-aggregation, separates one adhesion
      mechanism from another.
  - name: "Attachment assay with a function-blocking antibody"
    blurb: >-
      A known number of cells is put into wells coated with a matrix protein, unbound cells are
      washed off, and the rest are counted. An antibody that lowers the count, compared with a
      control antibody, implicates its target in attachment but does not show direct binding.
  - name: "Equilibrium gel filtration"
    blurb: >-
      A purified receptor runs through a gel filtration column pre-equilibrated with its
      ligand, so a complex that falls apart and re-forms quickly stays occupied throughout. A
      complex that runs larger than the receptor alone is evidence of direct association.
  - name: "Single-molecule force spectroscopy"
    blurb: >-
      One protein or one complex is held between a surface and a bead in magnetic tweezers or
      an optical trap and pulled with a known force of a few piconewtons. It shows whether
      domains unfold, binding sites open, or a bond lasts longer under load.

      - "Fibrillar type I collagen gathered into long ropes"
      - "Type IV collagen assembled into sheet-like networks"
      - "Proteoglycans that bind water and form a swollen gel"

      Proteoglycans carry many glycosaminoglycan chains that hold water, and the resulting

  - stem: "A reconstituted cadherin-catenin complex is held against an actin filament in an optical trap, and bond lifetimes are measured at different forces in made-up data. The median lifetime is 0.5 s at 2 pN, 3 s at 8 pN and 0.4 s at 20 pN. Which description fits these data?"

    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Lifetime rises from 2 to 8 pN and then falls by 20 pN, so force stabilises the bond only
      over a limited range, the behaviour of a catch bond. A slip bond would shorten steadily

The film followed one grip, from a neighbour's cadherin to the cell's actin, and showed calcium holding its outer link. The rest of the page names every link in the four anchoring junctions, uses the chain to predict where skin splits, takes apart the matrix at the far end, answers the film's closing question in "Which links feel the pull?", and ends with the experiments that found the links. Each section asks what one link is made of, or what happens when it fails or is pulled.

> **The one idea, in full.** In the anchoring junctions of epithelia (the cell sheets that line and cover the body) and of cells on matrix, every grip is one chain of four links: a partner outside (a neighbour's cadherin, or a matrix protein), an adhesion receptor, adaptor proteins, and a cable inside the cell. The film drew the simplest case, one unbranched chain, where the pull passes through every link and a missing link cuts the path. A real junction is the fuller case: many chains, some branched, share and shift the load while bonds let go and rebind. So a missing or weak part weakens adhesion, but how a junction fails also depends on load sharing, bond kinetics and remodelling.

A **cable** is a filament inside the cell (actin, or keratin, an intermediate filament); collagen, outside, is a **rope**.

## The chain, link by link

The film built the adherens and integrin chains; the table adds the two keratin chains and names every link.

| Junction | Joins | Partner outside | Receptor | Adaptors | Cable |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adherens junction | cell to cell | the neighbour's cadherin | classical cadherin | β-catenin and α-catenin (αE-catenin in epithelia); p120 near the membrane | actin |
| Desmosome | cell to cell | the neighbour's desmosomal cadherin | desmogleins and desmocollins | plakoglobin and plakophilins, then desmoplakin | keratin |
| Focal adhesion (cultured cells) | cell to matrix | fibronectin and other matrix proteins | integrin (an α-β pair) | talin, reinforced by vinculin | actin |
| Hemidesmosome | cell to basement membrane | laminin-332 | integrin α6β4, with collagen XVII | plectin | keratin |

In the keratin rows, desmogleins and desmocollins are the desmosome's own cadherins, held to keratin by a plaque of the three adaptors; laminin-332 is a basement-membrane laminin, and collagen XVII is a second membrane anchor beside the integrin.

**Cadherins.** A classical cadherin crosses the membrane once and usually binds the same kind of cadherin on the neighbouring cell (homophilic binding). Adherens junctions are belts of cadherin circling the top of an epithelial cell ([epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/) builds the full sheet). p120-catenin binds the tail close to the membrane, the juxtamembrane region, and helps keep the cadherin at the surface. β-Catenin has a second job: outside junctions it is the transcriptional co-activator of the Wnt pathway ([the Wnt lesson](/lessons/wnt-signaling/)), and cells regulate how much goes to each job.

**Integrins.** Mammals pair 18 α and 8 β subunits into 24 integrins, each with its own preferred ligands. Eight, including the main fibronectin receptor α5β1, recognise the film's RGD tag (Arg-Gly-Asp); others, such as α6β4, bind by other sites. The grip is switched from inside. Bent with its head closed, or extended with its head still closed, an integrin binds its ligand weakly; extended with its head open, it binds strongly. Talin, helped by a second adaptor called kindlin, binds the β tail and shifts the integrin toward the open shape. That shift is integrin **activation**.

Fibronectin also ties the chain into the rest of the matrix: besides integrins, it binds collagen and heparan sulfate (one of the matrix's sugar chains). Crawling cells make and break their focal adhesions constantly ([migration and cilia](/lessons/migration-and-cilia/)); the adhesions' signals through Rho GTPases belong to [integrins and Rho GTPases](/lessons/integrins-and-rho-gtpases/).

**Predict.** Carcinoma cells (cancer cells of epithelial origin) that make E-cadherin but no α-catenin are put in calcium. Do they stick together, and how well?

In the PC9 cells studied by Hirano and colleagues (*Cell*, 1992), E-cadherin was present, but calcium-dependent aggregation was greatly reduced and the cells grew mostly singly; putting α-catenin back restored firm, compact aggregates. Binding outside is not enough for strong adhesion, though the experiment did not measure the force on one contact.

</details>

## Where the chain breaks: blisters

Here the film's idea does real work: the level of a split points to the layer whose chain failed. In skin, basal cells, the bottom layer of the epidermis, hold their neighbours at desmosomes and the basement membrane at hemidesmosomes, and collagen VII anchors that membrane to the dermis below.

**Predict.** Three children have inherited blistering of the skin (epidermolysis bullosa), from faults in keratin 14, in laminin-332, or in collagen VII. Where does each blister split? Then two adults make antibodies against their own skin proteins, one against desmoglein 3 and one against collagen XVII. Where does each of theirs split?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

- **Keratin 14:** inside the basal cells, because the cable itself fails (epidermolysis bullosa simplex).
- **Laminin-332:** within the basement membrane, where the hemidesmosome's partner should be (junctional epidermolysis bullosa).
- **Collagen VII:** below the basement membrane, where it should anchor to the dermis (dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa).
- **Anti-desmoglein 3:** between epidermal cells just above the basal layer, as their desmosomes let go (pemphigus vulgaris).
- **Anti-collagen XVII:** below the epidermis, at the hemidesmosomes (bullous pemphigoid). Complement, inflammatory cells and protein-cutting enzymes add to the damage, so this is no simple snap of one link.

The split narrows the defect to a layer or adhesion complex; since several proteins can fail at one level, naming the protein takes genetic or antibody tests.

</details>

**Where the rule stops.** Some cell-cell contacts need no calcium. The Chinese hamster V79 cells that Masatoshi Takeichi studied in 1977 had a calcium-independent mechanism beside the calcium-dependent one, though it looked like simple clumping; immunoglobulin-family proteins such as NCAM are clearer cases. T-cadherin and one form of NCAM are held to the membrane by a lipid (GPI) anchor, so they bind with no adaptor and no cable. And not every integrin grips matrix: on white blood cells the integrin LFA-1 binds ICAM-1 on another cell.

## The far end: what the matrix is made of

Every integrin chain in the film ended in the matrix; here is that far end, taken apart.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/adhesion-and-ecm/matrix-parts-and-jobs.webp" width={1200} height={744} alt="AI-generated illustration: a row of epithelial cells sits on a thin basement membrane sheet; below it, collagen ropes, wavy elastic fibres, a fibronectin mesh, and a water-holding proteoglycan gel threaded by hyaluronan are drawn side by side." caption="The matrix has parts matched to jobs: collagen ropes resist pulling, elastic fibres recoil, fibronectin is a mesh cells grip, basement membranes are thin sheets of laminin and type IV collagen, and a proteoglycan gel resists compression." />

| Part | What it is | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Fibrillar collagens | three chains wound into a triple helix; the helices pack side by side into fibrils | the ropes: strength under tension in bone and tendon |
| Elastic fibres | stretchy fibres | let artery walls and lung stretch and recoil |
| Fibronectin | fibrillar networks | a mesh cells grip through integrins; also binds growth factors and other signalling molecules |
| Basement membrane | two linked networks, one of laminins and one of type IV collagen (which forms networks, not ropes) | a thin sheet under almost every epithelium |
| Proteoglycans | core proteins carrying long glycosaminoglycan chains, sugar polymers studded with negative charges | a hydrated gel that resists compression, as in cartilage |
| Hyaluronan | a very long, unsulfated glycosaminoglycan with no protein core | a water-binding polymer, and the backbone for aggregates of hyaluronan-binding proteoglycans such as aggrecan |

Cells secrete fibrillar collagens as soluble precursors and trim them outside ([the secretory pathway](/lessons/secretory-pathway/)); the triple helix is in [protein 3D structure](/lessons/protein-3d-structure/), and the sugar chemistry in [carbohydrates](/lessons/carbohydrates/).

The film's gel, charged chains drawing in water, was the short version. Fuller: the fixed negative charges attract mobile positive ions, and that excess of ions draws water in by osmosis, so the gel swells and pushes back when squeezed. Cartilage's aggrecan binds along hyaluronan into aggregates large enough to stay trapped in the collagen net, which holds the swelling gel in.

The matrix is also a store. Growth factors held in it can be released when the matrix is cut or pulled, and the pull can come from cells tugging through their own integrin chains. Latent TGF-β is the standard example ([TGF-β and Smads](/lessons/tgf-beta-and-smads/)). Neurons use basal lamina too: the agrin that organises the [neuromuscular junction](/lessons/neuromuscular-junction/) is deposited there.

**Predict.** A mutation puts alanine in place of one glycine in type I collagen's triple helix (the collagen-like peptide in the 3D viewer carries this kind of swap). Which tissues fail, and under which load?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Tissues that rely on collagen ropes for strength under tension, above all bone, which fractures easily. Glycine sits at every third position, where the strands pack closest, and a larger side chain loosens the helix there. Glycine substitutions in type I collagen are the commonest cause of severe osteogenesis imperfecta, brittle bone disease (Marini and colleagues, 2017).

</details>

## Which links feel the pull?

This section answers the film's closing question: does the pull change the links? Cells load their chains constantly: myosin II, the motor that slides actin filaments past one another, pulls on them from inside.

A puzzle: work in cells put α-catenin between the cadherin–β-catenin complex and actin, yet in 2005 purified α-catenin would not bind that complex and an actin filament at the same time. With no force applied, the link looked missing.

A bond's **lifetime** is how long it holds before letting go; for an ordinary bond, a **slip bond**, pulling harder shortens it. The forces here are piconewtons (pN), trillionths of a newton.

**Predict.** Suppose the α-catenin–actin link is weak at rest but holds longer when pulled, up to a point. Sketch how its bond lifetime should change as the force rises from zero to about 20 pN. Why might a test tube with no force miss the link?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Rise, then fall: short at almost no force, longer at a moderate force, short again once force tears the bond apart. A bond that lives longer as force rises, over a limited range, is a **catch bond**. On this hypothesis a zero-force assay mostly samples the weak state and might miss the link. In 2014 a reconstituted cadherin–catenin complex held against an actin filament in an optical trap (a laser that holds a bead and reads the force on it) behaved this way: some of its bonds lasted longer under a small force (numbers in Going deeper). That is one way to reconcile the 2005 result, not proof of why it came up empty.

</details>

The film's chain, where each link simply held or broke, was the simple case. A link's lifetime can depend on the pull: a moderate force can steady the catenin–actin link compared with its own low-force state (the experiment did not compare it with the junction's other links).

The integrin chain has another force-sensitive link. Talin's rod is a stack of helical bundles, some hiding vinculin-binding sites. Stretching a purified piece of the rod unfolds bundles and exposes those sites. Each vinculin that binds adds another tie to actin, so pulling can recruit reinforcements. α-Catenin has a hidden vinculin site of its own that tension opens (Yonemura and colleagues, 2010; Yao and colleagues, 2014), so the cadherin chain can reinforce itself the same way.

Force is not the only influence. Talin and vinculin each fold shut, hiding their binding sites, a state called **autoinhibition**. In mouse fibroblasts, engineered forms released from it bound each other even with myosin II blocked. So force and biochemical regulation can both shape assembly; whether a cell's own signals stand in for force at the same steps in a tissue is not known, and a few piconewtons on one molecule are not the traction of a whole cell.

In culture, naive mesenchymal stem cells on soft, brain-like gels drifted toward neuronal-like cells, and on rigid, bone-like gels toward bone; blocking myosin II removed the effect (Engler and colleagues, 2006). One reading is that a cell feels stiffness by pulling on its chains and sensing how much the far end gives. This is a culture result, not a statement about fate in a body.

## Reading adhesion assays

This section is a separate tool: the links were found by experiment, and reading one is how you would test a link yourself. Classic cadherin and integrin experiments used **dissociation and reaggregation** assays (separate cells, then measure re-clumping), **attachment** assays (count cells left on a coated well after washing) and **antibody-blocking** versions of them.

**Worked problem (made-up data).** Wells are coated with fibronectin or with bovine serum albumin (BSA) only, a protein cells do not grip. Each well gets 20,000 fibroblasts. After 30 minutes and a standard wash, the retained cells are:

- fibronectin, no antibody: 14,000
- fibronectin plus isotype-control antibody (same antibody class, irrelevant target): 13,600
- fibronectin plus integrin-blocking antibody: 3,200
- BSA only: 1,000

Viability is above 95% in every well, so lost cells were not dead cells.

**Predict.** The BSA well counts cells that stick to any surface. If you compared raw counts in the blocking and isotype wells without removing them, would the antibody look more or less effective than it really is?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Less effective: background cells sit in both wells and dilute the drop. Raw counts give 1 − 3,200/13,600, about 76.5%, against 82.5% in Step 2.

</details>

*Step 1: subtract the background.* The BSA well shows cells that stick to plastic or protein nonspecifically. Specific attachment is 13,600 − 1,000 = 12,600 with the isotype control, and 3,200 − 1,000 = 2,200 with the blocking antibody.

*Step 2: inhibition.*

> **inhibition = 1 − (blocking well − background) / (isotype well − background)**

1 − 2,200/12,600 = 1 − 0.175 = 0.825, or about 83%.

*Step 3: why the isotype well.* Of the 20,000 seeded, 70% attached with no antibody, 68% with the isotype control, 16% with the blocking antibody and 5% on BSA. An irrelevant antibody cost 2 points; comparing with the no-antibody well would credit that drop to the integrin.

*Step 4: what this does not show.* An antibody can block by sitting near the receptor, or by locking it in an inactive shape. Lower attachment implicates the integrin in attachment; it does not show that the integrin touches fibronectin. Horwitz and colleagues made that next step in 1985 with purified antigen in equilibrium gel filtration, and the antibody blocked the purified laminin association.

## Going deeper

### The measurements behind the force section

**Talin unfolding.** The rod has 13 helical bundles. In 2009 Armando del Rio and colleagues stretched a purified rod fragment at 12 pN and saw fluorescent vinculin-head binding events rise from one at most to as many as three. Magnetic-tweezer work (a magnet pulling on a bead tethered to the protein) on the mouse talin1 rod in 2016 found bundles unfolding at about 5 to 25 pN and refolding when force fell, and a model built on those rates suggests the rod can act as a force buffer.

**The catch-bond numbers.** Craig Buckley, Jiongyi Tan and colleagues (2014) built the complex with zebrafish αE-catenin and β-catenin. A subpopulation of bonds lasted up to 25 seconds at 5 to 10 pN, and a two-state catch-bond model fit the force-lifetime data better than the alternatives tested; 25 s is the long tail, not the typical lifetime.

**Gel relaxation in culture.** In 2024 Qiao and colleagues varied how fast gels relax under strain while holding initial stiffness fixed, and greater relaxation favoured astrocytes from rat and mouse neural stem cells. Like the Engler result, this is a culture system.
