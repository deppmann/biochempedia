**In the growing leg of a grasshopper embryo, the first two axons to head for the central nervous system have no other axons to follow. Kill a particular guidepost cell along their route before they get there, and they lose their way.**

That result, from David Bentley and Michael Caudy in 1983, is a clue to how a growth cone navigates. It does not carry a map. It reads its surroundings one stretch at a time, and some of what it reads is other cells. This lesson asks what those signals are, how a long route gets broken into short pieces, and why the same signal can mean different things to different axons.

## What provides directional information to growth cones?

The growth cone is the moving tip of a growing axon: a fan of actin with fingers called filopodia, backed by microtubules from the axon shaft. It advances when adhesion grips the substrate, and it turns when that grip differs between its two sides (see [that lesson](/lessons/growth-cone/)). The question here is what tells it which side to favour.

The answers sort into four classes, set by two questions. Is the cue fixed to a cell or to the matrix between cells, so that it acts on contact, or is it released and spread through the tissue, so that it acts at a distance? And does it draw the growth cone in or push it away?

- **Contact attraction.** Laminin in the extracellular matrix; adhesion molecules such as L1 on other axons.
- **Contact repulsion.** Ephrins on cell surfaces, which make growth cones carrying the matching Eph receptor collapse and retreat.
- **Long-range attraction.** Netrin-1, secreted by the floor plate at the ventral midline of the spinal cord, was long taught as the classic case: a gradient that draws commissural axons, the ones that cross the midline, toward it. That model is under revision. In mice, netrin-1 laid down by ventricular-zone progenitors along the path carries much of the effect and acts locally, while floor-plate netrin, with Sonic hedgehog, may still act at a distance near the midline.
- **Long-range repulsion.** Slit at the same midline, and secreted semaphorins such as Sema3A.

The classes describe how a cue behaves in a given experiment, not a fixed property of the molecule. Netrin can act at short range when it is held on cell surfaces, and later lessons take each family in turn.

The cleanest test of whether a molecule can act as a long-range cue is a pipette. In 1979 Gundersen and Barrett set a fine pipette releasing nerve growth factor (NGF) beside growing axons of chick sensory neurons, and the growth cones turned toward it within about twenty minutes. The turn was more than faster growth, which is what makes it chemotaxis: steering up a gradient. The assay shows what a molecule can do in a dish, not what it does in the embryo; NGF itself is now thought to act there mainly on survival and branching.

The genome holds far too few genes to name every connection in a brain (the arithmetic is in [that lesson](/lessons/wiring-problem/)). The genome gets around this in two ways. Cues act in combination, so a few families give many distinct readings. And the route is broken into short legs.

## Break the long journey into manageable legs

A long trajectory is hard to steer in one go. A trail with signposts every few hundred metres is easy. Axons use the same trick. The neuron's own ability to grow is not enough: growing axons need signposts along the way.

The first axons through a territory are **pioneers**. They travel when the tissue is small and distances are short, and they have nothing to follow. Later axons, the **followers**, often grow along them. Along the pioneers' route sit **guidepost cells** and **intermediate targets**: places where the growth cone stops, reads something and changes course.

The worked case is the grasshopper leg. Near the tip of the developing leg, a pair of sensory neurons called Ti1 sends axons toward the central nervous system. The route has several turns, and at some of them sit young neurons that the Ti1 growth cones touch just before they turn. One of these is a pair of cells called Cx1.

Bentley and Caudy wanted to know whether the growth cones need Cx1. Insect neurons carry a surface sugar that antibodies against horseradish peroxidase recognize, so antibodies of this kind can mark identified neurons. Bentley and Caudy selectively destroyed the Cx1 cells before the Ti1 growth cones arrived and then examined the pioneers' paths. The pioneers failed to take the normal route and sent branches in abnormal directions.

The conclusion is narrow, and that is its strength. At this turn, in this preparation, the Cx1 cells are needed. The experiment does not say what Cx1 provides, whether adhesion or a signal, and later work by Caudy and Bentley found that the full Ti1 route is built from several legs, read partly from guideposts and partly from boundaries in the surrounding tissue. A guidepost is one important cue among several.

## The labeled pathway hypothesis

Followers do not grow along just any axon. When axons grow bundled together they are **fasciculated**; when one leaves its bundle it **defasciculates**.

Jonathan Raper, Michael Bastiani and Corey Goodman showed how selective this is, again in grasshopper embryos, whose central neurons can be named and filled with dye one at a time. A neuron called G grows into a region crossed by many longitudinal axon bundles, joins one of them, the A/P fascicle, and grows forward along the P axons within it. Its filopodia reach other bundles, yet it chooses this one. When the team killed the P neurons before G arrived, G failed to extend far along its normal route and did not adopt any other bundle within reach. This led to the **labeled pathway hypothesis**: each bundle carries surface labels, and each growth cone has receptors for some of them. The ablation alone cannot tell whether P axons instructed G or simply offered the one usable surface.

The search for the labels moved to the fruit fly, Drosophila. It found adhesion molecules that control which axons bundle together, rather than a unique label for each bundle. **Fasciclin II** is a member of the immunoglobulin (Ig) superfamily of adhesion molecules and binds Fasciclin II on a neighbouring axon. Its vertebrate relative is NCAM. The two phenotypes are complementary. Without Fasciclin II, axons that normally travel together in certain pathways drift apart. With extra Fasciclin II in motor neurons, motor axons that should leave their nerve at a branch point stay stuck in it and bypass their muscles. Opposite manipulations give opposite results, so the amount of adhesion between axons matters, partly because it changes how growth cones respond to other cues.

A second fly gene, **beaten path** (*beat*), encodes a protein made by motor neurons, first described as secreted; later work places it on the axon surface. In *beat* mutants, motor axons fail to leave their nerve at choice points.

### Design problem

A Drosophila motor axon in a *beat* mutant fails to leave its nerve. Predict the phenotype of a *beat*; *fasII* double mutant, which lacks both Beat and Fasciclin II, and say what it tells you about how Beat works.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Consider two models. In the first, Beat steers the axon toward its muscle. Then removing Fasciclin II should not help: the axon would still have no instruction to go to the muscle, and the double mutant would look like *beat* or worse. In the second, Beat lets an axon leave by weakening the Fasciclin II adhesion that holds it to its neighbours. Then removing Fasciclin II should restore defasciculation, and the double mutant would look closer to normal.

The second is what Fambrough and Goodman reported in 1996: reducing Fasciclin II suppressed the *beat* phenotype. So at this choice point Beat acts at least partly by opposing adhesion between axons. Later work found that Beat also serves as a receptor for Sidestep, a cue on the tissues motor axons follow, so it helps point the way too. A further check follows: extra Fasciclin II in a normal fly should mimic a *beat* mutant, and it does: extra Fasciclin II on motor neurons produced bypass phenotypes (Lin and Goodman, 1994).

</details>

Vertebrates show the same logic at choice points. Kathryn Tosney and Lynn Landmesser labeled chick motor axons growing into the hindlimb. At the plexus, where motor axons sort into dorsal and ventral nerve trunks, and where muscle nerves branch off, the growth cones were larger, more lamellipodial and followed more convoluted paths. In straight stretches they were small and simple. Later time-lapse imaging, in mouse retinal axons, matched complex shapes to growth cones that had paused. And growth cone pausing is closely related to axon branching. Branches tend to form where a growth cone has stopped to read its surroundings.

## Do growth cones like more adhesive or less adhesive substrate?

Given a choice in culture, growth cones tend to follow the more adhesive surface (Paul Letourneau, 1975, with chick sensory neurons). That makes it tempting to say adhesion is what they want. But do growth cones grow faster on the more adhesive surface? Often not: neurons can grow more slowly on more adhesive substrates, and in several culture studies how sticky a surface is turned out to be a poor predictor of growth rate.

The clutch model explains why. With too little grip, the actin network slips backward and the edge does not advance. With too much, the growth cone cannot let go of old adhesions to move on. On simple charged coatings growth is fastest at intermediate adhesion. On matrix proteins and adhesion molecules, what the surface signals can matter more than how hard it grips.

**Laminin** is the clearest case. It is a large glycoprotein of basement membranes, and in the early 1980s several laboratories found that it strongly promotes neurite growth in culture. Neurons read laminin through **integrins**, receptors made of an alpha and a beta chain. In 1986 Kevin Tomaselli, Louis Reichardt and John Bixby blocked integrins with antibodies on dishes where cell bodies could still attach through a second coating. Outgrowth on laminin stopped while the cells stayed down, so integrins were needed for extension, not only to hold the cell on. Integrins link the matrix to actin and signal through Rho-family GTPases ([that lesson](/lessons/integrins-and-rho-gtpases/)).

Growth cones carry three main families of adhesion molecule:

- **Cadherins**, such as N-cadherin, which need calcium and bind the same cadherin on another cell.
- **Ig-superfamily CAMs**, such as NCAM and L1, which bind the same molecule or a different one. Fasciclin II and neuroglian are their fly relatives.
- **Integrins**, which bind matrix proteins such as laminin. Mammals have 18 alpha and 8 beta chains, forming 24 known pairs.

A uniform coat of laminin permits growth but gives no direction. A border or a gradient can.

## Attraction and repulsion can flip.

The same cue can attract one axon and repel another. Netrin-1 attracts growth cones that carry the receptor DCC alone and repels those that carry DCC with a second receptor, UNC5. Inside a single growth cone, the answer can change too. In cultured Xenopus spinal neurons, lowering cAMP signaling turned attraction to netrin-1 into repulsion. Without laminin, Xenopus retinal growth cones are attracted by netrin-1, but laminin turns that response into repulsion.

So a cue is a message, and the receptors and internal state of the growth cone decide what it means. That is why a few cue families can wire many different axons: a cell that changes its receptors or its cAMP level reads the same tissue differently.

There is one more reason to expect several cues at each choice point. At many choice points, a contact cue works beside a long-range one, and an attractant beside a repellent. Overlapping cues make a choice more reliable, though losing a single system can still cause lasting errors, as the knockouts in the next lessons show.
