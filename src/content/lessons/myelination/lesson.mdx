summary: Myelin is glial membrane wound around an axon and squeezed tight. With sodium channels packed into the short gaps between sheaths, the nodes of Ranvier, it lets an action potential jump from node to node instead of creeping along, so the fastest myelinated fibers conduct many times faster than bare ones. In the brain and spinal cord, the oligodendrocytes that make myelin also help keep their axons alive. This lesson then asks how activity biases which axons keep their sheaths, how a peripheral axon tells its Schwann cell how thick to make the wrap, what holds the layers together, and what is lost when myelin is destroyed. Afterwards you can predict the effect of silencing an axon, changing the axon's neuregulin signal, removing myelin basic protein, or losing oligodendrocytes.

**Wrap an axon in tight layers of glial membrane, leave short bare gaps between the wraps, and its action potential stops creeping. It jumps from gap to gap. The largest myelinated fibers in mammals conduct at tens of meters per second, up to around 100, while thin bare fibers manage about a meter per second or less.**

That is myelin's main job. It has a second one: in the brain and spinal cord, the oligodendrocytes that make myelin also help keep their axons alive. This lesson starts with how the wrap makes the signal jump and how the glia support the axon. Then it asks how a glial cell decides which axons to wrap and how thickly, what holds the layers together, and what is lost when disease strips the myelin away.

## The main idea: insulation that makes the signal jump

Myelin is glial plasma membrane wound many times around an axon and squeezed until almost no cytoplasm is left between the turns.

Why wrap at all? Think of a leaky garden hose. A bare axon is like that: current leaks out across its membrane, and the action potential has to be rebuilt on every patch it reaches, so it creeps. Myelin stacks many membranes in series. That raises the resistance across the axon wall and lowers its capacitance, so current entering at one spot travels farther and charges the membrane ahead faster.

The axon exploits this by packing its voltage-gated sodium channels into short gaps between sheaths, the nodes of Ranvier. Each wrapped stretch is an internode. The action potential is regenerated only at the nodes and seems to leap from one to the next: saltatory conduction, from the Latin for "to jump".

The gain is large. In unmyelinated axons, conduction velocity rises roughly with the square root of diameter. In myelinated fibers it rises roughly in proportion to diameter. A classic estimate from mammalian peripheral nerve (Hursh, 1939) is about 6 meters per second for each micrometer of fiber diameter, so the largest mammalian fibers reach tens of meters per second, up to around 100, while thin unmyelinated fibers conduct at about a meter per second or less. Sheath thickness is described by the g ratio:

<ConductionSpeedPlot />

> **g ratio = axon diameter ÷ fiber diameter (axon plus myelin). Velocity of a large myelinated mammalian fiber ≈ 6 m/s × fiber diameter in µm.**

Healthy peripheral fibers usually have g ratios near 0.6 to 0.7, close to the value theory predicts gives the fastest conduction for a fiber of a given size.

The membrane itself suits the job. Like any membrane it is a lipid bilayer with proteins in it (see [that lesson](/lessons/membrane-structure-function/)), but it is unusually rich in lipid, roughly 70 to 80 percent of its dry weight, with a large share of cholesterol and of galactolipids such as galactocerebroside (see [that lesson](/lessons/lipids/)).

Two kinds of glia do the wrapping. In the brain and spinal cord, an oligodendrocyte sends out many processes, and each one makes an internode, usually on a different axon. In peripheral nerves, a myelinating Schwann cell makes a single internode on a single axon. Schwann cells that meet small axons do not myelinate; they hold several of them in grooves of their membrane. (Where both cell types come from is the subject of [that lesson](/lessons/origins-of-glia/).)

## A second job: oligodendrocytes keep axons alive

Insulation is not myelin's only job. Until recently saltatory conduction was treated as the whole purpose of myelin. In the brain and spinal cord, oligodendrocytes also help keep the axons they wrap alive, and broad reviews now count this support as a second major function.

The first evidence came from mouse mutants. Mice lacking the myelin membrane protein PLP, or the oligodendrocyte enzyme CNP, show long-term degeneration of their axons even though their myelin sheaths look normal or nearly so. The sheath was there; something else the glial cell provides was missing. In people, Pelizaeus-Merzbacher disease, caused by mutations in the *PLP* gene, also involves axon loss.

What does the glial cell supply? Part of the answer is fuel. A sheath covers most of the axon's surface, which limits the axon's access to nutrients from the fluid outside. Oligodendrocytes pass energy metabolites to the axon through a monocarboxylate transporter, MCT1 (Lee and colleagues, 2012). One proposal is that the oligodendrocyte breaks glucose down to lactate and hands the lactate to the axon, whose mitochondria use it to make ATP; the lactate would travel through narrow channels of cytoplasm left in the non-compact parts of the sheath. The axon's dependence on this support grows with age. How much of the support is lactate, and what else passes from glia to axon, is still being worked out.

## Which axons get wrapped: a program that activity tunes

Myelination is late and slow. In humans it begins before birth in the spinal cord and brainstem and continues through childhood and adolescence, and some association areas of the cerebral cortex are still gaining myelin in early adulthood.

In the brain and spinal cord, much of the program is built into the oligodendrocyte. In culture, oligodendrocytes will even wrap inert plastic fibers of a suitable diameter. This suggests that in the central nervous system the program is partly built into the glial cell and the axon biases it.

Once an oligodendrocyte can wrap, which axons does it keep? Zebrafish larvae have transparent spinal cords, so the first sheaths can be filmed as they form. Jacob Hines, Bruce Appel and colleagues found that most early wraps were withdrawn. When they silenced one labeled axon among normal neighbours, by making it express tetanus toxin, which blocks vesicle release, the silenced axons were less often myelinated and carried shorter sheaths. Sigrid Mensch, David Lyons and colleagues found that blocking vesicle release throughout the nervous system reduced the number of sheaths each oligodendrocyte made, and that raising activity increased it. Activity biased how many sheaths formed and where they stayed.

In mice, Erin Gibson, Michelle Monje and colleagues reported in 2014 that light-driven stimulation of neurons in premotor cortex increased the division of oligodendrocyte precursor cells and the making of new oligodendrocytes, and thickened myelin within the stimulated circuit. Together these results describe a developmental program that lays down myelin and stays open to adjustment by activity. These are larval fish and mouse cortex. How much activity-dependent myelin contributes to learning in adult humans is still open.

## In nerves, the axon tells the Schwann cell how much

In peripheral nerve, who decides: the glial cell or the axon? Grafting experiments in the 1970s, by Albert Aguayo's laboratory and by Weinberg and Spencer, put Schwann cells from a nerve with unmyelinated axons into a nerve with myelinated ones, or the reverse. The Schwann cells myelinated, or did not, as their new axons dictated. In the periphery, the axon gives the deciding signal, though the Schwann cell can act on it only with its basal lamina in place.

That last requirement came from cultures of sensory ganglia in which Schwann cells myelinate axons in a dish, so the medium, the matrix and the cells can be changed one at a time. Mary and Richard Bunge and their colleagues found that Schwann cells needed contact with an extracellular matrix, and in later purified cocultures that ascorbic acid, which cells need to build collagen, was required for both the basal lamina (the sheet of matrix the Schwann cell lays down around itself) and myelin.

The main axonal signal turned out to be a membrane protein on the axon, neuregulin-1 type III. It is not the only input. Schwann cells also need a receptor of their own, Gpr126, which raises cAMP inside them; in zebrafish lacking it, Schwann cells stop just short of myelinating. Neuregulin-1 comes in several forms; type III keeps its active, EGF-like part tethered near the axon surface, so a Schwann cell reads it mainly by touching the axon. The Schwann cell reads it with two receptor tyrosine kinases of the EGF receptor family (see [that lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)). ErbB3 binds neuregulin but has a weak kinase. ErbB2 binds no ligand but has a strong kinase. Paired, they signal.

The key experiments measured dose. In 2004 Galin Michailov, Klaus-Armin Nave and colleagues reported that mice with reduced neuregulin-1 expression had thinner peripheral myelin for a given axon size, a higher g ratio. Mice overproducing type III in neurons had thicker myelin. In 2005 Carla Taveggia and colleagues in James Salzer's laboratory reported that in culture, sensory neurons lacking type III were not myelinated, and that sympathetic neurons, whose axons normally stay bare, became myelinated when made to produce more of it. Mice with one working copy of the type III gene were hypomyelinated. A threshold amount of type III decides whether an axon is myelinated, and above that the amount helps set how thick the sheath is.

Inside the Schwann cell, the signal fans out along routes you have met:

- PI3K and Akt, which in other cells promote growth and survival, support myelin growth (see [that lesson](/lessons/pi3k-akt-insulin/)).
- The Ras-ERK cascade is activated as well (see [that lesson](/lessons/ras-to-mapk/)).
- PLC-gamma raises calcium, which activates the phosphatase calcineurin and frees the transcription factor NFAT. NFAT was proposed to help turn on *Krox20* (also called *Egr2*), a transcription factor that Schwann cells need in order to myelinate. How much this arm matters is still debated (see Going deeper).

Keep the scope. This is the rule for peripheral nerve. In the mouse brain and spinal cord, Nave's group found that oligodendrocytes still myelinate when neuregulin-1 and ErbB signaling are removed, so the peripheral rule cannot be copied to the central nervous system unchanged.

## What holds the layers together

In the electron microscope compact myelin shows a repeating pattern. A dark major dense line marks where the cytoplasmic faces of successive turns meet; a fainter intraperiod line marks where their outer faces meet.

Myelin basic protein (MBP) is small, strongly positive and sits on the cytoplasmic side of the membrane. It binds the negatively charged lipids of two facing cytoplasmic leaflets and pulls them together, squeezing out the cytoplasm. The seam it leaves is the major dense line. Other proteins hold the outer faces: proteolipid protein (PLP) in the central nervous system, and protein zero (P0), an immunoglobulin-like adhesion protein, in peripheral nerve. The two are not related in structure.

A mutant mouse called shiverer shows what this one protein is worth. Its neurons are present and so are its axons, but it trembles, and its brain myelin lacks the major dense line and nearly all its MBP. The mutant *MBP* gene has lost a large stretch of DNA, including most of its exons. Peripheral nerve in shiverer is far less affected, because P0 can also hold the cytoplasmic faces together there. A large deletion could also have damaged DNA next to the gene, so the proof came from putting the gene back: a normal *MBP* transgene bred into shiverer mice restored compact central myelin with major dense lines and stopped the tremor (Readhead and colleagues, 1987).

## Myelin lost, and repaired

Multiple sclerosis shows what losing central myelin costs. In its usual account, the immune system attacks myelin and oligodendrocytes in the brain, optic nerves and spinal cord, leaving scattered lesions, and axons are damaged too. It is a leading cause of non-traumatic neurological disability in young adults. Most patients begin with relapsing-remitting disease, many later enter a secondary progressive course, and a minority progress from the start (primary progressive).

A lesion takes away both of myelin's jobs. Without the sheath, saltatory conduction is lost. Without healthy oligodendrocytes, the axon loses its support. In long-lasting lesions, axon loss is thought to come mainly from the missing myelin rather than from direct damage by inflammation. That helps explain why disability can keep growing in patients whose inflammation has been quieted by treatment.

The nervous system tries to remyelinate lesions, and remyelination restores saltatory conduction and helps keep axons healthy. In animal models, oligodendrocyte precursor cells move in, mature and make new sheaths, which are typically thinner with shorter internodes. Repair reuses much of the developmental program, but in an inflamed adult lesion. In people the source is less certain: carbon dating of MS tissue found that oligodendrocytes in remyelinated-looking lesions were old cells, so surviving oligodendrocytes may make some of the new myelin. Repair often falls short, especially in long-standing lesions and in older patients.

### Design problem

In long-standing multiple sclerosis lesions, axons keep degenerating. Two causes are possible. The immune attack could damage axons directly, or axons could die because they have lost their oligodendrocytes. In a patient both happen at once. Design a mouse experiment that separates the two, and say what result would show that losing oligodendrocytes is enough to harm axons.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Take the immune system out of the question. Use mice that have no functional lymphocytes, such as mice lacking the gene *Rag1*. Kill their oligodendrocytes with a genetic method that acts only in those cells, and compare them with lymphocyte-free mice whose oligodendrocytes are left alone.

If axons degenerate only in the mice that lost their oligodendrocytes, loss of the glia is enough, with no immune attack needed. If the axons survive, direct immune damage is the more likely cause. A sharper test keeps the oligodendrocytes and removes one thing they supply, such as the transporter MCT1, and asks whether axons still degenerate.

Pohl, Suter, Franklin and colleagues reported in 2011 that genetically induced death of adult oligodendrocytes was followed by axon damage even in *Rag1*-deficient mice. Deleting MCT1 from cells of the oligodendrocyte lineage was later reported to cause late-onset hypomyelination and axon degeneration (Philips and colleagues, 2021).

## Going deeper

### Growing myelin in a dish

Myelin forms inside a living animal, where it is hard to watch. Culture solved that. Edith Peterson and Margaret Murray reported in 1955 that sensory ganglia from chick embryos made myelin in a dish. In 1967 Mary Bunge, Richard Bunge, Peterson and Murray described long-term cultures of dorsal root ganglia from fetal rats. Over several weeks, Schwann cells in these cultures wrapped sensory axons in compact myelin with nodes, one cell to one axon, much as in a real nerve. Because each ingredient could be changed, the dish became an experiment, and the matrix and ascorbic acid requirements described above came from such cultures.

### Zebrafish sheaths, by the numbers

The two zebrafish studies appeared together in 2015 in *Nature Neuroscience*. In the work of Hines, Appel and colleagues, many early wraps were withdrawn within about an hour and a half, and silenced axons were myelinated about 32 percent of the time, against 55 percent for control axons. In the work of Mensch, Lyons and colleagues, blocking vesicle release throughout the nervous system reduced the number of sheaths each oligodendrocyte made by about a third, and raising activity with a convulsant drug increased sheath number unless release was blocked.

### The calcineurin-NFAT dispute

Work from Gerald Crabtree's laboratory proposed that NFAT, once freed by calcineurin, joins Sox10 in the nucleus in turning on *Krox20*. That study removed calcineurin from all neural crest cells. When a later study removed it from Schwann cells alone, myelination and Krox20 were close to normal.

### Shiverer, step by step

A shiverer mouse begins to tremble in the second or third week after birth. In 1979 a French group reported that shiverer brain myelin lacked the major dense line and nearly all its MBP. In the mid-1980s laboratories including those of Leroy Hood and Richard Lazzarini showed that the mutant *MBP* gene had lost about 20 kilobases of DNA, including most of the gene's exons.

Carol Readhead, Leroy Hood, Richard Sidman and colleagues then put the gene back. They injected a normal copy of the mouse *MBP* gene, with its own regulatory DNA so that it would be turned on in oligodendrocytes, into fertilized mouse eggs, and bred the resulting transgenic mice with shiverer mice until they had animals homozygous for the shiverer deletion that also carried the transgene. They compared these with shiverer littermates without the transgene and with normal mice. The transgenic shiverer mice made MBP at about a quarter of the normal level, formed compact myelin with major dense lines, and no longer shivered or died young. Even partial MBP goes a long way. Had a transgene made enough MBP, in the right forms and in oligodendrocytes, without improving the myelin or the tremor, something else in the deletion would probably have mattered. The 1987 study was one of the early uses of a transgene to cure a mouse mutant.

### Multiple sclerosis: history, numbers and risk

Jean-Martin Charcot's description of multiple sclerosis is usually dated to 1868. The disease is diagnosed most often between about 20 and 40 and affects women two to three times as often as men. Relapsing-remitting disease is by far the most common course at onset. A fourth category, progressive-relapsing, was dropped in the 2013 revision of the clinical course descriptions. Risk combines genes, with variants of the immune gene *HLA-DRB1* the strongest, and environment, notably earlier infection with Epstein-Barr virus.
