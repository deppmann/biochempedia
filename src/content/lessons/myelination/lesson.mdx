summary: Myelin is glial membrane wound around an axon and squeezed tight. With sodium channels packed into the short gaps between sheaths, the nodes of Ranvier, it lets an action potential jump from node to node instead of creeping along, and the speed gain is largest on large axons. Nerves do match the wrap to the axon, and this lesson follows how. In peripheral nerve the axon decides, mainly through the amount of neuregulin-1 type III on its surface, working with the Schwann cell's basal lamina and its receptor Gpr126. In the brain and spinal cord the oligodendrocyte brings much of its own program, and axon cues such as caliber and activity bias it. Then come what holds the layers together, the oligodendrocyte's second job of keeping axons alive, and what is lost when myelin is destroyed. Afterwards you can predict the effect of changing the axon's neuregulin signal, blocking an axon's vesicle release, removing myelin basic protein, or losing oligodendrocytes.

hook: Wrap an axon in tight layers of glial membrane, leave short bare gaps, and its signal jumps from gap to gap instead of creeping.

techniques:
  - name: "Myelinating dorsal root ganglion cultures"
    blurb: >-
      Sensory ganglia, or purified sensory neurons with Schwann cells, are grown for weeks until
      the Schwann cells myelinate the axons. Because the medium, the matrix and the cells can be
      changed one at a time, the culture shows what each contributes to myelin formation.
  - name: "Electron microscopy of myelin"
    blurb: >-
      Thin sections of fixed nerve are imaged at high magnification so that single membrane
      layers are visible. The method shows whether myelin is compact, whether the major dense
      and intraperiod lines are present, and how thick the sheath is relative to its axon (the
      g ratio).
  - name: "Transgenic rescue"
    blurb: >-
      A normal copy of a gene is injected into fertilized eggs, and the transgene is bred into a
      mutant strain. If the added gene restores the normal structure and behavior, loss of that
      gene is enough to explain the mutant's defect.
  - name: "Mosaic live imaging in zebrafish"
    blurb: >-
      Single neurons in a transparent larva are made to express a fluorescent protein, with or
      without a blocker such as tetanus toxin, among unaltered neighbours. Time-lapse imaging
      then compares how glia treat the altered and the normal axons in the same animal.

  - In part, the axons have lost the support that myelin and oligodendrocytes give them

- stem: Mice with only one working copy of the gene for neuregulin-1 type III are compared with normal mice. For a peripheral axon of a given diameter, what should electron microscopy show?

  - A lower g ratio, because the sheath is thicker than normal for that axon
  - The same g ratio, because sheath thickness is set by the Schwann cell alone
  - A higher g ratio, because the sheath is thinner than normal for that axon

**Wrap an axon in tight layers of glial membrane, leave short bare gaps between the wraps, and its action potential stops creeping. It jumps from gap to gap. The largest myelinated fibers in mammals conduct at about 100 meters per second or a little more, while thin bare fibers manage about a meter per second or less.**

Yet many thin axons in your peripheral nerves are never wrapped, while their large neighbours carry dozens of turns. Two questions run through this lesson. Why does wrapping pay, and where does it pay most? And how does a developing nerve decide which axons get the wrap, and how much?

## Why wrap an axon?

Myelin is glial plasma membrane, made by oligodendrocytes in the brain and spinal cord and by Schwann cells in peripheral nerve. It is wound many times around an axon and squeezed until almost no cytoplasm is left between the turns. To see why that speeds the signal, take it in three steps.

1. **A patch must be charged before it fires.** Current from the active stretch spreads along the axon's inside to the patch ahead. That patch of membrane is a capacitor, which the current must charge to threshold before it can fire. In a bare axon, current also leaks out at every point, so the action potential has to be rebuilt on every patch. It creeps.
2. **Charging and leaking both slow the signal ahead.** Leaked current is lost; current that charges the membrane on the way is held there for the moment. Either way, less is left for the next stretch, so it reaches threshold later.
3. **Wrapping cuts both.** Each turn of the sheath adds two membranes in series, one after another across the current's path. A 5 µm axon at g = 0.65 (defined below), with an illustrative 17 nm per turn, would carry about 80 turns, so about 160 membranes; in an ideal sheath of identical compact membranes, the capacitance of the wall falls, and its resistance rises, by roughly that factor. Far more of the current now reaches the next bare gap and charges it to threshold quickly.

The axon exploits this by packing its voltage-gated sodium channels into short gaps between sheaths, the nodes of Ranvier. Each wrapped stretch is an internode. The action potential is regenerated at the nodes, where sodium channels are densely packed, and seems to leap from one to the next: saltatory conduction, from the Latin for "to jump".

### Two diameters, and the g ratio

Myelin work uses two diameters: the **axon diameter** (the axon alone) and the **fiber diameter** (axon plus myelin).

> **g ratio = axon diameter ÷ fiber diameter.** A 6 µm axon inside a 10 µm fiber has g = 6 ÷ 10 = 0.6. A thinner sheath on the same axon gives a *higher* g ratio.
>
> **For large myelinated mammalian fibers (several µm and up), velocity ≈ 6 m/s × fiber diameter in µm** (Hursh, 1939, an estimate from mammalian peripheral nerve).

In unmyelinated axons, conduction velocity rises roughly with the square root of diameter. In myelinated fibers it rises roughly in proportion to diameter. Part of the reason: as the axon widens, the sheath thickens in step, so the capacitance per unit length of the internode stays about the same while axial resistance falls. Internodes also lengthen roughly in proportion to fiber diameter. The time to cross one internode therefore stays about constant while the distance covered per jump grows, so speed rises in proportion to diameter.

Healthy peripheral fibers usually have g ratios near 0.6 to 0.7, close to the value theory predicts gives the fastest conduction for a fiber of a given size. That optimum is broad, though, and fibers in the brain and spinal cord sit somewhat above it (see Going deeper).

**Now the puzzle.** In peripheral nerve, axons up to about 1 µm across stay bare. Some cable models of small central fibers (Waxman and Bennett, 1972) put the speed break-even for myelin near 0.2 µm, and the estimate depends on the assumptions, so speed alone may not explain the peripheral cutoff (see Going deeper). Space and energy cost may matter too, and something in the tissue may decide.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** At a fixed g ratio, myelin makes speed grow in proportion to diameter rather than with its square root, so the speed gain from wrapping is largest on large axons.
>
> Nerves do match wrap to axon: large axons carry thick myelin and the thinnest carry none. How does development produce that match? In peripheral nerve, the axon decides: the amount of neuregulin-1 type III on its surface is the main axonal signal for whether a Schwann cell wraps it and how thickly, working together with the basal lamina and Gpr126. In the brain and spinal cord, the oligodendrocyte brings much of its own program, and axon cues such as caliber and activity bias it.

## Two wrappers

Two kinds of glia do the wrapping. In the brain and spinal cord, an oligodendrocyte sends out many processes, and each one makes an internode, usually on a different axon. In peripheral nerves, a myelinating Schwann cell makes a single internode on a single axon. Schwann cells that meet small axons do not myelinate; they hold several of them in grooves of their membrane, an arrangement called a Remak bundle. (Where both cell types come from is the subject of [that lesson](/lessons/origins-of-glia/).)

## In nerve, the axon decides

Schwann cells give large axons dozens of turns and tuck thin ones, unwrapped, into Remak bundles. Who decides: the glial cell or the axon?

**Predict.** Take Schwann cells from a nerve whose axons are all bare, and let them settle on axons that are normally myelinated. If the Schwann cell decides, what do you expect? If the axon decides?

In grafting experiments in the 1970s, by Albert Aguayo's laboratory and by Weinberg and Spencer, Schwann cells from unmyelinated nerves myelinated the axons they met in their new nerve, and the reverse swap gave the reverse result. The Schwann cells did what their new axons called for. **In peripheral nerve, the axon decides.**

So the axon must carry a signal the Schwann cell can read. The main one turned out to be **neuregulin-1 type III**, a protein on the axon's surface. Neuregulin-1 comes in several forms. Type III keeps its active, EGF-like part tethered near the axon membrane, so a Schwann cell reads it mainly by touching the axon. Two receptor tyrosine kinases of the EGF receptor family read it ([the lesson on receptor tyrosine kinases](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)). ErbB3 binds neuregulin but has a weak kinase. ErbB2 binds no ligand but has a strong kinase. Paired, they signal.

Now the key question. A large axon has more membrane per unit length than a small one. If type III sits at similar density on both, the large axon carries more of it. Could the *amount* be what the Schwann cell reads?

**Below a threshold amount of type III, an axon stays bare. Above it, more type III helps make a thicker sheath.** The dose was changed in both directions, and the sheath followed both times.

Could the same readout match the sheath to the axon's size? To hold g at 0.65, the sheath must thicken in step with the axon. Its thickness is (fiber − axon) ÷ 2: about 0.54 µm on a 2 µm axon and 1.35 µm on a 5 µm axon. At an illustrative spacing of about 17 nm per turn, that is about 32 and 79 turns. If type III density is similar on every axon, a Schwann cell that reads the amount gets that size signal for free. This proposal is consistent with the dose data (see Michailov and colleagues, 2004, and later reviews). No one has shown that Schwann cells measure size this way, or that the readout is strictly proportional.

In the terms of [the lesson on the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/), type III is **instructive** here: giving extra to axons that would stay bare made Schwann cells myelinate them, so supplying it changes the outcome rather than just allowing it.

Inside the Schwann cell, ErbB2/ErbB3 signaling runs through PI3K–Akt and other routes to turn on a transcription factor Schwann cells need in order to myelinate, *Krox20* (also called *Egr2*). Which routes matter most is still argued.

The axon's signal is decisive but not enough on its own. Two other inputs are also **required**, and they are not mere on-switches. First, the Schwann cell must lay down its **basal lamina**, the sheet of extracellular matrix around it. In the Bunges' cultures, Schwann cells needed contact with an extracellular matrix, and in later purified cocultures ascorbic acid, which cells need to build collagen, was required for both the basal lamina and myelin. Through laminin-211, the basal lamina also restrains myelination of small axons and tunes the response to neuregulin. Second, the Schwann cell must signal through a receptor of its own, **Gpr126**, which raises cAMP inside it; in zebrafish lacking Gpr126, Schwann cells stop just short of myelinating (they reach a single axon but do not wrap it), and Gpr126 also takes part in sorting axons.

**Keep the scope.** This is the rule for Schwann cells in peripheral nerve, and it covers two outputs: whether an axon is wrapped and how thick the sheath is. Sheath length, node assembly and compaction need other machinery too, though neuregulin signaling can affect some of them.

## In the brain, the oligodendrocyte brings its own program

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Myelination in the central nervous system is late and slow. In humans it begins before birth in the spinal cord and brainstem and continues through childhood and adolescence, and some association areas of the cerebral cortex are still gaining myelin in early adulthood. That leaves a long window in which activity could shape it.

In the brain and spinal cord the balance differs. In culture, oligodendrocytes will wrap inert plastic fibers of a suitable diameter. In the mouse brain and spinal cord, Nave's group found that oligodendrocytes still myelinate when neuregulin-1 and ErbB signaling are removed (Brinkmann and colleagues, 2008). Yet extra neuregulin-1 made by neurons can still thicken central myelin. So neuregulin-1/ErbB signaling is not needed to start wrapping, and the oligodendrocyte brings much of the program itself. Axon cues, such as activity and the caliber of the fiber, do not decide alone; they tilt where it wraps and how much.

Once an oligodendrocyte can wrap, which axons does it keep? Zebrafish larvae have transparent spinal cords, so the first sheaths can be filmed as they form. Jacob Hines, Bruce Appel and colleagues found that most early wraps were withdrawn, many within about an hour and a half.

**Predict.** Hines and colleagues blocked vesicle release from one labeled axon among normal neighbours by making it express tetanus toxin. If oligodendrocytes ignore the axon's release, what should they do with the release-blocked axon? If they prefer axons that release?

In a companion paper (both appeared in *Nature Neuroscience* in 2015), Sigrid Mensch, David Lyons and colleagues found that blocking vesicle release throughout the nervous system cut the number of sheaths each oligodendrocyte made by about a third, and that raising activity with a convulsant drug increased it unless release was blocked. Activity biased how many sheaths formed and where they stayed.

In mice, Erin Gibson, Michelle Monje and colleagues reported in 2014 that light-driven stimulation of neurons in premotor cortex increased the division of oligodendrocyte precursor cells and the making of new oligodendrocytes, and thickened myelin within the stimulated circuit. Together these results describe a developmental program that stays open to adjustment by activity, in larval fish and mouse cortex. How much activity-dependent myelin contributes to learning in adult humans is still open.

## What holds the layers together

The membrane itself suits the job. Like any membrane it is a lipid bilayer with proteins in it (see [that lesson](/lessons/membrane-structure-function/)), but it is unusually rich in lipid, roughly 70 to 80 percent of its dry weight, with a large share of cholesterol and of galactolipids such as galactocerebroside (see [that lesson](/lessons/lipids/)).

In the electron microscope compact myelin shows a repeating pattern. A dark major dense line marks where the cytoplasmic faces of successive turns meet; a fainter intraperiod line marks where their outer faces meet.

Myelin basic protein (MBP) is small, strongly positive and sits on the cytoplasmic side of the membrane. It binds the negatively charged lipids of two facing cytoplasmic leaflets and pulls them together, squeezing out the cytoplasm. The seam it leaves is the major dense line. Other proteins hold the outer faces: proteolipid protein (PLP) in the central nervous system, and protein zero (P0), an immunoglobulin-like adhesion protein, in peripheral nerve. The two are not related in structure.

A mutant mouse called shiverer shows what this one protein is worth. Its neurons and axons are present, but from the second or third week after birth it trembles. Its brain myelin lacks the major dense line and nearly all its MBP (first reported by a French group in 1979), and the mutant *MBP* gene has lost about 20 kilobases of DNA, including most of its exons. Peripheral nerve in shiverer is far less affected, because P0 can also hold the cytoplasmic faces together there.

**Predict.** The mutant points to necessity: without MBP, no major dense line. But a large deletion could also have damaged DNA next to the gene. What experiment would show that losing MBP alone explains shiverer, and what result would you need?

Put the gene back and nothing else. A normal *MBP* transgene bred into shiverer mice was enough to restore compact central myelin with major dense lines and stop the tremor, even at about a quarter of normal MBP (Readhead and colleagues, 1987). So loss of MBP is enough to explain those defects; other features were not tested. This is the necessity-then-sufficiency logic of [the lesson on the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/), applied to a structural protein.

</details>

## A second job: feeding the axon

Insulation is not myelin's only job. In the brain and spinal cord, oligodendrocytes also help keep the axons they wrap alive, and broad reviews now count this support as a second major function.

The first evidence came from mouse mutants. Mice lacking the myelin membrane protein PLP, or the oligodendrocyte enzyme CNP, show long-term degeneration of their axons even though their myelin sheaths look normal or nearly so. The sheath was there; something else the glial cell provides was missing. In people, Pelizaeus-Merzbacher disease, caused by mutations in the *PLP* gene, also involves axon loss.

What does the glial cell supply? Part of the answer is fuel. A sheath covers most of the axon's surface, which limits the axon's access to nutrients from the fluid outside. Oligodendrocytes pass energy metabolites to the axon through a monocarboxylate transporter, MCT1 (Lee and colleagues, 2012). One proposal is that the oligodendrocyte breaks glucose down to lactate and hands the lactate to the axon, whose mitochondria use it to make ATP. The lactate would travel through narrow channels of cytoplasm left in the non-compact parts of the sheath, leave the oligodendrocyte through MCT1, and enter the axon through a related transporter. The axon's dependence on this support grows with age. How much of the support is lactate, and what else passes from glia to axon, is still being worked out.

## Myelin lost, and repaired

Multiple sclerosis shows what losing central myelin costs. In its usual account, the immune system attacks myelin and oligodendrocytes in the brain, optic nerves and spinal cord, leaving scattered lesions, and axons are damaged too. It is a leading cause of non-traumatic neurological disability in young adults. Most patients begin with relapsing-remitting disease, many later enter a secondary progressive course, and a minority progress from the start (primary progressive).

A lesion takes away both of myelin's jobs. Without the sheath, saltatory conduction is lost. Without healthy oligodendrocytes, the axon loses its support. In long-standing lesions, axons keep degenerating, and several causes are thought to act together: lost sheath and oligodendrocyte support; inflammation that persists inside the brain behind the blood-brain barrier, which treatments that quiet relapses may control only incompletely; and mitochondrial and energy failure. Together they help explain why disability can keep growing after relapses have been quieted.

The nervous system tries to remyelinate lesions, and remyelination restores saltatory conduction and helps keep axons healthy. In animal models, oligodendrocyte precursor cells move in, mature and make new sheaths, which are typically thinner with shorter internodes. Repair reuses much of the developmental program, but in an inflamed adult lesion. In people the source is less certain: carbon dating of MS tissue found that oligodendrocytes in remyelinated-looking lesions were old cells, so surviving oligodendrocytes may make some of the new myelin. Repair often falls short, especially in long-standing lesions and in older patients.

### Design problem

In long-standing multiple sclerosis lesions, axons keep degenerating. Take two of the possible causes. The immune attack could damage axons directly, or axons could die because they have lost their oligodendrocytes. In a patient both happen at once. Design a mouse experiment that separates the two, and say what result would show that losing oligodendrocytes is enough to harm axons. (Hint: mouse strains that lack mature T and B cells exist.)

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Take the adaptive immune system out of the question. Use mice that have no mature T or B cells, such as mice lacking the gene *Rag1*. Kill their oligodendrocytes with a genetic method that acts only in those cells, and compare them with *Rag1*-deficient mice whose oligodendrocytes are left alone.

If axons degenerate only in the mice that lost their oligodendrocytes, loss of the glia is enough to harm axons without an adaptive immune attack. Two limits remain. Microglia and other innate immune cells are still present, and dying oligodendrocytes activate them. And killing oligodendrocytes removes the myelin and its support together, so this test cannot say which loss matters. If the axons survive, oligodendrocyte loss was not enough under these conditions; check how completely the cells were killed and how long the mice were followed before concluding more.

A sharper test leaves the oligodendrocytes alive, removes one thing they supply, such as the transporter MCT1, checks that the sheath stays intact, and asks whether axons still degenerate.

Because the sheaths also thinned, that study could not fully separate the two effects.

## Going deeper

### Growing myelin in a dish

Edith Peterson and Margaret Murray reported in 1955 that sensory ganglia from chick embryos made myelin in a dish. In 1967 Mary Bunge, Richard Bunge, Peterson and Murray described long-term cultures of dorsal root ganglia from fetal rats. Over several weeks, Schwann cells in these cultures wrapped sensory axons in compact myelin with nodes, one cell to one axon, much as in a real nerve. Because the medium, the matrix and the cells could each be changed one at a time, the dish became an experiment, and the matrix and ascorbic acid requirements described above came from such cultures.

### Where myelin stops paying in speed

Extended down to very small fibers, Hursh's straight line would have myelin paying down to a few hundredths of a micrometer, which is physically meaningless; the linear rule holds only for large fibers. The Waxman and Bennett crossover near 0.2 µm fits the brain and spinal cord, where axons as small as about 0.2 µm can be myelinated, though a caliber that permits wrapping does not decide which axons are wrapped. It does not fit the bare peripheral axons up to about 1 µm. The peripheral cutoff looks like a biological threshold rather than the speed crossover, and a threshold amount of type III is one candidate, alongside basal-lamina signals that restrain myelination of small axons.

### How sharp is the g ratio optimum?

Rushton's 1951 analysis, for a fiber of fixed total diameter, puts the fastest conduction at a g ratio of about 0.61. The peak is flat. Relative to the maximum, speed is about 97 percent at g = 0.5, 99.5 percent at 0.65, 97.5 percent at 0.7, 94 percent at 0.75 and 88 percent at 0.8. In this model, central fibers, which typically run 0.75 to 0.8, would give up only about 6 to 12 percent of the maximum speed. The optimum also depends on what is held fixed: in this simple model, for a fixed *axon* diameter, more myelin always adds speed, so the limit is set by other costs, such as space.

### Signaling inside the Schwann cell

Downstream of ErbB2/ErbB3, the signal fans out along routes you have met:

- PI3K and Akt, which in other cells promote growth and survival, support myelin growth (see [that lesson](/lessons/pi3k-akt-insulin/)).
- The Ras-ERK cascade is activated as well (see [that lesson](/lessons/ras-to-mapk/)).
- PLC-gamma raises calcium, which activates the phosphatase calcineurin and frees the transcription factor NFAT. Work from Gerald Crabtree's laboratory proposed that NFAT joins Sox10 in the nucleus in turning on *Krox20*, but that study removed calcineurin from all neural crest cells. When a later study removed it from Schwann cells alone, myelination and Krox20 were close to normal.

### Oligodendrocytes remember where they came from

On inert fibers, with no axon to instruct them, oligodendrocytes taken from different regions of the central nervous system made sheaths of different lengths, much as in the tissue they came from (Bechler and colleagues, 2015). Sheath length, too, is partly the glial cell's own program.

### Shiverer, step by step

The deletion was found in the mid-1980s by laboratories including those of Leroy Hood and Richard Lazzarini. Carol Readhead, Hood, Richard Sidman and colleagues then injected a normal copy of the mouse *MBP* gene, with its own regulatory DNA so that it would be turned on in oligodendrocytes, into fertilized mouse eggs, and bred the resulting transgenic mice with shiverer mice until they had animals homozygous for the shiverer deletion that also carried the transgene. Compared with shiverer littermates without the transgene and with normal mice, the transgenic shiverer mice no longer shivered or died young. Had a transgene made enough MBP, in the right forms and in oligodendrocytes, without improving the myelin or the tremor, something else in the deletion would probably have mattered. The 1987 study was one of the early uses of a transgene to cure a mouse mutant.

### Multiple sclerosis: history, numbers and risk

Jean-Martin Charcot's description of multiple sclerosis is usually dated to 1868. The disease is diagnosed most often between about 20 and 40 and affects women two to three times as often as men. A fourth category, progressive-relapsing, was dropped in the 2013 revision of the clinical course descriptions. Risk combines genes, with variants of the immune gene *HLA-DRB1* the strongest, and environment, notably earlier infection with Epstein-Barr virus.
