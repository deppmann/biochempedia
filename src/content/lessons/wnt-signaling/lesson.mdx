techniques:
  - name: "Proviral tagging"
    blurb: >-
      Infect mice with a retrovirus that inserts its DNA at many places in the genome, collect
      the tumours, and map where the virus sits in each one. A region hit in many separate
      tumours probably holds a gene that drives growth when the virus disturbs its expression.
      This is how int-1 was found.
  - name: "Genetic mosaics in the fly wing"
    blurb: >-
      Make a small patch of mutant cells, marked so that it can be seen, inside a normal wing.
      If only the mutant cells look abnormal, the gene acts inside the cell that makes it. If
      normal neighbours change too, the gene helps to pass information between cells, which is
      what patches lacking frizzled showed.
  - name: "TCF reporter assay"
    blurb: >-
      Place a luciferase gene behind a promoter that carries several TCF binding sites and put
      it into cells. The light the cells give off reports how much β-catenin is working with
      TCF, and a control reporter with mutated sites shows what is unrelated to TCF. Colon
      cancer cells without APC light up with no Wnt added.

    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      With no Wnt, the destruction complex phosphorylates free β-catenin, an SCF ligase adds
      ubiquitin and the proteasome degrades it. Storage on TCF in the nucleus is wrong because
      little free β-catenin survives to reach TCF while destruction runs. Secretion is wrong because β-catenin is
      an intracellular protein, and Wnt is the secreted one. A silent gene is wrong because the
      protein is made all the time: the control is on destruction, not on synthesis.
  - stem: "Which protein reads the phosphates that GSK3 places on β-catenin and so selects β-catenin for ubiquitination?"
    choices:
      - "Groucho, the co-repressor that sits on TCF"
      - "Axin, the scaffold of the destruction complex"
      - "β-TrCP, the substrate-binding part of SCF"
      - "Dishevelled, the protein that Frizzled recruits"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      β-TrCP is the substrate-binding protein of the SCF ligase, and its pocket holds β-catenin
      only when serines 33 and 37 carry phosphate. Axin holds β-catenin next to the kinases but
      does not read the phosphates or add ubiquitin. Dishevelled works at the receptor after Wnt
      binds, and it opposes destruction. Groucho is in the nucleus, where it keeps TCF target
      genes off.
  - stem: "What does β-catenin change when it reaches TCF on the DNA of a Wnt target gene?"
    choices:
      - "It phosphorylates TCF, so TCF can now enter the nucleus"
      - "It removes TCF from the DNA, so the gene is no longer blocked"
      - "It displaces a co-repressor, so TCF now activates the gene"
      - "It cuts TCF in two, so one half can now recruit polymerase"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      At many target genes TCF is on the DNA before the signal. Without β-catenin it holds the
      co-repressor Groucho. In the standard model β-catenin displaces Groucho and recruits the proteins that start transcription,
      so the same TCF now activates the gene. Removing TCF from the DNA is wrong because TCF is
      what holds β-catenin at the gene. Phosphorylating TCF is wrong because β-catenin is not a
      kinase and TCF is already in the nucleus. Cutting TCF is wrong because β-catenin is not a
      protease.

    choices:
      - "Both use Frizzled and Dishevelled, but only one uses β-catenin"
      - "Both need LRP5 or LRP6, but only planar polarity needs Frizzled"
      - "Both need a Wnt with its lipid, but only one needs Dishevelled"

    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Frizzled and Dishevelled are shared. In the β-catenin pathway they work with LRP5 or LRP6

  - stem: >-
      A drug blocks Porcupine, the enzyme that attaches the fatty acid to Wnt, so cells can no
      longer secrete working Wnt. A colon tumour has lost both copies of APC. What should the
      drug do to β-catenin signalling in the tumour cells?
    choices:
      - "Shut it off, because APC needs the lipid in order to fold"
      - "Very little, because the fault lies after the receptor"
      - "Raise it, because Wnt without lipid binds Frizzled tightly"
      - "Shut it off, because Frizzled now has nothing to bind"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Porcupine acts in the cell that makes Wnt, so the drug removes the ligand. A tumour cell
      without APC destroys β-catenin poorly whether or not Wnt is present, so removing the ligand
      should usually change little: the fault lies after the receptor. These drugs are aimed instead at
      tumours that still depend on the ligand, such as those that have lost RNF43, a ligase that removes Frizzled. Shutting off because Frizzled has
      nothing to bind would be right for a normal cell, but not for one whose destruction
      complex is already broken. Wnt without its lipid binds Frizzled poorly, not tightly. APC
      is a cytoplasmic protein that never carries the Wnt lipid.

      Suppose a student stops all protein synthesis in cultured cells with cycloheximide and
      follows β-catenin on western blots. Without Wnt, half of the β-catenin is gone in 15
      minutes. With Wnt, half is gone in 120 minutes. In a separate measurement, Wnt does not
      change the rate at which β-catenin is made. Taking the steady level as the rate of
      synthesis divided by the rate constant of destruction, how does Wnt change the steady
      level of β-catenin?
    choices:
      - "It leaves the level unchanged"
      - "It raises the level about 2-fold"
      - "It raises the level about 105-fold"
      - "It raises the level about 8-fold"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      For first-order loss, the rate constant equals ln 2 divided by the half-life. The
      half-life rises from 15 to 120 minutes, which is 8-fold, so the rate constant falls
      8-fold, and with synthesis unchanged the steady level rises 8-fold. An unchanged level
      would follow only if synthesis alone set the level. A 2-fold rise mistakes the word half
      in half-life for the size of the change. A 105-fold rise comes from subtracting the two
      half-lives instead of taking their ratio.

**Most of your cells make β-catenin all day and, without a secreted Wnt signal, destroy the free copies soon after.** Embryos use Wnt to lay out pattern, and tissues such as the gut lining use it to renew themselves. The name **Wnt** splices mouse *int-1* and fly *wingless*—fly mutants also gave us *armadillo*, the fly's β-catenin (*Going deeper*). The first film showed how slowing destruction flips the switch; here are the phosphorylation order, the rate arithmetic, colon cancer and the receptor's second job.

> **The one idea.** Wnt raises free β-catenin by slowing its constant destruction; planar polarity aligns cells as neighbours sort Frizzled to one edge. **Scope:** Clause 1 covers free cytoplasmic β-catenin (not the stable cadherin-bound junctional pool), assuming constant synthesis and unsaturated, first-order destruction. Clause 2 covers core planar cell polarity, which uses Frizzled and Dishevelled without LRP5/6, β-catenin or TCF.

## Marking free β-catenin for destruction

β-Catenin has a second, stable job linking **cadherins** to the cytoskeleton at cell junctions. Every free cytoplasmic copy meets the **destruction complex**: the scaffold **Axin** (as in `adaptors-and-scaffolds`), the large protein **APC** (which binds β-catenin and is required for efficient ubiquitination handover) and two kinases, **CK1** and **GSK3**.

Their order matters because GSK3 prefers a target already phosphorylated four residues toward the C-terminus. CK1 phosphorylates **serine 45**, priming GSK3 to step back in fours across **threonine 41**, **serine 37** and **serine 33**. Then **SCF targets β-catenin:** **β-TrCP**, the substrate-recognition subunit of an **SCF** E3 ubiquitin ligase (`ubiquitin-and-proteolysis`), reads phosphoserines 33 and 37, SCF adds ubiquitin, and the **proteasome** degrades β-catenin.

**Predict.** A mutation changes serine 45 of β-catenin so it cannot accept a phosphate, leaving threonine 41, serine 37 and serine 33 intact. Without Wnt, will β-TrCP still tag this β-catenin for destruction?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Without CK1's priming phosphate at serine 45, GSK3 is not primed to step back through 41, 37 and 33. With no phosphates on serines 33 and 37, β-TrCP cannot bind, SCF skips it, and free β-catenin accumulates without Wnt.

</details>

Picture a bathtub with the tap running and the drain open: the level reflects both. Under the first-order scope above:

> **steady level of β-catenin = rate of synthesis ÷ rate constant of destruction**

Slow destruction ten-fold (say from 0.5 to 0.05 per minute at constant synthesis) and the steady level rises ten-fold without changing synthesis. Because synthesis is already running, free β-catenin starts to rise as soon as destruction slows, and clears quickly when destruction resumes.

## How Wnt slows destruction and flips TCF

The first film traced Wnt to the membrane and β-catenin to DNA; here are the lipid grip, nuclear switch and two boundaries of the model.

Wnt is an unusual secreted protein carrying a fatty acid that **Porcupine** attaches to a serine in the producing cell. That lipid makes Wnt cling to membranes, hard to dissolve, and mostly short-range in many tissues.

Its receptor **Frizzled** is a seven-pass membrane protein with the overall shape of Unit 3 receptors. In the crystal structure of Wnt bound to Frizzled's outer domain, the fatty acid sits in a receptor groove, forming part of the grip. Wnt brings Frizzled together with its single-pass co-receptor, **LRP5** or **LRP6**. Inside, Frizzled recruits **Dishevelled**, and the phosphorylated LRP6 tail binds Axin, pulling the destruction complex to the receptor so it stops delivering β-catenin to the ligase. How it is stopped is still argued, yet newly made β-catenin survives, builds up and enters the nucleus.

There it meets **TCF**, bound to the DNA of many Wnt target genes even before a signal arrives, holding the co-repressor **Groucho** to keep them off. When β-catenin arrives, Groucho leaves and β-catenin recruits transcription machinery, turning TCF from repressor to activator. The simple model is that β-catenin pushes Groucho off; how Groucho is actually removed is still being worked out. Which genes answer depends in part on where TCF sits in that cell type.

## Wnt in disease: a drain stuck shut

Because Wnt raises free β-catenin by slowing its constant destruction, a mutation that slows destruction on its own can switch TCF on without Wnt.

Most people who inherit one faulty copy of **APC** grow hundreds to thousands of colon **polyps** (**familial adenomatous polyposis**), because each cell is one hit away from losing its remaining working copy; nearly all get colon cancer unless the colon is removed. APC is also mutated in about four in five sporadic colon cancers.

In 1993 Paul Polakis's lab and Bert Vogelstein and Kenneth Kinzler's lab each reported that APC binds β-catenin. In 1997 Hans Clevers's lab and the Vogelstein and Kinzler lab published side by side: colon cancer cells without working APC had β-catenin and TCF active all the time, and restoring APC shut that transcription off. Some colon tumours with normal APC carried β-catenin mutations instead, at the CK1 and GSK3 phosphorylation sites.

Both routes plug the drain. Gut lining stem cells at the base of **crypts** and dividing cells above them depend on Wnt: newborn mice without TCF4 lack the dividing compartment of the small intestine. A cell without APC behaves as if it never left the crypt base.

## Planar cell polarity: how cells agree which way to point

A direction shared across the plane of a sheet—like fly wing hairs pointing toward the wing tip—is **planar cell polarity** (**PCP**). The second film showed the mutant-patch test and edge sorting; here are the genetics behind that discovery and its vertebrate genes.

**UVa moment: Paul Adler.** No cell can see the whole wing, yet every hair aligns. At the University of Virginia in 1987, Charles Vinson and Paul Adler made patches of *frizzled*-null cells in normal wings: normal cells on the tip side grew misdirected hairs, while those on the other side did not. Two years later they cloned *frizzled* as a seven-pass membrane protein—the Wnt receptor family carries the name of untidy fly hairs. Later work from Adler's lab showed that hairs point from cells with more Frizzled toward cells with less.

**Predict.** Using Adler's finding (hairs point from more Frizzled toward less), suppose a patch of wing cells makes **more** Frizzled than normal. Which side—body side or tip side—shows backward hairs on normal neighbours?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The body side. Normal neighbours point away from the high-Frizzled patch: normal on the tip side (already pointing tipward), flipped backward on the body side.

</details>

In each wing cell, **Frizzled** and **Dishevelled** sit on the tip-side edge, **Van Gogh** and **Prickle** on the opposite edge, and the cadherin **Flamingo** links every border. Each cell builds its actin hair on the Frizzled side—none of this uses β-catenin or TCF.

Vertebrates reuse these genes as the flat **neural plate** narrows, lengthens and folds into the **neural tube** (future brain and spinal cord) while cells squeeze between neighbours toward the midline. **Disrupting PCP causes neural tube closure defects**, including spina bifida: mice with two mutant copies of **Vangl2** (a vertebrate Van Gogh) keep the tube open along most of its length, and rare human **VANGL1** variants appear in some patients with neural tube defects. Vertebrates also require particular Wnts—**Wnt5a** and **Wnt11** among them—one reason PCP is counted as a Wnt pathway.

### Design problem

Each cell in a sheet grows one hair, and every hair must point the same way. No cell can see the whole sheet. Design a way for each cell to tell its two sides apart using only proteins in its own membrane and in the membranes of the cells it touches. Then predict what a patch of cells that lacks one of your proteins does to the normal cells beside it.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use two membrane proteins, A and B, and two rules. Between cells, A on one cell holds B on the cell it touches, so each keeps the other in place across the gap. Within a cell, A and B drive each other away, so no edge keeps both. A weak bias across the tissue is then enough. Each cell ends with A on one side and B on the other, and every boundary has A facing B. Grow the hair on the A side.

Now make a patch of cells with no A. Those cells have B on every edge. Each normal neighbour gathers its A on the edge that touches the patch, so its hair points toward the patch. On one side of the patch that is the normal direction and nothing looks wrong. On the other side the hairs point backwards. Mutant cells have misdirected normal ones, and on one side only.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How int-1 and wingless became Wnt

Around 1980 Roel Nusse, a postdoctoral fellow in Harold Varmus's UCSF laboratory, studied mouse mammary tumours caused by mouse mammary tumour virus. This retrovirus inserts a DNA copy (**provirus**) into mouse chromosomes; repeated insertions in one stretch of DNA probably flag a gene that favours tumour growth when disturbed.

In 18 of 26 tumours a provirus sat within one region of about 19 kilobases (19,000 base pairs). The few tumours tested made RNA from that region, whereas the lactating mammary glands tested did not. They named the site **int-1** (integration site 1); their 1982 paper cautiously proposed that insertion favours tumours, perhaps by raising a cellular gene's expression.

Its normal role stayed unknown for five years until Frank Rijsewijk, in Nusse's Amsterdam group, cloned the fly version: 54 percent identical to mouse, with all 23 cysteines conserved, and the gene proved to be **wingless**. Weak *wingless* mutations (R. P. Sharma and V. L. Chopra, 1976) often remove adult wings; stronger ones kill the embryo and scramble segment pattern. One gene kept across more than 500 million years caused mouse tumours when misexpressed and patterned fly embryos. In 1991 eight researchers signed a one-page note splicing *wingless* and *int* into **Wnt**, and other fly mutants with the same segment defect—including *armadillo*, the fly's β-catenin—mapped the rest of the pathway.
