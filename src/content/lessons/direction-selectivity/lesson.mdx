  - name: "Two-photon calcium imaging"
    blurb: >-
      A pulsed infrared laser excites a calcium indicator in a small volume of living tissue, so
      you can read calcium changes from single dendrites. Calcium rises with activity but is not a

  - name: "Serial block-face electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      An automated blade removes a thin slice from a block of stained tissue after each image of
      the block face, which gives a three-dimensional stack at synaptic resolution. Cells are
      then traced through the stack, and contacts between them are counted.
  - name: "Paired whole-cell voltage clamp"
    blurb: >-
      You record from two connected cells at once, stimulate one and measure the synaptic current
      in the other. This gives the strength of one connection, which a population recording
      cannot give.
  - name: "Targeted cell ablation"
    blurb: >-
      A genetic strategy removes one cell population from an adult animal so you can test whether
      it is needed for a response or a behaviour. It shows necessity in that assay only.

**A ganglion cell in a rabbit's retina fires hard when a spot moves one way across its field and hardly at all when it moves the other way. The difference is made largely one synapse earlier, by starburst amacrine cells, although tuned excitation also contributes in some cells.** Each dendrite of a starburst cell prefers motion directed outward from the cell body. The dendrites that point along a ganglion cell's null direction, the direction it ignores, make the strongest inhibitory connections onto it, so motion in that direction is suppressed. The rest of the lesson shows how each step was found: where the computation sits, which cells do it, how they are wired, and how the asymmetry develops.

The [retina lesson](/lessons/retina/) built centre-surround fields from bipolar and amacrine circuitry, and [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/) described lateral inhibition between neighbours. Neither gave a cell a sense of direction. This lesson starts where the retina does that, one synapse before the optic nerve. Motion in the cortex belongs to [visual cortex](/lessons/visual-cortex/). The cell census this circuit belongs to is in [retinal cell types](/lessons/retinal-cell-types/).

## Two algorithms for the same job: suppress the null or enhance the preferred

A direction-selective ganglion cell (DSGC) is defined by what it does. It fires strongly to motion in its preferred direction and little or not at all to the opposite, called the null direction. Barlow and Hill reported such cells in the rabbit retina in Science in 1963, and they noted that replacing a light spot with a black spot kept the preferred direction. The 1965 paper by Barlow and Levick is the mechanism paper, and it is often credited with the discovery, which it was not.

Barlow and Levick recorded spikes from ON-OFF direction-selective units in the rabbit, in vivo. They stimulated two neighbouring places in one receptive field in sequence. From the results they inferred that small subunits of the field suppress responses to sequences running in the null direction. Their guesses about which cells do this were tentative, and you should read the paper as a statement of what the mechanism must do, not as an identification of starburst cells.

There are two simple ways to build a direction detector from two inputs. One suppresses: a delayed inhibitory signal from one side vetoes the excitation when motion runs the null way. The other enhances: a delayed signal from one side is multiplied with the signal from the other, so the response grows when motion runs the preferred way. The Barlow-Levick scheme is the first, and the Hassenstein-Reichardt scheme is the second. A two-flash test tells them apart. Measure each flash alone, add the two responses, and subtract the sum from the paired-flash response. A positive remainder in one order is enhancement, and a negative remainder in the other order is suppression.

## A dendrite that is more selective than its own cell body (2002)

The starburst amacrine cell (SAC) is an interneuron whose dendrites radiate from the soma like the spokes of a wheel and which overlap the dendrites of DSGCs. Euler, Detwiler and Denk imaged ON SACs in isolated rabbit retina, using two-photon calcium imaging, and compared the dendrites with the voltage at the soma. Calcium signals in a dendrite were larger for motion outward from the soma, called centrifugal motion, than for motion inward. The somatic voltage did not show the same directional preference under the conditions tested.

This changes what you should picture. A SAC does not have one preferred direction that its soma reports. Each branch has its own, set by where the branch points. Calcium is also not transmitter release, so the result shows a local directional signal and leaves the mechanism of that signal open.

How necessary is the starburst cell? Yoshida and colleagues ablated the SAC population in adult mice with an immunotoxin-based method and then recorded ganglion-cell responses and tested the optokinetic reflex, the eye movement that follows a moving visual field. Both direction selectivity in the ganglion cells and the reflex they tested were abolished. This is necessity in that assay. Because the SACs release both acetylcholine and GABA onto DSGCs (shown in rabbit by Lee and colleagues in 2010, with its role tested by Sethuramanujam and colleagues in 2016), taking the whole population away also removes both transmitters together.

## Wiring read directly: imaging followed by electron microscopy (2011)

If centrifugal dendrites inhibit a DSGC, then the dendrites that contact it should point along its null direction, because that is the motion that drives them hardest. Paired recordings in rabbit retina had already shown this in function: Fried, Münch and Werblin (2002) found that SAC processes pointing in a DSGC's null direction delivered inhibition and those pointing in its preferred direction did not, and that SAC inhibition was stronger for null-direction motion. Briggman, Helmstaedter and Denk tested the anatomy in mouse retina. They first imaged calcium in DSGCs in a retina from a wild-type mouse, using moving bars, to read each cell's preferred direction. They then cut the same tissue with serial block-face electron microscopy, which images the face of a tissue block after each slice is removed, and reconstructed the cells and their contacts. The [microscopy and images](/lessons/microscopy-and-images/) lesson covers how that stack is made.

Most reconstructed SAC input onto a DSGC came from dendrites pointing roughly along that cell's null direction. The orientation of the individual branch predicted whether it connected better than the line between the two cell bodies did. That second result fits the 2002 finding that the branch, not the cell, is the unit.

Two limits apply. Anatomy does not measure the strength of a synapse, so a contact count or area is an indirect sign of connectivity, not a conductance. And a snapshot of an adult retina says nothing about how the pattern arose.

## Where the asymmetry comes from: strengthening, not pruning (2011)

An asymmetric circuit could arise by pruning: start with symmetric connections and delete the preferred-side ones. Wei, Hamby, Zhou and Feller tested this in mouse retina with paired recordings from SACs and nasal-preferring ON-OFF DSGCs, stimulating one SAC and measuring the inhibitory current in the ganglion cell. At postnatal days 4 and 7 the inhibition was similar from the two sides. Later, the null-side unitary inhibition increased while the preferred-side inhibition stayed about constant. Preferred-side connections were still detectable in adult retina.

So development strengthened one side and did not delete the other. The authors also found that direction selectivity developed despite intraocular injections, during the period when the asymmetry forms, of muscimol (a GABA-A agonist used here to silence retinal activity) or gabazine (which blocks GABA-A receptors). They concluded that the null-side strengthening follows a developmental program that does not depend on neural activity, at least within that treatment window. For the activity patterns that wire the retina early on, see [spontaneous activity](/lessons/spontaneous-activity/), and for how cell types find their partners, see [partners, layers and self-avoidance](/lessons/partners-layers-self-avoidance/). Later paired recordings from the same laboratory (Morrie and Feller, 2015) asked why the null-side connection grows. Quantal events became more than three times more frequent for null-side pairs, with no change in their size, so the growth comes from more inhibitory synapses per null-side pair, not from stronger single synapses. The asymmetry is in how many synapses each pair makes.

## Tuned input, tuned output, and a fly that solved it differently

The wiring story is about output from the SAC. How the SAC dendrite gets its own preference is less settled. One line of work points inside the dendrite: in rabbit, Hausselt and colleagues (2007) found that SAC direction selectivity persisted with GABA and glycine receptors blocked and depended on voltage-gated channels and a voltage gradient from soma to dendritic tip. Another line looks at the inputs, and that side is under revision.

A model built from contact areas in a mouse electron microscopy volume, an indirect measure, proposed that bipolar cells of different types, contacting different parts of a starburst dendrite with different timing, could favour outward motion, and later recordings found that distal excitation is faster and more transient than proximal excitation. This input mechanism is a conditional contribution under revision, and the studies are under Going deeper.

Excitation may also not always be untuned. Matsumoto and colleagues found that single bipolar cells have output boutons with different preferred directions in mouse retina, and that this depended on SAC cholinergic excitation and on wide-field amacrine inhibition. The DSGCs they recorded received both direction-tuned and untuned glutamatergic input. This is debated. Park and colleagues (2014) imaged glutamate arriving on ON-OFF DSGC dendrites in mouse and found no direction tuning, and they argued that apparent tuned excitation in voltage clamp comes from poor voltage control during null-direction inhibition. Either way, an upstream cell type does not make a clean feed-forward origin. For the primary-visual-cortex consequences, Hillier and colleagues reported in mice that disrupting retinal direction selectivity abolished the over-representation of posterior-motion-preferring layer 2/3 cells in V1, while other cortical motion computations persisted.

In Drosophila the same job is done by T4 cells (the ON channel) and T5 cells (the OFF channel). In vivo calcium imaging of their axon terminals by Haag, Mishra and Borst found both preferred-direction enhancement and null-direction suppression in all four subtypes of each cell type, which differ in their preferred direction. The fly and the mouse reach a similar algorithm with different cells, so this is convergent computation, not homology, and no single idealised detector accounts for either circuit.

On the primate side, a textbook line says that primates lack DSGCs. That is out of date: physiological direction selectivity has been reported in macaque ON-OFF cells (Kim and colleagues, 2022) and ON cells (Wang and colleagues, 2023). The macaque ON-OFF cells form a single mosaic, about 1.5% of peripheral ganglion cells, unlike the four ON-OFF types with different preferred directions in mouse and rabbit, so the rodent circuit should not be copied onto the primate wholesale. The 2023 human cell was identified as a candidate homolog without a human motion recording.

### Worked problem: indices and timing

(a) Kim and colleagues (2022) reported, for dendritic calcium in macaque SACs (adult isolated retina, radial gratings), a mean inward-to-outward response ratio of 0.32 (SD 0.18) across 36 regions of interest on the terminal dendrites of one starburst cell. For this exercise only, normalise the outward response to 1 and define D = (Rout - Rin) / (Rout + Rin).

> **D = (1 - 0.32) / (1 + 0.32) = 0.68 / 1.32 = 0.515, outward-preferring**

This transforms a published mean ratio. It is not a published direction-selectivity index, and because the formula is non-linear it is not the mean of individual indices, which would need each region's own ratio. The 36 counts regions of interest on one cell, not cells or animals.

(b) Fransen and Borghuis found that distal excitation in mouse SACs is faster than proximal excitation. For this exercise only, take the gradients in time to peak of excitation that they reported, about 26 ms per 100 micrometres in OFF SACs and 8 ms per 100 micrometres in ON SACs, and treat them as pure delays. That treatment is the exercise's assumption, not the paper's. A pure delay line makes the signals coincide when the stimulus crosses 100 micrometres in the delay time.

> **OFF: 100 / 26 = 3.8 micrometres per ms, about 3,800 micrometres per second. ON: 100 / 8 = 12.5 micrometres per ms, about 12,500 micrometres per second.**

Treated as pure delays, these gradients would match only far faster stimuli than the slow regime, below about 500 micrometres per second, in which the 2022 model of Srivastava and colleagues found its strongest space-time contribution. The mismatch comes from the pure-delay assumption, which ignores the difference in response shape, and is not a finding of either paper. Do not restate these values as a universal delay.

### Design problem

Inhibition from starburst cells on the null side is stronger, SACs release both acetylcholine and GABA, and removing the SAC population abolished direction selectivity in the assay of Yoshida and colleagues. Does asymmetric GABAergic inhibition cause direction selectivity? Say what experiments would settle it.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start by separating necessity from sufficiency. Removing the whole population removes two transmitters and every other function of the cells at once, so it shows that the cells are needed, not that GABA is.

Test the transmitter directly. Remove GABA release from SACs while leaving the cells and their cholinergic output in place, and compare the result with acute, reversible silencing of the same cells. If the ratio falls but does not reach one, other mechanisms contribute.

Much of this has been done. In rabbit, the GABA receptor blocker picrotoxin abolished the direction selectivity of ON-OFF and ON DSGCs (Caldwell, Daw and Wyatt, 1978), though a bath-applied drug acts on every GABAergic cell. In mouse, Pei and colleagues (2015) deleted the vesicular GABA transporter from SACs only. Inhibition lost its direction tuning, and direction selectivity survived only in a subset of ON-OFF DSGCs, carried by tuned excitation and a timing offset between excitation and inhibition. SAC GABA is therefore important but not the whole mechanism. A genetic deletion can act through development, so the next point still matters.

Separate development from the adult requirement. A manipulation begun before about postnatal day 7 could change the wiring, and a manipulation in the adult changes signalling. The two answer different questions.

Add a control for tuned excitation. Some work finds direction-tuned bipolar boutons whose tuning depends partly on wide-field amacrine GABA, so a global GABA block would remove that tuning too, while a SAC-specific removal may not. Other work finds untuned glutamate release onto ON-OFF DSGCs, so measure glutamate input directly rather than assume either result.

State the species scope: the physiology is established in rabbit and mouse, and in macaque for direction-selective ganglion cells, while the human cell is a candidate homolog only.

## Going deeper

### The Barlow and Levick preparation

Barlow and Levick recorded spikes from ON-OFF direction-selective units in the rabbit, in vivo, from unopened eyes of decerebrate animals (higher brain regions surgically disconnected) or lightly anaesthetized animals. They stimulated two neighbouring places in one receptive field in sequence.

### Input kinetics onto the starburst dendrite

The EyeWire project (Kim and colleagues, Nature, 2014) reconstructed 79 OFF SACs in a mouse retinal electron microscopy volume. Contact area suggested that type BC2 bipolar cells contact proximal parts of a SAC dendrite and type BC3a cells contact more distal parts. Their model proposed that this spatial arrangement, with different timing in the two inputs, could favour outward motion. That is a model prediction, resting on contact area as an indirect measure.

Measurements followed. Fransen and Borghuis recorded excitation in ON and OFF SACs in whole-mount retina from mice aged 1.5 to 3 months, in vitro. Distal excitation was faster and more transient than proximal excitation in both types. Srivastava and colleagues imaged glutamate on SAC dendrites directly in mouse retina in vitro and found relatively sustained proximal input. Deconvolution suggested about three times larger steady release proximally, and their constrained model gave its strongest space-time contribution below about 500 micrometres per second.

### Limits of the pure-delay exercise

These speeds sit well above the below-500 micrometres per second regime in which the 2022 constrained model found its strongest space-time contribution. Treated as pure delays, these gradients would match only far faster stimuli than that regime. That mismatch comes from the pure-delay assumption, which ignores the difference in response shape (sustained proximal, transient distal), and is not a finding of either paper. Srivastava and colleagues measured similar proximal and distal latencies and found the contribution of sustained and transient kinetics at low speeds. Real latency differences depend on the radial positions and the preparation, so do not restate these values as a universal delay.
