## The big idea

Children walk past the same trees every day and rarely notice them change. Adopting one tree slows that down. Your child visits the same tree again and again, from the same spot, and the changes pile up in the photos and drawings.

Many Virginia trees, such as maples, oaks, dogwoods and tulip poplars, are **deciduous**: they lose all their leaves each fall and grow new ones in spring. The tree does not die in winter. It goes **dormant**, which means it is alive but resting, with growth paused. Many of next year's leaves and flowers are already packed inside **buds**, small wrapped bundles on the twigs, before the old leaves fall.

These changes follow the seasons, and the seasons come in the same order every year. Shorter days and cooler weather in fall start leaf drop. Warmer days in spring wake the buds. In grade 1, children notice budding, growth and losing leaves and link them to temperature and light. Grade 2 adds the idea of dormancy.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child at the tree on every visit. Pick a tree you can reach without crossing a busy road, and ask permission for a tree at a school, a park or a neighbor's yard. Skip visits in thunderstorms or strong wind, when branches can fall, and stay out from under dead or hanging branches. On hot days, bring water and find shade.
- Feet stay on the ground: no climbing, swinging or hanging from the tree, and no pulling branches down. Nothing from the tree goes in a mouth. Leave fruit, nuts, acorns, seeds and berries alone (small ones are a choking risk for children under 4), and never eat any wild plant, berry or mushroom. If a child is choking or struggling to breathe, call 911.
- Some Virginia caterpillars sting, including the saddleback and the puss caterpillar, mostly in late summer. Teach "look, don't touch" for any fuzzy, spiky or brightly colored caterpillar, and never poke one or brush it off. If one stings, press tape on the spot and lift it to pull out the spines, wash with soap and water, and call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 (free, any hour; poison.org has the full steps). Call 911 for trouble breathing, fainting or swelling of the face or lips.
- Poison ivy often climbs tree trunks as a hairy, rope-like vine, and even bare winter vines can carry the rash-causing oil. Check the trunk before you choose a tree, teach "leaves of three, let it be," and rinse skin with lots of water and soap soon after any touch.
- Keep away from bees, wasps and any nest in the tree. Wear long pants tucked into socks. After each visit, wash hands and check skin and hair for ticks. Pull an attached tick straight out with fine-tipped tweezers close to the skin, and call a doctor if a fever or rash follows in the weeks after.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Choose a tree.** Pick one that loses its leaves in fall, not a pine or holly. In fall, look for leaves on the ground beneath it. In winter, look for bare twigs with buds. Let your child name the tree if they like. With a class, one schoolyard tree for everyone works well.
2. **Mark your photo spot.** Stand 10 to 20 steps back, so the whole tree fits in the photo. Choose a landmark, such as a fence post or a sidewalk crack, and take every photo from there, facing the same way.
3. **Predict first.** Before the first look, ask, "What do you think this tree will look like next month? In winter?" Your child draws or says a prediction. Keep it in the folder.
4. **Take a close look.** Spend about 10 minutes at the tree. Look at the bark, a low twig and a few leaves. Find the buds at the tip of a twig and where each leaf joins it. Your child draws one twig or leaf up close.
5. **Notice the weather and the light.** Is it warm or cold? Sunny, cloudy or rainy? Do you need a coat? Is it dark by dinnertime? Record the answers in words or simple pictures.
6. **Look for animals.** Watch for birds, squirrels, insects, nests, holes and chewed leaves. Look, but don't touch. Ask, "What is this animal using the tree for, food or shelter?"
7. **Visit again.** Return about once a month for a 15-minute visit. Take the same photo, draw the tree, and fill in a row of the sheet. Before each visit, ask your child to predict one change.
8. **Know what to expect.** In much of Virginia, buds swell and open in March and April. Leaves are full from May to September. Colors and leaf drop come mostly in October and November, a little earlier in the mountains. Most trees stay bare from December to February, though some oaks and beeches, especially young ones, hold dry brown leaves into late winter. Dates vary by tree and by year.
9. **Make a four-season poster.** Once you have a visit in each season, put one photo or drawing from each in order: spring, summer, fall, winter. Ask, "What happens next?"

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder what is inside this bud. What do you think will come out?"
- "What do you think the tree will look like on our next visit?"
- "Which leaves will change color first, the ones at the top or the bottom?"
- "Is it getting dark earlier or later than last time? What might the tree do about that?"
- "What animals do you think use our tree in winter?"
- "What do you think will happen if we come back on the same day next year?"

## Why it works

In winter, broad thin leaves cost a tree more than they give. They lose water through tiny pores, and frozen ground cannot replace it. Freezing can also damage them. Dropping them lets the tree wait out the cold.

The tree does not simply lose its leaves. As nights grow longer and temperatures fall, it stops making the green pigment chlorophyll and pulls some nutrients back into the twigs. A band of cells at the base of each leaf stalk, the **abscission layer**, weakens until the leaf breaks off. A seal forms over the scar. The exact mix of signals differs between kinds of trees.

Buds form in summer and wait through winter, many tightly wrapped in scales. After enough cold, warm spring days start them growing. The tree also feeds and shelters animals year-round: squirrels nest in it, birds eat its seeds, and insects eat its leaves.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Visit four times, once in each season. At each visit, take the photo, draw the tree, and mark the weather with a picture: sun, cloud, raindrop or snowflake. Add "coat" or "no coat." Your child describes the tree in one sentence, which you write down. Make the poster with four big boxes, one for each season.

### Ages 8 to 10
Visit monthly. At about your child's chest height, wrap string around the trunk, mark the string where it meets its end, and measure the string up to that mark in cm. Mark the spot so you measure there every time. Trunks grow slowly, so "no change" may be a real result. From a weather site, record the high temperature and the hours from sunrise to sunset. In Virginia, day length runs from about 9 and a half hours in late December to almost 15 hours in late June. Graph day length by month, then mark when the leaves changed.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If the photos are hard to compare, you probably shot from different spots. Mark the spot with a painted stone or a note on your phone. If the tree stays green all winter, it may be an evergreen, such as a holly or a southern magnolia. Keep it as a comparison and adopt a second tree. If you miss a month, just carry on. Even four visits a year show the pattern.
