## The big idea

Visiting one tree through the year gives your child practice looking closely, recording slow changes, and testing guesses against real evidence. Your child finds out that a bare winter tree is resting, not dead, with next spring's leaves already waiting in its buds.

> **Say it together** (read aloud):
> "Our tree stays alive all year. In winter it is resting, not dead. Small buds on the twigs already hold next spring's leaves."

Children walk past the same trees daily and rarely see them change. Visiting one tree from one spot slows that down, and the photos and drawings make slow changes clear.

Many Virginia trees—such as maples, oaks, dogwoods and tulip poplars—are **deciduous**: they lose their leaves mostly in fall and grow new ones in spring. In winter, the tree goes **dormant** (alive but resting, with most growth paused). Many of next year's leaves and flowers are already packed inside **buds**, small wrapped bundles on the twigs, before old leaves fall.

Seasons come in the same order each year. Shorter days and cooler weather in fall start leaf drop; warmer spring days wake the buds.

<summary>For teachers (Virginia standards)</summary>

In grade 1, children notice budding, growth and losing leaves and link them to temperature and light. Grade 2 adds dormancy.

<SafetyNote>
- **Stay together in a safe spot.** Stay with your child on every visit. Pick a tree you can reach without crossing a busy road. Ask permission at a school, park or neighbor's yard. Skip visits in thunderstorms or strong wind, when branches can fall. Stay out from under dead or hanging branches. On hot days, bring water and find shade.
- **Feet on the ground; nothing in mouths.** No climbing, swinging, hanging or pulling branches down. Never taste or eat any wild plant, berry or mushroom. Leave fruit, nuts, acorns, seeds, berries and small stones alone—small items are a choking risk for children under 4. **If a child is choking or struggling to breathe, call 911.**
- **Allergies and tools.** Leave fallen nuts and acorns alone, especially if your child has a tree nut allergy. An adult handles any glass hand lens (or use plastic). Never look at the sun through a hand lens or let it focus sunlight on skin, paper or dry leaves, which can burn or start a fire.
- **Wash hands** with soap and water after every visit.

<details>
<summary>More safety: caterpillars, poison ivy, stings, ticks and tools</summary>

- **Stinging caterpillars:** Some Virginia caterpillars sting, including the saddleback and puss caterpillar, mostly in late summer. Teach "look, don't touch" for fuzzy, spiky or brightly colored caterpillars; never poke or brush one off. If stung, press tape on the spot and lift to remove spines, wash with soap and water, and call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 (free, any hour. See poison.org for the full steps.) Call 911 for trouble breathing, fainting or swelling of face or lips.
- **Poison ivy:** It often climbs trunks as a hairy, rope-like vine; even bare winter vines can carry the rash-causing oil. Check the trunk first, teach "leaves of three, let it be," and rinse skin with lots of water and soap soon after any touch.
- **Bees, wasps and stings:** Keep away from bees, wasps and tree nests. Bring prescribed epinephrine on every visit for a known sting allergy, and call 911 for trouble breathing or swelling of the face or lips after any sting.
- **Ticks and sharp tools:** Wear long pants tucked into socks; an adult applies EPA-registered insect repellent per label for your child's age. Check skin and hair for ticks after each visit. An adult pulls an attached tick straight out with fine-tipped tweezers close to the skin, and calls a doctor if a fever or rash follows in the weeks after. An adult also handles any metal tape measure or cutting of string.

</details>
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Choose a tree.** Check the trunk and ground first. Pick one tree that loses its leaves in fall, not a pine or holly. In fall, look for leaves below it; in winter, look for bare twigs with buds. Say, *"This is our tree for the whole year. What should we name it?"* With a class, one schoolyard tree works well.
2. **Mark your photo spot.** Stand 10 to 20 steps back so the whole tree fits. Pick a landmark, like a fence post or sidewalk crack. Take the first photo here. Say, *"We will stand here and face the same way for every photo."*
3. **Predict first.** Ask, *"What do you think our tree will look like next month? What about in winter?"* Your child draws or says a guess. Keep it in the folder to check later.
4. **Look closely.** Spend about 10 minutes at the tree. Check the bark, a low twig and a few leaves. Find the buds at the twig tip and where each leaf joins. Ask, *"What do you notice?"* Your child draws one twig or leaf up close.
5. **Check weather and light.** Ask, *"Warm or cold? Do we need a coat? Is it dark by dinnertime?"* Was it sunny, cloudy or rainy? Record the weather and light answers in words or simple pictures.
6. **Look for animals.** Watch for birds, squirrels, insects, nests, holes and chewed leaves. Look, don't touch, and leave animals where you find them. Ask, *"Food or shelter—what is this animal using our tree for?"*
7. **Check your guess and return.** Visit about once a month for 15 minutes. Check last month's guess: *"What matched? What changed?"* Take the same photo, draw the tree, fill in a row, and guess one change for next time.
8. **Know what to expect.** In much of Virginia, buds swell and open in March and April. Leaves are full from May to September. Colors and leaf drop come mostly in October and November, a little earlier in the mountains. Most trees stay bare from December to February, though some oaks and beeches, especially young ones, hold dry brown leaves into late winter. Dates vary by tree and by year.
9. **Make a four-season poster and debrief.** Line up one photo or drawing from spring, summer, fall and winter in order. Ask two closing questions:
   - *"When our tree had no leaves in winter, was it dead or resting? How do you know?"*
   - *"What happens next?"*
   Read **Say it together** aloud again.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder what is inside this bud. What do you think will come out?"
- "What do you think the tree will look like on our next visit?"
- "Which leaves will change color first, the ones at the top or the bottom?"
- "Is it getting dark earlier or later than last time? What might the tree do about that?"
- "What animals do you think use our tree in winter?"
- "What do you think will happen if we come back on the same day next year?"

## Why it works

In winter, broad thin leaves cost a tree more than they give. They lose water through tiny pores, and cold or frozen ground makes water hard to replace. Freezing can also damage them. Dropping leaves lets the tree wait out the cold.

As nights grow longer and temperatures fall, the tree stops making chlorophyll, its green pigment, and pulls some nutrients back into the twigs. A band of cells at the base of each leaf stalk, the **abscission layer**, weakens until the leaf breaks off. A seal forms over the scar. The exact mix of signals differs between kinds of trees.

Buds form in summer and wait through winter, many tightly wrapped in scales. After enough cold, warm spring days start them growing. The tree also feeds and shelters animals year-round: squirrels nest in it, birds eat its seeds, and insects eat its leaves.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Visit four times, once in each season. Take the photo, draw the tree, and mark the weather with a picture: sun, cloud, raindrop or snowflake. Add "coat" or "no coat." Ask your child to describe the tree in one sentence. Write it down. Make the poster with four big boxes, one for each season.

### Ages 8 to 10
Visit monthly. At about your child's chest height, wrap string around the trunk, mark where it meets its end, and measure to that mark in cm (an adult handles any metal tape measure or cutting). Mark the spot so you measure there every time. Trunks grow slowly, so "no change" may be a real result. From a weather site, record the high temperature and hours from sunrise to sunset. In Virginia, day length runs from about 9 and a half hours in late December to almost 15 hours in late June. Graph day length by month, then mark when the leaves changed.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If photos are hard to compare, you likely stood in different spots. Mark your spot with a large painted stone (too big to swallow—never a small stone around children under 4) or a note on your phone. If the tree stays green all winter, it may be an evergreen like a holly or southern magnolia. Keep it as a comparison and adopt a second tree. If you miss a month, just carry on. Even four visits a year show the pattern.
