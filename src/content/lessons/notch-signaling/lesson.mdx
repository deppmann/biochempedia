**A fruit fly with one broken copy of a gene called *Notch* has nicks in the edges of its wings. An embryo with no working copy builds too much nervous system and too little skin.**

The nicked wing, first described in 1914, gave the gene its name. The embryo tells more. In the part of the embryo's outer layer that gives rise to the nervous system, each cell chooses between becoming a nerve precursor and becoming skin. Normally only some choose nerve. Without Notch, far too many do. So Notch is not what makes a nerve cell. It is what lets a cell be told "not you".

The fly gene was sequenced in 1985 by the laboratory of Spyros Artavanis-Tsakonas, and soon after by that of Michael Young. It encodes a large protein that crosses the membrane once, with 36 repeats on its outer part that resemble epidermal growth factor. In the same issue of *Cell*, Iva Greenwald reported the same kind of repeat in *lin-12*, a worm gene whose mutants make one cell take the fate of its neighbour. A fly and a worm use one family of receptors to settle what two touching cells become. The sequence did not say how. The answer has two parts: contact, and cutting.

## The idea: the signal is a touch and a pull

Most signals you have met leave the cell that makes them. Notch's ligands do not. In flies they are Delta and Serrate; in mammals, the Delta-like and Jagged proteins. Each crosses the membrane of the sending cell and stays anchored there, while the receptor is anchored in the receiving cell. The two can meet only where two cell surfaces meet, so a cell hears Notch only from cells it touches. In one early test, cultured fly cells that do not normally stick together were made to produce either Notch or Delta, and the Notch cells clumped with the Delta cells.

Binding is not enough. In culture, a piece of ligand free in solution generally fails to switch Notch on, while the same piece fixed to a dish works. In the standard account the receptor has to be pulled. After binding, the sending cell draws its ligand back inside by endocytosis, the pinching-in of membrane to form a vesicle, and the tug passes to the receptor it holds.

Just outside the membrane, Notch folds into a compact lock called the negative regulatory region. Three small modules wrap around the spot where the receptor can be cut and hide it from proteases. The pull peels the lock open. At this point the receptor has registered one fact: a neighbour is holding on and pulling.

## Three cuts: how the receptor becomes the message

1. **While the receptor is made.** In mammalian cells a furin-like protease in the Golgi cuts the new receptor once in its outer part. The two pieces stay clasped together and reach the surface that way. This cut prepares the receptor and carries no signal.
2. **After the pull.** An ADAM protease (ADAM10 in mammals) cuts at the newly exposed site, just outside the membrane. The outer part leaves with the ligand, and a stub stays in the membrane.
3. **Inside the membrane.** γ-Secretase, a complex of four proteins whose cutting subunit is presenilin, cuts the stub within its membrane-spanning segment. It accepts a membrane protein only after most of the outer part has been trimmed away, which is why the second cut must come first.

The third cut happens in a strange place. Cutting a peptide bond uses water, and the middle of a membrane is oily, so presenilin keeps its two catalytic aspartates in a pocket that water can reach.

That cut frees the inner part of the receptor, the Notch intracellular domain (NICD), which enters the nucleus. A DNA-binding protein called CSL already sits on the target genes and, in the standard account, keeps them off with co-repressors. NICD binds CSL, displaces the co-repressors and recruits a co-activator called Mastermind. The genes turn on. Many encode repressors of the Hes family.

How was it shown that the cut-off inner part is the signal? In steps, each answering an objection to the one before.

In 1993 Gary Struhl, Kevin Fitzgerald and Iva Greenwald made flies and worms that produced only the inner part of Notch or LIN-12, with nothing left to bind a ligand. It changed cell fates as an activated receptor does, and in flies it was in the nucleus. Other fly laboratories reported active truncated Notch that year.

But a fragment made in excess can end up anywhere. In 1996 Raphael Kopan, Eric Schroeter, Harold Weintraub and Jeffrey Nye used a mouse Notch1 that lacked its outer part and was still anchored in the membrane. It was cut, its inner part went to the nucleus, and protease inhibitors stopped the cut.

In 1998 Struhl and A. Adachi tested the intact receptor in a living fly, where too little reaches the nucleus to see by staining. They placed a transcription activator inside Notch, so that a reporter gene turned on only if that part of the receptor reached the nucleus. It did, and only with ligand. Kopan's laboratory reported ligand-induced release from mouse Notch1 the same year, and two other fly laboratories (Lecourtois and Schweisguth; Kidd, Lieber and Young) reported ligand-dependent processing of Notch.

In 1999 Struhl and Greenwald showed that flies without Presenilin cannot signal through Notch; other laboratories reported the same need in flies and in mouse cells at the same time.

## What this design gives up, and what it buys

Set Notch beside a kinase cascade. There, each active enzyme modifies many substrates, so the signal grows at every step, and phosphatases can reset it. Notch has no second messenger and no kinase relay. Between the membrane and the gene nothing multiplies: one cut receptor gives one NICD. A cut cannot be undone, so each receptor signals once.

Set it beside the STAT and Smad routes from Unit 4. There too a protein travels from the membrane to the nucleus and acts on genes. But those receptors are kinases, or borrow one, and one active receptor can phosphorylate one STAT or Smad after another. In Notch the traveller is a piece of the receptor itself.

What the cell buys is a count. The amount of NICD made follows, one for one, the number of receptors a neighbour has pulled and cut. The signal cannot spread to cells that are not touching. It is also brief: a kinase in the nucleus marks NICD's tail, an E3 ligase adds ubiquitin, and the proteasome destroys it (see the lesson on ubiquitin and proteolysis), so the genes go quiet soon after the cutting stops.

The price is sensitivity to dose. With no amplification to cover a shortfall, half the normal amount of a component can show. That is a likely reason a fly with one working copy of *Notch* has nicked wings.

## Two equal neighbours, two fates

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

In the gonad of a worm larva two cells, Z1.ppp and Z4.aaa, lie side by side. In every worm one becomes the anchor cell and the other a ventral uterine precursor, but which is which varies: each takes the anchor fate in about half of animals. Kill either one with a laser and the survivor always becomes the anchor cell, so the other fate needs a neighbour. Greenwald's thesis work with Paul Sternberg and Robert Horvitz tied the choice to *lin-12*. With no LIN-12, both cells become anchor cells. With overactive LIN-12, neither does.

### Design problem

Two neighbouring cells start out the same. One must become a nerve cell and the other a skin cell, and nothing outside tells them which. Design a circuit between the two cells that turns a small chance difference into opposite fates. Then predict what the tissue looks like if the receptor in your circuit is missing from every cell.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Give each cell a ligand on its surface and a receptor for that ligand. Add two rules. First, signal received through the receptor blocks the nerve program, so a cell that receives enough becomes skin. Second, the same received signal lowers the cell's own production of ligand.

Now let chance give cell A slightly more ligand. Cell B receives more signal and makes less ligand. Cell A then receives less signal and makes more. Each turn of the loop widens the gap, until A sends without receiving (nerve) and B receives without sending (skin). Two inhibitions in a row make positive feedback, the switch-making loop from the lesson on signaling circuits, here stretched across two cells.

Without the receptor, no cell can be told "not you". Every cell runs the nerve program, and the tissue has too many nerve cells and too little skin. That is the fly embryo at the top of this lesson. It is also the worm with no LIN-12, where both cells take the same fate.

</details>

Real cells are wired much like this, and the arrangement is called lateral inhibition. In the worm, both cells at first make LIN-12 and its ligand, LAG-2. Before the fates are fixed, the future uterine precursor keeps the receptor and loses the ligand, and the future anchor cell does the reverse. In mosaic worms in which only one of the two cells lacks LIN-12, that cell always becomes the anchor cell: the cell that cannot hear ends up as the sender. In the textbook model of the fly, received signal turns on Hes-type repressors, which shut off the genes that drive both the nerve fate and Delta production. Because the signal neither spreads nor multiplies, each cell compares itself only with the cells it touches, and the pattern comes out fine-grained: one nerve precursor among cells that become skin.

## When Notch is stuck on, or missing

**Stuck on.** In 1991 the human gene *NOTCH1* was found broken by a chromosome rearrangement in rare cases of T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, leaving a shortened receptor. In 2004 mutations in *NOTCH1* were reported in more than half of cases. They cluster in two places. Some loosen the lock near the membrane, so the second cut happens without a pull. Others remove the part of the tail that marks NICD for destruction, so each NICD lasts longer.

**Missing.** Alagille syndrome is a dominant genetic disease. In most cases one copy of *JAG1*, the gene for the ligand Jagged1, is broken. An affected child has too few bile ducts in the liver and often a heart defect. As in the fly's wing, one good copy is not enough.

**Shared scissors.** γ-Secretase also cuts the amyloid precursor protein, and one product is the peptide that builds up in the brain in Alzheimer's disease. A worm made the connection in 1995, when Diane Levitan and Greenwald found that *sel-12*, a gene that helps *lin-12* signal, resembles a human gene whose mutations cause an inherited, early-onset form of the disease. Drugs that block γ-secretase were developed to lower the peptide, and they block the Notch cut as well. In rodents they turned dividing cells of the gut lining into mucus-secreting goblet cells, the change expected when Notch is lost there. A large trial of one such drug, semagacestat, was stopped in 2010 after patients taking it did worse than those on placebo.
