**A fruit fly with one broken copy of a gene called *Notch* has nicks in the edges of its wings. An embryo with no working copy builds too much nervous system and too little skin.**

The nicked wing, first described in 1914, gave the gene its name. In the embryo's outer layer, each cell chooses between nerve precursor and skin; without Notch, far too many choose nerve. So Notch does not make a nerve cell—it lets a cell be told "not you". Fly *Notch* and the worm cell-fate gene *lin-12* encode one family of single-pass membrane receptors (see Going deeper). The first film showed how a neighbour's pull turns Notch into a nuclear signal; below are the biochemical details, the experiments that showed them, and how equal neighbours use that signal.

## Touch, pull and the lock

Notch's ligands are single-pass membrane proteins fixed in the sending cell: **Delta** and **Serrate** in flies, and the **Delta-like** and **Jagged** proteins in mammals. Because Notch is anchored in the receiving cell, the main fly and mammalian pathways signal only where cells touch (worms have a few secreted ligands, and cells can touch across several diameters via thin extensions). In an early test, cultured fly cells that do not normally stick together were made to produce either Notch or Delta, and Notch cells clumped with Delta cells.

The first film showed why binding alone fails: just outside the receiving membrane, three small modules fold into a lock—the **negative regulatory region**—that hides Notch's cutting site. In the standard account, the sending cell pulls bound ligand inward by **endocytosis** (the pinching-in of membrane to form a vesicle), and the tug peels the lock open.

## One preparation cut and two signal-triggered cuts

Mammalian Notch undergoes three cuts, but only the last two carry a signal:

1. **Preparation (Cut 1, mammalian Golgi).** While the receptor is made, a furin-like protease cuts its outer part once. The two pieces stay clasped together and reach the surface that way; this cut prepares Notch and carries no signal.
2. **Outside the membrane (Cut 2, after the pull).** An **ADAM protease** (**ADAM10** in mammals) cuts the newly exposed site. The outer part leaves with the ligand, leaving a membrane-anchored stub.
3. **Inside the membrane (Cut 3).** **γ-Secretase**—a four-protein complex whose cutting subunit is **presenilin**—cuts the stub within its membrane-spanning segment. It accepts a stub only after most of the outer part is trimmed away, so Cut 2 must come first. Because hydrolysing a peptide bond needs water in an oily bilayer, presenilin holds its two catalytic aspartates in a pocket water can reach.

Cut 3 frees the **Notch intracellular domain (NICD)**, which enters the nucleus. There, the DNA-binding protein **CSL** holds target genes off with co-repressors in the standard account; NICD binds CSL, displaces co-repressors and recruits the co-activator **Mastermind** to switch targets on. Many targets encode **Hes-family repressors**—a two-step sign flip in which activating a repressor shuts off the nerve programme and tells the cell "not you". Live imaging in flies refines the off state: without signal, CSL binds DNA only briefly, staying far longer once NICD and Mastermind arrive.

### How proteolytic release was shown

Four experiments showed, step by step, that Notch's freed inner part is the signal, each answering an objection to the one before. In 1993, Gary Struhl, Kevin Fitzgerald and Iva Greenwald expressed only the free inner part of fly Notch or worm LIN-12 (other fly groups saw the same), leaving the objection that excess free fragment might drift into nuclei without real cutting. In 1996, Raphael Kopan, Eric Schroeter, Harold Weintraub and Jeffrey Nye tested outer-deleted, membrane-anchored mouse Notch1 with and without protease inhibitors. Still, endogenous nuclear NICD from *intact* Notch is too scarce to see by antibody staining.

**Predict.** Struhl and A. Adachi (1998) inserted a transcription activator into the inner tail of *intact*, full-length Notch in living flies so a sensitive reporter turned on only if that tail reached the nucleus. Where should the reporter turn on?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only where ligand is present. Without a neighbour's ligand, the intact receptor stays uncut at the membrane and keeps the activator out of the nucleus.

</details>

Kopan's laboratory reported ligand-induced release from full-length mouse Notch1 that same year (parallel fly reports are in Going deeper), and in 1999 Struhl and Greenwald—alongside other fly and mouse laboratories—showed that Notch needs Presenilin to signal.

## What this design gives up, and what it buys

The first film contrasted Notch with a kinase cascade; **STAT and Smad** routes from Unit 4 sharpen the point. There too a protein moves from membrane to nucleus, yet the receptor (or its borrowed kinase) acts catalytically on one STAT or Smad after another. In Notch, the traveller is a piece of the receptor itself.

| Pathway | Amplification before nucleus | Receptor reuse | Nuclear traveller |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kinase cascade (RTK) | High; phosphatases reset | Reused | Downstream kinase or factor |
| STAT / Smad (Unit 4) | Catalytic (many copies modified) | Reused | Phosphorylated STAT or Smad |
| Notch | None (1 cut → 1 NICD) | Used once (irreversible) | Freed receptor inner domain |

Put numbers on the gap: 100 pulled Notch receptors release 100 NICDs and leave 100 fewer intact receptors, whereas 100 active receptor kinases driving a 20-fold cascade produce 2,000 modified substrates while all 100 receptors stay intact.

What the cell buys is a local, non-diffusing count. The signal is also brief: a nuclear kinase marks NICD's tail, an E3 ligase adds ubiquitin, and the proteasome destroys it (see the lesson on ubiquitin and proteolysis), so many direct target genes go quiet soon after cutting stops. Standing nuclear NICD thus reflects both 1:1 release and rapid clearance.

**Predict.** A fly with one broken copy of *Notch* makes roughly half the normal number of receptors. Why does a 50% drop show up readily here (nicked wings), unlike in many kinase cascades?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

With no amplification before the gene to cover a shortfall, half the receptors yield roughly half the NICD count—a likely reason one broken *Notch* copy nicks the wing.

</details>

One pulled receptor delivers one unamplified message to a touching cell; the next question is how two identical neighbours use that local count to split into opposite fates.

## Two equal neighbours, two fates

The second film traced how **lateral inhibition** widens a tiny chance lead across two touching cells. The sharpest genetic test comes from a worm larva's gonad, where two equivalent neighbouring cells (Z1.ppp and Z4.aaa) lie side by side. Normally one becomes the **anchor cell (AC)** and the other a **ventral uterine precursor (VU)**, each taking the AC fate in about half of animals. Greenwald's thesis work with Paul Sternberg and Robert Horvitz tied this choice to *lin-12* (the worm Notch receptor), and both cells initially express LIN-12 and its ligand **LAG-2** before diverging into receptor-only VU and ligand-only AC.

**Predict.** If a mutation locks LIN-12 constitutively ON in both worm gonad cells without any ligand, what fate do the two cells take?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Neither becomes an anchor cell. Constitutive LIN-12 runs the received-signal programme in both cells, suppressing the default AC fate.

</details>

In the textbook model of the fly, received Notch turns on Hes-type repressors that shut off both the nerve-fate genes and Delta production. Once again, when Notch lowers a cell's sending, equal neighbours split into opposite fates—and because the signal neither diffuses nor multiplies, each cell compares itself only with cells it touches, including neighbours a few diameters away reached by thin moving extensions on the fly's back (live imaging shows these help space bristles).

### Design problem

Two neighbouring cells start out the same. One must become a nerve cell and the other a skin cell, with no outside cue. Design a circuit between them that turns a small chance difference into opposite fates, then predict what the tissue looks like if the receptor is missing from every cell.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Give each cell a surface ligand and a receptor with two rules: received signal blocks the nerve programme (favouring skin) and lowers the cell's own ligand production. A slight chance lead in cell A lowers cell B's ligand, which lowers the signal back to A—two inhibitions in a row create positive feedback (the switch-making loop from the lesson on signaling circuits), stretched across two cells until A sends (nerve) and B receives (skin).

Without the receptor, no cell can be told "not you". Every cell runs the nerve programme, giving too much nervous system and too little skin—the fly embryo at the top of this lesson, and the worm without LIN-12 where both cells become anchor cells.

</details>

## When Notch is stuck on, or missing

**Stuck on.** In 1991, human *NOTCH1* was found broken by a chromosome rearrangement in rare cases of **T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia**, leaving a shortened receptor. In 2004, *NOTCH1* mutations were reported in over half of cases, clustering in two places: some loosen the lock near the membrane so Cut 2 happens without a pull, while others remove the tail region that marks NICD for destruction so each NICD lasts longer.

**Missing.** **Alagille syndrome** is a dominant genetic disease in which, in most cases, one copy of *JAG1* (encoding Jagged1) is broken. Most affected children have too few bile ducts in the liver and many have a heart defect; severity varies widely. As in the fly's wing, one working copy is not enough.

**Shared scissors.** γ-Secretase also cuts amyloid precursor protein, producing the peptide that builds up in the brain in Alzheimer's disease. In 1995, Diane Levitan and Greenwald found that worm *sel-12* (which helps *lin-12* signal) resembles a human gene mutated in inherited, early-onset Alzheimer's disease. Drugs that block γ-secretase lower that peptide but also block the Notch cut: in rodents they turned dividing cells of the gut lining into mucus-secreting goblet cells, the change expected when Notch is lost there. A large trial of one such drug, **semagacestat**, was stopped in 2010 after patients taking it did worse than those on placebo.

## Going deeper

### How fly Notch and worm lin-12 were linked (1985)

In December 1985, Spyros Artavanis-Tsakonas's laboratory sequenced fly *Notch* (followed soon after by Michael Young's laboratory), finding a large single-pass membrane protein with 36 outer repeats resembling epidermal growth factor. In the same issue of *Cell*, Iva Greenwald reported the same repeats in worm *lin-12*, uniting a nicked-wing fly mutant and a worm cell-fate gene into one receptor family.

### Parallel 1998 and 1999 cleavage reports

Alongside Struhl and Adachi's 1998 fly reporter and Kopan's mouse Notch1 release report, two other fly laboratories—Lecourtois and Schweisguth, and Kidd, Lieber and Young—reported ligand-dependent processing of Notch. In 1999, other fly and mouse laboratories likewise reported the Presenilin requirement at the same time as Struhl and Greenwald.
