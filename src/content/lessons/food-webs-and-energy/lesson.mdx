## Explain: living and nonliving parts of an ecosystem

<WordBank terms={['ecosystem', 'biotic factor', 'abiotic factor', 'producer', 'first-order consumer', 'second-order consumer', 'third-order consumer', 'decomposer', 'food web', 'energy pyramid']} />

An **ecosystem** is all the living things in an area plus their nonliving surroundings. The Chesapeake Bay, a pond and a forest are all ecosystems.

A **biotic factor** is any living or once-living part of an ecosystem. That includes organisms, their parts and their wastes:
- A menhaden or an osprey.
- A fallen leaf or a fish scale.
- Osprey droppings.
- Parasites and diseases.

An **abiotic factor** is a nonliving part of an ecosystem. Abiotic factors decide which kinds of organisms can live in a place, and how many. Examples:
- Temperature.
- How much rain falls in a year.
- The pH of a lake or pond (how acidic or basic the water is).
- Minerals in the soil.
- How much light reaches different depths of the ocean.

Light fades as you go deeper in water, so deep down plankton capture little energy. A change in temperature or rainfall can also change what lives in a place.

## Explain: food chains and food webs

Nearly every food web you will meet starts with a **producer**, an organism that captures energy from sunlight and stores it in food. In the open water of the Bay, the main producers are phytoplankton, tiny drifting algae. You saw how they capture energy in [Photosynthesis Feeds the World](/lessons/photosynthesis-feeds-the-world/).

Consumers get energy by eating other organisms:
- A **first-order consumer** eats producers. Many zooplankton, tiny drifting animals, eat phytoplankton.
- A **second-order consumer** eats first-order consumers.
- A **third-order consumer** eats second-order consumers.

A **decomposer**, such as many bacteria and fungi, breaks down dead matter and wastes from every level. You will study them in [Producers, Consumers and Decomposers](/lessons/producers-consumers-decomposers/).

A **food chain** is a straight-line model of one path that energy takes. The arrows point from the food to the eater. They show which way the energy moves:

> sunlight → phytoplankton → zooplankton → menhaden → osprey

In this chain, the osprey is a third-order consumer. But menhaden also eat phytoplankton directly. In the chain phytoplankton → menhaden → osprey, the osprey is a second-order consumer.

A **food web** shows many food chains linked together. Larger fish eat menhaden too. If a disease killed many menhaden, the ospreys, the larger fish and the plankton could all change. A web shows those links.

## Explain: why each level has less energy

Stack the energy of each level as a block, with producers on the bottom. Each block is smaller than the one below. This stack is an **energy pyramid**, a model of how much energy is available at each level.

Why does each level have less? Most of the energy in the plankton a menhaden eats does not become new menhaden.
- Much of it is given off as thermal energy (heat) during the fish’s life processes. These include swimming and breaking down sugar in respiration. You met respiration in [Releasing Energy from Food](/lessons/releasing-energy-from-food/).
- Some of the food passes through the fish without being digested. Decomposers can use that energy.
- Only the rest is built into the fish’s body, where an osprey can get it.

No energy change is perfect. Energy is not used up. It changes form, and much becomes heat that spreads into the water and air. No living thing can use that heat to make food.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/food-webs-and-energy/where-a-menhadens-energy-goes.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a menhaden takes in energy from plankton. A wide wavy arrow shows much of that energy leaving the fish as heat into the water and air, a thin dashed arrow carries undigested food down to decomposers, and a narrow arrow carries the energy built into the fish's body to an osprey." caption="Most of the energy a menhaden takes in does not become new menhaden. Much leaves as heat, some passes through to decomposers, and only the rest can reach an osprey." />

As a rough rule, about one-tenth of the energy in one level is available to the next. Real measurements vary from place to place. Here is a model with made-up numbers and exactly one-tenth per step:

| Level | Energy (units per square meter per year) |
|---|---|
| Producers | 10,000 |
| First-order consumers | 1,000 |
| Second-order consumers | 100 |
| Third-order consumers | 10 |

Only 10 of the 10,000 units reach the top. That helps explain why a place can feed fewer top predators than small fish. But an energy pyramid is not a head count. One large tree can feed many insects. Energy limits top predators but does not give an exact number.


In most ecosystems, energy flows one way: in as sunlight, out as heat. Matter is different. Decomposers return carbon and nutrients that producers use again, as you will see in [The Carbon and Water Cycles](/lessons/carbon-and-water-cycles/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** At each step much of the energy is given off as heat, so only roughly one-tenth is available to the next level.</Support>

## Elaborate: real energy numbers from a Florida spring

In the 1950s, the ecologist Howard T. Odum studied the clear water at Silver Springs, Florida. He estimated the energy flowing into each level for each square meter of the spring in a year. His unit was the kilocalorie. One kilocalorie is one Calorie on a food label, about 4,184 joules.

| Level at Silver Springs | Energy (kilocalories per square meter per year) | Percent of the level below |
|---|---|---|
| Producers (plants and algae) | 20,810 | starting level |
| First-order consumers (plant eaters) | 3,368 | 16.2 |
| Second-order consumers (meat eaters) | 383 | 11.4 |
| Third-order consumers (top meat eaters) | 21 | 5.5 |
| Decomposers (bacteria and others) | 5,060 | a side branch fed by all levels |

Source: Howard T. Odum, Ecological Monographs, 1957, Figure 7, yearly estimates for the upper Silver Springs run in Florida. Producers: energy captured from sunlight. Consumers: energy taken in. Odum called these estimates tentative. We calculated the percents.

1. Describe the trend in energy from producers to third-order consumers.
2. Which step was closest to 10 percent? Which was farthest from 10 percent, counting in percentage points?
3. Decomposers took in more energy than the plant eaters. Where could that energy come from?

### Design problem

Mealworms are beetle larvae that eat oat bran. Plan an investigation to compare how much food mealworms eat with how much mass they gain.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** How does the mass of bran eaten compare with the mass the mealworms gain?
- **Independent variable:** whether a container has mealworms in it or not.
- **Dependent variable:** mass of the mealworms at the start and end, and mass of the bran left after sifting out the mealworms and their waste, in grams, over 7 days.
- **Constants:** 20 mealworms and 20 grams of bran per container, same temperature, same container size.
- **Control:** a container of bran with no mealworms. It shows how much mass the bran loses by drying out.
- **Trials:** three containers with mealworms and three without. Report the mean.
- **Safety:** wash your hands after handling, keep containers closed, and never release mealworms outdoors.

If the mealworms gain much less mass than the bran they ate, most of the food did not become new mealworm. Respiration and waste account for much of the rest.

</details>
