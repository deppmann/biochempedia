    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Eugene Landis had shown in 1927 that red cells in a blocked frog capillary drift with the
      fluid crossing its wall. In 1974 C. Charles Michel, with Mason, Curry, Tooke and Hunter, published
      a development of that method for single capillaries of the frog mesentery. The vessel is
      perfused through a micropipette, so the experimenter sets the pressure and the perfusate;
      it is blocked briefly, and the movement of marker cells gives the filtration rate per unit
      of wall. Repeated at several pressures, the slope of filtration against pressure gives the
      hydraulic conductivity and the intercept gives the effective oncotic pressure across the
      wall. Because each vessel can be measured before and after a treatment, it serves as its
      own control, and the approach was later carried to rat mesentery by several laboratories.
      Michel's later papers developed the idea that the oncotic pressure opposing filtration is
      the one just beneath the glycocalyx. His 2010 review with Levick, and later reviews with
      T. E. Woodcock, set out the revised principle for physiologists and for anaesthetists.
  - name: "J. Rodney Levick"
    contribution: >-
      Concluded from a review of mammalian data (1991) that sustained downstream reabsorption
      matching upstream filtration is difficult to uphold, and coauthored the 2010 statement of
      the revised Starling principle with Michel.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Levick, Experimental Physiology 76:825-857 (1991), doi:10.1113/expphysiol.1991.sp003549; Levick and Michel, Cardiovascular Research 87:198-210 (2010), doi:10.1093/cvr/cvq062."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The textbook picture of fluid leaving at the arterial end of a capillary and returning at
      the venous end makes a prediction: in steady state, tissues should return most of their
      filtrate through the capillary wall, and lymph should carry only the remainder. In a 1991
      review in Experimental Physiology, J. Rodney Levick set the mammalian measurements against
      that prediction. He treated the forces outside the vessel, tissue pressure and tissue
      protein, as quantities that change as filtration changes rather than as fixed numbers. His
      conclusion was that the traditional conception, upstream filtration largely matched by
      sustained downstream reabsorption, is difficult to uphold. Nineteen years later, in
      Cardiovascular Research, Levick and Michel brought the evidence together as the revised
      Starling principle: in most tissues absorption is transient, slight filtration persists
      in the steady state even in venules, and lymph returns the filtered fluid. Levick also
      wrote a widely used introductory textbook of cardiovascular physiology. The 1991 argument
      gave later single-vessel and whole-tissue studies a clear alternative to test.

      swelling caused by a defect in the lymphatic vessels themselves? Kari Alitalo's laboratory
      in Helsinki had been studying VEGFR3, also called FLT4, a receptor tyrosine kinase found on

      lymphatic endothelium. They made hereditary lymphedema a genetic test of the idea that

techniques:
  - name: "Landis-Michel micro-occlusion of single perfused microvessels"
    blurb: >-
      Cannulate one capillary or venule of a thin tissue such as frog or rat mesentery, perfuse
      it at a set pressure, block it briefly and follow marker red cells to get the filtration
      rate per unit area. The slope against pressure gives hydraulic conductivity (Lp); the
      pressure intercept gives the effective oncotic pressure (sigma times the oncotic difference).
  - name: "Tracer electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Put a particle or protein that electron microscopy can see (colloidal mercuric sulfide,
      carbon, ferritin) into the blood, fix the tissue at set times, and find where the tracer
      sits: in vesicles, in junctions, or in gaps between cells. It shows a route at the moment
      of fixation, not the rate of transport.
  - name: "Venous-occlusion plethysmography"
    blurb: >-
      Inflate a cuff around a human limb to raise venous pressure in steps and record limb
      volume. After a fast rise as the veins fill, the slow continuing rise is fluid filtering
      into the tissue, and its slope against pressure estimates the limb's filtration coefficient.
  - name: "Lymphatic cannulation"
    blurb: >-
      Cannulate a lymph vessel draining one tissue, before it reaches a node, and collect lymph
      in an anaesthetized animal. Lymph flow and the lymph-to-plasma protein ratio report that
      tissue's filtration and its capillaries' sieving of protein.

      - "Continuous, with tight clefts and caveolae"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Sinusoids have large openings (open fenestrae or gaps between cells) and a broken or absent basement membrane, so they restrict
      even large molecules little and their reflection coefficient approaches zero. Continuous
      capillaries with tight clefts and caveolae are typical of skeletal muscle and skin, and
      they hold back most plasma protein. Fenestrated capillaries with diaphragm-covered pores
      are found in gut villi and renal peritubular capillaries, not in liver sinusoids.
      Brain-type tight junctions describe the most restrictive continuous capillaries, the
      opposite of a leaky sinusoid.
  - stem: "In one tissue the albumin reflection coefficient of the microvessels falls from 0.9 to 0.6 while capillary pressure, interstitial pressure and both oncotic pressures stay the same. What happens to filtration?"
    choices:
      - "It reverses, as tissue protein now wins"
      - "It stays the same, as pressures are fixed"
      - "It rises, as the oncotic opposition falls"
      - "It falls, as more protein pulls water in"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The oncotic term is sigma times the oncotic difference, so a lower sigma means the same
      protein gradient opposes filtration less, and net filtration rises. Saying more protein
      pulls water in reverses the arrow: a leakier wall lets protein's pull act less, not more.
      Saying it stays the same forgets that sigma multiplies the oncotic difference, so the
      balance changes even with fixed pressures. A reversal to absorption would need the hydrostatic
      difference to fall below the oncotic opposition, and a lower sigma only shrinks that opposition.
  - stem: "Use Jv proportional to (Pc - Pi) - sigma (pi_p - pi_g) with Pc = 22 mmHg, Pi = -2 mmHg, pi_p = 25 mmHg, pi_g = 5 mmHg and sigma = 0.9. What is the net filtration pressure?"

      - "+4 mmHg, outward"
      - "+1.5 mmHg, outward"
      - "+2 mmHg, outward"
      - "+6 mmHg, outward"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The hydrostatic difference is 22 - (-2) = 24 mmHg, and the oncotic opposition is 0.9 x
      (25 - 5) = 18 mmHg, so the net is 24 - 18 = +6 mmHg outward. +4 mmHg comes from leaving

      ignoring the subglycocalyx oncotic pressure and subtracting 0.9 x 25.

  - stem: "In a leg muscle, capillaries filter a little all along their length. By the revised Starling principle, how does most of that fluid get back to the blood?"
    choices:
      - "Through lymph, moved by lymphangions and tissue squeezing"
      - "Through venous-end capillaries, drawn in by plasma protein"
      - "Through lymph, pushed along by capillary blood pressure alone"
      - "Through venous-end capillaries, pushed in by tissue pressure"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      In the steady state the venous ends filter slightly too, so the filtrate returns as lymph.
      It enters initial lymphatics, and collecting lymphatics move it on by intrinsic pumping of
      lymphangions, the segments between secondary valves, plus extrinsic squeezing from muscle

  - stem: "After a sudden haemorrhage, capillary pressure in skeletal muscle falls and the capillaries start to absorb fluid. If the pressure stays low, what happens next?"
    choices:
      - "Absorption continues steadily, as plasma protein is unchanged"
      - "Absorption speeds up, as filtration washes tissue protein away"
      - "Absorption stops, as protein builds up beneath the glycocalyx"
      - "Absorption stops, as the reflection coefficient falls to zero"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Water drawn into the plasma leaves protein behind in the subglycocalyx space, so pi_g rises,
      the protein difference shrinks and absorption stops; the vessel then filters slightly
      again. This temporary absorption is autotransfusion. Steady absorption with unchanged
      plasma protein is the fixed-force picture, which ignores protein left behind by the
      absorbed water. Washout of tissue protein happens during filtration, not absorption, so it
      cannot speed absorption. The reflection coefficient is a property of the wall and does not
      fall to zero when pressure drops.

After an enzyme is perfused, the slope is 4.0 x 10^-7 and filtration reaches zero at 27 cmH2O. Which interpretation fits?"
    choices:
      - "Lp doubled; sigma rose from 0.75 to 0.92"
      - "Lp halved; sigma fell from 0.92 to 0.75"
      - "Lp doubled; sigma fell from 0.92 to 0.75"
      - "Lp unchanged; sigma fell from 0.92 to 0.75"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Filtration is Lp x (P - sigma x 36), so the slope is Lp and the zero-filtration pressure
      is sigma x 36. Before: sigma = 33 / 36 = 0.92. After: sigma = 27 / 36 = 0.75, and the slope
      doubled from 2.0 to 4.0 x 10^-7, so Lp doubled. Saying Lp was unchanged ignores the change
      in slope. A rise in sigma reverses the arrow: a lower intercept means less oncotic
      opposition, so more leak to albumin. Lp halved inverts the slope reading. This is the
      pattern Betteridge and colleagues reported after neuraminidase in rat mesenteric vessels.
      The simple ratio follows this question's assumption that no protein sits beneath the
      glycocalyx; under steady filtration they took sigma as the square root of that ratio,
      which gives about 0.96 and 0.87 from these same intercepts.
  - stem: "In mice, the lymphatics of the tail are cut near its base and the tail swells over the following weeks. Compared with the second group, what would you predict for interstitial fluid of the lymph-blocked tail?"

      - "Protein lower; oncotic opposition to filtration larger"
      - "Protein higher; oncotic opposition to filtration larger"
      - "Protein higher; oncotic opposition to filtration smaller"

    rationale: >-
      Lymph is the route that removes filtered protein from the tissue, so when it is blocked

  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Capillary walls are one cell thick. Gases pass straight through the cells, small dissolved
      substances like glucose and salt pass through gaps between them, and big proteins mostly
      stay in the blood. Two pressures decide how much water leaks out: blood pressure pushes
      water out, and the pull of blood proteins, called oncotic pressure, holds it in. In most
      tissues a small amount of fluid leaks out all along the capillary, all the time: the
      proteins slow the leak but cannot reverse it for long. Lymph vessels collect it and return it to the blood. Swelling,
      called edema, happens when leakage outruns the lymph vessels: when blood pressure in the
      capillaries rises, when blood proteins are low, when the vessel wall becomes leaky, or
      when lymph vessels are blocked.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      Lipid-soluble solutes cross the endothelial membranes; small hydrophilic solutes diffuse

  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      Because the wall leaks protein, there is no true Starling equilibrium. Filtration sets the
      protein concentration beneath the glycocalyx, which feeds back on the oncotic term: at high
      Jv, convection washes the subglycocalyx space toward protein-free filtrate, pi_g falls
      toward zero and the steady-state Jv-Pc relation has slope Lp S; below an inflection just

      flattens the curve (the hockey stick). In single perfused rat mesenteric microvessels,

      and colleagues, 2017), extending Adamson's 1990 pronase result in frog capillaries.

**The diagram in most textbooks shows fluid leaving a capillary at its arterial end and coming back in at its venous end. When physiologists tested it, most of that fluid did not come back that way.**

It was a reasonable model, one Starling himself floated: blood pressure falls along a capillary, so the outward push should win near the arterial end and lose near the venous end. In the reabsorption model as usually taught, about 24 L a day filters out and 20.4 L of it, about 85 percent, comes back in at the venous ends, leaving lymph only the remainder. In fact, in most tissues the microvessels filter a little along their whole length, and lymph carries the fluid home.

Keep one puzzle for the end. Adults born with almost no albumin in their plasma usually have only mild edema, and a few have none. (In newborns and young children the condition can be serious, with fluid retention.) Albumin supplies most of the pull that holds water in the blood, so what protects them?

[Blood vessels, blood and lymph](/lessons/blood-and-vessels/) gave the first look at Starling's balance. Two terms before the rule. **Oncotic pressure** is the osmotic pull of the plasma proteins, drawing water toward the side with more protein. The **glycocalyx** is a coat of proteoglycans and glycoproteins (sugar-covered proteins) lining every capillary, its fibres covering the openings between and through the cells.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Water crossing a capillary wall is pushed out by the pressure difference and held back by the protein difference across the glycocalyx, and that protein difference is set by the flow itself. Filtering rinses protein from under the glycocalyx and keeps the hold-back strong. Absorbing leaves protein behind there and weakens it. So plasma protein can slow filtration but cannot reverse it for long (the no-absorption rule). In the steady state almost every microvessel filters a little along its whole length, lymph carries the fluid home, and edema is filtration outrunning lymph.

The rule covers the steady state in most systemic beds. It does not cover the moments after a sudden fall in pressure, the few beds that absorb steadily, sinusoids, or the glomerulus ([its own balance](/lessons/glomerular-filtration/)). It governs water; nutrients and gases cross mainly by diffusion.

## Push, hold-back and conductance

Take the three forces one at a time, with round values for resting skeletal muscle.

**The push** is the pressure inside the capillary, Pc, minus the pressure in the tissue, Pi. Pc sits between arterial and venous pressure, placed by the ratio of pre- to postcapillary resistance ([resistance, compliance and local flow](/lessons/resistance-compliance-local-flow/)), so a rise in venous pressure moves it more than an equal rise in arterial pressure. Take Pc as 18 mmHg. Pi is slightly below atmospheric in loose tissues such as skin, intestine and lung, about -1 mmHg, though positive in some organs such as liver and kidney. A rise in Pi opposes filtration. Here the push is 18 - (-1) = 19 mmHg.

**The hold-back** comes from the protein difference across the wall. Plasma oncotic pressure, pi_p, is about 26 mmHg in adults by one review. Albumin supplies most of it, about 65 to 80 percent by different reviews, and globulins supply the rest. Some protein reaches the tissue side, and the oncotic pressure of the protein just beyond the glycocalyx, written pi_g and taken here as 6 mmHg, is subtracted. It is not the average for the whole tissue; the next section shows where it sits and why it is not fixed.

A protein can only pull water back across a wall it cannot easily cross. If some albumin can be carried through the wall along with the water, it pulls with only part of its strength. That fraction of its full pull is the **reflection coefficient**, sigma: 1 if the wall passes water and no solute, 0 if it hinders the solute no more than water. For albumin it is high, about 0.8 to 0.96 in the single vessels and tissues measured, varying with the tissue; take 0.9. Here the hold-back is 0.9 x (26 - 6) = 18 mmHg.

**The conductance** is the **hydraulic conductivity**, Lp, times the wall area, S; for whole tissues their product is the **filtration coefficient**, Kf. It sets how much water flow each mmHg of net pressure buys. Push minus hold-back: 19 - 18 = +1 mmHg, outward.

### Why sigma differs from bed to bed

Capillary walls differ in how their endothelial cells join ([epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/)), and sigma follows the wall.

- **Continuous** capillaries (skeletal muscle, skin, lung; the tightest kind in the brain) have an unbroken lining. Cells meet at narrow **intercellular clefts** and, outside the brain, carry many caveolae, small membrane pits and vesicles ([endocytosis](/lessons/endocytosis/)). They hold back most plasma protein.
- **Fenestrated** capillaries (intestinal villi, renal peritubular capillaries, endocrine glands) add **fenestrae**, pores through the thinnest parts of the cell, here covered by a thin diaphragm. They pass more water and small solute.
- **Sinusoidal** capillaries (liver, spleen, bone marrow) have wide openings, large open fenestrae in the liver and gaps between cells elsewhere, and an incomplete or absent basement membrane. They hold back even large molecules little.

Oxygen, carbon dioxide and other lipid-soluble molecules diffuse through the endothelial membranes over the whole surface ([membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/)). Outside the brain, small water-soluble solutes such as glucose, ions and urea diffuse through clefts and fenestrae; brain capillaries close this route, and glucose crosses them on transporters. For them, diffusion moves far more across the wall than filtering water carries. In the **two-pore model**, many small pores, thought to be the spaces between glycocalyx fibres over clefts and fenestrae, pass water and small solutes, and rare large pores pass plasma proteins and other macromolecules. What the large pores are is not settled.

## Why the venous end does not reabsorb

Now do the textbook picture's arithmetic. Pressure falls from about 32 mmHg where blood enters a capillary to about 12 mmHg where it leaves (round textbook end values; because resistance places the mean nearer the venous end, the resting mean used above is 18 mmHg, not the 22 mmHg midpoint of 32 and 12). Hold everything else at the values above, so the hold-back is 18 mmHg everywhere.

| Point along the capillary | Pc (mmHg) | Push, Pc - Pi | Net, push minus 18 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arterial end | 32 | 33 | +15, filters |
| | 24 | 25 | +7, filters |
| | 20 | 21 | +3, filters |
| | 16 | 17 | -1, absorbs |
| Venous end | 12 | 13 | -5, absorbs |

This is the familiar diagram. It predicts that a venule held at low pressure should keep absorbing for as long as you watch it.

**Predict.** The table assumed that the tissue-side protein stays at 6 mmHg wherever water is moving in. But the wall holds protein back. What happens to the protein on the tissue side when water is drawn *into* the blood across that wall?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It is left behind. Water crosses the sieve into the plasma, but most of the protein cannot, so the protein just outside the sieve gets more concentrated. As it rises, the protein difference across the wall shrinks, the hold-back weakens, and absorption slows to a stop. The table's venous rows assumed a hold-back that the absorption itself destroys.

</details>

That is the revised Starling principle in one move: **the hold-back is set by the flow**. The sieve is the glycocalyx, lying over the mouths of the clefts between endothelial cells like a coffee filter over a narrow channel. Just outside the filter, inside the cleft, is a thin film of fluid, the **subglycocalyx space**. Its oncotic pressure is pi_g, not the average protein of the whole interstitium.

- **When the vessel filters**, nearly protein-free fluid streams out along the cleft, rinsing the film. Protein reaching the tissue by large pores must diffuse back against this stream, so pi_g stays below average interstitial oncotic pressure and the hold-back stays strong. Faster filtration rinses harder.
- **When the vessel absorbs**, the stream reverses. Water leaves the film for the plasma and protein is left behind, so pi_g climbs until the hold-back falls to match the push. Then absorption stops.
- **If water stopped moving**, protein would diffuse out until the difference vanished. So there is no true Starling equilibrium.

Now let pi_g follow the flow and redo the capillary. The steady-state numbers come from a simple sieving model tuned so that resting muscle (push 19 mmHg) settles at pi_g = 6 mmHg; they are illustrative, not measurements.

| Pc (mmHg) | Net with pi_g fixed at 6 | pi_g at steady state | Net at steady state |
|---|---|---|---|
| 32 | +15 | about 2.6 | about +12 |
| 24 | +7 | about 3 | about +4 |
| 20 | +3 | about 4.5 | about +1.6 |
| 16 | -1 | about 7.8 | about +0.6 |
| 12 | -5 | about 11.9 | about +0.3 |

Every row filters. Near the arterial end, fast filtration rinses pi_g toward its floor. Near the venous end, where the push is weaker than the plasma proteins' full pull, pi_g rises until only a trickle of filtration is left, and the trickle never becomes absorption.

Plot steady filtration against capillary pressure and you get the **hockey stick**. Above a bend where the push nears sigma x pi_p (0.9 x 26, about 23 mmHg; Pc about 22 mmHg here, just below plasma oncotic pressure), filtration rises steeply, close to a straight line. Below it the filtrate is too slow to wash protein away, so the curve flattens toward zero but stays positive. The old diagram is the straight line continued below zero.

Two refinements. **Absorption is real but temporary.** After a sudden fall in capillary pressure, as after blood loss, a vessel absorbs until protein builds up under the glycocalyx, then filters slightly again. That is **autotransfusion** (reflexes in [blood pressure regulation](/lessons/blood-pressure-regulation/); Part C below). And **steady absorption needs a second stream that keeps the tissue side rinsed with protein-poor fluid.** That happens in the intestinal mucosa ([absorption and the liver](/lessons/absorption-and-liver/)) and around renal peritubular capillaries, where an epithelium delivers nearly protein-free fluid, and in lymph nodes, where incoming lymph, lower in protein than plasma, does the rinsing. Everywhere else, absorption switches itself off, and lymph is the way home.

**How we know.** Levick concluded in 1991 that sustained downstream reabsorption is difficult to uphold, and Woodcock and Michel's 2021 review states that the arterial-end, venous-end model has no reproducible experimental evidence behind it. In single frog mesenteric capillaries perfused through a micropipette (Michel and Phillips, 1987), a vessel held at low pressure absorbed only briefly; in the steady state, fluid movement fell to near zero or slight filtration. When enzymes strip part of the glycocalyx, the same vessel becomes leakier to water and albumin. Both fit the glycocalyx being the sieve. Whether the glycocalyx or the junctions beneath it are the main barrier to protein is still argued, and nearly all of this evidence comes from animal vessels.

## Where the fluid goes: lymph

**Predict.** A resting muscle filters a little all along its capillaries, day and night. Where must that fluid go, and what else must the same route carry?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Into lymph vessels, and through them back to the blood. They must also carry the plasma protein that leaked through the large pores, or protein would build up in the tissue and the hold-back would fail.

</details>

Filtrate enters blind-ended **initial lymphatics**, whose overlapping cells meet at discontinuous **button junctions**, with flap-like openings between the buttons acting as the primary valves. Collecting lymphatics have **secondary valves**, and each segment between two of them is a **lymphangion**, a contractile pump. Lymph moves by this intrinsic pumping plus extrinsic squeezing from muscle and breathing. Lymphatics running inside skeletal muscle are reported to have little or no smooth muscle, so muscle contraction probably drives flow there.

Recent reviews put the fluid filtered from an adult's blood capillaries at about 8 L a day and lymph transport at 8 to 12 L a day. Roughly half of it is thought to be absorbed back into blood within lymph nodes, an estimate resting mainly on animal studies ([lymphoid organs and immune traffic](/lessons/lymphoid-organs-immune-traffic/)). The rest enters the veins by the thoracic duct, at the junction of the left subclavian and left internal jugular veins, and by the right lymphatic duct on the right.

Lymph also carries fat. Long-chain fats leave intestinal cells as chylomicrons, fat-carrying lipoprotein particles, enter the gut's lymph vessels, the **lacteals**, and reach the blood through the thoracic duct, bypassing the liver at first. Medium-chain fatty acids go mostly by the portal vein.

## Worked problem: one muscle, three states

Made-up round numbers for resting adult human skeletal muscle, steady state unless stated: Kf = 0.005 mL per min per mmHg per 100 g (illustrative, of the order estimated in human limbs), 10 kg of muscle (100 units of 100 g), Pc = 18 mmHg, Pi = -1 mmHg, pi_p = 26 mmHg, pi_g = 6 mmHg, sigma = 0.9. Assume one lumped Pc and constant Kf and sigma.

**A, rest.** Push 19 mmHg, hold-back 18 mmHg, net +1 mmHg, as above. Jv = 0.005 x 1 x 100 = 0.5 mL per min, or 720 mL per day, returned as lymph.

**B, venous pressure rises so Pc = 28 mmHg** (as in heart failure; [cardiac output and venous return](/lessons/cardiac-output-venous-return/)). At first net = 29 - 18 = 11 mmHg and Jv = 5.5 mL per min. Over hours, in this example, Pi rises to +2 mmHg and the faster filtrate rinses pi_g down to 3 mmHg: net = (28 - 2) - 0.9 x (26 - 3) = 5.3 mmHg, and Jv = 2.65 mL per min, about half the first value.

**Predict.** Lymph from this muscle can carry at most 1.5 mL per min. Does the muscle swell?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes. Filtration of 2.65 mL per min beats a lymph ceiling of 1.5 mL per min by 1.15 mL per min, about 1.7 L a day: edema. The rise in Pi and the fall in pi_g roughly halved the filtration, but not enough.

</details>

**C, blood loss lowers Pc to 10 mmHg.** Net = 11 - 18 = -7 mmHg, absorption of 3.5 mL per min at first. It stops when 0.9 x (26 - pi_g) = 11, at pi_g = 13.8 mmHg, and then the vessel goes back to filtering slightly, on the shallow arm of the hockey stick.

## Four ways to make edema, and what holds it back

**Edema** is interstitial fluid accumulation when filtration exceeds lymph drainage. Each cause moves mainly one term of the equation, or the lymph.

| Route | What changes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Raised capillary pressure | The push rises | Heart failure (Part B) |
| Low plasma protein | pi_p falls, so the hold-back falls | Hypoalbuminemia, as in congenital analbuminemia; a contributor in liver disease and nephrotic syndrome, where renal sodium retention also drives swelling |
| Leaky microvessels | sigma falls, so the hold-back falls and protein escapes | Inflammation: with histamine in rat cremaster, the leak is at venules, between endothelial cells |
| Blocked lymph | Drainage fails, and protein may build up in the tissue and shrink the hold-back | Lymphedema, including hereditary forms |

The inflammation row comes from Guido Majno and George Palade's electron microscopy of the rat cremaster in 1961 ([innate immunity](/lessons/innate-immunity/)). In **lymphedema**, chronic impaired drainage, the swelling includes fat deposition; one hereditary form is tied to a receptor on lymphatic endothelium.

Three **safety factors** limit swelling: Pi rises as fluid collects, washout (protein rinsed away by faster filtration) lowers tissue and subglycocalyx protein, and lymph flow rises. Part B used the first two.

### Design problem

Raised venous pressure and a histamine leak can both raise lymph flow severalfold. What in the lymph tells them apart?

An anaesthetized dog has a cannula in a prenodal lymphatic draining the hind paw, so lymph reports that tissue's capillaries before a node alters it. Plasma protein is 60 g per L. You can raise limb venous pressure, infuse histamine into the femoral artery, or lower plasma protein by plasmapheresis. Predict lymph flow, the lymph-to-plasma protein ratio (L/P) and protein flux for each, and interpret these invented results: baseline 0.05 mL per min, L/P 0.5; raised venous pressure 0.25 mL per min, L/P 0.25; histamine 0.25 mL per min, L/P 0.7.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

1. *Protein flux = lymph flow x lymph protein.* Baseline 0.05 x 30 mg per mL = 1.5 mg per min; venous pressure 0.25 x 15 = 3.75 mg per min; histamine 0.25 x 42 = 10.5 mg per min, seven times baseline.
2. *Pressure.* Water flow rises more than protein flow, so L/P falls (sieving, the washout safety factor).
3. *Permeability.* Histamine opens venular gaps and sigma falls, so L/P stays high or rises and protein flux climbs. Histamine also dilates arterioles and raises Pc, so the rise in lymph flow alone does not separate the two causes; the high L/P is what points to permeability.
4. *Low plasma protein.* Lymph flow rises while lymph protein falls with plasma protein, so L/P may change little (a simplification).
5. *Controls.* Reach a new steady state at each step, keep the cannula prenodal, compare the other limb, allow for anaesthesia, measure Pi if you can. A dog's paw does not stand for every tissue or for people.

</details>

## Back to the opening: life without albumin

Start with **hypoalbuminemia**, low plasma albumin. It contributes to edema in liver disease and nephrotic syndrome, where renal sodium retention also plays a large part. Suppose albumin falls from 4 to 2 g per dL and pi_p from 26 to 14 mmHg, a round illustrative value, not a clinical conversion (oncotic pressure rises more steeply than linearly with protein concentration). With pi_g fixed at 6 mmHg, Part A's net rises from 1 to 19 - 0.9 x 8 = 11.8 mmHg, enough for severe edema if nothing else adjusted. Washout lowers pi_g: at 2 mmHg, net = 19 - 0.9 x 12 = 8.2 mmHg. Lower, but still large.

| Quantity | Direction after plasma protein falls |
|---|---|
| Plasma oncotic pressure, pi_p | Down |
| Filtration, Jv | Up, most at first |
| Lymph flow | Up |
| Interstitial protein | Down |
| Interstitial pressure, Pi | Up |

So low protein is not harmless: filtration does rise. What holds the line is the three safety factors above, acting together, and in **congenital analbuminemia**, where albumin is almost absent from birth, globulins, which may partly stand in for albumin. That may be why edema in congenital analbuminemia is common but usually mild, and a few people have none.

By the same rule, raising plasma oncotic pressure can slow steady filtration but not reverse it.

## Going deeper

The main line above does not depend on these details.

### Who wrote the equation

Starling argued in 1896, from perfused hind limbs of anaesthetized dogs, that capillary pressure and plasma oncotic pressure set fluid exchange. Staverman defined the reflection coefficient, and Kedem and Katchalsky gave the flow equation that uses it. Putting the subglycocalyx oncotic pressure into it came later, from Michel's work on the revised principle. The hockey stick, the absence of steady absorption, transient absorption and the steadily absorbing beds are drawn from reviews by Levick and Michel (2010) and Michel, Woodcock and Curry (2020).

### Tissue pressure

The push used above takes Pi as about -1 mmHg, slightly below atmospheric, and that round value rests on few human measurements: Noddeland found a mean Pi of about -1.4 mmHg in subcutaneous tissue at heart level in 10 people.

### Routes across the wall

In rat tissues a thin diaphragm across each fenestra is labelled by the protein PV-1 (Stan, Kubitza and Palade, 1999). Glomerular fenestrae have no diaphragms ([glomerular filtration](/lessons/glomerular-filtration/)). Bruns and Palade (1968) found intravenous ferritin inside endothelial vesicles of rat diaphragm-muscle capillaries, not in junctions, and concluded that vesicles are the large pores; a 2021 review lists rare channels or possibly vesicles.

### Testing the sieve

If the glycocalyx is the sieve, stripping part of it should raise Lp and lower sigma in the same vessel. Adamson (1990) found that a minute of pronase, a broad protease, raised Lp of single perfused frog mesenteric capillaries from 2.0 to 4.9 x 10^-7 cm per s per cmH2O with clefts unchanged. Betteridge, Arkill and colleagues (2017) cannulated single mesenteric microvessels in anaesthetized rats and measured Lp by Landis-Michel occlusion (the slope of filtration against pressure), the albumin reflection coefficient, and glycocalyx depth. Lp and sigma were measured in each vessel before and after neuraminidase, which removes sialic acids (n = 7 pairs): Lp rose 1.9-fold, from 1.39 to 2.68 x 10^-7 cm per s per cmH2O, and sigma for albumin fell from 0.96 to 0.87. Depth by electron microscopy was 78 nm in vehicle-treated vessels and 40 nm in neuraminidase-treated vessels, a comparison between different vessels.

That ties sialic acids to water and albumin permeability in rat mesentery. It does not show the effect in human vessels or disease, neuraminidase may act beyond the glycocalyx, and depth estimates in one rat study ranged from 0.17 to 3.02 micrometres with label and method. Dull and Hahn (2022) argue that junctions, not the glycocalyx, limit protein permeability; the debate is open.

### Where an inflammatory leak happens

Majno and Palade injected histamine or serotonin under the scrotal skin of rats, carried a colloidal mercuric sulfide tracer in the blood, and fixed the cremaster muscle 1 to 12 minutes later for electron microscopy. If leak occurred where exchange normally happens, capillaries should open. Instead, openings about 0.1 to 0.8 micrometres wide appeared between endothelial cells in vessels of 7 to 8 micrometres or larger, venular-sized, while capillary-sized vessels (3 to 5 micrometres) looked intact, and the tracer passed the gaps between endothelial cells but was held back by the intact basement membrane. That located this leak in venules, between cells, and ruled out capillaries as its site in this tissue. It did not show how the gaps form, that every organ and mediator behaves this way, or anything about baseline protein leak.

### More on lymphatics

In isolated rat mesenteric lymphatics and thoracic ducts, imposed flow lowered contraction frequency and amplitude (Gashev, Davis and Zawieja, 2002). In mice, endothelial deletion of Nrp1 and Flt1 raised VEGF-A signalling through VEGFR2, zippered lacteal junctions, blocked chylomicron uptake and gave resistance to diet-induced obesity (Zhang and colleagues, 2018). The hereditary form behind the blocked-lymph row of the edema table has been traced to the same lymphatic endothelium: in one family, Milroy disease, a congenital hereditary lymphedema, cosegregated with a mutation in VEGFR3 (Irrthum and colleagues, 2000).

### Nephrotic protein loss

Low plasma albumin is only part of the story in nephrotic edema: the underfill hypothesis (low oncotic pressure drains the plasma) is debated, and renal sodium retention also drives the swelling.
