**Water holds far less oxygen than air, yet a trout's gill can send its blood out at a higher oxygen partial pressure than the water leaving it. Your lung cannot do that with air. Why not?**

The first film built the answer from **diffusion**, the net movement of a gas from higher **partial pressure** to lower (the pressure one gas in a mixture would exert alone: PO2 for oxygen, PCO2 for carbon dioxide), and showed that partial pressure is not amount. Vertebrate blood widens that gap: most of its oxygen is bound to haemoglobin, which adds to the amount without raising the PO2. In this lesson "richer" always means higher PO2.

> **The one idea.** Oxygen and carbon dioxide cross every exchange surface only by diffusion, which pulls the blood's partial pressure toward that of the water or air beside it. So blood that takes up oxygen all along the surface can leave with no higher PO2 than the water or air it last touched, and blood that gives up carbon dioxide all along, with no lower PCO2. Fick's law (area, thickness) and the flows of blood and of water or air set how close the blood gets; how those flows run past each other sets what it last touches; breathing keeps that water or air fresh.
>
> *Scope:* a ceiling on partial pressure, not total gas, for passive exchange at **steady state** (flows and rates constant), one gas at a time, while the water or air stays richer than the blood beside it all along the contact (lower in PCO2, for carbon dioxide), as in every case here. A small or thick surface, short contact time, mismatched air and blood flow, or blood that bypasses the surface leave real blood below it. When streams that touched different gas mix, as in birds, the ceiling holds for each stream, not the mix.

## Fick's law: how fast gas crosses

**Predict.** These numbers are invented. Barrier 1 is 50 m2 and 0.5 micrometres thick; barrier 2 is 30 m2 and 1.0 micrometre thick. With the same gas, partial-pressure difference and material, does barrier 2 manage more or less than half of barrier 1's rate?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Less than half: 30%, because the lost area and the added thickness multiply.

</details>

The rate across a sheet of tissue follows **Fick's law** (see also [size and scaling](/lessons/size-and-scaling/)):

> **rate of gas transfer = k x A x (P1 - P2) / T**

A is the area, P1 - P2 the partial-pressure difference (ΔP), T the barrier thickness, and k a constant for that gas and barrier. For scale, dry air at sea level (about 21% oxygen at about 760 mmHg, 101 kPa) has a PO2 of roughly 160 mmHg (21 kPa).

**Worked problem.**

1. *Ratio.* Rate 2 / Rate 1 = (A2 / A1) x (T1 / T2), since k and ΔP cancel; thickness flips because it sits below the line.
2. *Area.* 30 m2 / 50 m2 = 0.60.
3. *Thickness.* 0.5 micrometres / 1.0 micrometre = 0.50.
4. *Multiply.* 0.60 x 0.50 = 0.30, so 30% remains.

## Water or air: skin, gills and tracheae

Water is hard to breathe: air-saturated fresh water at 15 to 20 degrees C holds roughly 30 times less oxygen per litre than air, and it is about 800 times denser and far more viscous, so a gill does more work for less oxygen.

**Skin.** An earthworm exchanges gases across its moist skin. Frogs combine skin and lungs, and adult lungless salamanders (family Plethodontidae) breathe through the skin and mouth lining alone. Skin breathing works where skin stays wet and oxygen demand per unit of surface is modest.

**Gills.** In teleost fish, the large group of bony fishes that includes trout, each gill is folded into filaments carrying thin plates called lamellae: large area, thin barrier. Most teleosts move water one way, in through the mouth and out under the gill cover; tunas ram it through an open mouth as they swim. Blood runs opposite to the water inside each lamella, the **countercurrent exchange** the first film built, which raises the ceiling to the PO2 of the incoming water. Reaching it is another matter.

**Predict.** A trout's lamellae have plenty of area, but suppose the fish pumps only a trickle of water, carrying less oxygen per minute than the blood could take up. Does countercurrent flow still bring the leaving blood close to the incoming water?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The ceiling is unchanged, but the trickle loses much of its oxygen on the way, so its PO2 falls toward the blood's before the blood is full. Getting near the incoming water takes both a large, thin surface and a water flow whose oxygen-carrying capacity per minute is at least the blood's.

</details>

Blood from a teleost gill goes on to the body in a single circuit, as in [circulation and the heart](/lessons/circulation-and-heart/).

**Tracheae.** Most insects carry oxygen in air, not blood: air enters openings called **spiracles** into **tracheae**, branching tubes whose finest branches reach the tissues. Many larger insects pump the abdomen, and in 2003 Mark Westneat and colleagues used synchrotron X-ray imaging to watch tracheae in live beetles, crickets and ants compress and expand rhythmically.

A leaf, by contrast, exchanges gases by diffusion through pores called stomata, with no pump; see [moving water and sugar in plants](/lessons/plant-transport/).

## Three lungs: mammal, bird, alligator

A **lung** is an internal, air-filled exchange organ.

**Mammals: tidal.** Air flows in and out through the same airways, **tidal ventilation**, into tiny dead-ended sacs, the **alveoli**. Their PO2 sits near 100 mmHg in a resting adult human, below the roughly 150 mmHg of warmed, humidified inhaled air, for the reasons the first film gave.

**Birds: one way, crosscurrent.** Birds have no diaphragm, and their stiff lungs keep a nearly constant volume and exchange gas in narrow tubes called **parabronchi**. Thin-walled **air sacs** in front of and behind the lungs work as bellows as the sternum and ribs move. Through most parabronchi, air flows the same way during inspiration and expiration, so fresh air keeps arriving at one end.

Capillaries cross the parabronchus at right angles, each at a different point along it: **crosscurrent** exchange. Each capillary can at best match the air at its own point: nearly fresh air near the inflow end, only drained air near the outflow end. When the streams join, their oxygen contents combine, so the mixed blood lands between its richest and poorest streams and can carry a higher PO2 than the air leaving the parabronchi.

**Alligators: one way, no air sacs.** In 2010 C. G. Farmer and Kent Sanders reported one-way airflow in the bronchi they measured in the American alligator, Alligator mississippiensis, which has no air sacs. Birds and crocodilians are both **archosaurs**, the reptile group that holds them and their extinct relatives, and the shared trait fits inheritance from a common ancestor without proving it. Later work found one-way flow in monitor lizards and green iguanas too, so the trait may be older than archosaurs, or may have evolved more than once; see [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/).

So air moves one way through most bird parabronchi and the alligator bronchi measured so far, while a mammal's alveoli are dead ends whose gas depends on how much fresh air each breath delivers. One reminder of the rule: blood can at best match the gas it last touched, so a surface works only while that gas is renewed. The second film follows one breath in.

## Moving the gas: aspiration and the frog's pump

The second film showed **aspiration**, the **diaphragm** flattening to lower chest pressure, but not the ribs or the breath out. The external intercostal muscles lift the ribs as the diaphragm contracts, and the lungs follow the enlarging chest. Quiet expiration in adult humans is normally passive recoil of lungs and chest wall once the muscles relax; forceful expiration adds the internal intercostals and abdominal muscles.

The wet lining of the alveoli has surface tension, pulling inward like a soap bubble. **Surfactant**, a lipid and protein mixture secreted by alveolar cells, lowers it, raising **compliance** (how much the lung stretches for a given pressure change), so the lung inflates with less effort.

A frog works the other way. In **buccal pumping** it lowers the floor of its mouth with the nostrils open, drawing in air, then closes the nostrils, opens the glottis (the opening into the airway) and raises the floor, pushing air into the lungs under positive pressure. Mammals and birds both draw air in by lowering pressure: mammals in the chest, birds in the air sacs. Buccal pumping is thought to be the older method, from which aspiration in amniotes (reptiles, birds and mammals) evolved, but a living frog is its own lineage, not an ancestor of mammals or birds.

## Carbon dioxide runs the controller

Breathing rhythm comes from brainstem neurons (see [the nervous system map](/lessons/nervous-system-map/) and [the hypothalamus and autonomic system](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/)). The second film gave J. S. Haldane and J. G. Priestley's 1905 result, which made carbon dioxide the main resting stimulus in these men. Their method: because windpipe gas mixes fresh and stale air, they took the end of a forced breath out as alveolar air, with themselves and colleagues as resting subjects breathing added carbon dioxide or less oxygen. Alveolar PCO2 also stayed nearly constant for each man at rest.

Later work found two kinds of sensor: the **central chemoreceptors** the second film showed, which read the pH of brain extracellular fluid, and the **carotid bodies**, sensory clusters where each carotid artery forks, which respond mainly to low arterial oxygen and are also affected by carbon dioxide. In adult humans, ventilation rises steeply with **hypoxia**, too little oxygen, only once arterial PO2 falls well below normal. In teleosts, low oxygen in the water is a major ventilatory stimulus.

**Predict.** A resting person breathes a gas mixture low enough in oxygen to drop arterial PO2 well below normal, and ventilation rises steeply. Carbon dioxide production stays the same. Which sensor is mainly driving the rise, and what happens to alveolar PCO2 while it does?

</details>

**Worked problem.** The second film showed the direction; here are the numbers. Illustrative values for an adult human at rest: tidal volume (one breath) 500 mL, 12 breaths per minute, and 150 mL of **dead space**, the conducting airways, which have no alveoli and exchange no gas.

1. **Minute ventilation**, total air moved, = 12 x 500 mL = 6,000 mL per minute.
2. **Alveolar ventilation**, fresh air reaching alveoli, = 12 x (500 - 150) = 4,200 mL per minute.
3. Breathe rapidly and shallowly, 24 breaths of 250 mL: minute ventilation = 24 x 250 = 6,000 mL per minute, unchanged.
4. Alveolar ventilation = 24 x (250 - 150) = 2,400 mL per minute, 57% of before.
5. At steady state, breathing ordinary air, alveolar PCO2 is about 0.863 x carbon dioxide production (mL per minute) / alveolar ventilation (L per minute). With production held at an illustrative 200 mL per minute, alveolar PCO2 would rise from about 41 to about 72 mmHg.

### Design problem

Birds have one-way airflow driven by air sacs. Alligators have none. How would you find out whether air moves one way through an alligator's lung, and what would the result tell you?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Put directional probes, such as heated thermistors, in identified bronchi of live alligators: flow the same way during inspiration and expiration means one-way flow there. This is the approach Farmer and Sanders reported in 2010. Then take the brain and breathing muscles out of the question by pumping air in and out of an excised lung through the trachea. If one-way flow persists and the airways have no flap valves, the lung's own structure, most likely its airway geometry, produces it. As a control, a mammal lung on the same pump should show flow reversing with each breath; if not, the probes or pump are at fault.

Limits: Farmer and Sanders's pump tests used four lungs, one live animal had a direction-sensing probe, and only some bronchi were measured.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Ranking flow arrangements, and a tracer aside

In 1972 Johannes Piiper and Peter Scheid compared idealized models of the three arrangements under the matched conditions of the Predict above: countercurrent transferred the most gas by their measure, crosscurrent next, the mixed pool of a mammal lung least. When the surface is small or thick, the arrangements barely differ. The textbook rule that air takes two breaths to pass through the bird lung is a schematic; helium tracer work in songbirds found some inhaled gas reaching the front air sacs within one breath.

### What Haldane and Priestley could not yet see

They argued that carbon dioxide reached the brain's respiratory centre through the arterial blood; separate sensor cells had not yet been found.

### Sensing carbon dioxide in the brainstem

In mice, retrotrapezoid nucleus neurons carry two pH sensors that work in parallel, the proton-sensing receptor GPR4 and the potassium channel TASK-2; losing GPR4 alone removes much of this response (see [GPCRs and G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/)).
