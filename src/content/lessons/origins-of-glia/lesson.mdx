  - name: "Pío del Río-Hortega"
    dates: "1882–1945"
    contribution: >-
      Developed a silver carbonate stain that split Cajal's unclassified "third element" into two
      cell types: microglia, described in a series of papers in 1919, and oligodendroglia, named
      in 1921.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Sierra et al., Glia 64:1801 (2016), translation of the 1919 microglia papers; Río-Hortega 1921, translated by Iglesias-Rozas & Garrosa, Clin. Neuropathol. 31:440 (2012)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Río-Hortega learned his methods in Madrid, first with Nicolás Achúcarro and then in the
      circle of Santiago Ramón y Cajal. Cajal's stains showed star-shaped astrocytes clearly but
      left a crowd of small nuclei with almost no visible processes, which Cajal grouped as a
      residual "third element". Río-Hortega kept adjusting a silver carbonate variant of the Golgi method.
      With it, the third element split in
      two. In papers of 1919 he described microglia, small cells with fine branching processes. In
      1921 he named the other population oligodendroglia, glia with few processes, and described
      many of them lying between bundles of nerve fibres. In 1922 he proposed that they might stand to
      central axons as Schwann cells stand to peripheral ones, a proposal that later work on
      myelin confirmed. His stain could name cells but could not follow their ancestry, which
      left the question this lesson is about.
  - name: "Martin Raff"
    contribution: >-
      With Robert Miller and Mark Noble, found that single A2B5-positive cells from young rat
      optic nerve become oligodendrocytes in serum-free medium and astrocyte-like cells in serum,
      and with Brenda Williams and Miller showed that the choice can be reset by switching media.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Raff, Miller & Noble, Nature 303:390 (1983); Raff, Williams & Miller, EMBO J. 3:1857 (1984)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In 1983 Raff, Robert Miller and Mark Noble reported a glial progenitor in the optic nerve of
      seven-day-old rats. The cells carried a surface ganglioside recognized by the antibody A2B5.
      Grown without serum, they became cells carrying galactocerebroside, the oligodendrocyte
      marker. Grown in fetal calf serum, they became cells carrying GFAP, the astrocyte marker,
      with a shape unlike most astrocytes from the brain. The lineage was named O-2A, for
      oligodendrocyte-type-2 astrocyte. An obvious objection was that each medium simply kept
      alive a different set of already decided cells. In 1984 Raff, Brenda Williams and Miller
      moved cultures from one medium to the other after one, two or three days, and counted all
      recovered cells to check for selective death. The outcome followed the medium. A move after
      one or two days in serum could still reverse the choice, after which it became fixed. The work made the timing of a glial decision
      something that could be measured in a dish, and led to the later finding that PDGF from
      astrocytes keeps these precursors dividing.
  - name: "Florent Ginhoux"
    contribution: >-
      With Miriam Merad and colleagues, used a pulse-labeling fate map in mouse embryos to show
      that adult microglia descend mainly from yolk-sac progenitors labeled before embryonic day 8.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Ginhoux et al., Science 330:841 (2010)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By 2009 there was a proposal, from Fabrice Alliot, Isabelle Godin and Bernard Pessac, that
      microglia arise from yolk-sac progenitors, and a competing idea that blood monocytes from the
      bone marrow replace them. Ginhoux, working in Miriam Merad's group in New York with a team
      of twelve authors, tested this in mice. First they showed that in newborn bone marrow
      chimeras most microglia remained of host origin, while irradiated adults gained donor
      microglia over time, which suggested that damage opens the brain to outside cells. Then
      they used a Runx1 reporter mouse built by Igor Samokhvalov's group, in which one dose of
      tamoxifen permanently marks cells that express Runx1 at that moment and all their
      descendants. A pulse at about embryonic day 7.5 labeled a similar fraction of yolk-sac
      macrophages and adult microglia, but very few adult blood cells. Later pulses labeled blood
      but few microglia. The paper gave microglia a birthplace outside the nervous system and set
      up later work on the precursors involved.
  - name: "Kristján Jessen and Rhona Mirsky"
    contribution: >-
      Defined the Schwann cell precursor of embryonic rat nerves, a crest-derived stage that dies
      without axons, and with colleagues showed that neuregulin can replace the axonal signal for
      its survival.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Jessen et al., Neuron 12:509 (1994); Dong et al., Neuron 15:585 (1995); Jessen, Mirsky & Lloyd, Cold Spring Harb. Perspect. Biol. 7:a020487 (2015)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Working together in London for decades, Jessen and Mirsky asked what happens between the
      neural crest cell that leaves the neural tube and the Schwann cell that later wraps an
      axon. In embryonic rat nerves they described an intermediate cell, the Schwann cell
      precursor, that differs from crest cells and from Schwann cells in its markers and its
      behaviour. Taken out of the nerve and grown alone, these precursors died, but neurons kept
      them alive, which pointed to a survival signal from axons. In a 1995 paper led by Zhong
      Dong, the group reported that neuregulin, a growth factor made by axons and read through
      ErbB receptors, supported precursor survival and drove their conversion into Schwann cells.
      Mouse genetics from other laboratories fitted: mice lacking neuregulin-1 or the receptor
      ErbB3 have nerves almost empty of Schwann cell precursors. The work turned the Schwann cell
      lineage into a sequence of steps, each with its own dependence on the axon.

techniques:
  - name: "Clonal culture with medium switching"
    blurb: >-
      Single progenitors, or cultures of known composition, are grown in two defined media and
      moved from one to the other at chosen times. Counting all surviving cells as well as their
      markers separates a signal that instructs a fate from one that only selects survivors.
  - name: "Inducible genetic fate mapping"
    blurb: >-
      A Cre recombinase fused to an estrogen-receptor fragment is made from a lineage gene's
      promoter. A single dose of tamoxifen lets Cre act for a short window, about a day in the Runx1 line used for microglia, so it switches on a
      permanent reporter only in cells expressing the gene then, and in all their descendants.
      The pulse marks only some of those cells, so a labeled fraction is read against the
      fraction marked in the source.
  - name: "Bone marrow chimeras and parabiosis"
    blurb: >-
      Marked bone marrow is transplanted into a host, or two mice are joined so that they share
      blood. If a tissue's resident cells are replaced from blood, they take on the donor's mark.
      Irradiation before transplantation can itself let blood cells enter, so parabiosis is the
      cleaner test.
  - name: "Conditional knockout with Cre lines"
    blurb: >-
      A gene is flanked by loxP sites so that it is deleted only in cells that make Cre, the
      recombinase that cuts and rejoins DNA at loxP sites, removing the stretch between them. Choosing a Cre line active in one place and time
      shows when and where a receptor such as ErbB2 is needed.

**Glia do not share one birthplace. Astrocytes and oligodendrocytes come mostly from the same pool of radial glia that made the neurons. Microglia, in mice, descend from cells born in the yolk sac, a membrane outside the embryo, before the brain had a blood-brain barrier. Schwann cells come from the neural crest.**

"Glia" covers several kinds of non-neuronal cell that live among neurons. This lesson asks where each kind comes from, how anyone found out, and what tells a progenitor to make one. By the end you should be able to take a mutation and say which glia it is likely to remove, and what else may go with them.

## One job, three birthplaces

Here is the cast. In the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) there are **astrocytes**, star-shaped cells that touch blood vessels and synapses; **oligodendrocytes**, which wrap central axons in myelin, and their dividing **oligodendrocyte precursors (OPCs)**; **ependymal cells** lining the ventricles; and **microglia**, the resident immune cells. In the peripheral nervous system there are **Schwann cells** along nerves and **satellite glia** around neuron cell bodies in ganglia.

Lineage methods gave this answer:

- **Astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and ependymal cells** come from the neuroepithelium of the neural tube, mostly through the same pool of radial glia that made the neurons. In the mouse cortex, many astrocytes are then added after birth by local division of astrocytes that have already differentiated.
- **Microglia** come from the yolk sac, as fate mapping in mice showed.
- **Schwann cells and satellite glia** come from the neural crest, the cells that leave the edges of the closing neural tube (see [the neural crest lesson](/lessons/neural-crest/)).

The neural crest gives a Schwann cell wrapping one peripheral axon and satellite glia around a neuron cell body in a ganglion." caption="Glia is a job, not a lineage. Astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and ependymal cells come from the neural tube, microglia from the yolk sac outside the embryo, and Schwann cells and satellite glia from the neural crest." />

Two of these cells do the same job: oligodendrocytes and myelinating Schwann cells both wrap axons in myelin, in the central and the peripheral nervous system respectively.

**Predict.** Mice lacking the genes Olig1 and Olig2 have no spinal cord oligodendrocytes. Would you expect them to lose the Schwann cells of their nerves too?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No, not as a lineage. Oligodendrocytes come from a strip of the spinal cord marked by Olig2, a transcription factor gene. Schwann cells come from neural crest and are not expected to need Olig genes themselves, so they are not lost as a lineage. Nerves also depend on axons, though, and these mice lack motor neurons, so Schwann cells of the ventral roots could be reduced secondarily.

The reverse holds too. Mice lacking the receptor ErbB3 lose the Schwann cell precursors of their nerves, but oligodendrocytes do not need ErbB3: oligodendrocyte lineage cells lacking it develop normally. Same job, different early requirements in these examples.

A different birthplace does not guarantee different genes, though. Sox10 is needed in both lineages, at different stages. The birthplace tells you which progenitor and which window to ask about; the gene's known role there gives the answer.

</details>

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Glia is a job, not a lineage. To predict which glia a gene or signal removes, ask where the cell was born and what its progenitor needed at that time. What the cell does is a weaker guide: a shared job brings some shared genes, such as Sox10, but the early requirements differ.

The rule predicts the first deficit, in the window when a lineage is made; its limits follow the table at the end.

## How do you learn a cell's ancestry?

Pío del Río-Hortega's silver stain was the first to [tell microglia and oligodendroglia apart](#how-glia-were-first-told-apart). But a stain shows a cell's shape and position. It cannot follow a cell's descendants, so it could not settle where any glial cell came from.

To give a cell a birthplace, you mark a progenitor and look for the mark later in everything it made. That is **fate mapping**, and the modern version is genetic. **Cre** is a recombinase, an enzyme that cuts and rejoins DNA at two short sequences called loxP sites. In a reporter mouse, two loxP sites flank a stop sequence in front of a reporter gene. Make Cre from the promoter of a gene a progenitor uses, and Cre removes the stop in that cell, switching the reporter on. The change is in the DNA, so it is permanent, and every descendant inherits it, whatever genes the descendant uses later.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/origins-of-glia/cre-fate-map.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: three steps. A progenitor carries a reporter gene kept silent by a stop sequence between two loxP sites, with Cre above it; Cre removes the stop and the cell switches the reporter on; a family tree below shows every descendant, whatever cell type it becomes, carrying the same mark." caption="In genetic fate mapping, Cre removes a stop sequence in front of a reporter gene. The change is in the DNA, so every descendant inherits it, whatever genes that descendant uses later." />

**Inducible** fate mapping adds a timer. Cre is fused to a fragment of the estrogen receptor, and a single dose of the drug tamoxifen lets it act for a short window, about a day in the line used for microglia. Only cells expressing the gene during that window are marked, and, as with most pulses, only a fraction of those. Every later count has to be read against that fraction.

## Microglia come from outside

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Microglia are macrophages, and for decades it was argued whether they come from blood monocytes made in the bone marrow or from an earlier source. In 1999 Fabrice Alliot, Isabelle Godin and Bernard Pessac proposed, from where and when the cells appeared in mouse embryos, that microglia arise from the yolk sac.

In 2010 Florent Ginhoux, Miriam Merad and colleagues tested this with an inducible fate map in mice. The Cre was driven by Runx1, a gene used by early blood-forming cells. A dose of tamoxifen at about embryonic day 7.5 marked cells expressing Runx1 then, which were in the yolk sac. When yolk-sac macrophages were examined later in the embryo, about a third carried the mark, not all of them.

**Predict.** Call the yolk-sac labeling 30 percent, and suppose the same pulse marks very few of the cells that make adult blood, say 2 percent. What fraction of adult microglia should carry the mark if they all come from the yolk sac? If half do? If they all come from blood?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Each microglial cell either carries the mark or does not. If labeled and unlabeled founders expand alike, and there is no third source, the labeled share of microglia mirrors the mix of their sources, so the three hypotheses predict clearly different numbers.

| If adult microglia come from | Labeled microglia expected |
| --- | --- |
| the yolk sac, all of them | about 30% |
| half the yolk sac, half blood | about 16% (the average of 30% and 2%) |
| blood, all of them | about 2% |

In adults, about a third of microglia carried the mark, while very few blood cells did. That is the top row. Later pulses marked blood but few microglia.

Read alone, "a third of microglia" sounds like a minority. Read against a source labeled at a third, it means most of them. The reading is [sensitive to the source fraction](#how-sure-is-the-microglia-fate-map), though. So the right words are "most", or "mainly", and the data fit all of them. The comparison cannot give an exact percentage or exclude a small share from another source.

</details>

Two more results say how the cells arrive and what they need. Embryos lacking Ncx1, a sodium-calcium exchanger the heart needs to beat, made yolk-sac macrophages but had no brain microglia, so the progenitors travel in through the early circulation. Microglia also need the receptor CSF1R: mice without it have few or none.

One limit matters. In healthy adult mice, microglia renew themselves and blood monocytes do not replace them. After irradiation or some injuries, monocytes can enter the brain, which is why early bone marrow transplant experiments seemed to show replacement. Microglial roles in pruning synapses belong to [the microglia and complement lesson](/lessons/microglia-complement-pruning/).

## Radial glia switch from neurons to glia

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Radial glia are the stem cells of the developing cortex (see [that lesson](/lessons/radial-glia-progenitors/)). In the mouse cortex they make neurons first, and most astrocytes appear late in gestation and after birth. The same pool of progenitors changes what it makes. So what changes?

In rodent cortex, the best-studied route to astrocytes runs through cytokines, and the evidence for it sorts into three conditions, each tied to a molecule. They frame this route; they are not a complete account of every astrocyte.

1. **A progenitor must still be there.** Proneural genes push a dividing cell to become a neuron (see [proneural genes and lateral inhibition](/lessons/proneural-and-lateral-inhibition/)). Notch signaling turns on Hes repressors, which hold proneural genes down, so a cell receiving Notch stays a progenitor (see [the Notch lesson](/lessons/notch-signaling/)). Notch does not make astrocytes by itself. It keeps a reserve of progenitors undifferentiated through the neurogenic phase, so cells are still there when the later signals arrive (whether Hes also pushes cells toward glia is [less clear](#does-hes-push-progenitors-toward-glia)).
2. **The signal must arrive.** The astrocyte signal is a family of cytokines, CNTF, LIF and cardiotrophin-1, members of the interleukin-6 family. Their receptors share the subunit gp130, which activates JAK kinases. JAK phosphorylates STAT3, which pairs up, enters the nucleus and switches on astrocyte genes such as *GFAP*, which encodes glial fibrillary acidic protein, the standard astrocyte marker (see [the JAK-STAT lesson](/lessons/jak-stat/)). In cultures of rodent cortical progenitors, CNTF drives the appearance of cells expressing GFAP.
3. **The progenitor must be able to read it.** This is **competence**, a step that is easy to overlook.

**Predict.** Take cortical progenitors from a mouse early in neurogenesis and give them CNTF. They have the receptor, and STAT3 can be activated. What do you expect?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Few GFAP-positive astrocytes. The STAT3 binding site in the *GFAP* promoter is methylated in these early cells: a methyl group sits on a CpG (a C followed by a G in the DNA) within the site and keeps STAT3 from binding there. STAT3 is activated, but the *GFAP* gene does not answer. As development goes on, the site is demethylated, other chromatin changes follow, and the cell becomes able to answer the same cytokine. Gliogenesis is timed partly inside the cell, not only by when the signal shows up.

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/origins-of-glia/gfap-competence.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two progenitor cells receiving the same cytokine. In the early progenitor, methyl groups sit on the binding site in the GFAP gene and the activated STAT3 cannot bind. In the later progenitor the site is free, STAT3 binds, and the cell becomes an astrocyte." caption="The signal arrives in both cells, but only the later progenitor can answer it: the STAT3 binding site in the GFAP promoter is methylated early and demethylated later, so gliogenesis is timed partly inside the cell." />

What unlocks the site? In chick and mouse spinal cord, the transcription factor NFIA comes on in progenitors just as gliogenesis begins and is needed for it to start on time. In mouse cortical precursors, cells that have just committed to becoming neurons carry Notch ligands. Notch signaling in neighbouring progenitors has been shown to induce NFIA, which helps demethylate the *GFAP* promoter so that cytokine-activated STAT3 can read it. So Notch, which earlier held progenitors back from becoming neurons, has a second job late in development: it helps confer competence.

Where does the cytokine come from? One proposal, from work in mouse cortex, is the neurons themselves: newborn neurons make cardiotrophin-1, and mice lacking it have been reported to make fewer cortical astrocytes. Put together, the proposed feedback is this: new neurons supply both the Notch ligands that unlock the *GFAP* site and the cytokine that the unlocked progenitors read. So the more neurons a region has made, the stronger its push toward glia, and glial onset is timed by the work already done. A fourth signal, [BMP](#bmp-at-astrocyte-genes), works alongside the cytokines at astrocyte genes.

## Oligodendrocytes start in a ventral strip

Oligodendrocyte precursors are born from specific domains, and in the spinal cord the main one is ventral. Recall from [the Shh and BMP lesson](/lessons/shh-bmp-neural-tube/) that Sonic hedgehog from the floor plate divides the ventral cord into progenitor domains. One of them, **pMN**, expresses the bHLH factor Olig2. In mice and chicks, pMN first makes motor neurons (see [the motor neuron lesson](/lessons/motor-neuron-code/)) and then switches to making OPCs.

Genetic fate mapping of Olig-expressing pMN cells finds both motor neurons and oligodendrocytes among their descendants. They share a birthplace, so a gene the whole pMN domain needs, such as Olig2, should be needed for both. Mice lacking both Olig1 and Olig2 have almost no spinal motor neurons and no spinal oligodendrocytes. The pMN cells are not simply lost, though. In these mutants they take other fates, first V2 interneurons and later astrocytes.

The ventral strip is not the only source. In the mouse spinal cord, a later, smaller wave of OPCs comes from dorsal domains. In the mouse forebrain, Cre lineage tracing by Kessaris and colleagues found three waves: from a ventral domain, then from the lateral and caudal ganglionic eminences (swellings in the wall of the ventral forebrain), then from the cortex itself after birth. Much of the first ventral wave is lost after birth and replaced by the later ones.

**Predict.** Kill one forebrain wave of OPCs as it forms. The rule says that wave's cells are lost first. What do you expect to find in the adult forebrain?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Normal numbers of OPCs. When Kessaris and colleagues killed any one wave, the other waves expanded and filled the gap. The rule predicted the first deficit correctly, but not the final count, because the animal compensated. Whether the waves are interchangeable in what they do as adults is still open.

</details>

Once born, OPCs migrate widely and divide in response to **PDGF-A**, acting on the receptor tyrosine kinase PDGFRα (see [receptor tyrosine kinases](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)). How they then wrap axons belongs to [the myelination lesson](/lessons/myelination/).

### One cell, two futures

In 1983 Martin Raff, Robert Miller and Mark Noble cultured cells from the optic nerve of seven-day-old rats. Cells carrying the surface marker A2B5 became oligodendrocytes in serum-free medium, carrying galactocerebroside (GC). In 10% fetal calf serum they became GFAP-positive cells with an unusual shape, called type-2 astrocytes to set them apart from the more common type-1 astrocytes of brain cultures. They named the progenitor O-2A. A clear counterpart of the type-2 astrocyte in the living animal has been hard to find, so treat it as a fate seen in a dish.

### Instruct or select? A design problem

A fate that follows the medium can mean two things. Serum might instruct each cell, or it might only select which already-decided cells survive. How would you tell them apart? The logic is the same as the three controls in the design problem of [the neurotrophic hypothesis lesson](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/): count across the window, mark division, and check identity.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Follow single cells or clones, not proportions of a mixed culture: plate cells at a density where each can be followed, and count every cell at the start and the end in both media.

If serum selects, cells headed for the wrong fate die or stop dividing in each medium, so the cells left in each medium are a different subset. If serum instructs, the same starting cells survive in both media, and the fate of each clone follows the medium.

The stronger test is to move clones between media at different times. A clone moved from serum to serum-free medium on day two that ends as oligodendrocytes, with no loss of cells and no extra divisions to make up for losses, was redirected, not culled. Repeating the move on later days tells you when the decision becomes fixed. A reciprocal move, from serum-free to serum, tells you whether the window closes at the same time in both directions.

</details>

In 1984 Raff, Brenda Williams and Miller ran the timed version. They switched media at different times and counted the recovered cells, to argue that the shift was not explained by death. The choice could still be reversed after one to two days in serum, after which it became fixed (the MCAT question below works through illustrative numbers).

## Schwann cells come from the crest, in steps

In the peripheral nervous system, satellite glia and the enteric glia of the gut wall are crest derivatives. Schwann cells pass through stages that Kristján Jessen, Rhona Mirsky and colleagues defined in embryonic rat nerves: crest cell, then **Schwann cell precursor**, then immature Schwann cell, then myelinating or non-myelinating Schwann cell.

Each step has a different relationship to the axon. Schwann cell precursors die in culture unless neurons are present, and in a 1995 paper the group reported that **neuregulin**, a growth factor on axons read by ErbB2 and ErbB3, can keep them alive and drive their maturation into Schwann cells. Mouse genetics agreed: mice without ErbB3 lack Schwann cell precursors along their nerves.

This is the logic of [the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/) turned toward glia, though these experiments did not test whether the supply is limiting and competed for, as NGF is for sympathetic neurons.

When is ErbB2 needed? A **conditional knockout** deletes a gene only in cells that make Cre. In one such mouse, Cre came on early in the spinal roots, about embryonic day 11, but in the sciatic nerve only around birth, and ErbB2 was deleted with it.

**Predict.** If ErbB2 is needed early in the precursor pool and much less later, what should the Schwann cell counts look like a few days after birth, in the roots and in the sciatic nerve?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

At postnatal day 3.5, ventral-root Schwann cells fell from 51 to 5 per section, a loss of about 90%. Sciatic nerve counts barely changed, 588 in controls against 564, about 4% lower and not a significant change, though their myelin was thin.

That fits a need for ErbB2 early in the precursor pool, but the comparison is between two places as well as two times. Root Schwann cells come partly from boundary cap cells, which sit where the roots meet the cord, and the Cre line is active in them early. So the result does not fully separate when from where.

</details>

How glia later feed axons is in [the metabolic partners lesson](/lessons/glia-metabolic-partners/).

Every row asks the same two questions: where was the cell born, and what did its progenitor need then? Where the answer falls short, common causes are compensation, a change of fate, secondary effects of losing neighbouring cells or axons, a gene needed by both lineages, and a design that cannot separate timing from place. These rows do not test whether job or birthplace predicts better.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which cells.** The glia and birthplaces listed at the start, in the mouse, with the chick for the spinal cord. The yolk-sac origin of microglia is a mouse result. In zebrafish, adult microglia come mainly from later, definitive blood-forming sites rather than from the earliest wave, so the birthplace has to be shown in each species.
- **Which manipulation.** Loss of a progenitor, or of a signal it reads, during the window when that lineage is being made. Later losses behave differently. Removing ErbB2 near birth barely changes Schwann cell number. Microglia lost in adults are rebuilt mostly by surviving microglia; blood-derived cells engraft only after irradiation, injury or particular depletion conditions, and whether they become true microglia is open.
- **What it predicts.** The first deficit, together with everything else the same progenitor makes, for a gene that progenitor needs at that stage. It does not predict the final count after the animal compensates, or whether a progenitor dies or switches fate.

## Going deeper

### How glia were first told apart

Cajal's stains showed astrocytes well but left many small nuclei with no visible branches, which he grouped as a "third element". Pío del Río-Hortega, using a silver carbonate stain he kept refining, split that group in two: microglia, described in 1919, and oligodendroglia, the "glia with few processes", named in 1921. He saw many oligodendroglia lying between bundles of nerve fibres and, in a paper of 1922, proposed that they were the central counterpart of Schwann cells.

### Instruction or selection: the 1984 medium-switch and later work

The serum signal itself was not identified in 1984; later work showed that bone morphogenetic proteins in serum can push these cells toward the type-2 astrocyte fate, as can CNTF together with extracellular matrix. Separately, in 1988, Raff's group and Noble's group showed that PDGF made by astrocytes keeps O-2A progenitors dividing in culture.

### BMP at astrocyte genes

In fetal neural progenitors, BMP's effector Smad1 and STAT3 are bridged by the coactivator p300, and together they induce astrocytes.

### Does Hes push progenitors toward glia?

Hes1 maintains neural stem cells: forced Hes1 expression in mice keeps stem cells going and prolongs the making of later-born neurons. Whether Hes itself pushes cortical progenitors toward glia is less clear. In mouse telencephalon, forced Hes1 and Hes5 kept cells as stem cells, and those cells later made both neurons and astrocytes.

### How sure is the microglia fate map?

Later work resolved the yolk-sac source as c-kit-positive erythromyeloid precursors, which depend on the transcription factors PU.1 and Irf8. That refinement carries a caveat for the arithmetic above. The one-third labeling was measured on yolk-sac macrophages, not on these precursors. Take the adult count as 30 percent and blood as 2 percent. If the precursors had been labeled at 40 percent while the macrophages were at 30, the yolk-sac share would be (30 − 2) ÷ (40 − 2), about 74 percent, or three-quarters: still most, but further from all. This is an illustration of sensitivity, not a measured contribution.

### A job that hides a birthplace

In zebrafish, motor exit point glia are glia born in the central nervous system that leave it and ensheathe peripheral motor roots. They do a peripheral job with a central birthplace.

### What else Schwann cell precursors make

Schwann cell precursors are not committed only to Schwann cells. They also give rise to melanocytes, endoneurial fibroblasts and parasympathetic neurons, so a mutation that removes the precursors removes more than glia.
