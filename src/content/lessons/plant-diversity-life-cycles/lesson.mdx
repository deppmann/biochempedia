    story: >-
      Hofmeister had shown that every land plant alternates two generations. Bower, a botanist at
      the University of Glasgow, asked where the second one came from. His starting point was the
      green algae closest to land plants, in which the zygote is often the only diploid cell and
      undergoes meiosis when it germinates, often after a dormant period. Suppose, he reasoned, the zygote of an early land
      plant divided by mitosis for a while before meiosis. The result would be a small diploid body
      wedged between two haploid generations, a new stage inserted into the cycle rather than a
      modified copy of the old one. In his 1908 book he laid out this interpolation idea against
      evidence from living mosses, clubmosses and ferns, treating the capsule of a moss as a
      sporophyte still mostly devoted to spores. Rival botanists held that the two generations
      were versions of one body. The interpolation (antithetic) view is widely favoured today, partly because the algae closest to land plants have a single-celled diploid stage. The argument also framed a question that genetic work in mosses now
      tests directly: what tells a dividing cell which of the two bodies to build?

techniques:
  - name: "Comparative light microscopy of development"
    blurb: >-
      Follow the same stages (spore, gametophyte, egg, embryo) in many groups under the microscope
      and compare the sequences. Hofmeister used it to find one life cycle across land plants;
      today cleared ovules viewed with differential interference contrast optics show the nuclei
      of an embryo sac.
  - name: "Gene deletion with chemical rescue"
    blurb: >-
      Delete a gene, measure what changes, then supply a compound made downstream of the missing
      enzyme. Partial rescue ties the defect to the pathway rather than to an unrelated side
      effect of the deletion.
  - name: "Toluidine blue permeability and electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      A brief dip in a dilute dye shows where a surface barrier lets the dye in, and transmission
      electron microscopy shows the cuticle's layers. Dye uptake indicates a leaky barrier; it is
      not a measurement of water loss.
  - name: "Phylogenomics"
    blurb: >-
      Build trees from hundreds or thousands of genes across many species and combine them into a
      species tree. It tests which groups share a common ancestor, which is how mosses, liverworts and hornworts
      came to be drawn as one branch rather than a series of steps.

**The green cushion of moss on a shady wall is made of cells with one set of chromosomes each, the same count as a human egg, yet it photosynthesizes, grows and lives for years.** You have no body like that: your only one-set cells are made while eggs and sperm form. So where is the diploid moss?

The first film found it in the stalk and capsule. This page adds what the films leave out.

A cell with one **chromosome set** (one copy of each chromosome) is **haploid (n)**; with two sets it is **diploid (2n)**.

> **The one idea.** Chromosome sets change at only two kinds of event: **meiosis**, the division that halves them, and **fusion**, where two nuclei join and their sets add. **Mitosis**, ordinary cell division, copies a cell and never changes the count. So find meiosis and every fusion first, and each stage's label follows, whichever body happens to be the big one: n or 2n for the two bodies, and for endosperm the sum of the sets that fused.
>
> *Scope:* normal sexual life cycles of land plants, with labels counted against the species' own n. The rule gives each stage its label; it does not decide which body grows (see the KNOX2 section). Exceptions are in Going deeper.

## Two bodies in one life cycle

The first film's wheel is **alternation of generations**: a diploid **sporophyte** ("spore plant") makes haploid **spores** by meiosis, and a haploid **gametophyte** ("gamete plant") makes eggs and sperm by mitosis. Wilhelm Hofmeister found this scheme by microscope in 1851 (Going deeper); each division's mechanics are in [meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/) and [mitosis](/lessons/mitosis/).

In land plants both bodies are multicellular. Many fungi share the film's algal pattern: the zygote, the only diploid cell, undergoes meiosis soon after it forms (see [microbes, protists and fungi](/lessons/microbes-protists-fungi/)).

## Moss and fern: exposed gametophytes, different dominant bodies

**Moss.** Take *Physcomitrium patens* (long called *Physcomitrella patens*) or the haircap moss *Polytrichum*. The sporophyte (2n) stays attached to the leafy gametophyte (n) and depends largely on it for nutrients. A cap called the **calyptra** covers the young capsule.

**Predict.** The calyptra sits on the diploid capsule, but it grows from the leafy gametophyte's cells. Is it n or 2n?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

n. It is gametophyte tissue, made by mitosis from a haploid spore's descendants with no fusion on the way. Sitting on a 2n body cannot change its label.

</details>

**Fern.** The film's fern makes one kind of spore, so it is **homosporous**, as is *Ceratopteris richardii*. Its prothallus lives on its own and photosynthesizes. The fern has what the moss lacks: **xylem**, which carries water and dissolved mineral nutrients up from the roots, and **phloem**, which carries sugars. Ferns are **vascular** even though they have no seeds.

Vascular tissue did not free the fern from water: its sperm, like the moss's, still swim to the egg. The second film takes that problem to a pine.

## Seed plants: the gametophyte moves inside

Beyond the second film: plants that kept swimming sperm, and the counts in the sac.

**Pine.** The second film's pine is **heterosporous**: it makes two kinds of spores, and its **ovule** becomes the seed. Yet in cycads and *Ginkgo* the pollen tube still releases large swimming sperm into fluid inside the ovule.

**Flowering plant.** The second film's two sperm fusions are **double fertilization**, one sperm fertilizing the egg while the other fuses with the central cell (more in [plant reproduction](/lessons/plant-reproduction/)). In *Arabidopsis thaliana* the **embryo sac** comes from a **megaspore mother cell**, the cell that undergoes meiosis.

**Predict.** Some flowering plants build their embryo sacs differently. If a central cell held a single polar nucleus and one sperm fused with it, what would the endosperm be?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

2n: one set from the polar nucleus, one from the sperm. The rule holds; only the number of fusing nuclei changed.

**Worked problem.** Suppose an invented plant with embryo sacs exactly as in Arabidopsis has 2n = 24 (real Arabidopsis has 2n = 10). One ovule's megaspore mother cell completes meiosis, and development is normal, with no losses beyond the three of four megaspores that degenerate.

<details>

</details>

1. *Megaspores.* 4 megaspores come from meiosis; 3 degenerate, leaving 1 functional megaspore.
2. *Nuclei.* Three nuclear mitoses, in which the nucleus divides but the cell does not, double the count three times: 2 x 2 x 2 = 8 nuclei in the sac.
3. *Cells.* 6 single cells + 1 central cell = 7.
4. *Chromosomes.* Egg: n = 24 / 2 = 12. Zygote: 12 + 12 = 24. Central cell after polar fusion: 12 + 12 = 24. Primary endosperm nucleus: 24 + 12 = 36, which is 3n.

## Problems of land and the innovations that eased them

Land plants arose from green algae (see [history of life](/lessons/history-of-life/)). Water keeps an alga wet and holds it up, and in algae with swimming sperm it carries them; air does none of these. Lineages with the traits below left descendants on dry ground; no plant evolved them on purpose.

| Problem on land | Trait that eased it | Seen in |
|---|---|---|
| Drying out | Cuticle, a waxy layer on the outer surface | moss, fern, pine, flowering plant |
| Gas exchange through a sealed surface | Stomata, pores bordered by guard cells | fern, pine, flowering plant (moss capsules have stomata too, but their role there is debated) |
| Transport and upright support | Lignified xylem with phloem | fern, pine, flowering plant |
| Fertilization without a water film | Pollen and the pollen tube | pine, flowering plant |
| Protecting and feeding the embryo | Seed | pine, flowering plant |
| Getting pollen and seeds moved | Flowers and fruit | flowering plants |

A **cuticle** trades one problem for another. It slows water loss (in *P. patens* a faulty cuticle lets dye in, a sign of a leaky barrier), but a seal that keeps water in also slows carbon dioxide entry, and photosynthesis fixes carbon dioxide drawn from the air (the chemistry is in [the Calvin cycle](/lessons/calvin-cycle/)). **Stomata** are adjustable pores that regulate exchange of gases and water vapour. In *P. patens* they occur on the capsule, not the leafy shoot, and wet-grown capsules typically have them plugged, so do not assume moss stomata work like a leaf's.

**Lignin**, a tough polymer in xylem walls, also stiffens the plant for upright growth. Phloem carries sugars from where they are made or stored (**sources**) to where they are used (**sinks**), which can be up or down. Mosses lack lignified vascular tissue and stay low; they need water to grow actively and for sperm to swim, though many survive long dry spells (details in [plant transport](/lessons/plant-transport/)).

**Pollen** lands at the ovule in a pine or on a flower's stigma. A **seed** wraps an embryo in a food store and a coat, and **dormancy**, a resting state, lets germination wait. **Flowers** promote pollination, often by animals, and the ovary wall can develop into a **fruit** that protects seeds and aids dispersal.

## Branches, not a staircase

Living mosses did not turn into ferns; each living group tips a branch that has evolved as long as every other. A trait such as stomata can be old or new on the tree, but a living moss is no older than a pine (see [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/)).

The tree itself has shifted. In 2019 the One Thousand Plant Transcriptomes Initiative, comparing gene sequences from more than 1,100 species, recovered living mosses, liverworts and hornworts as one branch in its nuclear gene trees. All its analyses rejected the hypothesis that liverworts alone are the **sister group** (closest relative) of a branch holding all other living land plants. A moss is a cousin of vascular plants, not a picture of their ancestor.

### Design problem

In 2017 Renault and colleagues deleted the gene PpCYP98, whose relatives in flowering plants act in lignin synthesis, in the moss *Physcomitrium patens*. Mosses are considered not to make lignin. Design the test of whether the gene is required for a normal cuticle. Say what you would compare and measure, what result would support a role, and what the test cannot tell you.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Compare wild type with several independent deletion lines, so that a stray second mutation in one line cannot explain the result; the paper used three. Measure surface permeability (dip the leafy shoots, called gametophores, in dilute toluidine blue and score dye uptake; the paper used 0.05 percent dye for two minutes), cuticle structure by transmission electron microscopy, and whether leafy gametophores form normally.

Then rescue. Grow mutants with caffeic acid, a compound made downstream of the missing enzyme (20 micromolar in the paper). If the pathway matters, mutants should take up more dye, show a disrupted cuticle and grow poorly, and supplementation should restore growth and the barrier at least partly. Renault and colleagues reported partial rescue.

Limits: dye uptake shows a leaky barrier but does not measure water loss, so add a weighed drying test for that claim. And a living moss's cuticle is not the first land plants'; the result shows what this pathway does in this moss.

## Going deeper

### How Hofmeister compared the groups

In the 1840s some botanists, Matthias Schleiden among them, held that a seed plant's embryo formed from the tip of the pollen tube. Wilhelm Hofmeister followed germinating spores, the plants they grew into, their flask-shaped female organs (archegonia) and the youngest embryos in mosses, liverworts, ferns, horsetails, clubmosses and conifers. If spore and seed plants shared no plan, the sequences should differ. Instead, in every group, the conifer seed included, the embryo arose from an egg inside the female organ, and a spore-bearing generation alternated with a gamete-bearing one. His 1851 comparison ruled in one alternation of generations across land plants; his 1847 flowering-plant work traced the embryo to an egg in the embryo sac, ruling out the pollen-tube origin. He knew nothing of chromosome numbers; tying the generations to n and 2n came decades later.

### Where the labels need care

The one idea covers normal sexual cycles. It does not apply to apomixis, in which seeds form without the usual meiosis and fertilization, or to ferns with apospory or apogamy, in which one body grows from the other without meiosis or without fertilization. Many plant cells also copy their DNA without dividing (endoreduplication), so a nucleus can hold more DNA than its label suggests; the label names the stage of the life cycle, not the DNA content of every nucleus.

### The worked problem with five ovules

Take the same plant, 2n = 24, with five ovules that each have one megaspore mother cell, all completing meiosis. Megaspores: 5 x 4 = 20. Survivors: 5 x 3 = 15 degenerate, so 20 - 15 = 5 functional megaspores, one per embryo sac. Nuclei: 5 x 8 = 40. Cells: 5 x 7 = 35. Check: 15 + 5 = 20, and 40 nuclei exceed 35 cells by 5, one extra nucleus per central cell.
