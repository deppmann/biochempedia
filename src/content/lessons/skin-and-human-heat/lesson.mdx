techniques:
  - name: "Electron-dense tracers and freeze-fracture electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Introduce water-soluble tracers that show up in the electron microscope, such as horseradish
      peroxidase or lanthanum, and see how far they travel between cells; fracture frozen tissue
      along its membranes to view junctions and lipid. Together they map where a barrier lies.
  - name: "18F-FDG PET-CT with controlled cold exposure"
    blurb: >-
      Inject a radioactive glucose analogue and image where it accumulates, with CT for anatomy,
      once at a thermoneutral temperature and once after mild cold. Uptake that appears only in the
      cold marks active brown fat; it reports glucose uptake, not heat output.
  - name: "Environmental chamber step protocols"
    blurb: >-
      Hold a volunteer at a low, fixed metabolic rate while air temperature or humidity rises in
      steps, and record core temperature continuously. The point where core temperature stops
      holding steady and climbs estimates the limit of compensable heat stress for that protocol; longer exposures can still fail below it.
  - name: "In vitro photoconversion in excised skin"
    blurb: >-
      Expose samples of human skin, or a radiolabelled precursor, to sunlight or to narrow bands of
      ultraviolet light, then measure how much 7-dehydrocholesterol became previtamin D3. It isolates
      the photochemistry from diet, clothing and blood levels.

    rationale: >-

**A gram of sweat that evaporates from your skin carries off about 2.4 kJ of heat. A gram that drips off carries away almost none.**

Sweat cools you only as fast as the air takes up water vapour, and on a humid day that can lag behind the heat your muscles make. The heat that cannot leave is stored.

**Predict.** Two cyclists ride at the same workload in equally hot air, one dry, one humid. Both are dripping sweat. Whose core temperature keeps rising? Would measuring their sweat rates tell you?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The cyclist in humid air. Humid air already holds a lot of water vapour, so it takes up less from the skin, and less heat leaves by evaporation. Sweat rate would not tell you: dripping shows only that some sweat is not evaporating. In dry air, evaporation can still meet the need; in humid air, it cannot.

</details>

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Body temperature holds steady only while heat lost equals heat made, so that heat storage is zero. The body has two levers on the loss side. **Skin blood flow** decides how much heat from the core reaches the skin. **Evaporation of sweat** decides how much leaves when dry loss to the air is not enough, as in hot air or hard exercise; once the air is as warm as the skin, it is the only exit. Evaporation has a ceiling, E_max, set by the gap in water-vapour pressure between wet skin and the air (and by air movement and clothing), not by how much you sweat. When the heat that must go exceeds that ceiling, the extra sweat drips and the difference is stored.

The rule covers a whole person in air, at rest or exercising. The skin's other jobs, its water barrier and vitamin D, come at the end.

The four routes of heat exchange, thermal conductance and the comparative thermoneutral zone are in [metabolic rate and body temperature](/lessons/thermoregulation/). Here they get human numbers.

A quick map of the skin first, since both levers sit in it. The outer **epidermis** has no blood vessels. The **dermis** beneath it holds the vessels, nerves and sweat glands. The **hypodermis** below that is mostly insulating fat.

## The heat budget

Picture the body's heat as a ledger. Coming in is **heat production**: the metabolic rate minus any energy that leaves as external work, such as pushing the pedals. **Dry exchange**, by radiation, convection and conduction, runs either way: a loss when the surroundings are cooler than the skin, a gain when they are warmer. **Evaporation** almost always runs out. What is left over is **storage**.

## Lever 1: skin blood flow, a variable insulator

Blood carries heat from the core to the skin, so the skin circulation works as an insulator whose thickness the body can change. With little flow, heat must creep out through tissue, as if through a thick coat; with full flow, blood carries it straight to the surface, and the coat is thin. In a model of an average man, opening the skin vessels fully passes about four times as much heat for the same core-to-skin difference: with a 2 °C difference, about 16 W per m² of skin with vessels constricted and 65 W per m² with them open.

Two nerve systems set the dermal vessels:

- **Vasoconstriction** in the cold: sympathetic nerves release noradrenaline onto skin arterioles.
- **Active vasodilation** in heat: sympathetic cholinergic nerves (sympathetic in anatomy, but releasing acetylcholine, not noradrenaline) actively dilate skin vessels. Silent at rest, this system switches on as body temperature rises and supplies 80 to 90 percent of the vasodilation in hairy skin in whole-body heating; withdrawal of constrictor tone supplies the rest. Its mediator is an open question: acetylcholine, nitric oxide and cotransmitters each contribute, and no single one has been identified.

The hairless (glabrous) skin of palms and soles has no active vasodilator system; warming dilates its vessels mainly by withdrawing constrictor tone, much of it in **arteriovenous anastomoses**, direct links from small arteries to veins.

In severe whole-body heating, total skin blood flow can reach 6 to 8 L per min, a load on [cardiac output and venous return](/lessons/cardiac-output-venous-return/).

The **thermoneutral zone** is the range of air temperatures where this lever alone is enough, with no regulatory change in heat production or evaporation. Below it, heat production must rise; above it, regulatory sweating begins. For resting nude people in air, the lower limit is about 28.5 °C.

**Predict.** A resting, nude volunteer sits in a chamber whose air cools by a couple of degrees but stays inside the thermoneutral zone. What changes: skin blood flow, heat production, or sweating?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only skin blood flow, which falls. Noradrenaline constricts the skin arterioles, insulation rises, and less heat reaches the skin. Heat production stays at its resting level, since shivering starts only below the lower limit, and there was no sweating to turn off.

</details>

TRP-channel sensing is in [thermosensation](/lessons/thermosensation/), the preoptic integrator in [the hypothalamus and autonomic control](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/). The regulated temperature is a range, not one number (see the anecdote "Thirty-seven degrees came from the armpit").

## Lever 2: sweat, and its ceiling

Thermal sweat comes from a few million **eccrine** glands. Sweating responds mainly to core temperature, with a skin contribution, through the preoptic area. As in active vasodilation, the nerves are sympathetic but the transmitter is acetylcholine: postganglionic fibres release it onto **muscarinic** receptors of the gland. Apocrine glands are not thermoregulatory.

The secretory coil makes a primary fluid close to plasma in osmolality, and the duct reabsorbs sodium and chloride (chloride through CFTR, see [membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/)) while staying relatively impermeable to water. Sweat leaves **hypotonic**, and saltier at high flow, when the duct has less time per millilitre. Heavy sweating therefore loses more water than salt: plasma volume falls and osmolality rises, driving ADH release and thirst ([water, sodium and potassium](/lessons/water-sodium-potassium/); [body fluid compartments](/lessons/body-fluid-compartments/)).

### Why humid air sets a ceiling

Sweat cools only when it turns to vapour, and each gram that does carries off about 2.43 kJ of heat at skin temperature, its **latent heat** of evaporation, or 580 kcal per kg (2.26 kJ per g is the value at 100 °C).

What drives water off the skin as vapour? A difference in **water-vapour pressure**, the part of the air's pressure due to water vapour. A film of sweat on skin at 35 °C saturates the air touching it, at about 5.6 kPa. Air at 35 °C and 40 percent relative humidity holds about 2.2 kPa, so vapour moves from skin to air down a gap of about 3.4 kPa. At 80 percent humidity the air holds about 4.5 kPa, and the gap shrinks to about 1.1 kPa, a third as large.

The most heat that evaporation can remove is called **E_max**. It scales with that gap and with how fast moving air carries the vapour away; clothing lowers it. Notice what is missing from that sentence: your sweat glands. Sweat rate sets how much water is offered. The gap sets how much can leave.

**Predict.** A cyclist must lose 850 W by evaporation, and the humid air allows at most 700 W. She sweats 1.3 L per hour. Would sweating 2.5 L per hour help?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only up to a point. At 1.3 L per hour her sweat could carry off about 880 W if every gram evaporated, more than the 700 W the air allows. If her skin is already fully wet, the rest drips and sweating harder adds dripping, not cooling. If some of her sweat is already dripping, a little more sweat could raise evaporation, but never above 700 W. Either way at least 150 W is stored.

</details>

In a real person the shortfall between E_req and E_max closes from both sides. As skin warms, the vapour pressure of its sweat rises (about 6.3 kPa at 37 °C), raising E_max, and dry heat gain shrinks, lowering E_req, so heating often slows toward a new, higher steady state. Heat stress becomes uncompensable when no such state exists, which is what the chamber studies below look for.

## When the ceiling is too low

Once E_max falls below E_req, core and skin temperatures rise. Both levers now load the circulation: skin blood flow and heart rate rise, and sweat keeps flowing.

**Predict.** Trained cyclists ride for an hour at a fixed workload in hot, humid air, without drinking, dripping sweat as core temperature climbs. By the end, which way have heart rate, stroke volume and plasma osmolality moved?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Heart rate up, stroke volume down, osmolality up. Sweat loss shrinks plasma volume and the faster heart shortens filling time, so cardiac filling and stroke volume fall. Sweat is hypotonic, so losing it removes more water than salt and osmolality rises. How much pooling of blood in skin veins adds during exercise is debated.

</details>

When dehydration adds to hyperthermia, the skin circulation joins the systemic vasoconstriction, cutting heat delivery to the skin just when it is needed. In 7 trained cyclists exercising in 35 °C heat, cutaneous vascular resistance rose despite a higher oesophageal temperature (González-Alonso and colleagues, 1995; later data in [exercise physiology](/lessons/exercise-physiology/)).

If storage goes on, heat illness can follow, and brain function separates two of its named forms:

- **Heat exhaustion** is heat strain with weakness, faintness or a fast heart rate, without marked central nervous system dysfunction.
- **Heat stroke** is severe heat illness defined by central nervous system dysfunction, such as confusion, seizures or coma, often with injury to other organs.

Sweating often persists early in exertional heat stroke, so sweating does not mean heat balance.

**Acclimation** is the adaptation. With repeated heat exposure, sweating starts earlier and rises more steeply with core temperature, and aldosterone lowers the sodium in sweat.

### How humid is too humid?

The **wet-bulb temperature**, read from a wetted thermometer cooled by evaporation, combines air temperature and humidity. A wet-bulb of 35 °C was long cited as the theoretical limit, since fully wet skin near 35 °C could then lose no net heat to the air. A living person also makes heat, even at rest, and must lose it, so the real limit should lie lower.

Vecellio and colleagues (2022), in Kenney's laboratory, tested young healthy adults at low metabolic rates. Picture their record: core temperature against time as the chamber's heat stress rises in steps. At first the trace stays nearly flat: at each step the body keeps pace, or close to it. Then, at one step, it bends upward and keeps climbing. That bend, the **inflection**, marks the critical wet-bulb for that person. No one reached 35 °C: in humid air, the mean inflection came at a wet-bulb of about 30.5 °C, and in hot, dry air dry heat gain outpaced evaporation. These were indoor chamber tests, and longer exposures can still fail below the inflection (Going deeper).

## In the cold: constrict, shiver, burn brown fat

In the cold, evaporation is near its floor and the levers change. Vasoconstriction raises insulation and limits loss. Heat production rises, mainly from **shivering** muscle in adults ([skeletal muscle mechanics](/lessons/skeletal-muscle-mechanics/)); if core temperature keeps falling, shivering wanes below about 31 °C. Small **arrector pili** muscles raise hairs, but in sparsely haired humans the goosebumps trap little air; the response is usually called vestigial.

**Brown adipose tissue** makes heat without contraction: the protein UCP1 lets protons leak back across the inner mitochondrial membrane without making ATP, so the gradient's energy becomes heat ([the proton-motive force](/lessons/proton-motive-force/); [thyroid hormone](/lessons/thyroid-hormone/)).

Brown fat was thought to regress after infancy. In 2009, a study that scanned 24 healthy men by PET-CT after mild cold found active brown fat in 23 of them, mainly in depots above the collarbone and in the neck. A review of clinical scans taken without controlled cold found it in about 3 to 8 percent, more often in cold weather. Detection needs activation. The scans traced a radioactive glucose analogue, FDG, so they report glucose uptake, not heat.

## The skin that does all this

The **epidermis** is a stratified epithelium, thinnest on the eyelids, about 0.05 mm, and thickest on the palms and soles, around half a millimetre or more. It lacks lymph vessels too and is fed by diffusion from below. The **dermis** is connective tissue; its deeper, **reticular** part holds hair roots and glands. The **hypodermis**, mostly white fat, anchors the dermis to muscle and bone.

The epidermis is a conveyor belt. Keratinocytes divide in the **stratum basale** and move outward through the **spinosum**, the **granulosum**, a clear **lucidum** found only in the thick skin of palms and soles, and the **corneum**. On the way they fill with keratin and die, ending as **corneocytes**: flat cells with no nucleus, packed with keratin. Repeated friction thickens the stratum corneum into a **callus**; nails and hair are hard, keratinized products of specialized epidermis.

**Melanocytes** sit in the basal layer and make melanin, which absorbs ultraviolet light. Skin colour differs with the amount and type of melanin and the size, distribution and fate of melanosomes, not with melanocyte number, which is similar between people. **Langerhans cells**, antigen-presenting cells most prominent in the spinosum, carry antigen to lymph nodes ([lymphoid organs and immune traffic](/lessons/lymphoid-organs-immune-traffic/)).

### The water barrier is lipid between dead cells

The barrier keeps most water in, so the body can spend water on purpose: a small, steady leak crosses the skin all the time, and large losses go through sweat ducts when the brain calls for them. Where is the seal? Gut and kidney epithelia seal the spaces between cells with tight junctions ([epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/)), so the natural guess was that skin does too.

In 1975 Peter Elias and Daniel Friend tested this in the epidermis of neonatal mice. They followed water-soluble tracers that are dense under the electron microscope between cells, and examined freeze-fracture replicas, casts of membranes split open while frozen, from the upper layers. If tight junctions between living cells were the seal, the tracers should stop there. Instead they moved between cells up to the upper stratum granulosum and stopped where **lamellar bodies**, lipid-filled granules, had emptied their contents into the space between cells. The authors placed the main barrier in that extruded lipid, not in tight junctions.

Later work resolved the lipid as patterned **lamellae** between corneocytes, often pictured as bricks in mortar. Then tight junctions returned: Furuse and colleagues (2002) found that mice lacking claudin-1 died within a day of birth with excess water loss through the skin, so tight junctions in the granular layer also contribute, and the knockout shows what an uncontrolled leak costs.

## Sunlight makes vitamin D in the living epidermis

UVB at 290 to 315 nm photolyses **7-dehydrocholesterol** to **previtamin D3**, and a temperature-dependent isomerization, not light, converts that to **vitamin D3**. Most synthesis is in the living basale and spinosum, beneath the dead corneum. MacLaughlin, Anderson and Holick (1982) put the optimum at 295 to 300 nm.

**Predict.** Webb, Kline and Holick (1988) exposed excised human skin, and radiolabelled 7-dehydrocholesterol, to sunlight on cloudless days, month by month. In Boston, at 42.2 °N, would January sunlight make any previtamin D3?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. In Boston no previtamin D3 formed from November through February, and in Edmonton (52 °N) none from October through March, while at 34 and 18 °N it formed even in midwinter. Low winter sun sends its light through more atmosphere, where ozone absorbs UVB.

</details>

This is photochemistry in vitro, not blood levels in people: it shows when sunlight can start vitamin D synthesis. Melanin also absorbs UVB, so more pigmented skin makes less previtamin D3 from the same exposure and needs a longer exposure to make the same amount. Sunlight has not been reported to cause vitamin D toxicity, because further UVB converts excess previtamin D3 to lumisterol and tachysterol, which do not act as vitamin D3. Activation in liver and kidney is in [calcium, phosphate and bone](/lessons/calcium-phosphate-bone/).

### Design problem

Do we lose most of our body heat through the head? Design an experiment in healthy volunteers in cold water that measures heat loss from the head and the rest of the body: null prediction, comparisons, measurements.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Null prediction: each region loses heat roughly in proportion to its surface area. The head is about 7 percent of the surface, so submerging it should add roughly that, not the myth's 40 to 50 percent.

Use a 2 x 2 design: head in or out of the water, body insulated or not. Suppress shivering with a drug, so changing heat production cannot mask loss. Measure heat loss and the core cooling rate until both are steady.

Pretorius, Bristow, Steinman and Giesbrecht (2006) did this with 8 men in 17 °C water. With the body uninsulated, submerging the head added only about 10 percent to total heat loss, so the head did not lose disproportionately more. Yet the core cooled about 42 percent faster, possibly through redistribution of blood flow.

Limits: 8 men, one water temperature, no shivering, water not air. With a clothed body and bare head, the head is the main uninsulated surface and can carry a larger share. A 1970 US Army survival manual gave 40 to 45 percent; the experiment behind the figure has not been identified.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Insulation in model units

Core-to-skin heat flow per unit area is roughly (core − skin temperature) ÷ body insulation. In the model of an average man, insulation falls from about 0.124 to 0.031 m² °C per W as skin blood flow goes from minimal to maximal, which gives the 16 and 65 W per m² above.

### Who set the thermoneutral zone's lower limit

Hardy and DuBois reported the 28.5 °C lower critical temperature, with skin at 33.5 °C. Kingma and colleagues (2014), reporting that work, argued that skin temperature belongs in the definition of the human thermoneutral zone.

### Sweat gland numbers

Humans have about 2 to 4 million eccrine sweat glands, densest on the palms and soles at about 250 to 550 per cm². No new glands form after birth, so their density falls as the body grows. An older figure, that the total number is fixed by about age 2 to 3, is still repeated in reviews; it appears to come from Kuno's work on how many glands become active, not on how many exist.

### The specific heat in the worked example

The 2.98 kJ per kg per °C used in the example is a tissue-based estimate. With the older, assumed 3.47, the example's heat capacity is 242.9 kJ per °C and temperature rises 540 ÷ 242.9 = about 2.2 °C per hour, against 2.6 with 2.98: the newer value predicts heating about 16 percent faster. The anecdote "The specific heat nobody measured" tells where both values come from.

### Below the inflection

Vecellio and colleagues reported a mean critical wet-bulb of 30.55 °C in humid air of 36 to 40 °C, and sweat rates did not differ between environments. Meade and colleagues (2025) then held 12 young volunteers for up to 9 hours above and below each person's inflection. Above it, core temperature kept rising; below it, the rise was much slower but still did not level off.

### The 2009 brown fat scans

Wouter van Marken Lichtenbelt and colleagues' 24 men were 10 lean and 14 overweight or obese, with indirect calorimetry at 22 and 16 °C. Activity was lower in the overweight and obese men, and three men with high uptake, rescanned at 22 °C, showed none. In the same issue, Cypess and colleagues reviewed clinical scans retrospectively and found brown fat in 7.5 percent of women and 3.1 percent of men; those scans used no controlled cold exposure, and detection was more likely when outdoor temperature was lower.

The study does not show how much heat brown fat adds (brown fat burns mostly fatty acids, not the glucose FDG traces), how women's brown fat responds to cold (all imaged subjects were men), or whether low activity causes obesity or follows it.

### Normal body temperature

In the anecdote "Thirty-seven degrees came from the armpit", Wunderlich's 37.0 °C came from axillary readings and Mackowiak's 36.8 °C from oral ones. Armpit readings usually run lower than oral ones, so the site does not explain why the later mean is lower.

### Skin cells in more detail

Corneocytes are packed with keratins 1 and 10. Each melanocyte supplies about 30 to 40 keratinocytes. In mice, Langerhans cells are seeded before birth and renew locally (see the anecdote "Langerhans cells that outlast the marrow"). Elias and Friend's tracers included horseradish peroxidase and lanthanum.
