      balance between the immigration of new species and the extinction of resident ones, and
      expanded the idea into the 1967 book The Theory of Island Biogeography.
    profileSourceAnchor: "MacArthur & Wilson, An equilibrium theory of insular zoogeography, Evolution 17:373-387 (1963); MacArthur & Wilson, The Theory of Island Biogeography, Princeton University Press (1967)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      MacArthur came to ecology with training in mathematics, and his best-known early study, on
      warblers that forage in different parts of conifer trees in the northeastern United States
      (Ecology, 1958), asked how several similar species can live in the same forest. The island
      problem was a different kind of question. Biogeographers had long known that large islands
      hold more species than small ones and that remote islands hold fewer than islands near a
      mainland. MacArthur and Wilson asked what process could produce both patterns. In a paper in
      Evolution in 1963 they treated the species count on an island as the result of two rates:
      arrivals of species not already present, which slow as the island fills, and local
      extinctions, which speed up as more species are present. Where the two rates cross, richness
      stays roughly constant while species keep coming and going. They worked the argument out with
      bird faunas from the Sunda Islands to Polynesia, the recolonisation of Krakatau and other island groups, and expanded it into a book in 1967. The model made a
      quantitative, testable prediction about a community, which is what made an experiment on real
      islands worth doing.

      Island biogeography reached conservation through an analogy: a patch of habitat surrounded by
      farmland or plantation is something like an island, and a strip of habitat linking two
      patches, a corridor, might work like a bridge that raises immigration. The analogy is easy
      to state and hard to test, because corridors also add habitat area and change patch shape.

techniques:
  - name: "Experimental defaunation and repeated census"
    blurb: >-
      Every species on a small island is recorded, the fauna is removed (here by methyl bromide
      fumigation), and the island is censused again at intervals. Comparing species lists between
      censuses gives colonisations and local extinctions, though events that begin and end
      between visits are missed.
  - name: "Experimental area reduction"
    blurb: >-
      Parts of an island are removed and the remaining fauna is recensused. Because the same
      island is compared before and after, area changes while habitat type and location stay much
      the same, which a comparison across different islands cannot achieve.
  - name: "Replicated connectivity experiment"
    blurb: >-
      Equal habitat patches are created and assigned to connected or unconnected treatments, with
      controls for the area a corridor adds. Colonisation and extinction are estimated separately
      from repeated surveys of species identity over many years.
  - name: "Presence-absence comparison (Jaccard similarity)"
    blurb: >-
      Two species lists are compared by dividing the number of species they share by the number
      found in either. Equal richness can hide a low similarity, so the index separates how many
      species are present from which species they are.

    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      At equilibrium the immigration and extinction rates are equal, so in the model net change is

      confuses constant richness with a frozen species list. Saying richness is rising ignores the
      5 extinctions per day that balance the arrivals.

The first film showed why near, large islands hold the most species, but not how fast an emptied island refills, or whether the returning species are the ones that left.

> **The one idea.** An island's species count changes at the rate new species arrive minus the rate resident species are lost. Arrivals of new species slow as the island fills and losses speed up, so the count settles near where the two rates are equal, while the species themselves keep being replaced.
>
> *Scope:* one island fed by a mainland (or nearby islands), with new species forming on the island left out (Going deeper); this scope holds throughout. It is a model of rates: it predicts how many species and how fast they turn over, not which ones. Habitat fragments on land are only partly islands.

## Two rates and a crossing

A cistern with a float valve works the same way: as the water rises, the float throttles the inflow and the drain runs faster, so the level settles while the water keeps changing.

In symbols, the film's crossing S* is where dS/dt = 0. It is **stable** (nudge richness away and it returns), and the turnover is I(S*) = E(S*).

The count wobbles around S* because arrivals and losses are chance events. A smaller pool, which the film skipped, also lowers S*, since immigration reaches zero sooner.

## Working the numbers

**Worked problem.** The numbers are invented, not estimates for any real island. An island draws on a pool of P = 100 species. Immigration is I(S) = 10(1 - S/100) species per day and extinction is E(S) = 0.1S species per day: extinction is proportional to S, as when each resident has the same independent chance of loss. Assume continuous, deterministic change (no chance events), constant coefficients, no speciation, and an empty island at time zero.

1. *Equilibrium.* dS/dt = 10 - 0.1S - 0.1S = 10 - 0.2S. Setting this to zero gives **S* = 50 species**.
2. *Turnover.* At S = 50, I = 10 x 0.5 = 5 species per day and E = 0.1 x 50 = 5 species per day. Net change is zero, yet about **5 species are replaced each day** (10 events per day if you count gains and losses separately).
3. *Approach (calculus; optional).* The 0.2 per day in step 1 is a continuous rate constant (0.1 from slowing arrivals plus 0.1 from faster losses), so the gap to S* decays exponentially, shrinking about 18% each full day (e^(-0.2) = 0.82). Solving from S = 0 gives S(t) = 50(1 - e^(-0.2t)). Richness reaches 90% of S*, 45 species, when e^(-0.2t) = 0.1, so t = ln 10 / 0.2 = **11.5 days**. Check: at S = 45, I = 5.5 and E = 4.5 species per day, so richness is still rising by 1 species per day.

**Predict.** A smaller island has a lower S*. Does it also turn over more slowly, or faster?

The worked island, started empty, reaches about 47 species, 94% of S*, within two weeks, though the count says nothing about which species. The rare real islands that are refilling, such as Krakatau, cannot be replicated or controlled, so the second film's islands were emptied on purpose.

## Same number, different species

The second film's three E9 mites shared one third of their list, and it was not just those three: in the 1969 report, the species on each island were not all the ones present before treatment. A caution the film did not give: dividing the mites' changes by 364 days does not give a turnover rate, because other arrivals and losses between censuses are not counted. Is one third low for an island at equilibrium?

**Predict.** Make the worked island a chance process in which every species comes and goes independently of the others: each absent pool species arrives at a rate of 0.1 per day, and each resident is lost at a rate of 0.1 per day (continuous rates, as in step 3, not daily chances), every species alike. Census it at equilibrium, then 5, 10 and 100 days later. Does the shared fraction keep falling toward zero?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. With equal rates in and out, each species is present half the time, and because species act independently, S wobbles around 50 (standard deviation about 5); species that came and went together would make the count swing far more. The expected Jaccard similarity falls from 1 to about 0.52 after 5 days and 0.40 after 10, then levels off near 0.33. It stops at a third because, long after, each species' presence at the two censuses is like two independent coin flips: on both lists with chance 1/4, on neither with chance 1/4, so on at least one with chance 3/4, and 1/4 ÷ 3/4 = 1/3. With every species alike, a steady count sharing a third of its list is what this model expects; species present more or less than half the time give other values. The E9 three are hand-picked, so their one third is only a scale check.

</details>

## Fragments, area and connectivity on land

A forest remnant surrounded by farmland looks like an island, and the species-area pattern is often summarised as S = cA^z, where A is area and the fitted constants c and z set the overall level and how fast richness rises with area. The exponent z varies among taxa (groups of organisms) and sets of islands, so treat any single value as illustrative.

**Worked problem, continued.** Take z = 0.25.

4. *Habitat loss.* A habitat holding 50 species is cut from 100 to 10 km^2. Richness is multiplied by (10/100)^0.25 = 0.56, so it falls toward about 50 x 0.56 = 28 species. If the same curve holds for the remnant, this is the long-run value after **relaxation**, the slow loss of species that a smaller area can no longer hold, not a loss on the day of the cut. Treat it as a rough projection: some species survive in the surrounding land, and the method itself is debated (Going deeper).

Fragments are not true islands: the surrounding land, the **matrix**, is not open sea. Some species live in it, some cross it readily and some cannot cross it at all. That ties the model to the [**metapopulation**](/lessons/spatial-populations/) view: a set of local populations linked by dispersal, each subject to local extinction and recolonisation.

Rates can be a more sensitive signal than counts. In Swiss Jura grassland patches that Jasmin Joshi and colleagues isolated by mowing from 1993 to 1999, plant colonisation was 30% lower than in unisolated controls, yet after seven years the patches had only marginally fewer species, perhaps because counts lag behind rates, as in relaxation.

Connectivity has been tested over a longer span. In a longleaf pine savanna experiment reported by Ellen Damschen and colleagues (Science, 2019), patches joined by corridors had annual colonisation about 5% higher and annual extinction about 2% lower than unconnected patches across 239 plant species. After almost two decades, connected patches held nearly 14% more species and were still gaining; a five-year study would have seen much less of the effect. For reserve planning, see [conservation](/lessons/conservation/).

### Design problem

A planner must choose between one 100 ha reserve and four 25 ha reserves, or decide whether to link existing patches with corridors. Design a study that tests whether connection itself changes the species found in patches.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Island biogeography alone does not settle the reserve choice: one large reserve may hold more area-sensitive species, while several small ones may sample more habitat types and spread the risk of a local disaster. So test the mechanism.

1. *Replicate equal-area patches* in the same kind of habitat and matrix, and assign them at random to connected or unconnected treatments.
2. *Control for the corridor's own area.* A corridor adds habitat and edge. Give some unconnected patches dead-end side strips matching the corridor's area and edge-to-area ratio, and others the same extra area as a plain block, so a difference can be attributed to connection.
3. *Census species identity repeatedly*, at a fixed effort and season, for many years, recording which species are present as well as how many.
4. *Estimate colonisation and local extinction separately*, from changes in each species' presence between censuses, and say how many events between visits you could have missed.
5. *Report both richness and composition* (for example Jaccard similarity to a reference patch). Effects can keep growing for years, although in the same system the effect on bird-dispersed plants had levelled off.

The result applies to that habitat, matrix and set of species; whether it transfers to another matrix or to animals with other dispersal modes needs its own test.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Speciation and the book

MacArthur and Wilson also counted speciation on the island as a further source of species, which matters on large, remote islands, not small mangrove ones. Where it is appreciable, its rate adds to immigration, and the count settles where immigration plus speciation equals extinction. Their 1967 book, The Theory of Island Biogeography, developed the theory at length.

### The defaunation method

The islands were red mangrove, *Rhizophora mangle*, and each was tented and fumigated with methyl bromide; the full one-year report appeared in Ecology in 1969, and a second report in 1970 followed four islands to the end of the second year (the steady count the second film ends on). The 1969 appendix marks each record as seen, inferred present or inferred absent; the film's E9 mites were chosen for unambiguous records. Treatment had its own side effects: on island E7, fumigation killed 85% of the mangrove tree.

### How rough the measured rates were

Most observed immigration and extinction rates lay between 0.05 and 0.50 species per day, against about 0.1 to 1.0 from a simple noninteractive model (species do not change one another's chances). The authors suggested many events between censuses went unrecorded.

### Running a species-area curve backwards

Step 4 used a species-area curve in reverse, predicting losses from a shrinking area. Whether that overestimates extinctions after habitat loss is debated.
