techniques:
  - name: "Chronic fistula and sham feeding"
    blurb: >-
      A surgically made opening to the outside lets juice be collected from a gland or organ of an
      animal that has recovered from surgery, awake and eating. Combined with an opening of the
      esophagus at the neck, it separates what eating does from what food in the stomach does.
  - name: "Pharmacological blockade"
    blurb: >-
      Give a drug that blocks one signaling route, such as atropine for the acetylcholine receptors
      that nerves use on many gut glands, and measure how much of a response remains. The fraction
      that disappears shows how much of the response depends on that route under those
      conditions; other tests are needed to show where along the route the signal acts.
  - name: "In vitro fermentation cultures"
    blurb: >-
      Gut or fecal microbes from donor animals are grown without oxygen at body temperature with a
      chosen food added, and pH and organic acids (measured by high-performance liquid
      chromatography) are followed over hours. It shows what a community can do with a substrate,
      not what happens in the intact animal.
  - name: "Comparative method with phylogenetic regression"
    blurb: >-
      Measure a trait such as intestine length across many species, scale it by body mass, and fit
      a model that accounts for how the species are related. It asks whether a pattern holds beyond
      what shared ancestry and body size already explain.

Why would a rabbit eat some of its own droppings? The first film answered with a one-way tube. This text puts numbers on a second puzzle: on round figures, about 9 L of water a day enter an adult human's small intestine, yet only about a tenth of a litre leaves in the feces. Where does all that water come from, and why does so little leave?

> **The one idea.** A complete gut is a one-way line, so its stretches can specialize. Large food molecules must be broken down before or within the stretch that can absorb them. What a stretch does not absorb moves on, to be taken up or changed further down or lost in the feces, unless the animal sends it round again. For water in any stretch: what leaves = what came in + what the gut adds − what is absorbed.
>
> *Scope, stated once for the whole lesson:* complete guts at steady state. Water, salts and single sugars (monosaccharides) need no breakdown; sugars of two or more units, starch included, must be split first. Digestion and absorption overlap along the small intestine; microbes change some molecules into others; and other stretches absorb particular things. The water balance counts no exit but absorption and onward flow; a balance for one nutrient must also count what digestion and microbes make or use up. Some newborn mammals absorb whole antibodies from milk, an immune exception, not nutrition.

## One tube, four jobs and two helpers

A gut has four jobs.

- **Mechanical breakdown** tears and grinds food into smaller pieces.
- **Enzymatic hydrolysis** splits large molecules (starch, proteins, fats) into small subunits by adding water across their bonds. The enzyme chemistry is in [the Biochemistry lesson on digestion](/lessons/digestion/).
- **Absorption** moves those subunits across the gut lining, an [epithelium](/lessons/epithelia/) (a sheet of tightly joined cells), mostly through the transporters in [membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/).
- **Elimination** gets rid of what is left.

Two helpers reach what the host's enzymes alone cannot:

- **Bile salts** **emulsify** fat, breaking large drops into small droplets that lipase, the fat-digesting enzyme, can reach.
- **Fermentation** is the breakdown of food by microbes living in the gut, without oxygen. With their own enzymes the microbes split bonds the host has no enzyme for, then ferment the products.

In Hydra, a small freshwater cnidarian, and in a planarian flatworm, the gut is **incomplete**: a sac with one opening, the gastrovascular cavity, where food is digested and the leftovers go out the way they came in. The cavity may branch, but nothing flows through it from mouth to anus. A **complete gut**, as in earthworms, insects and vertebrates, is the first film's one-way tube ([animal body plans](/lessons/animal-body-plans/)).

## Mouth to anus in a human

The first film named the stations. In an adult human, food passes from the mouth to the pharynx, the esophagus, the stomach and the small intestine (duodenum, then jejunum, then ileum). It then enters the large intestine (the **cecum**, a blind pouch where the small and large intestine meet, then the ascending, transverse, descending and sigmoid colon), the rectum and the anal canal, which opens at the anus.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/digestive-systems/human-gut-block-map.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a block diagram of the human digestive tube from mouth to anus with salivary glands, stomach, liver with gallbladder, pancreas, small and large intestine, and an inset of one villus with a blood capillary and a lacteal." caption="The body's own digestion starts in the mouth and stomach and is mostly done in the small intestine, where it overlaps with absorption; the liver, gallbladder and pancreas all feed the duodenum, its first part." />

- **Mouth and salivary glands.** Teeth reduce particle size. Three pairs of major salivary glands (parotid, submandibular, sublingual) add saliva, whose amylase starts digesting starch.
- **Esophagus.** Waves of muscle contraction, called **peristalsis**, push the swallowed lump (the bolus) to the stomach.
- **Stomach.** It mixes food with acid and enzymes into a soup called **chyme**. Parietal cells secrete hydrochloric acid and chief cells secrete pepsinogen, the inactive form of pepsin, which starts protein digestion. The layers of its wall are in [tissues and homeostasis](/lessons/tissues-and-homeostasis/).
- **Small intestine.** The main site of digestion and of nutrient absorption. Circular folds, finger-like villi and the microvilli on each cell multiply its surface.
- **Liver and gallbladder.** The liver makes **bile**; the gallbladder stores and concentrates it. Besides emulsifying fat, bile salts gather the products of fat digestion into tiny clusters, **micelles**, that carry them to the cell surface; without enough bile salts, fat absorption is greatly reduced. Bile is not an enzyme.
- **Exocrine pancreas.** It sends digestive enzymes and bicarbonate, which neutralizes the stomach's acid, into the duodenum.
- **Large intestine.** It absorbs water and salts, and its microbes ferment what escaped digestion.

Blood from the villus capillaries the first film showed drains first to the liver, through the **hepatic portal vein**; the fat particles it sent into **lacteals** are **chylomicrons**.

## The water budget: the one idea in numbers

Take an adult human at steady state (the gut holds the same water from day to day), with round, illustrative figures (textbooks give somewhat different splits). About 9.0 L of water a day enters the small intestine: food and drink, plus saliva, gastric juice, bile, pancreatic juice and intestinal juice. Most of it is the gut's own secretions, poured in and taken back. About 90% is absorbed in the small intestine.

1. *Small intestine.* Absorbed = 9.0 L per day × 0.90 = 8.1 L per day. Passed on to the colon = 9.0 − 8.1 = 0.9 L per day.
2. *Colon.* Say the colon absorbs 0.8 L per day. Fecal water = 0.9 − 0.8 = 0.1 L per day. Total absorbed = 8.1 + 0.8 = 8.9 L per day, so overall absorption = 8.9 / 9.0 = 98.9%.

**Predict.** Now halve the colon's absorption, to 0.4 L per day. Does fecal water double?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No, it rises fivefold. Fecal water = 0.9 − 0.4 = 0.5 L per day, five times 0.1. Overall absorption = 8.5 / 9.0 = 94.4%, a drop of only 4.4 percentage points. Check: 8.5 + 0.5 = 9.0 L per day, as 8.9 + 0.1 did.

</details>

Why so sensitive? Fecal water is a small leftover after two large subtractions, 9.0 − 8.1 − 0.8 = 0.1 L per day, so a small change in either large flow is a large change in the leftover. If the colon still takes exactly 0.8 L per day, a fall in the small intestine's share from 90% to 89% raises the flow into the colon from 0.90 to 0.99 L per day and fecal water from 0.10 to 0.19 L, 1.9 times as much. These are model results: a real colon has spare capacity and partly compensates. And since most of the water is the gut's own, a gut that stops taking it back drains the body.

## Where the microbes sit: cow, horse, rabbit, hoatzin

The first film's fiber was **cellulose**, the main fiber of plant cell walls; humans make no cellulase, the enzyme that breaks it. The microbes ferment cellulose and other plant polysaccharides into **short-chain fatty acids** (acetate, propionate, butyrate), which the host uses for energy. The partnership is a mutualism, treated in [mutualism, parasitism and coevolution](/lessons/mutualism-parasitism-coevolution/).

**Foregut fermenters** ferment before the acid-secreting region. A cow has one stomach with four compartments: the rumen and reticulum hold the microbes that break down fiber, the omasum follows, and the **abomasum** is the glandular compartment that secretes acid and enzymes, equivalent to a single stomach. Kangaroos ferment in an enlarged front part of the stomach that evolved separately in marsupials. The hoatzin, *Opisthocomus hoazin*, a leaf-eating bird of South America, ferments leaves in its **crop**, the enlarged pouch of the esophagus where many birds store food. A chicken's crop mainly stores food, and the chicken grinds it in a muscular gizzard.

**Hindgut fermenters**, the film's horse and rabbit, ferment after the small intestine; a rabbit takes its soft pellets directly from the anus.

## Nerves and hormones run the line

Cutting the vagus nerves did not leave the dog's gut without nerves. Its wall holds the **enteric nervous system**, a network of nerve cells that runs local reflexes, such as peristalsis, by itself. The **vagus nerves** connect the brainstem with the gut and add **parasympathetic** signals, from the rest-and-digest division of the autonomic nervous system, that increase secretion ([the nervous system map](/lessons/nervous-system-map/), [the hypothalamus and autonomic system](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/)). The second film's **cephalic phase** is that vagus-driven flow, in the dog.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/digestive-systems/pavlov-sham-feeding-setup.webp" width={1200} height={753} alt="AI-generated illustration: a dog eating meat that falls out of an opening in its neck into a dish, while a flask attached to an opening in its stomach collects drops of gold gastric juice. An inset shows the same setup with the vagus nerve line cut and the flask empty." caption="Sham feeding separates eating from food in the stomach: in the dog, eating alone brings acid juice only while the vagus nerves are intact." />

Later phases are partly chemical, carried by **hormones**, substances released into the blood that act on distant target cells ([the endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/)).

**Predict.** Take a dog whose vagus nerves have been cut. Meat is placed through the fistula straight into its stomach. Will any juice flow?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes, some, in experiments like the MCAT item below. Food in the stomach can drive secretion without the vagus, for example through **gastrin**, a hormone from **G cells** of the lower stomach lining.

</details>

Three gut hormones:

| Hormone | Source | Trigger | Main targets and effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gastrin | G cells of the stomach lining | Food (especially protein) in the stomach; vagal input | Stomach: more acid, partly by releasing histamine from nearby cells |
| Secretin | Lining of the duodenum | Acid chyme entering the duodenum | Pancreatic duct cells: bicarbonate-rich fluid |
| CCK (cholecystokinin) | Lining of the duodenum | Fats and proteins in the duodenum | Pancreas: enzymes; gallbladder: contracts, releases bile |

Secretin was the first gut hormone found, by Bayliss and Starling, a story told in [signals and distance](/lessons/signals-and-distance/). Gastrin and CCK act on receptors of the type described in [GPCRs and G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/). Nerves and hormones also act on the same glands (Going deeper).

## Essential depends on who is eating

An **essential nutrient** is one an animal must get from food because its body cannot make it, or cannot make enough. For humans, nine amino acids are essential: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine ([amino acids](/lessons/amino-acids/)). Two fatty acids are essential for humans, linoleic acid and alpha-linolenic acid. **Vitamins** are organic molecules needed in small amounts; **minerals** are inorganic elements, such as calcium and iron.

Most mammals make their own vitamin C, but humans, some other primates and guinea pigs cannot and need it in the diet. The domestic cat is an **obligate carnivore**: it depends on animal tissue and needs taurine, a small sulfur-containing compound related to amino acids, in its diet; adult humans and many other mammals make taurine for themselves. "Essential" is a statement about an animal and its partners, not about a molecule.

**Predict.** Would a cow need dietary vitamin B12 to the same degree as a human?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Ruminant microbes make B vitamins, including B12 when the diet contains cobalt, and the microbial protein a cow digests supplies much of its amino acids. So a ruminant's dietary needs differ from a human's. They do not vanish: a ruminant still needs cobalt, and high-producing cattle benefit from protein that bypasses the rumen.

</details>

The one idea again: on a one-way line, a nutrient must be supplied, by food or by microbes, before or within the stretch that can absorb it.

Is the herbivore gut always longer? Not simply. Humans are **omnivores**, eating plant and animal foods, with a modest cecum and colon where microbes ferment fiber. Larger animals have longer intestines whatever they eat, and related species resemble each other through shared ancestry, their **phylogeny**. With both accounted for, gut length tracks diet only loosely, and by region.

### Design problem

Across many mammal species, herbivores have longer large intestines than carnivores. A classmate concludes that eating plants makes the large intestine grow longer. What would you need to check before accepting a link between diet and gut length, and what kind of evidence would test cause?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

First, scale by body mass: compare length at a given body mass, for example with a regression of log gut length on log body mass, with diet as a second predictor.

Second, account for phylogeny ([reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/)): fifty closely related herbivores are not fifty independent tests. A phylogenetic regression asks whether diet explains length beyond shared ancestry. Duque-Correa and colleagues (2021) did this for 519 mammal species, with body mass in the model, and found strong phylogenetic signal.

Third, test each region separately. In that study diet was associated with large-intestine length, but adding diet did not improve the small-intestine model, so "herbivores have longer guts" is too broad.

Fourth, a correlation across species does not show cause: diet might change the gut during a lifetime, or lineages with long large intestines might have taken up plant diets. Feeding experiments within one species would test cause, though a change within one life can mislead: in many frogs, plant-eating tadpoles have a long coiled gut that shortens at metamorphosis when the animal turns to eating animals, but thyroid hormone drives that whole transformation, so diet and gut change are tangled.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Pavlov's preparation

From about 1889 Pavlov's St Petersburg laboratory gave dogs an **esophagotomy** (the esophagus opened at the neck) and a **gastric fistula** (a stomach opening for draining juice).

### Cellulose is not the only fiber

Cellulose is the main fiber of plant cell walls, though pectins and hemicelluloses make up much of the rest.

### Gastrin and secretin: discovery dates

Bayliss and Starling reported secretin in 1902. J. S. Edkins proposed gastrin in 1905, but it was purified only in 1964.

### How much of CCK's effect needs nerves?

In healthy human volunteers given CCK-8 at one dose, atropine, a drug that blocks the acetylcholine receptors nerves use on many glands, cut the pancreatic trypsin response by 84%. So at that dose much of the hormone's effect depended on acetylcholine, and tests on rat pancreas suggested the acetylcholine came from nerves. At a higher, above-normal dose, atropine blocked less of the response.

### Hormone cells that talk to nerves

In mice, Kaelberer and colleagues reported in 2018 that some hormone-releasing cells of the intestine form synapses on vagal neurons and signal with glutamate, a fast nerve-like route alongside slower hormones.
