      label they take up comes from filling the gaps left by excision repair, so the amount
      measures how well the cells cut out and replace damage.
  - name: "Cell-fusion complementation"
    blurb: >-
      Cells from two patients with the same recessive repair defect are fused into one cell with
      both nuclei. If repair returns, each patient supplies the gene product the other lacks, so
      their mutations are in different genes; if not, they are in the same gene.
  - name: "Reconstitution from purified proteins"
    blurb: >-
      A repair reaction is rebuilt in a test tube from separately purified proteins and a DNA
      substrate with a defined lesion. It shows which proteins are sufficient for the reaction
      and lets each step be read separately, though it does not show how the reaction runs on
      chromatin in a living cell.
  - name: "Site-specific break reporter"
    blurb: >-
      The yeast endonuclease I-SceI cuts a recognition site placed once in a chromosome, inside an inactive copy of a reporter gene such as GFP;
      a nearby fragment of the same gene serves as the donor. Repair of the break by homologous recombination restores a working
      reporter, so the fraction of reporter-positive cells measures homology-directed repair at
      that one site.

**Every day, in the DNA of your cells, some cytosines lose their amino group and turn into uracil, a base that pairs with adenine.**

Water and body temperature are enough. If the uracil stayed, the next replication would put an A opposite it, and the round after that would leave an A·T pair where a G·C pair had been: a mutation made by chemistry alone. This lesson asks how cells find a few wrong bases among billions of right ones, and what happens to people who inherit a broken repair system.

## Damage is constant: the lesions

A **lesion** is any change to DNA's chemical structure, and it is not yet a mutation. It usually becomes a **mutation**, a heritable change in sequence, when replication copies it into a normal-looking base pair on both strands. Repair can also change the sequence, as when end joining loses a few bases at a break.

The lesions fall into a few kinds:

- **Spontaneous chemistry.** Cytosine deaminates to uracil, and purine bases fall off their sugars, leaving an **abasic (AP) site**, a sugar with no base.
- **Oxidation and alkylation.** Reactive oxygen produces 8-oxoguanine, which can pair with adenine. Alkylating agents add methyl groups; O6-methylguanine pairs with thymine.
- **Ultraviolet light.** UV joins two neighboring pyrimidines on the same strand into a **cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer** or a **6-4 photoproduct**. Both distort the helix, the 6-4 photoproduct much more than the dimer, and both stall polymerases.
- **Strand breaks.** Ionizing radiation and collapsed replication forks can break both strands at once.
- **Replication errors.** The polymerase pairs normal bases wrongly or slips on repeats (see [mutation](/lessons/mutation/)).

One rule runs through almost all of repair: the double helix holds its information twice. If one strand is damaged, a cell can cut out the bad stretch and copy the partner strand, the same templating used in [DNA replication](/lessons/dna-replication/). A break through both strands removes that option at one spot, which is why it needs separate machinery.

## Reversing and cutting out: direct reversal, BER and NER

**Direct reversal** undoes the chemistry in place, without cutting the backbone. Photolyases use blue and near-UV light to split pyrimidine dimers back into separate bases. They work in bacteria, yeast and plants, but humans lack the photolyases that reverse UV dimers, so your skin cannot use this route. The second example is **O6-methylguanine methyltransferase** (MGMT). It moves the methyl group from the guanine to a cysteine in its own active site, which restores the base and inactivates the protein for good. One molecule repairs one lesion, so it is called a suicide enzyme.

**Base excision repair** (BER) handles most small, non-bulky base lesions that direct reversal does not: uracil, 8-oxoguanine, some alkylated bases, AP sites. It runs in steps:

1. A **DNA glycosylase** that recognizes one damaged base, or a few related ones, flips that base out of the helix and cuts the bond to its sugar, leaving an AP site.
2. **AP endonuclease** cuts the backbone at the AP site. Some glycosylases, including the one that removes 8-oxoguanine, also cut the backbone themselves, and AP endonuclease then trims the cut end.
3. A polymerase (polymerase beta in human cells) inserts the correct nucleotide, using the partner strand, and removes any leftover sugar fragment.
4. A ligase seals the nick.

This is the short-patch route, which replaces one nucleotide. A long-patch route replaces several.

In 1974 Tomas Lindahl reported an E. coli activity that released uracil from deaminated DNA and left the backbone uncut. In 1996 his group rebuilt human uracil repair from four purified proteins, which filled a one-nucleotide patch.

**Nucleotide excision repair** (NER) handles bulky lesions that distort the helix: UV dimers and 6-4 photoproducts, and adducts from drugs such as cisplatin. It recognizes distortion of the helix rather than one chemical change, so one pathway copes with many lesions. In human cells the sensor XPC finds distorted DNA anywhere in the genome, helped by UV-DDB for the weakly distorting cyclobutane dimers, and an RNA polymerase stalled at a lesion flags damage in genes being transcribed. TFIIH, a complex with helicase activity, then opens the DNA and checks that a lesion is there. NER then cuts the damaged strand on both sides and removes a short oligonucleotide. Aziz Sancar and Dean Rupp showed this dual incision in 1983 with the purified E. coli proteins UvrA, UvrB and UvrC. The E. coli fragment is about 12 to 13 nucleotides; in a human cell-free system the removed piece was 27 to 29 nucleotides long. In human cells the cut on the 5′ side is made by XPF-ERCC1 and the cut on the 3′ side by XPG. A polymerase then fills the gap, and a ligase seals it.

## Fixing the copier's mistakes: mismatch repair

A **mismatch** is two normal bases paired wrongly, such as G with T. Neither base is damaged, so which one is wrong? The answer is whichever is on the new strand, because the template was correct before the polymerase arrived.

In E. coli the new strand carries a mark of absence. The Dam methylase adds a methyl group to the adenine in GATC sequences, but only some time after the fork passes. For that window, new DNA is **hemimethylated**: methylated on the template strand only. **MutS** binds the mismatch, **MutL** joins it, and together they activate **MutH**, which nicks the unmethylated strand at a GATC site. The helicase UvrD unwinds the new strand from the nick past the mismatch, exonucleases degrade it, and the polymerase and ligase fill in. In Paul Modrich's purified system a GATC site about 1 kilobase away still directed repair.

That GATC signal belongs to E. coli and its relatives. It is unusual, not universal. Eukaryotes keep MutS and MutL relatives (MSH2 paired with MSH6, and MLH1 paired with PMS2 in humans) but have no MutH. They appear to read **strand breaks** instead: new DNA has free ends, at the tip of the growing strand and between Okazaki fragments. A purified human system could be directed by a nick on either side of a mismatch. Human mismatch repair also corrects small loops left when the polymerase slips on a repeat.

**Worked problem.** The numbers here are round and chosen for illustration, not measured for one polymerase. Assume a replicative polymerase inserts a wrong base about once per 10^5 nucleotides, proofreading removes 99% of those errors, mismatch repair removes 99.9% of what is left, and a human diploid cell copies about 6.4 x 10^9 base pairs per S phase.

**Step 1. Turn each percentage into a fraction that survives.** Proofreading lets 1% through, 10^-2. Mismatch repair lets 0.1% through, 10^-3.

**Step 2. Multiply the stages, because each acts on what the last one missed.**

> **final error rate = 10^-5 x 10^-2 x 10^-3 = 10^-10 per base pair**

**Step 3. Scale to the genome.**

> **6.4 x 10^9 bp x 10^-10 = 0.64 errors per genome per replication**

**Step 4. Remove mismatch repair.** The rate is now 10^-5 x 10^-2 = 10^-7 per base pair, and 6.4 x 10^9 x 10^-7 = 640 errors.

So with these numbers, losing mismatch repair raises the error rate a thousandfold, from under one error per division to hundreds.

## Broken chromosomes: end joining and homologous recombination

A **double-strand break** cuts the chromosome in two. Picture a rope cut through: you can knot the ends, or splice in a matching length from an identical rope. Cells do both. These are the two main routes. Others, such as single-strand annealing and alternative end joining by polymerase theta, join resected ends at short matching sequences and usually delete DNA in the process.

**Non-homologous end joining** (NHEJ) knots the ends. The ring-shaped **Ku** protein binds each end, recruits the kinase DNA-PKcs, and nucleases such as Artemis and polymerases trim or fill ends that do not fit. XRCC4 with DNA ligase IV joins them. NHEJ uses no template, so if the ends had to be processed, a few bases may be gained or lost at the joint. Calling it error-prone overstates this: how accurate it is depends on the structure of the ends, and clean, compatible ends are often rejoined exactly. NHEJ works throughout the cell cycle.

**Homologous recombination** (HR) splices from a matching rope. Nucleases resect the 5′ ends, leaving 3′ single-stranded tails. A recombinase coats each tail, **RecA** in E. coli and **Rad51** in eukaryotes, and the filament searches for a matching duplex and invades it, forming a displacement loop (**D-loop**). The invading 3′ end primes DNA synthesis, copying the information lost at the break. The best template is the **sister chromatid**, the identical copy made during replication, so HR acts mainly in S and G2.

Most often the extended end is then released from the D-loop and pairs with the other end of the break, a route called **synthesis-dependent strand annealing** (SDSA) that forms no crossover. In another branch, which makes most meiotic crossovers, the second end is also captured and the two duplexes are linked by two **Holliday junctions**, four-stranded crossings named for Robin Holliday, who proposed the structure in 1964. In their 1983 yeast model, Szostak, Orr-Weaver, Rothstein and Stahl made the double-strand break the event that starts recombination. Before the chromosomes separate, the junctions are either dissolved, giving no crossover, or cut by resolvases, giving a crossover or not depending on which strands are cut. Related machinery, started by programmed breaks and joined by meiosis-specific proteins, makes the meiotic crossovers of [chromosomes and meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/) that [linkage mapping](/lessons/linkage-and-mapping/) measures. How a CRISPR cut is repaired is taken up in [forward and reverse genetics](/lessons/crispr-and-functional-genomics/).

## When repair fails: three human conditions

Unrepaired damage also triggers checkpoints, signaled by the kinases ATM and ATR and enforced in part by p53, described in [stress signals](/lessons/stress-signals/). Three inherited conditions show what happens when a repair pathway is lost: from birth in XP, and in tumor cells of people with Lynch syndrome or BRCA variants.

**Xeroderma pigmentosum** (XP) is autosomal recessive: affected people have two nonworking alleles of one gene. Most forms disrupt NER, so UV dimers in skin cells go unrepaired, and the condition is associated with sunlight-induced skin cancers. Cell fusion sorted patients into several complementation groups, each a different NER gene. One form, the **XP variant**, has normal excision. Its cells cannot replicate efficiently past UV damage, and in 1999 two groups, Masutani and colleagues and Johnson, Prakash and colleagues, found mutations in the gene for DNA polymerase eta, a bypass polymerase, in XP variant cell lines.

**Lynch syndrome** is a dominantly inherited predisposition to colorectal, endometrial and other cancers, with risks that vary by gene. Carriers are heterozygous for a germline variant in **MLH1, MSH2, MSH6 or PMS2**, or have a deletion at the 3′ end of EPCAM that silences the neighboring MSH2. One working allele is usually enough for repair, so their normal cells repair mismatches. Typically a tumor arises in a cell lineage that loses or inactivates the second allele and with it mismatch repair, though some cancers in carriers keep mismatch repair. The link was made in 1993, when Fishel and colleagues and Leach and colleagues each reported MSH2 mutations in such families. The two-hit logic belongs to [cancer genetics](/lessons/cancer-genetics/).

**BRCA1 and BRCA2** were cloned by Miki and colleagues (1994) and Wooster and colleagues (1995). Both are needed for efficient HR: Brca1-deficient mouse stem cells repaired chromosome breaks poorly by HR, and BRCA2-mutant human and mouse cell lines showed 6-fold to more than 100-fold less homology-directed repair, depending on the line. Carriers inherit one nonworking allele in an autosomal dominant pattern of risk for breast and ovarian cancers, and tumors typically lose the remaining allele. HR-deficient cells are unusually sensitive to PARP inhibitors, an example of synthetic lethality.

### Design problem

Fibroblasts from four unrelated XP patients, P1 to P4, each show low unscheduled DNA synthesis after UV. You fuse cells in pairs and score the fused cells after UV: P1+P2 normal, P1+P3 low, P1+P4 normal, P2+P3 normal, P2+P4 low, P3+P4 normal. How many genes are involved? What would you do next to identify them?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use the logic of [complementation and pathways](/lessons/complementation-and-pathways/): XP is recessive, so a fused cell repairs normally only if each partner supplies the working gene the other lacks.

1. P1+P3 stays low, so P1 and P3 are defective in the same gene. P2+P4 stays low, so they share a gene.
2. Every pair across those groups (P1+P2, P1+P4, P2+P3, P3+P4) is normal, so the two groups are different genes.
3. Answer: two complementation groups, \{P1, P3\} and \{P2, P4\}, and so at least two genes.

To identify them, transfect cells of each group with cloned candidate NER genes, one at a time, and look for the gene that restores UV-induced repair synthesis in one group but not the other. Then sequence that gene in the group's patients, expecting two nonworking alleles in each. A patient sensitive to UV but with normal unscheduled DNA synthesis would point instead to the XP variant, a defect in bypass by polymerase eta.

</details>
