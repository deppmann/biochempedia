## The big idea

Pick up a rock from a shallow creek and turn it over. Small animals scramble off the bottom of it. Most children have never noticed them, and most adults haven't either.

A creek is an **ecosystem**: living things together with the nonliving things they need, such as water, rocks, sunlight and air. Many of the animals under the rocks are the young of insects like mayflies, stoneflies and caddisflies. They live underwater for months or years before they grow wings and fly away. Snails, crayfish and worms live there too.

Some of these animals can live only in clean water with plenty of oxygen. Others can live in dirtier water. So the mix of animals you find is a clue to the creek’s health. Volunteers with Virginia Save Our Streams sample creeks this way, and the state uses their counts.

Virginia’s grade 4 standard groups ocean animals by how they move: floating, swimming, or staying fixed to the bottom. Your child can try the same sort with creek animals.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay within arm’s reach of your child the whole time they are in or beside the water. A child under 5 who comes along stays on the bank with a second adult. With a class, follow your school’s field-trip rules, and let only as many children wade at a time as an adult can stay within arm’s reach of; the rest work at the tray on the bank with another adult.
- Wade only where the water is ankle-deep for your child and slow or gently rippling, never rushing. Stay out for about three days after heavy rain, or when the water is high, fast, muddy, an odd colour, has a film on top or smells bad, or near a pipe draining into the creek. Leave at the first sign of rising water or thunder.
- Everyone wears closed-toe water shoes. Rocks are slippery, and creeks can hide broken glass.
- Never drink creek water, and keep hands away from mouths. No snacks until hands are washed. Cover cuts with waterproof bandages, or keep that person out of the water. If anyone has a weakened immune system, ask their health care provider before they wade. Wash hands with soap and water afterwards, and wash the strainer, spoon and trays before they go back in the kitchen.
- Lift only small rocks a child can move easily, and look before you reach, because snakes live near creeks. Move animals with a spoon or strainer, not fingers. Crayfish and hellgrammites (big, dark larvae with strong jaws) pinch. Leave snakes, salamanders, fish and mussels alone (taking mussels is against Virginia law), and put every animal back where you found it.
- On the banks, watch for poison ivy, and check everyone for ticks afterwards (CDC suggests a shower within two hours). Follow park rules and posted water advisories, and don’t collect anything where it isn’t allowed. Many parks do not allow catching animals, and Virginia’s Department of Wildlife Resources (DWR) requires a permit to catch and release wildlife for educational use, so teachers and group leaders should ask the park and DWR first.
- A child can drown in a few inches of water. Never leave the tray, or any bucket or tub of creek or pond water, where a small child can reach it. Pour the tray back at the creek as soon as you finish, and empty anything you bring home.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Choose the spot and the day.** Pick a public park creek with a **riffle**: a shallow stretch where water ripples over fist-sized rocks. Late spring to fall works best, when the water is warm enough to wade. Go when the creek is low and clear, at least three days after heavy rain.
2. **Set up on the bank.** Fill the white tray with creek water about 2 to 3 cm deep. Keep it in the shade so the water stays cool.
3. **Predict.** Before anyone touches the water, ask, "What do you think lives in this creek?" Have your child draw their guess.
4. **Catch.** One person holds the strainer flat on the creek bed, just downstream of a rock. The other lifts the rock, looks first for crayfish or hellgrammites, which pinch, and then gently rubs its underside with their hands for about 30 seconds, so loose animals wash into the strainer. Tip the strainer into the tray. Try three or four rocks.
5. **Look.** Wait a minute for the mud to settle. Things that looked like specks will start to move. Use the hand lens. Look for tails, legs and feathery gills. Mayfly young usually have three tails and gills along their sides. Stonefly young have two tails. Caddisfly young often live in little cases made of sand or sticks.
6. **Sort by how they move.** Use the spoon to move animals into the ice-cube tray. Make groups: crawlers and clingers, swimmers, and ones that stay still or stuck to things. Ask how these groups compare to floating, swimming and fixed ocean animals.
7. **Read the clues.** Did you find mayfly, stonefly or caddisfly young? That is usually a sign of a healthy creek, though one common kind of caddisfly, the net-spinner, also lives in dirtier water. Even a healthy creek is never safe to drink. If you found mostly worms, leeches or tiny red wiggly larvae, the water may be less healthy. One visit is a clue, not a final answer.
8. **Return everything.** Pour the tray back slowly at the water’s edge, near where you caught the animals. Put rocks back the way they were. Wash hands with soap.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "What do you think we will find under this rock?"
- "I wonder why so many animals live under the rocks and not on top."
- "What do you think this one eats? How could we find out?"
- "Which things in the tray are living, and which are not living?"
- "What do you think would happen to these animals if mud from a building site washed into the creek?"
- "If a fish lived here, which of these animals might it eat?"

## Why it works

Scientists call these animals **benthic macroinvertebrates**: animals without backbones, big enough to see, that live on the bottom. They are useful for judging a stream because they stay in one stretch for months and cannot easily escape pollution. A single water test shows one moment. The animals show what the creek has been like over time.

Stonefly, mayfly and caddisfly young mostly breathe through gills and need cool water with plenty of dissolved oxygen. Mud, too much fertilizer and other pollution lower the oxygen or smother the rocks they live on. Worms, leeches and some midge larvae can live with much less oxygen. A healthy creek usually has many different kinds of animals. A creek with only a few tolerant kinds may have a problem. Sensitivity varies between species within each group, so trained volunteers count and identify carefully before drawing conclusions.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Skip naming. Ask your child to sort animals into "has legs" and "no legs," or "fast" and "slow." Count the tails on one animal together. Have them draw one creature large on the sheet. Keep the visit to 20 minutes, and let them hold the strainer while you lift the rock.

### Ages 8 to 10
Make a tally table with columns for each group: mayflies, stoneflies, caddisflies, worms and leeches, snails, others. Compare two spots, such as a riffle and a slow stretch, or the same spot in two seasons. Wade only where the water is ankle-deep for your child; sample a deeper pool from the bank, or have the adult do it. Add a simple key: "Does it have three tails? Does it have two? Do gills run along its sides? Does it live in a case?" Ask what the counts suggest about the creek, and what else they would want to know.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If you find almost nothing, try a faster, rockier spot, rub the rocks longer, or come back in another season. With no safe creek nearby, try a pond: you collect a cup of water and leaves from the edge while standing on firm ground. Or, with permission, the adult can tie a mesh onion bag of dead leaves to a rock in a riffle and lift it out three to four weeks later, only in water that is ankle-deep and slow, at least three days after heavy rain.
