## The big idea

Turn over a rock in a shallow creek and small animals scramble off the bottom. Most children — and most adults — have never noticed them. In 40 minutes, your child practices looking closely, sorting what they see, and checking a guess against real evidence.

> **Say it together:** "A creek is more than water. Tiny animals hide under the rocks, eat bits of leaves, and feed the fish. Some live only in clean water, so they are clues to how healthy the creek is."

A creek is an **ecosystem**: living things together with nonliving things they need, like water, rocks, sunlight and air. Many rock-dwelling animals are the young of insects — mayflies, stoneflies and caddisflies — that live underwater for months or years before growing wings to fly away. Snails, crayfish and worms live there too.

They depend on one another: many eat fallen leaf bits and tiny plants, some eat other small animals, and many fish depend on them for food. Because some need clean, oxygen-rich water while others tolerate dirtier water, the mix you find is a clue to creek health. Virginia Save Our Streams volunteers sample creeks this way, and the state uses their counts.

<SafetyNote>
- **Water and arm’s reach:** Stay within arm’s reach whenever your child is in or beside water. Never leave a child alone near a creek, pond or filled tray. Any child under 5 stays on the bank with a second adult. With a class, follow school field-trip rules. Let only as many children wade as an adult can stay within arm’s reach of. The rest work at the bank tray with another adult. A child can drown in a few inches of water. Keep the tray and any bucket or tub of creek or pond water out of small children’s reach. Pour the tray back at the creek as soon as you finish. Empty any containers you bring home.
- **Safe water and weather:** Wade only where water is ankle-deep for your child and slow or gently rippling, never rushing. Stay out about three days after heavy rain, or if water is high, fast, muddy, odd-colored, filmy, bad-smelling or near a drain pipe. Leave at the first sign of rising water or thunder.
- **Shoes and glass:** Everyone wears closed-toe water shoes. Rocks are slippery, and creeks can hide broken glass. Use a plastic cup and leave glass containers at home. An adult checks the spot first and keeps everyone clear of broken glass or sharp trash.
- **Choking (under 4) and emergencies:** Keep anything small enough to choke on out of reach of children under 4. This includes tiny creek animals, pebbles, snail shells, small sticks and caps. **Call 911 if choking** or struggling to breathe. Call **Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222** if anyone swallows repellent, soap or an unknown plant or berry.
- **Plants, water and hands:** Never drink creek water or pick, eat or taste wild plants, berries or mushrooms on the bank. Watch for poison ivy; avoid tall grass and brush. Keep hands out of mouths and wait to snack until hands are washed. Cover cuts with waterproof bandages (latex-free if needed), or stay out of the water. Anyone with a weakened immune system should ask their health care provider before wading. Wash hands with soap afterward, and wash strainer, spoon and trays before kitchen use.
- **Animals and rocks:** An adult checks before lifting rocks, since snakes live near creeks; lift only small rocks a child can move easily. Use a spoon or strainer, not fingers. Do not handle pinching, biting or stinging animals: crayfish and hellgrammites (big, dark, strong-jawed larvae) pinch; giant water bugs and backswimmers (in slower water) can bite. Leave snakes, salamanders, fish and mussels alone (taking mussels breaks Virginia law), and return every animal and rock where found.
- **Roads, allergies, ticks and rules:** Watch for traffic in parking lots. Stay well away from roads and bridge embankments. Check sunscreen, repellent, snacks and gear for allergies. Skip egg cartons if anyone has an egg allergy. An adult applies sunscreen first, then EPA-registered insect repellent following the label for the child’s age. Keep the bottles out of reach. Check everyone for ticks afterward (CDC suggests a shower within two hours). Follow park rules and posted water advisories. Collect animals only where allowed. Many parks do not allow catching animals. Virginia’s Department of Wildlife Resources (DWR) requires a permit to catch and release wildlife for educational use. Teachers and group leaders should ask the park and DWR first.

## What to do

1. **Choose the spot and day.** Pick a public park creek with a **riffle**: shallow water rippling over fist-sized rocks. Late spring to fall is warm enough to wade. Some stonefly young are scarce in midsummer, so finding none then does not mean the creek is unhealthy. Go when water is low and clear, at least three days after heavy rain.
2. **Set up on the bank.** Staying within arm’s reach, fill the white plastic tray with 2 to 3 cm of creek water. Keep it in the shade so it stays cool.
3. **Predict first.** Before anyone touches a rock, hand over the pencil and sheet. Say: *"What do you think lives under these rocks? Draw your guess before we check."*
4. **Catch together.** Stay within arm’s reach. Your child holds the strainer flat on the creek bed just downstream of a rock. Look before reaching. Lift the rock and check underneath before rubbing. If you see a crayfish, hellgrammite or another animal that can pinch, bite or sting, gently put the rock back and try another. Otherwise, gently rub the underside for about 30 seconds so loose animals wash into the strainer. Tip the strainer into the tray. Try three or four rocks.
5. **Wait, notice and check.** Say: *"Hold still one minute while the mud settles, and watch the specks."* **What your child will notice:** specks that looked like dirt start to dart, wiggle or crawl. Compare what moves against your child’s drawing. Then use the hand lens and check one body clue at a time:
   - **Mayfly young:** usually three tails and feathery gills along their sides.
   - **Stonefly young:** two tails and no gills along the sides of their belly.
   - **Caddisfly young:** often live in little cases of sand or sticks.
6. **Sort by movement.** Introduce one sorting rule first. Use the plastic spoon (not fingers) to move animals into the ice-cube tray. Ask: *"Does this one crawl and cling, swim, or stay stuck?"* Group them: crawlers and clingers, swimmers, and ones that stay still or stuck.
7. **Read the clues.** Connect the food web first. Ask: *"What do you think this one eats, and what might eat it?"* Then check the mix. Several kinds of mayfly, stonefly or caddisfly young usually signal a healthy creek. Tolerance varies even within mayflies and caddisflies (see **For older children**), so many kinds tell you more than one. Mostly worms, leeches or tiny red wiggly larvae may mean less healthy water. One visit is a clue, not a final answer.
8. **Return everything and debrief.** Pour the tray back slowly at the water’s edge near where you caught the animals. Put rocks back as they were, and wash hands with soap. Close with two quick questions that return to the "Say it together" idea:
   - *"What surprised you most under the rocks?"*
   - *"What do those animals tell us about this creek’s water?"*

## Questions to ask while you do it

Let your child guess first, then check.

- "What do you think we will find under this rock?"
- "I wonder why so many animals live under the rocks and not on top."
- "What do you think this one eats? How could we find out?"
- "If a fish lived here, which of these animals might it eat?"
- "Can you draw an arrow from what one animal eats to the animal, and then to what eats it?"
- "Which things in the tray are living, and which are not living?"
- "What do you think would happen to these animals if mud from a building site washed into the creek?"

## Why it works

Scientists call these animals **benthic macroinvertebrates**: animals without backbones, big enough to see, living on the bottom. They stay in one stretch for months and cannot easily escape pollution. A single water test shows one moment; the animals show what the creek has been like over time.

Most stonefly young, and many mayfly and caddisfly young, need cool water with plenty of dissolved oxygen, taken in through gills or skin. Mud can smother rocks. Too much fertilizer feeds growth and decay that use up oxygen, and some other pollution harms them directly. Worms, leeches and some midge larvae survive with much less oxygen. A healthy creek usually holds many kinds, while a creek with only a few tolerant kinds may have a problem. Sensitivity varies between species within each group, so trained volunteers count and identify carefully before drawing conclusions.

## Easier and harder

### For younger children (ages 5 to 7)
Skip naming. Sort animals into "has legs" and "no legs," or "fast" and "slow." Count tails on one animal together and draw one large on the sheet. Keep the visit to 20 minutes, with your child holding the strainer while you check and lift rocks.

### For older children (ages 8 to 10)
Make a tally table for mayflies, stoneflies, caddisflies, worms and leeches, snails, and others. Compare a riffle with a slow stretch, or visit the same spot in two seasons. Wade only in water that is ankle-deep for your child. Sample a deeper pool only from a firm, low bank while you stay within arm’s reach. Skip any pool you cannot reach safely.

Use a multi-clue key: *"Three tails or two? Side gills along its belly? Does it live in a case?"* Some mayflies have two tails with side gills, and damselfly young have three flat, leaf-like tails, so check every clue. Net-spinner caddisflies and some small mayflies also live in dirtier water — another reason many kinds tell more than one. Ask what the counts suggest and what else they would want to know.

You can also compare Step 6’s groups with ocean life: Virginia’s grade 4 standard groups ocean animals as floating, swimming or fixed to the bottom. Ask how their creek groups match up.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If you find almost nothing, try another rocky riffle with slow, ankle-deep water, rub the rocks longer or come back in another season. With no safe creek nearby, try a pond. The adult collects a cup of water and leaves from the edge while standing on firm ground. With permission, the adult can also tie a mesh onion bag of dead leaves to a riffle rock and lift it out three to four weeks later. Do this only in ankle-deep, slow water at least three days after heavy rain.
