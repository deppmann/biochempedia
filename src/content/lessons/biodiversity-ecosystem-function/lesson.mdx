    story: >-
      By the late 1990s the biodiversity experiments had a dispute at their centre. A mixture can
      yield more than its average monoculture because its species use resources in different ways
      or help one another, or because a random draw of several species is more likely to include a
      highly productive one that then dominates. Both produce a richness effect. Michel Loreau, a
      theoretical ecologist, worked with Andy Hector on an algebraic way to separate them. Their
      2001 paper in Nature wrote the net biodiversity effect as the sum of two terms, in a form
      analogous to the Price equation of evolutionary biology. One term grows when species on
      average do better in mixture than expected from their monocultures. The other grows when the

      ranged from negative to positive among sites. The partition became a widely used
      way to report what mixtures in later experiments were doing.

techniques:
  - name: "Richness manipulation with random species composition"
    blurb: >-
      Assign each plot a number of species, then draw which species at random from a fixed pool,
      with several different draws at each richness level. Because composition is random, a
      richness effect cannot come from the experimenter choosing productive species for rich plots.
  - name: "Clip, dry and weigh harvests"
    blurb: >-
      Cut the aboveground vegetation in sampling strips, sort it by species where needed, dry it
      and weigh it to give biomass in grams per square metre. Repeated each year, it gives the time
      series from which productivity and stability are computed.
  - name: "Loreau-Hector additive partition"
    blurb: >-
      Compare each species' yield in mixture with its yield in monoculture, then split the
      mixture's net effect into complementarity and selection terms. It needs species-level yields
      in both mixtures and monocultures, and it partitions statistically without naming a
      mechanism.
  - name: "Temporal stability as an inverse coefficient of variation"
    blurb: >-
      Divide the mean of a community's total biomass across years by its standard deviation. A
      higher value means steadier production; the result depends on the time window chosen and on
      whether a long-term trend is first removed from the series.

      The selection effect is species richness times the covariance between monoculture yield and
      the change in relative yield, so it is positive when the species that do best in monoculture

      gaining relative yield describes a positive complementarity effect, not selection. Despite

      natural selection. Beating the best monoculture is transgressive overyielding, a different
      comparison that neither term alone establishes.

**In grassland experiments that assigned the number of plant species at random, richer plots generally grew more aboveground biomass, and their totals varied proportionally less from year to year.**

> **The one idea.** A plot's production is the sum of what its species produce. A mixture yields extra when that sum beats the yield expected from its monocultures, and the sum can steady from year to year because species' swings partly cancel, unless the species rise and fall together.
>
> *Scope:* aboveground plant biomass in communities assembled at random, within the richness tested (1 to 16 species at Cedar Creek) and the years measured. Expected yield is each species' monoculture yield times its share of the seed; with an even sowing, that is the average monoculture, not the best. Out-of-step swings help but do not by themselves make a total steadier: that also depends on the species' sizes and swings, and on whether one species dominates. "Steadier" means steady across years, not resisting one drought or recovering afterwards.

## The question: does richness change what an ecosystem does?

An **ecosystem function** is a rate or stock of a process: a field's yearly plant production (primary productivity, from [energy flow](/lessons/energy-flow/)), the nitrogen it holds, how fast litter decays. **Species richness** is the number of species present, the simplest diversity measure from [diversity and succession](/lessons/diversity-and-succession/).

Surveys of real grasslands often find more biomass where there are more species, though the strength and even the direction of that relationship vary between studies. Moisture or fertility could raise both richness and biomass (the first film's wet patch); such a third variable is a **confounder**. So ecologists built communities instead of finding them.

## Building communities at random: Cedar Creek and BIODEPTH

At Cedar Creek, Minnesota, Tilman and colleagues sowed plots in spring 1994 with 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 perennial grassland and savanna species chosen at random from a pool of 18, with the same seed mass in every plot (10 g per square metre in 1994 and 5 g in 1995) so rich plots did not simply get more seed. Each year they measured **aboveground biomass**, the dry mass of plant material above the soil per square metre.

Random draws do not remove species identity: a richer draw is still more likely to include a very productive species, such as a legume. If such high-yielding species also gain the most relative yield in mixture, the next section's partition records a positive **selection effect**.

BIODEPTH repeated the logic at eight European sites with local species, and Hector and colleagues (1999) found that, across sites, communities assembled with fewer plant species had lower average aboveground biomass.

Composition still mattered: at a fixed species number, communities with fewer functional groups (such as grasses, legumes and other herbs) were less productive.

## Splitting the extra yield (worked problem)

Picture a shared garden. A group can out-harvest its members working alone because they grow different crops, deep-rooted and shallow (**complementarity**), or because a random group is more likely to include the gardener who is best alone, and that gardener gains the most from the group (**selection**). In plants, complementarity covers resource partitioning (the niche differences from [competition](/lessons/competition/)) and help between species, such as legumes adding nitrogen. A positive selection effect is what the older sampling-effect hypothesis predicted.

Loreau and Hector (2001) measured both from one number per species, its **relative yield** (RY): its yield in mixture, Y, divided by its yield alone in monoculture, M. In the first film's mixture, A makes 300 g per square metre against 400 alone, so RY_A = 0.75. Expected RY is its sown proportion (the first film's dashed bar), here 0.5. **ΔRY** ("delta R Y") is observed RY minus expected: A's is 0.25, better than its monoculture predicts. The **net effect**, observed mixture yield minus expected, then splits exactly:

> **net effect = complementarity + selection**

**Worked problem** (the first film's numbers, constructed; plots of the same area, same year). Alone, A yields M_A = 400 and B yields M_B = 100 g of dry biomass per square metre. In the mixture, Y_A = 300 and Y_B = 100.

1. *Net effect.* With an even sowing the expected yield is the average monoculture, 250, so net effect = 400 − 250 = **+150 g per square metre**.
2. *Relative yields.* ΔRY_A = 0.25 (above). RY_B = 100/100 = 1.00, so ΔRY_B = 0.50. Mean ΔRY = 0.375; mean M = 250.
3. *Complementarity* = 2 × 0.375 × 250 = **+187.5 g per square metre**.
4. *Selection.* Deviations from the means: A has M − 250 = +150 and ΔRY − 0.375 = −0.125; B has −150 and +0.125. Both products are −18.75, and their mean (divide by 2) is −18.75. Selection = 2 × −18.75 = **−37.5 g per square metre**.
5. *Check.* 187.5 − 37.5 = 150, the net effect.

Both species did better than expected, so complementarity is positive; the low-yielding B gained more, so selection is negative. Mean ΔRY above zero does not guarantee extra yield: had A made 120 and B 100, complementarity would be +75 but selection −105, and the mixture's 220 would miss the expected 250.

**Predict.** Species A makes 300 of the mixture's 400 g. Does a negative selection effect mean A failed to dominate?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. A dominates the biomass, but selection asks whether the relative-yield gains went to the species big in monoculture. They went to B, so selection is negative though A is the largest species present.

</details>

Beating the best monoculture, not just the average, is **transgressive overyielding**; the first film's mixture only matches A alone, so it shows none.

A positive complementarity term does not prove resource partitioning: the partition is exact algebra on yields, and legume nitrogen or reduced pathogen pressure could produce it too. It needs species-level yields in both mixtures and monocultures.

## Stability: how to score it, and when cancelling fails

Scoring the second film's steadier totals starts with a warning: "stable" has several meanings, and they need not agree:

- **Temporal stability** is the mean of total community biomass across years, μ ("mu"), divided by its standard deviation, σ ("sigma").
- **Resistance** is how little a function changes during a disturbance such as a drought.
- **Resilience** is how fully or quickly it returns afterwards.

<EquationCard
  purpose="How steady a community’s total biomass is from year to year."

  <Fragment slot="example">
    <p>The first film's single species: a mean of 50 g per square metre and a standard deviation of 25, so μ/σ = 50/25 = 2.</p>
  </Fragment>

The first film's total of four independent species swung only twice as much because standard deviations do not add; **variances** (squared standard deviations) do, when the species are uncorrelated. Four variances of 25² sum to 2,500, whose square root is 50, so μ/σ = 200/50 = 4. Stability grows with the square root of species number (16 species give 8), with no species any steadier.

Real species often move partly together; their **correlation** runs from +1 (lockstep) through 0 (no linear relation) to −1 (one rises exactly as the other falls). In general, the variance of a total is the sum of the species' variances plus twice the **covariance** of every pair, where a pair's covariance is their correlation times their two standard deviations.

**Predict.** Give the four species a correlation of 0.5 with each other: they tend to move together, but not perfectly. Is μ/σ closer to 4, closer to 2, or exactly halfway?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Closer to 2: about 2.5. Each pair's covariance is 0.5 × 25 × 25 = 312.5, and four species make six pairs, so the variance is 4 × 625 + 2 × 6 × 312.5 = 6,250. Its square root is about 79, and μ/σ = 200/79 ≈ 2.5. Partial synchrony removes most of the averaging gain: the one idea's "unless the species rise and fall together" is a matter of degree.

</details>

Negative correlations cancel more: one species rising as another falls is **compensatory dynamics**. If richer plots had more of it, part of their extra steadiness would be a **covariance effect**. At Cedar Creek, Tilman, Reich and Knops found no evidence of a covariance effect. They credited statistical averaging and overyielding: over 2001 to 2005, the 16-species plots averaged 180 percent more biomass than monocultures, and a higher mean raises μ/σ (see the card's check). Later analyses differ in how strongly they support averaging and in how long a record is needed to infer the mechanism (see Going deeper).

**Predict.** Suppose diverse plots lose a smaller share of their production in a drought. Will they also recover more fully a year later?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not detectably. Across 46 grassland diversity experiments, Isbell and colleagues (2015) found that during wet or dry climate events, productivity in communities of one or two species changed by about 50 percent, against about 25 percent at 16 to 32 species. That is greater resistance. Yet by one year after each event, they detected no dependence of recovery on diversity.

</details>

Stability measures can even move in opposite directions: in 690 aquatic ciliate microcosms, Pennekamp and colleagues (2018) found that richness raised temporal stability but lowered resistance to warming.

## Beyond the plots

In the BEF-China experiment, a planted subtropical forest, Huang and colleagues reported in 2018 that 16-species mixtures had accumulated more than twice the carbon of the average monoculture after eight years, and an amount similar to two commercial monocultures. The benchmark matters.

Real landscapes do not assign richness. Duffy and colleagues (2017) synthesized 67 field studies of wild systems, 133 estimates in all, and found positive biodiversity-biomass associations after statistically adjusting for environmental covariates. Adjustment is not random assignment: an unmeasured variable could still drive both. These results carry forward into [global change](/lessons/global-change/) and [conservation](/lessons/conservation/).

### Design problem

An observational survey finds that grassland plots with more plant species have more aboveground biomass. Design a study to decide whether richness causes the difference.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Soil fertility or moisture could raise both; break the link by assigning richness yourself.

1. Divide a uniform field into blocks by soil and slope. Within each block, randomly assign plots to 1, 2, 4 or 8 species.
2. At each richness level, draw several independent random compositions from a fixed local pool, and include a monoculture of every species so the partition can be computed.
3. Keep plot area and seed mass constant, and weed every plot alike to hold its assigned composition.
4. Measure soil nitrogen, moisture and light before sowing, and check they do not differ by treatment.
5. Cross richness with a randomly assigned nitrogen or drought treatment, to see whether the effect holds in each environment.
6. Treat the plot as the unit, harvest species separately each year for several years, and analyse assigned richness, with block as a factor.
7. Report net, complementarity and selection effects alongside raw yields, compare mixtures with the best monoculture as well as the average, and predefine the stability measures (μ/σ over a fixed window, resistance and recovery for any event).

</details>

## Going deeper

### Uncorrelated is not the same as independent

Variances add whenever species' swings are uncorrelated. Independent species are always uncorrelated, but uncorrelated species need not be independent.

### The mechanism debate

Zhao and colleagues (2022) found statistical averaging was the principal mediator in their North American grassland analysis. Luo and colleagues (2025) showed that synchrony patterns can reverse with the length of the time series, so the inferred mechanism depends on how long you watch.

### Assembled is not the same as lost

These experiments compared communities assembled with fewer species; they did not remove species. Real extinctions are not random draws from a pool, so random assembly does not mimic the order in which species are lost in nature. Species loss can still change what an ecosystem does; the experiments show what richness can do in the communities they built.
