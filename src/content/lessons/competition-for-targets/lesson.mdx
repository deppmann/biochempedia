    story: >-
      The question was an old one: the neurotrophic hypothesis said neurons compete for NGF, but
      not how a winner is picked. Working in David Ginty's laboratory, Deppmann and colleagues
      used mice lacking the death gene Bax, whose sympathetic neurons survive with or without NGF.
      Comparing ganglia from Bax-null mice with ganglia from mice lacking both Bax and NGF let
      them list genes that depend on NGF in the living animal. One was TrkA, the NGF receptor

      neurotrophins that can kill through the receptor p75. With the computational neuroscientists
      Stefan Mihalas and Ernst Niebur, the group built a model in which positive feedback amplifies
      small differences and punishment speeds the split, then tested its predictions in mutant
      mice. The 2008 paper gave the field a concrete, testable mechanism for selection, and his
      laboratory at the University of Virginia went on to ask how NGF controls the duration of
      its own signal.

    profileSourceAnchor: "Pittman & Oppenheim, Nature 271:364 (1978); Pittman & Oppenheim, J. Comp. Neurol. 187:425 (1979); Oppenheim, Annu. Rev. Neurosci. 14:453 (1991); Oppenheim, Neuron 17:195 (1996)."

    story: >-
      Oppenheim was a student of Viktor Hamburger, whose counts in the chick had shown that many
      spinal motor neurons die while the limb is being innervated, and that an extra limb reduces
      the loss. The trophic idea suggested that anything helping a motor neuron connect with
      muscle would help it survive. Pittman and Oppenheim tested a related idea by treating chick
      embryos with curare, which blocks the acetylcholine receptors of muscle, during the death
      period. The embryos were paralysed, and fewer motor neurons died, the opposite of what a
      simple "active connections win" rule predicts. That result set a puzzle the field has worked
      on since. Oppenheim's 1991 review in the Annual Review of Neuroscience reviewed cell death in the developing nervous system and is widely cited for the statement that roughly
      half of the neurons in many developing populations die. His 1996 commentary called motor
      neuron survival factors "an embarrassment of riches", because so many different factors
      could keep at least some motor neurons alive.

    blurb: >-
      RNA from whole ganglia of two genotypes is measured on microarrays and compared. Genes lower

      hybridization, immunostaining or culture with and without NGF.
  - name: "Computational modelling of competition"
    blurb: >-
      Neurons and their receptor levels are written as equations and simulated competing for
      NGF that the target makes at a constant rate. Turning feedback or punishment on and off in the model shows which
      features are needed for a clean, fast selection, and yields predictions for mutant mice.
  - name: "Pharmacological paralysis of the chick embryo"
    blurb: >-
      Curare or a related blocker of muscle acetylcholine receptors is applied to the embryo
      during the death period. Counting motor neurons afterwards shows how neuromuscular
      activity affects survival.

**If every sympathetic neuron needs NGF, and the target makes too little for all of them, which neurons get to live?**

Earlier in this unit you met the neurotrophic hypothesis in its short form: "Neurons compete for a limiting amount of neurotrophic factor for survival." Targets such as the eye, the salivary gland and the heart release nerve growth factor (NGF). Sympathetic neurons, which live in small clusters of nerve cells called ganglia, send axons to those targets. Neurons that get enough NGF live; those that do not die through the pro-death gene *Bax*. In many developing populations roughly half the neurons die during normal development. When Margaret Hollyday and Viktor Hamburger grafted an extra limb onto chick embryos in 1976, fewer of the motor neurons supplying that side died: more target, more survivors, though they could not tell whether the limiting resource was a trophic factor or synaptic sites. See [that lesson](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/) for the evidence.

## The contest and its open questions

The hypothesis says there is a contest. It does not say how the contest is decided, or how the outcome sets the fit between a target and its neurons: both the number of neurons that survive and how densely the survivors cover the target have to be matched.

This lesson follows one answer, worked out in the sympathetic neurons of the mouse superior cervical ganglion, a ganglion in the neck that supplies the head, studied in mice and in cultured rat neurons. Three puzzles organize it.

1. **What turns a small, chance difference between neurons into life or death?** A limited supply makes a contest, but it does not pick the winners.
2. **Do winners only outlast losers, or do they push them?**
3. **Does surviving mean innervating?** And, in a different system, why does paralysing a chick embryo save motor neurons?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** The target sets how many; the neurons decide which. A limited supply of NGF makes a contest, and its size sets roughly how many neurons can win. Among neurons that are otherwise alike, a small lead can grow only if a neuron's NGF signal rises *faster than in proportion to* that lead. In the published simulations, neurons separated only when NGF raised both its receptor, TrkA, and how long each TrkA signal lasts; the toy arithmetic below suggests why. In the proposed model, winners then speed the death of losers through BDNF, NT-4 and the receptor p75.

**What the rule covers.** NGF-dependent sympathetic neurons of the mouse superior cervical ganglion during the period of neuron death (late embryo to the first postnatal weeks), and the simulation in this lesson. It has three limits. It does not say where a neuron's first small lead comes from: arriving early, branching more and sitting closer are all candidates, and the rule amplifies a head start rather than replacing one. (A lasting advantage, such as better access to the target, is a different case: it can pull a neuron ahead even with proportional feedback.) "Faster than in proportion" is needed but not enough: the extra gain also has to beat basal synthesis, TrkA made at a steady rate whatever NGF does, which tops every neuron up equally and pulls neurons back together. And it does not cover chick motor neurons, which depend on several factors at once and whose muscle activity may change what the target offers (the last section).

## Why a limited supply is not enough

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Start with two neurons sharing one target. By chance, neuron A carries a little more TrkA, the receptor tyrosine kinase that binds NGF (see [that lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)). It catches 52% of the NGF, and neuron B catches 48%.

Suppose each neuron's catch is simply proportional to its TrkA, and each makes TrkA in proportion to the NGF it catches. Both neurons now make more TrkA, but B grows by the same *fraction* as A.

**Predict.** After many rounds, what share does neuron A hold?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Still 52%. For every 52 parts of NGF that A catches, B catches 48, so A makes 52 parts of new TrkA for every 48 that B makes, and the ratio never moves. Add basal synthesis and both neurons get the same top-up, so the shares drift back toward even. In this setup, where the neurons differ only in TrkA, feedback on TrkA level alone does not make a winner.

This also corrects a tempting shortcut, that fair sharing would make every neuron live or die together. That holds only for identical neurons. Real neurons differ, and under proportional sharing their starting differences persist: the contest never makes the ratio between them grow. Whether each lives still depends on whether its absolute support clears the survival threshold, so a small difference near that threshold can still decide a fate; what proportional sharing cannot do is widen the gap.

</details>

Now let NGF do two things. It raises TrkA, so the neuron catches more, and it lengthens each signal, so every caught molecule counts for longer. A neuron's signal is then roughly the NGF it catches times how long each catch keeps signaling. For simple arithmetic, assume that duration also scales with the catch, and that each neuron makes next round's TrkA in proportion to its signal. Then each round squares the ratio of A's share to B's (52/48 is about 1.08, then 1.17, 1.38, 1.90), so the lead compounds:

| Round | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A's share, proportional | 52% | 52% | 52% | 52% | 52% | 52% |
| A's share, level × duration | 52% | 54% | 58% | 65% | 78% | 93% |

The lead feeds itself. This is the positive feedback you met in the Cell Signaling course, where a signal that raises its own production can make a switch with two stable states (see [the lesson on signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/)). The condition: **a lead can grow only when the reward rises faster than the lead.** The squaring is an assumed update and the "rounds" are a teaching device, not a measured law, a measured time or the published equations.

What about the first axon to arrive? Arriving first may give a neuron its 52%. If the first axon simply took all the NGF, no amplifier would be needed, but nothing in that rule explains how a neuron already receiving some NGF could fall behind, and the target keeps releasing NGF throughout the death period. Arrival order alone cannot explain how a small lead grows instead of fading.

In the simulation below, set the feedback strength and watch the neurons. It is not the two-neuron table: it tracks TrkA level only, and its neurons differ slightly in access to the target. It reaches "faster than in proportion" by a second route: each neuron's TrkA synthesis responds to its share of NGF in a switch-like way, so a neuron just above an even share gains much more than one just below. Turn feedback off and they stay together.

## Who needs NGF? The *Bax* trick

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

To find what NGF does to a neuron in a living animal, Deppmann, Ginty and colleagues needed ganglia that lack NGF yet still have neurons. Without NGF the neurons die, so they used mice lacking *Bax*, whose sympathetic neurons survive with or without NGF. Comparing ganglia from *Bax*-null mice with ganglia from mice lacking both *Bax* and *NGF* gave a list of NGF-dependent genes: transcription factors, synaptic genes, genes for metabolism and cell size, and genes for axon growth.

The genotypes this lesson uses ("-null" means the gene is deleted), with *Bax*-null as the reference:

| Genotype | Sympathetic neurons | TrkA | Target innervation at birth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *NGF* removed, *Bax* present | most die | hard to measure: neuron loss confounds it | severely reduced, but neuron loss and axon growth cannot be told apart |
| *Bax*-null | survive | maintained by NGF (the reference) | the reference |
| *Bax*/*NGF* double null | survive | falls | missing from some targets (salivary glands), greatly reduced in others (heart), near normal in a few (trachea) |

Now ask which gene on such a list could build the loop the last section needs: one whose product helps a neuron catch more NGF. One was the receptor itself. NGF raises and maintains TrkA: in newborn ganglia and in cultured neurons, TrkA fell without NGF and rose with it. In culture, 24 or 48 hours without NGF also greatly reduced signaling downstream of TrkA, read out as phosphorylated ERK (a MAPK) and AKT, while neurons deprived for only 12 hours could still sustain signaling when NGF returned.

So a neuron that catches NGF makes more of the receptor that catches it. That is the first loop, and, by the two-neuron arithmetic, only half of the answer. Two other genes on the list matter later: *BDNF* and *NT-4*.

## NGF lengthens its own signal

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

NGF also controls how long its own signal lasts. Much NGF signaling travels from axon tip to cell body inside endosomes (the subject of [that lesson](/lessons/retrograde-signal/)). In 2014 Deppmann's laboratory, with Dong Suo as first author, reported that NGF induces Coronin-1, a protein that associates with these signaling endosomes, prolongs their signaling and is required for normal competition in the mouse. It is one mechanism; there may be others.

With the computational neuroscientists Stefan Mihalas and Ernst Niebur, the group simulated neurons competing for NGF that the target makes at a constant rate. They compared four conditions: no feedback, feedback on TrkA level alone, feedback on signal duration alone, and feedback on both.

**Predict.** Which of the four conditions separated the neurons into a winning group and a losing group?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only feedback on both; with either one alone, competition failed. The two-neuron arithmetic gives a toy reason. Level alone, with capture proportional to TrkA, keeps every ratio fixed. Duration alone makes the leader's signal larger once, but nothing feeds that back into the next catch, so the shares stay put. Only together do they close a loop with a step that rises faster than the lead. This is the toy model's reason, not the published equations.

</details>

The lab described the sequence as four stages.

1. **Naive.** A young neuron responds poorly to NGF.
2. **Sensitization.** A neuron that receives enough NGF crosses a threshold: from then on, NGF makes it more sensitive to NGF.
3. **Competitive advantage.** It raises TrkA and strengthens the signaling downstream of it.
4. **Selection.** It wins the supply, and neighbours that stayed behind lose.

## Punishment: winners push losers

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

Feedback explains how a winner pulls ahead. Does it need help? Before looking at the candidates, list what a punishment signal would have to do. It should come from strongly supported neurons, kill weak ones, spare strong ones, and start only after TrkA has risen.

The candidates were already on the list of NGF-dependent genes. Strongly supported sympathetic neurons make BDNF and NT-4, two other neurotrophins (members of NGF's protein family). Sympathetic neurons carry little of their receptor TrkB, but they do carry p75, a member of the tumour necrosis factor receptor family (see [that lesson](/lessons/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases/) and [the course's lesson on the receptors](/lessons/trk-and-p75/)). In this setting, p75 can promote death. Because BDNF and NT-4 are themselves NGF-dependent genes, a neuron makes them mainly when it is well supported, which fits the requirement that punishment come from strong neurons, though it does not by itself show that they appear only after TrkA has risen.

**Predict.** BDNF and NT-4 released by strong neurons can act on neighbouring cell bodies, including other strong neurons. Why don't they kill the winners?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A neuron with strong NGF-TrkA signaling is protected; one with weak signaling is not. One likely reason is that strong TrkA signaling keeps survival pathways, such as AKT, high enough to outweigh a death signal from p75, while a weakly supported neuron has no such cushion. That protection is what makes the punishment selective.

</details>

The lab tested three predictions in mouse superior cervical ganglia:

1. Competing neurons differ in TrkA level (tested in *Bax*-null ganglia).
2. Neurons with low TrkA show high p75 death signaling, read out by phosphorylated c-Jun, a transcription factor that JNK, a stress kinase downstream of p75, helps activate on the way to death.
3. Removing punishment delays but does not prevent the loss (tested in p75-null mice).

**Predict.** Sketch sympathetic neuron number against age, from before birth through the first weeks, for wild-type and p75-null ganglia.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In the model, p75 hastens the death of neurons that are already losing. It does not hand out NGF, and a starved neuron dies without it, through TrkA loss and *Bax*. So remove p75 and the winners still take the limited NGF, and the losers still die of trophic deprivation, only more slowly.

Both curves start at similar numbers before birth. The wild-type curve falls steeply in the first postnatal days. The p75-null curve falls more slowly, so it has more neurons than wild-type in the first days after birth.

</details>

All three predictions held, including the delay. In the simulations, punishment hastened the killing of weak neurons more than ten-fold, a result of the model; without it weak neurons lingered.

The endpoint is less settled, because speeding the loss does not by itself fix where the count ends; that takes counts at later ages, or a model that specifies it. In the 2008 study the p75-null ganglion reached the wild-type number by six months. An earlier study from Freda Miller's laboratory also reported delayed sympathetic neuron death in p75-null mice, but counted an excess that lasted into adulthood. So both found the loss delayed; they differ at the latest ages counted.

### Design problem

How could you test the p75-null prediction you just sketched?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Count neurons in serial sections of the superior cervical ganglion at several ages in both genotypes. A curve that never falls would argue that p75 is required for death. A curve that falls just as fast would fail to support a role for punishment through p75. A curve that falls more slowly and then meets the wild-type number supports the model. One that stays above it at every adult age you count would suggest that p75 also changes the final number; following the curve to later ages tells a lasting change from a longer delay.

</details>

## Systems matching: NGF also sets how much a target gets

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

Survival is one part of tuning a developing circuit. The broader task is **systems matching**: each target gets a fitting number of neurons, amount of innervation, synapses and dendrite shapes. NGF acts on several of these.

Look back at the last column of the genotype table. With *Bax* gone, neurons survive without NGF, yet innervation at birth ranges from missing to near normal depending on the target. So survival and innervation are separable, and NGF is needed for final innervation of some targets, such as the salivary glands and, in part, the heart, but not all, such as the trachea. Within a target, NGF promotes axon growth and branching, so the factor whose supply sets how many neurons live also helps decide how much of many targets each survivor covers.

## Activity: a different variable in a different system

Now a different system and a different variable. In work published in 1978 and 1979, Robert Pittman and Ronald Oppenheim treated chick embryos with curare, a drug that blocks the acetylcholine receptors of muscle, during the period of motor neuron death. The muscles stopped responding to their nerves.

**Predict.** If activity helps an axon win, as it does in many later forms of refinement, what should silencing the connection do to motor neuron death?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

You might expect more death. Instead, fewer motor neurons died. That is why the result looked like a contradiction: silencing the connection saved neurons.

</details>

The usual explanations are interpretations, so hold them loosely. The most common one is that paralysed muscle may allow more nerve branching and more synapses, giving each motor neuron more access to trophic support. Chick motor neurons do not depend on NGF; they draw on several factors at once, which Oppenheim called "an embarrassment of riches."

In other systems, activity helps a winner: active synapses can release more BDNF, which layers activity-dependent refinement on top of trophic competition. The two kinds of result need not conflict, because activity in the muscle and activity in the competing neurons are different variables.

## Putting it together

The first two puzzles share the rule's answer: the target sets how many; the neurons decide which, through feedback on TrkA and its signal and, in the proposed model, punishment through p75. The third has two parts. NGF also shapes how much of many targets each survivor covers. And in chick motor neurons, which draw on several factors, muscle activity is a separate variable that needs its own account.

## Going deeper

### The caricature, written out

A word-equation version of the feedback loop is

> **change in TrkA = basal synthesis + k × (NGF signal) − decay × TrkA**

where the NGF signal rises with both NGF and TrkA. Written this way it leaves out the ingredient that makes it a competition: a shared, finite supply, so that one neuron's gain is another's loss. With NGF held fixed outside every cell, nothing links one neuron's fate to another's: with a signal simply proportional to TrkA, identical neurons all settle toward the same TrkA level (or, with strong enough feedback, all grow without limit). Make the NGF signal each neuron's *share* of a fixed supply and you have a contest, but with shares proportional to TrkA the ratios freeze, as in the two-neuron table.

### Activity, muscle factors and axon pruning

An older idea, that inactive muscle makes more of the factors motor neurons need, has had little support from tests with extracts of paralysed muscle. In rodent sympathetic neurons, axon pruning uses a similar BDNF and p75 punishment logic, acting on losing axons rather than on cell bodies (see [that lesson](/lessons/axon-pruning/)).
