**If every sympathetic neuron needs NGF, and the target makes too little for all of them, which neurons get to live?**

Earlier in this unit you met the neurotrophic hypothesis in the form Chris Deppmann teaches it: "Neurons compete for a limiting amount of neurotrophic factor for survival." Targets such as the eye, the salivary gland and the heart release nerve growth factor (NGF). Sympathetic neurons, which live in small clusters of nerve cells called ganglia, send axons to those targets. Neurons that get enough NGF live; those that do not die through the pro-death gene *Bax*. See [that lesson](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/) for the evidence.

The hypothesis says there is a contest. It does not say how the contest is decided. This lesson follows one answer, worked out in the sympathetic neurons of the mouse superior cervical ganglion, a ganglion in the neck that supplies the head. Keep that scope in mind: this is one cell type, studied in mice and in cultured rat neurons.

## The Competition Model: a limited supply means a contest

The target-size evidence came first, from the chick. In 1976 Margaret Hollyday and Viktor Hamburger grafted an extra limb onto chick embryos, and fewer of the motor neurons supplying that side died. More target, more survivors, which fits competition for a limited resource, though the authors could not tell whether the resource was a trophic factor or synaptic sites.

A limiting supply alone does not pick winners. Suppose NGF were shared evenly. Every neuron would get the same fraction, and they would all live or all die together. Suppose instead the first axon to arrive took everything. The target keeps releasing NGF throughout the death period, and nothing in a first-come rule explains how a neuron already receiving some NGF could fall behind. Something has to turn a small, chance difference into a large one.

That is the job of positive feedback. In the Cell Signaling course you saw that a loop in which a signal raises its own production can make a switch with two stable states (see [that lesson](/lessons/signaling-circuits/)). Here is a caricature of the idea:

> **change in TrkA = basal synthesis + k × (NGF signal) − decay × TrkA, where NGF signal rises with both NGF and TrkA**

If TrkA, the receptor tyrosine kinase that binds NGF (see [that lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)), rises with its own signal, a neuron with a little more TrkA collects more NGF, makes more TrkA, and pulls further ahead. This caricature tracks TrkA level only. In the published model that was not enough: NGF also had to lengthen its own signal, as the next section shows.

## How is the developing circuit tuned? NGF raises its own sensitivity

To find what NGF does to a neuron in a living animal, Deppmann, Ginty and colleagues needed ganglia that lack NGF yet still have neurons. Without NGF the neurons die, so they used mice lacking *Bax*. In a *Bax*-null mouse, sympathetic neurons survive with or without NGF. Comparing ganglia from *Bax*-null mice with ganglia from mice lacking both *Bax* and *NGF* gave a list of "NGF Dependent Genes": transcription factors, synaptic genes, genes for metabolism and cell size, and genes for axon growth.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/competition-for-targets/bax-null-ngf-null-design.webp" width={1200} height={643} alt="AI-generated illustration: three ganglion clusters with axons to a target. In a normal cluster some neurons are dying; with Bax removed all neurons survive even when NGF is also removed; comparing the last two gives a list of NGF Dependent Genes." caption="Without Bax, sympathetic neurons survive with or without NGF. Comparing Bax-null ganglia with Bax and NGF double-null ganglia gave a list of NGF-dependent genes." />

One gene on the list was the receptor itself. A lecture slide puts it strongly, "NGF is Necessary and Sufficient for TrkA Expression." What the data show is that NGF raises and maintains TrkA: in newborn ganglia and in dissociated cultures, TrkA fell without NGF and rose with it. In culture, TrkA and its downstream signaling, measured by phosphorylated ERK (a MAPK) and AKT, two kinases downstream of TrkA, were greatly reduced after 24 or 48 hours without NGF. Neurons deprived for only 12 hours could still sustain signaling when NGF returned.

The lab called this "NGF Transcriptional Regulation of NGF Sensitivity" and described four stages:

1. **Naive.** A young neuron responds poorly to NGF.
2. **Sensitization.** A neuron that receives enough NGF crosses a threshold.
3. **Competitive Advantage.** It raises TrkA and strengthens the signaling downstream of it.
4. **Selection.** It wins the supply, and neighbours that stayed behind lose.

NGF also controls how long its own signal lasts. With the computational neuroscientists Stefan Mihalas and Ernst Niebur, the group simulated neurons competing for NGF that the target makes at a constant rate. They compared four conditions: no feedback, feedback on TrkA level alone, feedback on signal duration alone, and feedback on both. Only with both kinds of feedback did the neurons separate into a winning and a losing group; with either one alone, competition failed.

How does NGF lengthen its signal? Much NGF signaling travels from axon tip to cell body inside endosomes (the subject of [that lesson](/lessons/retrograde-signal/)). In 2014 Deppmann's laboratory, with Dong Suo as first author, reported that NGF induces Coronin-1, a protein that associates with these signaling endosomes, prolongs their signaling, and is required for normal competition in the mouse. It is one mechanism; there may be others.

## Punishment Signaling: winners push losers

Feedback explains how a winner pulls ahead. Does it need help? In his lectures Deppmann lists the qualities a punishment signal should have. It should come from strongly supported neurons, kill weak ones, spare strong ones, and start only after TrkA has risen.

The candidates were already on the list of NGF-dependent genes. Strongly supported sympathetic neurons make BDNF and NT-4, two other neurotrophins. Sympathetic neurons carry little of their receptor TrkB, but they do carry p75, a member of the tumour necrosis factor receptor family (see [that lesson](/lessons/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases/) and [the course's lesson on the receptors](/lessons/trk-and-p75/)). In this setting, p75 can promote death. A neuron with strong NGF-TrkA signaling is protected; one with weak signaling is not.

The lab tested three predictions in mouse superior cervical ganglia:

1. Competing neurons differ in TrkA level (tested in *Bax*-null ganglia).
2. Neurons with low TrkA show high p75 death signaling, read out by phosphorylated c-Jun.
3. Removing punishment delays but does not prevent the loss (tested in p75-null mice).

All three held, and the summary slide reads "A Model for Neuronal Competition." An earlier study from the laboratory of Freda Miller had also reported delayed sympathetic neuron death in p75-null mice.

"What is the benefit of punishment to the system?" In the simulations, punishment made the split faster: it hastened the killing of weak neurons more than ten-fold. Without it, weak neurons lingered.

### Design problem

If punishment through p75 is part of competition, what happens to sympathetic neuron numbers over the death period in a p75-null mouse? Sketch neuron number against age for wild-type and p75-null ganglia. How could you test this?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with what p75 does in the model. It hastens the death of neurons that are already losing. It does not hand out NGF, and it is not needed for a starved neuron to die, since that runs through TrkA loss and *Bax*.

So remove p75 and the winners still take the limited NGF, and the losers still die of trophic deprivation, only more slowly without the extra push. Your sketch should show both curves starting at similar numbers before birth. The wild-type curve falls steeply in the first postnatal days. The p75-null curve falls more slowly, so it has more neurons than wild-type in the first days after birth. In the 2008 study it reached the wild-type number by six months, although an earlier study counted an excess that lasted into adulthood.

To test it, count neurons in serial sections of the superior cervical ganglion at several ages in both genotypes. A curve that never falls would argue that p75 is required for death. A curve that falls just as fast would fail to support a role for punishment through p75.

</details>

## Activity Modulates Trophic Support

Now a result that seemed to point the other way. In work published in 1978 and 1979, Robert Pittman and Ronald Oppenheim treated chick embryos with curare, a drug that blocks the acetylcholine receptors of muscle, during the period of motor neuron death. The muscles stopped responding to their nerves, and fewer motor neurons died.

Why was that a contradiction? If activity helps an axon win, as it does in many later forms of refinement, then silencing the connection should increase death. Instead it saved neurons.

The usual explanations are interpretations, so hold them loosely. Paralysed muscle may allow more nerve branching and more synapses, giving each motor neuron more access to trophic support. An older idea, that inactive muscle makes more of the factors motor neurons need, was not supported when extracts of paralysed muscle were tested. Note that chick motor neurons do not depend on NGF. They draw on several factors at once, which Oppenheim called "an embarrassment of riches."

In other systems, activity helps a winner. Active synapses can release more BDNF, which layers activity-dependent refinement on top of trophic competition. In rodent sympathetic neurons, axon pruning uses a similar BDNF and p75 punishment logic, acting on losing axons rather than on cell bodies (see [that lesson](/lessons/axon-pruning/)). The two kinds of result need not conflict: activity in the muscle and activity in the competing neurons are different variables.

## Systems matching: NGF also sets how much a target gets

"How is the developing circuit tuned?" Survival is one answer. Deppmann calls the broader task "Systems matching": each target gets a fitting number of neurons, amount of innervation, synapses and dendrite shapes. NGF acts on several of these. In mice lacking both *Bax* and *NGF*, neurons survive, but sympathetic innervation at birth is missing from some targets, such as the salivary glands, and greatly reduced in others, such as the heart, while a few, such as the trachea, are near normal: "NGF Controls Final Target Innervation." In his teaching he puts it informally: losing axons "get more aggressive as they find a pot of NGF." So the same factor that picks which neurons live also shapes how densely the survivors cover their target.
