## The big idea

Most of the time we hear without listening. Ask a child to sit still for two minutes, eyes closed, and count the sounds, and the backyard fills up: a bird, a car, wind in a tree, a far-off dog.

Ears catch sound and the brain makes sense of it. Virginia’s kindergarten standard pairs ears with hearing and gives four words for sounds: loud, soft, high and low. Having two ears, one on each side of the head, helps the brain tell which way a sound came from.

Many animals have large outer ears they can turn, such as cats and horses. These ears gather sound like funnels. Cupping hands behind the ears gives a child a bigger funnel and lets them feel the difference. In grade 1, Virginia asks children to link body parts with what they do. In grade 3, it calls features like these **adaptations**: body parts or behaviours that help a living thing survive where it lives.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time, close enough to touch. Choose a spot you have permission to use, away from roads, driveways, ponds and streams. With a class, use buddy pairs and count heads before and after.
- Cup hands only for quiet or everyday sounds, such as a fan, wind or birdsong, and never toward a road. If you would have to raise your voice to talk to someone an arm’s length away, it is too loud: stop cupping and move away. If something loud starts, like a mower, siren or leaf blower, stop and move away or go inside. Never shout, whistle or clap near anyone’s ear.
- Nothing goes into the ear: no fingers, sticks, cotton swabs or earbuds.
- Before you sit, look for ant mounds, bees on clover and poison ivy ("leaves of three, let it be"). Dress for the weather and the sun.
- Use an EPA-registered insect repellent, following its label, especially near grass or woods. After sitting in grass or near woods, check skin and clothes for ticks. Wash hands with soap and water if your child touched soil.
- Children who are deaf or hard of hearing can map what they see move or feel shake, such as a hand on a window when a truck passes.
- Watch only animals you know, with you beside your child. Never approach a stranger’s or stray animal, never touch a pet that is eating or sleeping, and never walk behind a horse. Wash hands after touching an animal.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Pick a spot.** Find a place to sit outside, such as a yard, a park bench or the schoolyard. On a cold or rainy day, sit near a window opened no more than 4 inches, with chairs and anything to climb on moved away from it. A screen will not stop a child falling out.
2. **Predict.** Ask, "How many different sounds do you think we will hear in two minutes?" Write the number down.
3. **Sit and listen.** Set a timer for two minutes. Your child closes their eyes and holds up one finger for each new sound. Expect anything from a few sounds to a dozen or more, depending on the place and the time of day.
4. **Make a sound map.** Put a dot in the middle of the paper and label it "me." Your child draws each sound in the direction it came from. Near sounds go close to the dot and far sounds go near the edge. Loud sounds are drawn big, soft sounds small. Add an arrow pointing up for a high sound and down for a low one.
5. **Living or not.** Circle the sounds made by living things, such as birds, insects, dogs and people. Talk about the tricky ones. Rustling leaves come from a tree, but the wind makes them move.
6. **Cupped-hand ears.** Find one steady sound, such as an air conditioner, a fan or wind in the trees. Ask, "What will happen if you cup your hands behind your ears?" Your child faces the sound and cups both hands behind the ears, fingers together. Most children hear it louder.
7. **Turn around.** Ask your child to predict first. Then, keeping the cupped hands in place, they turn their back to the sound. The sound usually gets fainter, because the funnels now point the wrong way.
8. **Watch animal ears (optional).** If you have a cat, dog or horse that your child knows well, stay beside your child and watch its ears when someone quietly opens a cupboard nearby. Do not startle the animal, and never walk behind a horse. Do not use a stranger’s or stray animal. Or look at pictures of animals with large ears and talk about what those ears might help with.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "How many sounds do you think we will hear? Was it more or fewer?"
- "Is that sound near or far? How can you tell without looking?"
- "I wonder if that loud sound is close. What else could make it loud?"
- "What do you think will happen when you cup your hands behind your ears?"
- "What will happen if you turn your back to the sound?"
- "What do you think a cat’s big ears help it do?"

## Why it works

Sound is a vibration that travels through air. The part of the ear you can see is called the **pinna**. It funnels sound into the ear canal. Its shape boosts sounds from the front a little and dims sounds from behind. Cupped hands make a bigger funnel facing forward, so a sound in front seems louder and a sound behind seems fainter.

The brain finds direction partly by comparing the two ears. A sound from the left reaches the left ear a tiny bit sooner and a little louder than the right ear.

Cats have small muscles that let each pinna rotate up to about 180 degrees. Horses can point each ear in a different direction. Large, movable ears are a physical adaptation, and turning them toward a sound is a behaviour. Features like these build up in a population over many generations. A single animal does not grow bigger ears because it needs them.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Listen for one minute instead of two, and skip the arrows for high and low. Your child can draw each sound as a small picture and tell you whether it was loud or soft. For the cupped-hand test, ask only "louder or quieter?" Then let them be the one to say "Turn around" to you.

### Ages 8 to 10
Do the sound map at two places or two times of day, then compare in a table: number of sounds, living, nonliving, loudest, highest. Make a bar graph of living and nonliving sounds. For a fair test of cupped hands, use the same sound, the same spot and three tries each way, and rate loudness from 1 to 5. Ask what other things could change the result.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If your child cannot sit still for two minutes, start with 30 seconds and build up. If a busy road drowns out everything else, map it from where you sit (do not move closer to the road), then try again at a quieter time or place. If the cupped hands seem to make no difference, pick a softer, steadier sound and make sure the fingers are closed.
