## Explain: alleles can hide but do not disappear

<WordBank terms={['allele', 'dominant', 'recessive', 'genotype', 'phenotype', 'Punnett square', 'probability', 'ratio']} />

A pea flower shows one color, but each body cell of the plant carries two copies of the flower-color gene. An **allele** is one version of a gene. A pea plant gets one allele of each gene from each parent.

Pea plants have a purple allele and a white allele for flower color. Scientists write them as letters: P for purple and p for white.

- A **dominant** allele shows its trait even when there is only one copy. It gets a capital letter.
- A **recessive** allele shows its trait only when both copies are recessive. It gets a small letter.

When a dominant allele is there, it masks the recessive one. Masked means hidden from view, not destroyed. The p allele is still there, and it can still be passed on.

Your **genotype** is the pair of alleles you carry, such as Pp. Your **phenotype** is the trait that shows, such as purple flowers.

| Genotype | Alleles | Phenotype |
|---|---|---|
| PP | two same alleles (homozygous) | purple |
| Pp | two different alleles (heterozygous) | purple |
| pp | two same alleles (homozygous) | white |

A purple plant could be PP or Pp, so its looks do not tell you its genotype.

Sex cells carry only one allele of each pair, as you saw in [that lesson](/lessons/reproduction-and-meiosis/). A PP plant can only pass on P. A pp plant can only pass on p. A Pp plant passes on P or p, with a 50 percent chance for each.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A dominant allele can hide a recessive allele, but the recessive allele is still carried and can be passed to offspring.</Support>

## Explain: a Punnett square shows the chances

A **Punnett square** is a grid that shows every way the sex cells of two parents can join. It is a model that predicts offspring.

Here is how to build one for two Pp parents.

1. Write one parent’s sex cells, P and p, across the top.
2. Write the other parent’s sex cells, P and p, down the side.
3. In each box, put the letter from the top with the letter from the side.

| | P | p |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | PP | Pp |
| **p** | Pp | pp |

**Probability** is the chance that something will happen. Each box has the same chance, 1 in 4, because 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. A **ratio** compares two numbers, such as 3 purple to 1 white. You can write each prediction three ways.

| What you predict | As fractions | As percents | As a ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genotypes | 1/4 PP, 2/4 Pp, 1/4 pp | 25, 50, 25 | 1 PP : 2 Pp : 1 pp |
| Phenotypes | 3/4 purple, 1/4 white | 75, 25 | 3 purple : 1 white |

Now the hidden white makes sense. Each parent is Pp. If both pass on p, the offspring is pp and blooms white. That has a 1 in 4 chance. This prediction needs both parents to be Pp. If one parent is PP, every offspring gets at least one P.

A Punnett square gives the chance for each offspring, not an exact count. Four seeds from two Pp parents might all bloom purple. The chance of that is about 32 percent. Mendel’s rules, and squares like this one, explain how many inherited traits are passed on. Later courses add other patterns.

## Elaborate: Mendel’s pea counts

Mendel crossed pea plants that differed in one trait. Their first offspring all showed the dominant trait. He let those hybrid plants fertilize themselves and counted the next generation.

| Trait (dominant vs recessive) | What was counted | Dominant (number) | Recessive (number) | Ratio (dominant ÷ recessive) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seed shape: round vs wrinkled | seeds | 5,474 | 1,850 | 2.96 : 1 |
| Seed color: yellow vs green | seeds | 6,022 | 2,001 | ? |
| Flower color: purple vs white | plants | 705 | 224 | ? |
| Pod shape: full vs pinched | plants | 882 | 299 | 2.95 : 1 |
| Unripe pod color: green vs yellow | plants | 428 | 152 | 2.82 : 1 |
| Flower position: along the stem vs at the tip | plants | 651 | 207 | 3.14 : 1 |
| Stem length: tall vs short | plants | 787 | 277 | 2.84 : 1 |

### Design problem

Plan a model with beads that tests this question: does the number of offspring change how close a cross comes to 3 : 1?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Make two paper bags, one for each Pp parent. Put 20 purple beads (P) and 20 white beads (p) in each bag. All beads are the same size, about 1 centimetre across, so you cannot tell them apart by feel.

- **Make one offspring:** without looking, draw one bead from each bag. Record the pair, then put both beads back and shake the bags.
- **Independent variable:** the number of offspring you make: 4, 20 or 100.
- **Dependent variable:** the percent of offspring that are pp (white).
- **Constants:** the same bags, the same 20 and 20 beads, and putting beads back after every draw.
- **Control:** a PP bag (40 purple beads) paired with a Pp bag. It should never give a pp offspring, which checks that your method works.
- **Repeated trials:** make each group size 5 times and find the mean percent of white.
- **Safety:** pick up dropped beads so no one slips, and keep beads away from your mouth.

If the 100-offspring groups land closer to 25 percent than the 4-offspring groups, sample size matters. The bead model is not a real plant: it has one gene and no weather, soil or lost seeds.

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The white was hiding in the parents’ genotypes. Both purple parents were Pp, so each carried a masked p allele. Each offspring had a 1 in 4 chance of getting p from both parents, and those pp plants bloomed white. A Punnett square predicts that chance, but not the exact count in a small group.
