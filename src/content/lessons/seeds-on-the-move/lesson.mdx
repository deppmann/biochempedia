## The big idea

Pull burrs off a dog or a pair of socks after a walk and you have already seen a plant spreading its seeds. Your child probably has not thought about it that way yet.

A plant stays rooted in one place, but its seeds can move. Moving seeds away from the parent plant is called **seed dispersal**. Many seeds have shapes that suit one way of travelling. Winged and fluffy seeds ride the wind. Seeds with hooks catch on fur and clothes. Seeds inside juicy fruits are eaten by animals and dropped somewhere else. Some seeds float on water. Seeds that land away from the parent often have more room and light, and they can reach new places.

Virginia’s grade 2 standard says seeds are dispersed by wind, water and animals, including humans. The sock is your child’s proof that people carry seeds too.

The best time is late summer and fall, when weeds are full of ripe seeds. Some maples drop their keys in spring instead.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay within arm’s reach outdoors. Choose the patch yourself: a sunny weedy edge of your own yard, or a park where picking is allowed, away from roads, woods and leaf litter. Keep acorns and small seeds away from children under 4.
- Never eat wild berries or seeds. Pokeweed berries, common in Virginia, are poisonous and the plant can irritate skin. Jimsonweed seeds and castor bean seeds are highly poisonous. Collect only seeds you know, and just look at the rest. If a child is choking or struggling to breathe, call 911. If anyone swallows berries or seeds, call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222 (free, any hour).
- Ticks and chiggers live in tall grass and brush. Wear long pants tucked into socks and closed shoes, and stay on the edge of the patch. Before going indoors, check the sock and your child’s skin, scalp, hair and clothes for ticks, shower within two hours, and tumble the dry clothes (not the seed sock) in a hot dryer for 10 minutes, longer if they are damp. An EPA-registered repellent also helps; an adult chooses and applies it by its label. For taking a tick off and what to watch for afterwards, see the CDC page ‘After a tick bite’.
- Poison ivy grows across Virginia. Teach "leaves of three, let it be," and stay out of patches you cannot identify. The oil can stay on shoes, socks and clothes, so wash them in hot water with detergent, and wash skin with soap and water if you think anyone touched it.
- Late-summer weeds attract bees and wasps, and snakes rest in tall grass. Look before you step, keep hands out of the grass and from under logs, and look at any snake without touching it (Virginia has venomous copperheads). If anyone is bitten or has trouble breathing after a sting, get medical help right away.
- Some burrs scratch. You pull them off the sock with tweezers, not bare fingers, and point tweezers and scissors away from faces.
- Seeds from the sock can sprout into weeds, and some, such as pokeweed and jimsonweed, are poisonous. Keep the grow tray where children and pets cannot get to it, and nobody tastes the sprouts. When you are done, seal the sock, sprouts and soil in a bag and put it in the trash, not the garden or compost.
- Keep the bowl of water away from toddlers, and empty it when you finish. Wash hands with soap after handling seeds and plants.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Predict.** Before you go out, ask, "Plants can’t walk. How do you think their seeds get to new places?" Write down every idea.
2. **Sock walk.** Pull the old sock over one shoe. Walk slowly back and forth along the edge of an unmowed, weedy patch for 3 to 5 minutes. Brush through the plants without trampling deep into them.
3. **Collect what you see.** On the way back, look for other seeds without pulling up plants: maple keys, dandelion fluff, acorns, and the winged seeds that fall out of open pine cones. Put a few in the bag. Point out berries, but leave them on the plant.
4. **Check the sock.** Indoors, lay the sock on paper and look with the hand lens. You use tweezers to pull off what stuck, and look for ticks as you go. Count the seeds. Look at the hooks, spines or barbs that held on.
5. **Sort by travel.** Make four piles: flies, floats, sticks, gets eaten. Let your child decide where each seed goes and explain why. Some seeds fit two piles, and that is fine.
6. **Float test.** Ask your child to predict which seeds will float. Drop one of each kind into the bowl of water. Check again after 10 minutes.
7. **Helicopter drop.** Stand on the floor and hold a whole maple key as high as you can reach. Drop it and time the fall. Snap the wing off another key and drop the seed part from the same height. Do three drops of each. If you have no maple keys, cut a paper copy: a strip about 10 cm by 3 cm with a paper clip on one end as the seed.
8. **Grow the sock (optional).** Put the sock in a shallow tray, wet it, and keep it damp in a sunny window. In 1 to 3 weeks, some of the seeds may sprout. You check the tray, nobody tastes the sprouts, and when you are done you seal the sock, sprouts and any soil in a bag and put it in the trash, not the garden.

With a class, give each group of four one sock and walk together, then compare piles across groups.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder how many seeds will be stuck to the sock. What’s your guess?"
- "What do you think these little hooks are for?"
- "Which seeds do you think will float? Why?"
- "What do you think will happen if we drop the key without its wing?"
- "If every seed fell straight down under its tree, what would happen to all those seedlings?"
- "How do you think seeds got onto the sock that was on your shoe? Could people carry seeds too?"

## Why it works

Seedlings that sprout right under the parent compete with it for light, water and soil. Seeds that travel can reach open ground, and some land somewhere better.

Each shape works in a different way. A maple key spins as it falls, and the spinning wing slows its drop, so a breeze has more time to push it sideways. Dandelion fluff acts as a tiny parachute. Burdock and beggar’s ticks have hooks or barbs that cling to fur and cloth. The hooks on burdock burrs inspired the hook-and-loop fastener. Cherries and other fleshy fruits are eaten by birds, and the hard seeds often pass through unharmed. Squirrels bury acorns and forget some. Coconuts and the seeds of many streamside plants float.

Some plants scatter their own seeds. Jewelweed pods, common in damp Virginia woods, burst open when touched. Scientists usually describe these shapes as **adaptations**, traits that became common over many generations because they helped plants spread.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Skip the timing and float test. Do the sock walk, then sort into just two piles: "sticks to things" and "blows in the wind." Your child can tape one of each onto the observation sheet and draw how it travels. Then drop a maple key and watch it spin, without timing.

### Ages 8 to 10
Time three drops of each seed and work out the average. Then make a paper seed that falls as slowly as possible: change one thing at a time, such as wing length or the number of paper clips. For a wind test, you set a guarded fan on low on a table and keep fingers away from it. Your child drops each design 50 cm in front of the fan and measures in centimetres how far it lands.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

A sock with nothing on it usually means the plants were mowed or the seeds are not ripe yet. Try a taller, weedier edge a few weeks later, or look along fences and path edges. If you find no maple keys, use the paper copy, or try dandelion fluff in spring and pine cones in fall.
