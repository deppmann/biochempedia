    story: >-
      Del Castillo worked with Katz on the frog junction.
      Their 1954 paper on the quantal components of the endplate potential set out an
      experiment that became the model for quantal analysis. By lowering calcium and adding magnesium to
      the bathing solution, they reduced release until a single nerve impulse often produced
      no response at all. On the trials that did respond, the amplitudes did not spread
      smoothly. They clustered at the size of one spontaneous miniature, at twice that, and at
      three times that. Del Castillo and Katz treated each nerve impulse as a chance event at
      many independent release opportunities and found that the counts of failures and of
      steps fitted a Poisson distribution. That model let them estimate the mean number of
      units released by a single impulse, the quantal content, in two independent ways.
      Later work by Katz and others tied each unit to the contents of a single synaptic
      vesicle, and the same arithmetic is still used to locate changes at synapses throughout
      the nervous system.

    story: >-
      Calcium was known to be needed for transmitter release, and magnesium to oppose it, but
      the shape of that dependence was the next question. F. A. Dodge and Rami Rahamimoff
      answered it at the frog neuromuscular junction with intracellular recording. They changed
      the external calcium concentration step by step, recorded the endplate potential at each
      level, and plotted amplitude against calcium on logarithmic axes. At low calcium the
      points fell on a line with a slope of 3.78, with a standard deviation of 0.2, across 28
      experiments. As calcium approached its normal level the slope became shallower. They
      titled the 1967 paper as an account of co-operative action of calcium ions, and a slope
      near four became the classic sign that release depends steeply on calcium. The result
      made release a sensitive dial: small changes in calcium entry give large changes in
      transmitter output, which is why later work on presynaptic calcium channels and calcium
      sensors mattered so much for understanding synapses.

    blurb: >-
      A sharp glass microelectrode inside a muscle fibre near the nerve terminal records the
      endplate potential and the spontaneous miniatures. Their amplitudes, frequency and
      distance-dependence are the raw data of quantal analysis.
  - name: "Quantal analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Compare evoked responses with miniatures under low release. Mean quantal content comes
      from the ratio of mean amplitudes or, under a Poisson model, from the failure rate; a
      change in quantal content usually points before the synapse, while a change in miniature size can come from receptors after it or from vesicle filling before it.
  - name: "Electron microscopy with an extracellular tracer"
    blurb: >-
      Soak a stimulated terminal in horseradish peroxidase, fix it at timed points and find the
      tracer in internal membranes and vesicles. Where the tracer goes, and when, shows how
      vesicle membrane is retrieved and reused.
  - name: "Passive transfer of patient antibodies"
    blurb: >-
      Inject an immunoglobulin fraction from patient serum into animals, then repeat the
      recordings and count receptors. If the disease signs transfer, a circulating factor is
      enough to cause them in that animal.

    choices:
      - "Calcium entry through voltage-gated calcium channels"
      - "Diffusion of acetylcholine across the synaptic cleft"
      - "Hydrolysis of acetylcholine by acetylcholinesterase"
      - "Opening of nicotinic receptors on the muscle fibre"

      The arriving action potential opens presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels, and the

  - stem: "A frog nerve-muscle preparation in low calcium and high magnesium is stimulated 300 times, and 111 stimuli give no endplate potential. Assuming Poisson release, what is the mean quantal content?"
    choices:
      - "About 0.37 quanta per stimulus"
      - "About 1.0 quanta per stimulus"
      - "About 2.7 quanta per stimulus"
      - "About 0.63 quanta per stimulus"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The failure fraction is 111/300 = 0.37, and under a Poisson model m = −ln(0.37) = 0.99,
      about 1.0. The value 0.37 is the failure fraction itself, not m. The value 0.63 is the
      fraction of stimuli that released something, which ignores trials with two or more
      quanta. The value 2.7 is 1/0.37, an inverse that has no place in the Poisson formula.
  - stem: "Why is the failure method for quantal content used only when release is low?"
    choices:
      - "Acetylcholinesterase stops working in normal calcium"
      - "Quantal size doubles whenever calcium is raised"
      - "Failures become too rare to count reliably"
      - "Miniatures stop occurring when calcium is normal"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      With a quantal content of tens of quanta, the predicted failure fraction exp(−m) is
      vanishingly small, so there are essentially no failures to count, and the Poisson
      approximation also needs a small release probability per site. Miniatures continue in
      normal calcium. Raising calcium changes how many quanta are released, not the size of
      each one. Acetylcholinesterase does not depend on external calcium in this way, and it
      is irrelevant to counting failures.
  - stem: "In a made-up endplate, each quantum gives 0.40 mV, quanta sum linearly, and the muscle fibre fires when the endplate potential reaches 12 mV. How many quanta must one impulse release to fire the fibre?"
    choices:
      - "At least 12 quanta"
      - "At least 60 quanta"
      - "At least 48 quanta"
      - "At least 30 quanta"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Under linear summation the endplate potential is the number of quanta released × q, so one impulse must release at least 12 mV /
      0.40 mV = 30 quanta. Twelve quanta confuses the threshold in millivolts with a number of
      quanta. Forty-eight multiplies the threshold by four, as if a fourth-power law applied
      here. Sixty is the normal quantal content in the lesson's example, which gives a safety
      factor of 2 under this question's linear summation, not the minimum needed. (The lesson's
      balance-point model, which drops linear summation, would put the minimum near 35 quanta.)

**Put an electrode inside a resting frog muscle fibre at the point where the nerve ends, stimulate nothing, and you still see small depolarizations arriving at random, each about the same size and each far too small to fire the fibre.**

Paul Fatt and Bernard Katz reported these miniature endplate potentials in 1952. They turned out to be the bricks from which every nerve-evoked response at that junction is built.

## The relay and the blips

A **synapse** is a site where one neuron passes a signal to another cell. At a chemical synapse the presynaptic terminal releases a transmitter that crosses a gap, the synaptic cleft, and acts on receptors of the postsynaptic cell. The vertebrate neuromuscular junction, where a motor axon meets a skeletal muscle fibre, is the best-studied example. How it is built during development belongs to [the neuromuscular junction](/lessons/neuromuscular-junction/) in Developmental Neuroscience, along with [central synapses](/lessons/cns-synapses/) and [synapse elimination](/lessons/synapse-elimination/).

The sequence runs like a relay with five steps:

1. An [action potential](/lessons/action-potential/) arrives at the nerve terminal.
2. Calcium flows into the terminal.
3. Synaptic vesicles fuse with the terminal's membrane and release their transmitter, acetylcholine.
4. Acetylcholine crosses the cleft.
5. Acetylcholine opens receptor channels on the muscle fibre, and the fibre depolarizes.

That depolarization is the **endplate potential** (EPP), the muscle's version of an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP), a depolarization caused by transmitter. The **endplate** is the region of the muscle fibre that lies under the nerve terminal.

Now picture the record that started the story. Fatt and Katz put a fine glass electrode inside a frog muscle fibre at the endplate. Voltage runs up the side and time along the bottom. With no stimulus to the nerve, the trace should be flat. It is flat most of the time, but blips rise from it at irregular moments. Move the electrode away from the endplate and the blips shrink and fade.

Fatt and Katz called these **miniature endplate potentials**, or miniatures. They looked like scaled-down copies of the evoked endplate potential, so Fatt and Katz attributed them to spontaneous release of acetylcholine from the terminal.

The record raises five puzzles, and this lesson answers them in order.

1. **Units.** Is the nerve's evoked response built from blips like these?
2. **Counting.** If it is, how can you count units that merge into one smooth response?
3. **Calcium.** Why does release depend so steeply on calcium?
4. **Size and safety.** What sets the size of one unit, and how far above threshold does a normal response sit?
5. **Disease.** Two diseases both weaken muscle. How do you tell from a recording which side of the synapse is at fault?

## Packets: turning release down

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Del Castillo and Katz asked whether the evoked response was made of the same units as the miniatures. In normal calcium that is hard to see. One impulse releases dozens of units or more at once, and they merge into one smooth response, like a downpour where you cannot count the drops. To count drops you need a drizzle.

So they bathed the frog junction in a solution with less calcium and more magnesium, which cut release to a trickle. Now a nerve impulse often produced nothing at all, a **failure**. When it did produce a response, the response was small. Stimulate many times, and sort the response amplitudes into a histogram.

**Predict.** Suppose release were a smooth flow that the low calcium simply turned down. What shape would the histogram of response amplitudes have?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In one simple smooth-flow model, with sizes spreading around a small average and no all-or-none step, you would expect one smooth spread with no peaks at regular spacing. That is only one alternative: continuous release alone neither fixes the shape nor rules out failures. The telling evidence would be peaks spaced at multiples of one miniature.

</details>

That is what they found. Picture the histogram. Response amplitude runs along the bottom, and the number of trials with that amplitude runs up the side.

- **A tall bar at zero.** These are the failures.
- **A bump near the size of one miniature.** Lay the histogram of spontaneous miniatures over it, and the two line up.
- **A smaller bump near twice that size**, and a smaller one again near three times.

The bumps are broad, not sharp spikes, because one unit is not exactly the same size every time and the recording has noise. But the amplitudes cluster near whole-number multiples of the miniature. The evoked response came in packets.

Each packet is a **quantum**, and its postsynaptic effect, the miniature amplitude, is the **quantal size**, q. A quantum is a multimolecular packet, thousands of acetylcholine molecules, later identified with the contents of one vesicle. It is not one molecule of acetylcholine.

Think of coins dropped into a jar on a scale. You cannot see the coins, but if every reading is a multiple of one coin's weight, you can count them.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Every evoked response is a whole number of near-identical packets, so each **response ≈ count × size**, and on average **EPP ≈ m × q**, where m is the average count. The nerve terminal sets the count, and calcium sets it steeply. One vesicle's transmitter acting on the receptors sets the size, q. So a change in transmission at this junction can be asked one question first: did the count change, the size, or both?
>
> Three limits come with it. Big responses fall short of count × size (the section on packet size shows why). The count is random from impulse to impulse, so single responses scatter around the average. And with the muscle membrane and the recording held steady, the size depends on two things, how full the vesicle is and how many receptors face it, so a change in q alone does not say which side of the synapse moved.

## Counting quanta you cannot see

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

The histogram shows packets, but in most recordings the bumps blur together. What you want is one number, m, the **mean quantal content**. (This is not the m of Hodgkin and Huxley's gates in the [action potential](/lessons/action-potential/) lesson; the two fields happen to use the same letter.) There are two ways to get it. They rest on different assumptions, so if the model is right, the two estimates should agree within sampling error.

### The direct way: divide the average response by one packet

If every response is count × size, then dividing the average response by the size of one packet gives the average count. Rearrange the rule EPP ≈ m × q (divide both sides by q), counting each failure as a response of zero when you take the average:

This needs three things to be true. Evoked packets must be the same size, on average, as the spontaneous miniatures, that average must be stable, and packets must add linearly. In low calcium, where responses are only a millivolt or two, linear adding is a safe bet.

### The failure way: let the zeros tell you the average

This one sounds like a trick, so build it step by step. Picture the terminal as many release sites, N of them. When an impulse arrives, each site, independently of the others, has a small chance p of releasing one packet. On average the impulse releases m = N × p packets.

1. The chance that one site releases nothing is 1 − p.
2. A failure means *every* site released nothing. The sites are independent, so multiply: the chance of a failure is (1 − p) × (1 − p) × ..., N times, which is (1 − p)^N.
3. We want an answer that depends on N and p only through their product, m, and an exponential does that. When p is small, 1 − p is almost exactly e^(−p). Try p = 0.01: 1 − p = 0.99000, and e^(−0.01) = 0.99005.
4. So the chance of a failure is about (e^(−p))^N = e^(−N × p) = e^(−m).
5. Take the natural log of both sides (ln undoes e: ln of e^(−m) is −m), then flip the sign. The share of trials that fail, number of failures / number of trials, is your estimate of the failure chance:

The main assumptions are visible in those five lines. Sites must act independently (step 2), and each chance must be small (step 3). Small, not equal: sites with different small chances still give an approximately Poisson count, with m the sum of their chances (the exact failure chance is the product of the separate 1 − p values). Conditions must also stay the same across all the trials you pool, and you need failures that you can reliably tell apart from noise.

**Why bother with two methods?** The failure method never uses q. Suppose evoked packets were twice the size of spontaneous ones. The direct method would then report twice the true m, and the failure method would not. So when the two estimates agree, that agreement supports two things at once: the evoked and spontaneous packets are the same units, and release behaves like many independent small chances. This is the check del Castillo and Katz made.

The same reasoning gives the chance of releasing exactly k packets, which predicts every bump of the histogram, not just the bar at zero:

This is the **Poisson distribution**.

**Where it stops working.** Restore normal calcium and failures vanish: with m in the tens, e^(−m) is effectively zero, and there is nothing to count. Whether each chance is still small (step 3) then has to be checked rather than assumed, because it depends on what counts as a release site and how likely each is to release. At the frog junction, low calcium and high magnesium are how you make release chances small and failures common, which is what the method needs.

### Worked problem

In this made-up recording, a frog junction sits in low calcium and high magnesium. You stimulate the nerve 200 times. The mean miniature is q = 0.40 mV. The mean EPP, counting failures as zero, is 0.40 mV. Seventy-four stimuli give no response.

*Step 1: direct method.* m = 0.40 mV / 0.40 mV = 1.00 quantum per stimulus.

*Step 2: failure method.* P(0) = 74 / 200 = 0.37. m = −ln(0.37) = 0.99 (since e^−1 ≈ 0.37, ln 0.37 is close to −1). The two estimates agree, which supports the Poisson model for this recording. Notice that more than a third of impulses fail even though the average is a whole packet: e^(−1) = 0.37, and the average is pulled up by the trials that release two or three.

*Step 3: predict the rest.* With m = 1.0, P(1) = e^(−1) × 1 = 0.368, so about 200 × 0.368 = 74 trials should show one quantum. P(2) = 0.368 / 2 = 0.184, so about 37 trials should show two. If the amplitude histogram has peaks near 0.40 and 0.80 mV with roughly those counts, the data are consistent with the model.

Before you use the simulation, here is what to look for. Each run draws 200 stimuli. Watch the bar at zero: the failure estimate comes only from it, while the direct estimate comes from the average of all the bars. Raise the quantal content and see how few failures are left to count.

## Where the count comes from: calcium

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

The count, m, is set in the terminal, by step 2 and step 3 of the relay.

The action potential invades the terminal and opens voltage-gated calcium channels. Calcium flows in down a steep gradient. Synaptic vesicles near the membrane have already been docked and primed, made ready to fuse. Calcium binds a sensor protein, a synaptotagmin, and that triggers the last step of fusion. Three SNARE proteins (synaptobrevin on the vesicle, syntaxin-1 and SNAP-25 on the plasma membrane), already partly zipped during priming, finish zipping and pull the two membranes into one. The sensor is synaptotagmin-1 at many central synapses; at the mammalian neuromuscular junction it is synaptotagmin-2 together with synaptotagmin-1. The molecular mechanics of fusion are taught in [vesicle traffic](/lessons/vesicle-traffic/), and calcium handling in [calcium signaling](/lessons/calcium-signaling/).

Release needs external calcium, and magnesium opposes it. F. A. Dodge and Rami Rahamimoff (1967) measured how strongly. At the frog neuromuscular junction they varied calcium step by step and plotted EPP amplitude against calcium concentration, both on logarithmic axes, where each equal step along an axis multiplies the value by the same factor ([log scales](/math-toolkit/#log-scales)).

Picture their plot. At low calcium the points fall on a steep straight line with a slope near 4 (3.78, with a standard deviation of 0.2, across 28 experiments). As calcium approaches its normal level, the line bends over and becomes shallower.

A slope of about 4 means that release rises roughly as the fourth power of calcium over that range.

**Predict.** On the steep part of the line, you double the external calcium. Roughly how much more transmitter is released: twice as much, four times, or more?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Far more. If release rose exactly as the fourth power, doubling calcium would give 2^4 = 16 times more. With the measured slope of 3.78, it gives 2^3.78 (a calculator's power key), about 14 times.

</details>

Why would release be so steep, and why would the line bend over? A simple picture gives both.

- Suppose release at a site needs four calcium ions bound at once, like four seats that must all be filled.
- At low calcium each seat is rarely filled. The chance that all four are filled at once is the chance for one seat multiplied four times, so it grows as calcium to the fourth power, a log-log slope of 4.
- As calcium rises, the seats start to fill. Once most are already full, adding calcium cannot help much, and the curve bends over.

The slope fits a model like this, in which several calcium ions act together to trigger release. In Dodge and Rahamimoff's data, adding magnesium lowered the EPP without changing the slope, as expected if magnesium competes with calcium for the same seats. But the slope does not count binding sites. Even the four-seat picture gives a slope somewhat below four that falls further as calcium rises, and a real fitted slope is also shaped by how calcium spreads in the terminal. A slope of 3.78 is a reading of one curve in one preparation, not a molecular fact.

So calcium is a steep dial on the count. Lose some calcium entry, and you lose many packets. Keep that in mind for Lambert-Eaton syndrome.

## Where the size comes from: receptors and the balance point

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

The size, q, is what one vesicle's acetylcholine does to the muscle fibre.

Acetylcholine diffuses across the cleft and opens nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, whose gating belongs to [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/). A receptor normally needs two acetylcholine molecules bound to open. The channel passes both sodium and potassium, so its **reversal potential**, the voltage at which its current changes direction, sits near 0 mV, between the two ions' equilibrium potentials.

Now use the rule from the [action potential](/lessons/action-potential/) lesson. With no current injected through an electrode, the voltage moves toward a balance point: the average of the channels' reversal potentials, weighted by their conductances (how open each set of channels is). Opening nicotinic channels adds a conductance whose reversal potential sits near 0 mV. The balance point moves from rest toward 0 mV, and Vm follows: the endplate depolarizes. At a frog muscle fibre's resting potential, far more negative than the reversal potential, the **driving force** (Vm minus the reversal potential) is large, and the current it drives is inward.

To see how far the endplate can move, treat the resting fibre as one conductance, G_rest, pulling toward the resting potential, V_rest, and the open acetylcholine channels as a second conductance, g_ACh, pulling toward about 0 mV. The weighted average gives the target:

> **V_balance = (G_rest × V_rest + g_ACh × 0 mV) / (G_rest + g_ACh)**

Check the ends: with no channels open (g_ACh = 0) the target is V_rest; with g_ACh far larger than G_rest it approaches 0 mV, never beyond.

Vm chases this target and reaches it only if the channels stay open long enough. An EPP is brief, so it never fully settles.

Acetylcholinesterase in the cleft hydrolyzes acetylcholine and ends each quantum's action. Vesicle membrane is then retrieved: Heuser and Reese followed an extracellular tracer from the frog terminal's surface into internal cisternae and then into new vesicles, a story continued in [endocytosis](/lessons/endocytosis/) and in the anecdote "Following the tracer back into vesicles".

### Why big responses fall short of count × size

One packet adds a small g_ACh, and the balance point moves a fraction of a millivolt. Each further packet adds the same conductance, but it moves Vm less than the one before. As Vm climbs toward 0 mV, the driving force shrinks, so each newly opened channel pushes less hard. The same shrinking driving force helped keep the spike's peak short of E_Na, along with sodium inactivation and potassium current.

Put numbers on it in a simple steady-state model. Take the fibre's resting potential about 90 mV below the reversal potential, near −90 mV; 80 mV gives nearly the same answers. Use q = 0.40 mV. These numbers show how much the model says big responses fall short, not measured EPP peaks.

| Packets released | count × size | balance point (steady-state illustration) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4 (low calcium) | 1.6 mV | 1.6 mV (about 2% less) |
| 60 (normal calcium) | 24 mV | about 19 mV (about 20% less) |

### The safety factor

Now restore normal calcium and suppose an impulse releases 60 packets of q = 0.40 mV and, as an illustration, that the fibre fires when the EPP reaches 12 mV. These round numbers keep the arithmetic simple; a real junction in normal calcium releases more quanta and the fibre needs a larger depolarization to fire.

The **safety factor** is the ratio of the EPP to the depolarization needed to fire.

- By simple count × size, EPP = 60 × 0.40 = 24 mV, and the safety factor is 24 / 12 = 2.0.
- By the balance point, the EPP is about 19 mV, and the safety factor is about 19 / 12, nearer 1.6.

Smaller, but still comfortably above 1. A normal junction fires its muscle fibre with margin to spare.

**Predict.** Suppose antibodies remove some of the muscle's acetylcholine receptors. Which factor in count × size changes, and which stays the same?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The size, q, falls: each packet's acetylcholine finds fewer receptors to open. In this simple picture the count, m, is set by the terminal and calcium, so it stays the same (a real terminal can partly compensate by releasing more).

</details>

## Count or size? When the safety factor fails

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

Return to the rule: response ≈ count × size. A fibre fails to fire when its endplate potential falls below what it needs. Count × size approximates that potential for small responses; for big ones the steady-state balance-point model shows why it overestimates. Two diseases reach the threshold from opposite sides.

### Myasthenia gravis: the size falls

In **myasthenia gravis**, antibodies in most patients target the muscle's acetylcholine receptors; others have antibodies against MuSK, a protein needed at the junction. Antibodies to the receptor can cross-link receptors and speed their removal, block them, or recruit complement that damages the postsynaptic membrane. Fewer receptors means a smaller q. Toyka and colleagues (1975) transferred an immunoglobulin fraction from patients' serum into mice, which then had smaller miniatures and fewer junctional receptors (see the anecdote "A disease moved from patients to mice").

Return to the worked example and cut q from 0.40 to 0.24 mV. Release also runs down during repeated impulses, as ready vesicles are used faster than they are replaced. For a clean illustration, treat each row as one impulse releasing a fixed number of packets, k: 60 on the first impulse, a quarter fewer (45) after run-down.

| Junction | packets on this impulse, k | size, q | count × size | balance point (steady-state illustration) | fires at 12 mV? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal, first impulse | 60 | 0.40 mV | 24.0 mV | 18.9 mV | yes |
| Normal, after run-down | 45 | 0.40 mV | 18.0 mV | 15.0 mV | yes |
| Myasthenic, first impulse | 60 | 0.24 mV | 14.4 mV | 12.4 mV | yes, barely |
| Myasthenic, after run-down | 45 | 0.24 mV | 10.8 mV | 9.6 mV | no |
| Lambert-Eaton, low count | 20 | 0.40 mV | 8.0 mV | 7.3 mV | no |

Only the myasthenic fibre fails after run-down. This is the fatigable weakness of myasthenia: muscles that work at first and weaken with use. Real counts fluctuate around their average, so a junction whose average sits just above threshold fails on some impulses even before run-down (Going deeper puts a number on it).

Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors act by letting each quantum's acetylcholine last longer, so it survives long enough to reach more of the remaining receptors. That raises q partway back toward normal, the factor the disease lowered.

One caution. Poorly filled vesicles would also shrink q, so a small miniature does not by itself say which side is at fault. Pair the recording with a receptor count before you blame the receptors.

### Lambert-Eaton syndrome: the count falls

In **Lambert-Eaton syndrome** the fault is presynaptic. Antibodies against the terminal's voltage-gated calcium channels reduce calcium entry, so m is low while q is normal. An impulse releasing 20 packets of normal size gives about 7 to 8 mV, and the fibre fails.

**Predict.** Now stimulate a Lambert-Eaton junction rapidly, at 20 Hz. What happens to the count during the train, and why?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The count rises. At 20 Hz the calcium left over from one impulse adds to the calcium of the next, so calcium builds up in the terminal. Because release rises steeply with calcium, a little extra calcium brings a large rise in m. During the train the response can climb back toward what the fibre needs. Slow repeated stimulation can instead give a decrement, a response that shrinks with each impulse: calcium clears between slow impulses, so run-down wins.

</details>

So the two diseases leave opposite fingerprints, provided resting potential, membrane properties and recording site are unchanged (if those shift, q shifts too):

| Recording | Points to |
| --- | --- |
| q down, m normal | the size side: receptors after the synapse, or vesicle filling before it |
| m down, q normal | the count side: release from the terminal |

### Design problem

Two made-up muscle biopsies arrive for intracellular recording. In A, the miniature amplitude is reduced and the quantal content is normal. In B, the miniature amplitude is normal, the quantal content is low, and it rises during 20 Hz stimulation. Where is each defect, and what experiment would test your answer?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

A is most likely postsynaptic: a smaller q with normal m fits fewer acetylcholine receptors, as in myasthenia gravis. To test whether a circulating factor is responsible, transfer the patient's immunoglobulin fraction into mice and repeat the quantal analysis and a receptor count, as Toyka and colleagues did (1975, 1978). Predict smaller miniatures and fewer receptors, with m unchanged, as in their mice, or raised if the terminal compensates.

B is presynaptic: low m with normal q, rising during 20 Hz trains, fits reduced calcium entry, as in Lambert-Eaton syndrome. Test it by measuring m across a range of external calcium: if calcium entry is reduced, more calcium should raise release toward control levels, and you can compare the curve with control junctions. If an antibody is the cause, passive transfer of B's immunoglobulins should lower m without changing q.

These are mechanisms for interpreting data, not clinical advice.

</details>

### The contrast: a synapse with no packets

Not every synapse uses a transmitter. At an **electrical synapse**, channels called gap junctions join the two cells' cytoplasm, and current passes directly with no transmitter step. There are no vesicles, so there is no quantal count or size and no release failures. The coupled response still has a size, and it can be too small to fire the next cell. Most electrical synapses are very fast and pass current both ways, which helps them synchronize cells. Some rectify, passing current more easily one way.

Next, [transmitter systems](/lessons/transmitter-systems/) asks where the brain's other transmitters are made and cleared, and [synaptic plasticity](/lessons/synaptic-plasticity/) asks how release changes from one impulse to the next.

## Going deeper

### When the failure method goes wrong

The failure method follows from the count model only when p is small. Without the step-3 shortcut, the chance of a failure is exactly (1 − p)^N, and −ln P(0) overestimates m. With m = 1, the error is 0.5% for N = 100 and p = 0.01, and 5% for N = 10 and p = 0.1. With m = 3, it is 19% for N = 10 and p = 0.3, and 39% for N = 6 and p = 0.5. So high release probability both makes failures vanish and biases the estimate upward.

### Correcting for nonlinear summation

Write the endplate's balance point with the resting membrane as one conductance G at V_rest and the packets as a total conductance g at the reversal potential E_rev. One packet alone, small against G, moves Vm by q ≈ (g_one / G) × DF, where DF = E_rev − V_rest is the driving force at rest. Then k packets give

> **EPP = V_balance − V_rest ≈ (k × q) / (1 + k × q / DF)**

The tables use a fixed k. Real counts vary from impulse to impulse. Averaged over impulses the difference is small: for Poisson counts averaging 60 packets of 0.40 mV, the average of the formula is 18.90 mV, against 18.95 mV for exactly 60. But an average above threshold does not mean every impulse fires. With q = 0.24 mV this model needs at least 58 packets to reach 12 mV, and a Poisson count averaging 60 reaches that on only about 62% of impulses.

Inverting the formula gives v / (1 − v / DF) for a measured EPP v, the classical correction associated with Martin, which turns about 19 mV back into 24 mV. It assumes a steady state, and applied to brief EPPs it can overcorrect: McLachlan and Martin (1981) found this in their preparations and added an empirical factor. How well any such correction works depends on the response's time course, the membrane and cable properties and where the electrode sits, so use one validated for the preparation.

### Rectifying electrical synapses

Furshpan and Potter (1959) first showed rectification at the crayfish giant motor synapse. Shui and colleagues (2020) found that junctions built from the worm innexins UNC-7b and UNC-9, expressed in Xenopus oocytes, strongly favour current from the UNC-9 cell to the UNC-7b cell.

### Central synapses depart from the frog picture

The count × size rule was built at the frog endplate. At central synapses a release site can release more than one vesicle, receptors can saturate, and q varies a lot from site to site. In cultured rat hippocampal boutons (small presynaptic terminals), Maschi and Klyachko (2020) saw two vesicles fuse at the same bouton after a single action potential (see the anecdote "Two vesicles from one bouton").
