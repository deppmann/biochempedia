      that attention boosted V4 responses most at low contrast. The work made attention
      something you could measure in spikes.

techniques:
  - name: "Single-neuron recording in behaving monkeys"
    blurb: >-
      A microelectrode records the spikes of one neuron while a trained monkey performs a task
      with controlled stimuli, delays and responses. Aligning spikes to events (cue, motion
      onset, saccade) shows what the neuron's firing goes with, which is a correlate, not a cause.
  - name: "Electrical microstimulation"
    blurb: >-
      Weak current pulses through an electrode activate neurons and axons near the tip. In MT,
      nearby neurons share a preferred direction, so a site can be chosen for its tuning. If behaviour shifts in the direction those neurons prefer, as in MT during motion
      judgments, their activity can influence the choice in that task.
  - name: "Reversible pharmacological inactivation"
    blurb: >-
      A small injection of muscimol, a GABA-A receptor agonist, silences neurons near the tip for
      a limited time. A deficit shows the area contributes to that task. No deficit means no effect was detected
      under those conditions, which is informative only if a control shows the drug worked; other areas may still compensate.
  - name: "TMS with EEG decoding"
    blurb: >-
      A classifier trained on EEG patterns reads out which category of item (not the individual item) a person is holding in memory;
      a transcranial magnetic stimulation pulse perturbs cortex noninvasively. Together they can
      probe whether an item that cannot be decoded is still stored in a latent form.

  - stem: "In a noisy drift-to-bound model of a two-choice motion decision, both bounds are moved farther from the starting point while the drift rate at each motion strength stays the same. What does the model predict?"
    choices:
      - "Faster but less accurate choices at every motion strength"
      - "Slower, more accurate choices, with the largest delay at weak motion"
      - "No change in speed, because the slope is set by motion strength"
      - "Faster choices, because the accumulator fires at a higher rate"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Farther bounds mean more evidence must accumulate, so the time to a bound grows, and
      noise has more time to average out, so accuracy rises wherever there is net motion (at 0%
      it stays at chance). The added time is largest at weak motion, where noise rather than
      drift sets how long the total takes to reach a bound. Faster, less accurate choices follow
      from bringing the bounds closer. The slope is unchanged, but the distance to a bound is
      longer, so speed must change. Moving the bounds does not raise the rate of rise; it only
      moves the finishing lines.

**A monkey watches a cloud of dots, some moving together and the rest at random, and must decide which way the coherent dots are going. As it watches, a neuron in its parietal cortex, toward the top and back of the brain, on average fires faster and faster and reaches a similar level just before the monkey moves its eyes to give its answer.**

Is that neuron making the decision? It certainly looks that way. Hold the question; the lesson ends on it. Answering it rests on one piece of arithmetic about deciding under noise. The same arithmetic then helps explain some of what attention does to visual neurons, and the lesson asks where a brain might hold the goal that says what to count.

## The task and the puzzle

In the **random-dot motion task**, a patch of dots appears on a screen. **Coherence** is the percentage of dots moving together in one direction; the rest move randomly. At 51.2% coherence the motion is easy to see, and at 3.2% it is hard to see. At 0% there is no imposed direction: random fluctuations can favour either side on a given trial, but the expected signal is zero.

Two choice targets sit on either side of the dots. The monkey reports the direction with a **saccade** (a fast eye movement) to one of them. In the reaction-time version of the task it answers whenever it is ready.

Picture the behaviour as two graphs, each with coherence along the bottom.

- **Accuracy** (the psychometric curve) rises with coherence. At 0% the choices are at chance.
- **Reaction time** (the chronometric curve) falls with coherence. Strong motion gives fast answers, and weak motion gives slow ones.

That is the behaviour to explain, and it poses a puzzle. At any one moment the dots give only a weak, noisy hint about the direction. How does a brain turn a stream of unreliable hints into a choice, and why does that take longer when the motion is weak?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Adding up noisy signals makes them more reliable, because the signal adds in step while independent noise partly cancels.
>
> - Add n samples that each carry the same average push, with independent noise of the same size.
> - The signal grows n-fold.
> - The noise (its spread) grows only √n-fold, “root n”, the square root of n (see [powers](/math-toolkit/#powers)).
> - So reliability, signal divided by noise, grows as n / √n = √n.
>
> In the drift-to-bound model this lesson uses, a decision is this sum taken over time and stopped at a bound. It is a model of behaviour and of trial-averaged firing; it does not say where in the brain any adding happens, and that gap is the puzzle this lesson ends on.

### Why adding up helps

Picture it as a running tally. Each glimpse adds a small push toward the true direction plus a random jiggle. The pushes all point the same way and add in step; the jiggles point every which way and partly cancel.

**Predict.** You watch four times as long. Is your running total four times as reliable?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No, twice as reliable. The signal grows fourfold, but the noise grows too, by √4 = 2, so signal divided by noise doubles. In the same way, doubling the viewing time multiplies reliability by √2, about 1.41, not 2.

</details>

Two limits keep the rule honest.

- **Reliability means signal divided by noise, not percent correct.** Percent correct is a bounded, nonlinear function of reliability whose shape depends on the noise and the decision rule, so doubling reliability does not double percent correct. And there must be a signal to add: at 0% coherence waiting cannot help.
- **The √n gain needs samples with independent noise.** Over neurons, that means only the part of each neuron's noise that is private to it. Over time, noise that still resembles the last moment's gives fewer effective independent samples than raw ones, so √t growth is an approximation that holds only under the conditions in Going deeper.

Now the behaviour makes sense. Weak motion gives a small push per glimpse, so the total needs longer to become trustworthy. Waiting longer buys accuracy and costs time. A brain that adds up evidence therefore needs a rule for when to stop.

## Adding up evidence: a ramp to a bound

### The evidence comes from MT

The momentary evidence comes from **MT**, the middle temporal area, a visual area specialized for motion. Its neurons are tuned to direction (see [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/)). Newsome, Britten and Movshon (1989) found that most of the MT neurons they studied, on a task matched to the neurons' properties, discriminated motion about as well as, or better than, the monkey. That is a correlation. Salzman, Britten and Newsome (1990) then passed weak electrical current into small clusters of MT neurons with a shared preferred direction (microstimulation) and biased the monkeys' choices toward that direction. That was a causal test: MT supplies evidence, and in this task its activity can sway the choice. What adds the evidence up?

### A rule for when to stop

The simplest rule is a **bound**: keep adding until the total reaches a set level, then act. Picture it as a line drawn on a graph. Time since the dots appeared runs along the bottom, and the running total runs up the side. The total wobbles but drifts upward, and the model commits to a choice when the total touches the bound. Two features of the picture carry the whole model.

- **The drift**, the average slope of the total, is set by the strength of the evidence. Stronger motion means a steeper drift, so the total reaches the bound sooner.
- **The wobble** comes from noise. On some trials it carries the total to the bound early, on others late, so reaction times vary from trial to trial even at one motion strength.

For a choice between two directions, draw a second bound below the starting point for the other answer. The total drifts toward the correct bound, but on weak-motion trials the wobble sometimes carries it to the wrong one. That is an error.

This is a **drift-to-bound model** (also called bounded accumulation, or drift-diffusion). For the noiseless average, the timing comes out of two lines. The first says how long the climb takes: distance divided by speed.

The second line adds the fixed delays on either side of the climb:

> **reaction time = latency + time to bound + motor delay**

The **latency** is the time before the total starts to move, and the **motor delay** is the time from reaching the bound to the eye moving. In the model those two are fixed, so only the middle term depends on the drift.

**Predict.** If some neuron held this running total, what would its firing look like on strong-motion and weak-motion trials? Where would it be just before the eye moved?

On trials that end at the upper bound, a choice of the target this neuron represents, it should climb, more steeply when the motion is stronger, and reach about the same level just before the saccade whatever the motion strength, because the bound is the same on every trial. Choices of the other target end at the other bound, so they need not reach that level.

</details>

### The record: LIP

The **lateral intraparietal area (LIP)** in the parietal lobe holds neurons that fire before an eye movement to a target in their **response field**. A response field is LIP's version of a receptive field: the place a target must be for the neuron to fire before a saccade to it. Roitman and Shadlen (2002) placed one of the two choice targets in the recorded neuron's response field and let rhesus monkeys answer whenever they were ready. Picture their main figure: firing rate against time, one line for each motion strength, averaged over many trials and aligned to the start of motion.

- For the first 200 ms or so the lines lie together: motion strength has not yet shown up in the firing.
- Then they ramp. The ramp is steeper for stronger motion when the monkey chose the target in the response field, and the firing predicts the choice.
- Realigned to the saccade, the lines arrive at about the same rate shortly before the eye moves, whatever the motion strength.

That is what the model predicts for a neuron that holds the total. The authors read the common level as a threshold that appeared to mark the end of the decision. Two cautions. These are averages: whether single trials ramp or step is debated, because a smooth average is compatible with steps of varied timing on single trials, and population recordings are needed to settle it. And LIP firing also mixes in sensory and premotor signals and a rise that grows with elapsed time (an urgency signal), so a common level before the saccade need not mark a pure decision bound.

### Worked problem

The numbers are invented for teaching, and one model LIP neuron stands for the accumulator. It fires at 30 spikes/s until 200 ms after motion starts, then climbs. The bound is 70 spikes/s, and the saccade follows 70 ms later. The drift is 400 (spikes/s) per second at 51.2% coherence, 100 at 12.8% and 25 at 3.2%: it scales with coherence.

*Step 1, without noise.* The distance to the bound is 70 − 30 = 40 spikes/s. The time to bound is 40 / 400 = 0.10 s at 51.2%, 40 / 100 = 0.40 s at 12.8% and 40 / 25 = 1.60 s at 3.2%. Adding the latency and motor delay gives reaction times of 200 + 100 + 70 = 370 ms, 200 + 400 + 70 = 670 ms and 200 + 1,600 + 70 = 1,870 ms. Units: (spikes/s) divided by (spikes/s per s) gives seconds.

*Step 2, add the wobble.* Real reaction times do not stretch out like that. Add noise and a second bound for the other answer, the same 40 units below the start. A firing rate cannot go below zero, so from here read the total as a relative decision variable, not one neuron's rate. Choose the noise so that the model is 90% correct at 12.8%: Gaussian, the same size at every coherence (about 60 units per square-root second), with the same 270 ms of latency plus motor delay. The mean reaction times from this model, over both choices, become about 370, 590 and 700 ms. At 0% coherence the mean drift is zero, so only noise reaches a bound: times are long and variable, about 710 ms on average, and the choices fall to chance.

Why does noise make weak-motion answers faster? With two bounds you never wait forever: even with no drift, the wobble carries the total off to one side or the other. At 3.2% the drift alone would take 1.6 s, so the wobble gets most trials to a bound sooner, some of them to the wrong one. Strong motion is unchanged, because the drift does nearly all the work. The small drift is why weak motion gives slow answers; the wobble is why it also gives errors. Turn up the noise in the simulation below to watch this happen (its slider stops at 40, so it shows the effect, not these exact numbers).

*Step 3, compare.* In one illustrated experiment, Roitman and Shadlen reported a mean of 350 ± 9 ms (SEM) for correct choices at 51.2%. The invented numbers were chosen to land near this, so the match is not a test of the model. The test is the shape: steeper ramps, shorter and less variable reaction times, and higher accuracy as motion strengthens, all from one rule.

### The speed–accuracy trade

Raise the bound and every trial must gather more evidence. Choices get slower and more accurate wherever there is net motion; at 0% they stay at chance. In the noisy model the slowing is largest at weak motion, because there the wobble, not the drift, sets how long the total takes to reach a bound. In the worked problem's model, moving both bounds 10 units farther from the start (the upper one from 70 to 80) adds about 25 ms at 51.2% coherence, 120 ms at 12.8% and 230 ms at 3.2%.

## Attention: a stronger signal, less shared noise

Return to the rule, now across neurons instead of across time. Each neuron is like one glimpse, so a downstream cell that adds up many neurons gains in the same way: their private noise partly cancels, their shared noise does not, and a stronger signal helps either way. Attention acts on both.

**Attention** is selecting part of what is available for further processing. Keep it apart from **arousal**, the overall level of alertness, which shifts with [sleep and rhythms](/lessons/sleep-and-rhythms/). A **receptive field** is the region of space in which a stimulus changes a neuron's firing, as in [visual cortex](/lessons/visual-cortex/). **V4** is a mid-level visual area in the macaque.

### Attention biases a competition

Moran and Desimone (1985) recorded from neurons in macaque V4 and placed two stimuli inside one receptive field: one that drove the cell well and one that drove it poorly. Then they changed only where the monkey attended.

**Predict.** The monkey attends to the stimulus the cell responds to poorly. Does the response go up, go down, or stay the same?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It goes down. When the monkey attended to the poor stimulus, the response fell toward what that stimulus gave alone, although the retinal image was unchanged.

</details>

Stimuli seem to compete for a neuron, and attention biases the competition toward the attended one. In that study, primary visual cortex (V1) cells showed no such effect; later work found smaller attention effects there.

### Attention acts like more contrast

Picture a **contrast-response curve**: a neuron's response on the vertical axis against stimulus contrast on the horizontal. It rises steeply at low and middle contrasts, then flattens near the top, where responses saturate.

Reynolds, Pasternak and Desimone (2000) varied contrast and found that attention raised V4 responses more at low contrast than at high. On average, attention acted as if it multiplied the stimulus contrast by 1.51. An attended 10% contrast grating drove cells about as well as an unattended grating of 10 × 1.51 ≈ 15%. Draw the attended curve on a contrast axis with a log scale and it is the same curve slid to the left. So the same multiplication raises the response most where the curve rises steeply and hardly at all near saturation. This pattern is called **contrast gain**. **Response gain**, a separate pattern other studies have reported, scales the whole curve up instead; the turning-up-the-volume picture in the Like-I'm-five summary is closer to it. In terms of the rule, contrast gain is a stronger signal for the attended stimulus.

### Attention lowers shared noise

Attention also changes how neurons vary together. Show the same stimulus many times and count two neurons' spikes on each repeat. Plot one count against the other, one dot per repeat. If the two tend to fire more on the same repeats, the cloud of dots tilts; if not, it has no tilt. **Noise correlation** is the correlation of two neurons' spike counts across repeats of the same stimulus:

> **ρ = Cov(n1, n2) / √(Var(n1) × Var(n2))**

Read it as: ρ (“rho”) is how much the two counts move together (their covariance, Cov), scaled by the two counts' own variability (the square root of the product of their variances, Var), so that it runs from −1 to +1. Here n1 and n2 are the two neurons' spike counts; this n is a count, not the number of samples from the one idea above.

How much does that shared noise matter when a downstream cell averages many neurons? The guiding rule gives the answer:

Kanashiro and colleagues (2017) reanalyzed V4 recordings from two monkeys detecting orientation changes. Mean noise correlation was 0.065 unattended and 0.045 attended. As an illustration, in an idealized pool of 100 identical, similarly tuned neurons, that drop would cut the pooled noise by about 14%, even though each neuron's own variability rose slightly. That is a calculation from the averages, not a measured pool. So for a readout that adds up similarly tuned neurons, less shared noise makes the pool more reliable. For other readouts, whether a correlation hurts depends on how it lines up with the neurons' tuning; some correlations are harmless, or even helpful, for an optimal decoder.

Where does the bias come from? Attention also depends on parietal and frontal cortex, as damage in humans shows. After damage there, a patient may fail to notice or respond to things on the side of space opposite the lesion (contralesional **hemispatial neglect**). Neglect is commoner and more severe after right-hemisphere damage.

## Holding the goal: prefrontal cortex

Attention needs a target, and a decision needs to know which evidence counts. One answer to what sets them points to the front of the brain.

**Executive control** is the ability to organize thought and action according to internal goals rather than whatever the senses offer next. **Working memory**, holding information that is no longer in front of you so that you can use it, is one of its tools. Both are tied to the **prefrontal cortex**, the front part of the frontal lobe, which never works alone. Working memory is different from the long-term stores in [memory systems](/lessons/memory-systems/).

### Holding a place in mind

The classic recording came from monkeys. Funahashi, Bruce and Goldman-Rakic (1989) trained them in an oculomotor delayed-response task. The monkey fixated a central spot, a cue flashed for 0.5 s at one of several peripheral places, and the monkey kept fixating through a delay of 1 to 6 s before making a saccade to where the cue had been.

Fixation is the key control. With the eyes still and the screen blank, firing that depends on the cue's location cannot be a response to a visible cue or to an eye movement under way. Fixation does not rule out a movement being planned, though. Whether delay activity is a memory of the place or a plan to look there is a question this task alone cannot settle.

The recordings came from around the **principal sulcus**, a groove in the lateral prefrontal cortex. Of 288 neurons, 87 changed their firing in the delay and 79% of those were directionally selective: 0.79 × 87 ≈ 69 cells, about 24% of the sample, carried the remembered direction through the delay. A subset of principal-sulcus neurons did this, in this task, in monkeys.

### Holding a rule

Prefrontal neurons also code what to do. Wallis, Anderson and Miller (2001) trained monkeys to judge two pictures under one of two rules, respond if they match or respond if they differ. The most common activity in their prefrontal sample reflected the rule in force, and the monkeys applied the rules to pictures they had never seen. The new pictures matter: a cell that signals the rule for a novel picture cannot be signalling a memorized picture-response pairing.

### A theory that ties these together

Miller and Cohen (2001) proposed that prefrontal cortex maintains representations of goals, and these bias processing in other structures toward what the goal needs. Think of a coach on the sideline who holds the plan. The players do the running, but the coach's call tips which play the team runs. In this lesson's terms, the goal says which signal to count. That is not a claim that prefrontal cortex does the adding. It is a proposal, and the prefrontal cortex is one partner among several.

## When the best correlate is not necessary

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Go back to the neuron in the opening. LIP firing looks like the running total. But correlation shows only what activity goes with a choice. A manipulation asks two further questions: does changing the activity change the choice, and does the choice need it? MT passed the first test when stimulation swayed the choice in this task. Does the choice need MT, or LIP?

Katz, Yates, Pillow and Huk (2016) recorded MT and LIP in rhesus monkeys and then reversibly silenced each with muscimol, a drug that activates the inhibitory GABA-A receptors (see [synaptic transmission](/lessons/synaptic-transmission/)) and quiets nearby neurons.

**Predict.** If LIP is the accumulator, what should happen to motion discrimination when MT is silenced? When LIP is silenced?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If LIP were the one place the evidence is added up, and nothing else could cover for it, silencing either area should impair discrimination. In fact MT inactivation impaired motion discrimination, but LIP inactivation produced no measurable impairment of motion decisions in the tasks tested.

</details>

A null result is easy to dismiss: perhaps the injection missed, or was too small. So the same monkeys did a free-choice task with no right answer. After LIP inactivation they chose targets in the affected part of space less often, and their saccades to that side landed less accurately, so the drug had acted. The control is what makes the null result readable. Even so, a null result cannot exclude compensation by other areas.

**What other studies found.** The result is debated.

- Microstimulation of LIP has biased choices (Hanks, Ditterich and Shadlen, 2006).
- Zhou and Freedman (2019) found that parietal inactivation impaired motion and category decisions, most when the visual stimuli, rather than the response targets, sat in the inactivated field.
- Jeurissen and colleagues (2022) found that LIP inactivation biased decisions, but the biases faded while the area was still silenced, which suggests that other areas compensated.

The model survives: if a decision is built by adding up evidence, the adding is done somewhere, perhaps in several places at once. What the experiments show is that a neuron displaying the running total is not thereby the place the total is needed. Which areas a decision needs, and whether that changes with training and task, is an open question.

### Design problem

Muscimol in MT raises the motion-discrimination threshold; in LIP it leaves the threshold unchanged, though monkeys choose targets in the inactivated field less often in a free-choice task. What does this say about LIP as "the accumulator", and what would you do next?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

LIP activity correlates with the decision, but silencing the targeted LIP sites caused no measurable discrimination deficit under these conditions. The free-choice effect shows the inactivation worked, so the null result is not a missed injection. It does not rule out a contribution that spared tissue or other areas could cover. MT was needed for this discrimination, as expected of a stage that carries direction evidence, though direction-selective signals already reach MT from V1.

Several readings remain. Other areas that carry choice signals, such as the frontal eye field or the superior colliculus, may hold the signal redundantly or compensate when LIP is silenced. Or LIP may reflect a decision computed elsewhere. Next, inactivate several areas together, and record many neurons at once to see single-trial dynamics. Scope every conclusion to this task, this training and this species. The [circuit experiment design](/lessons/circuit-experiment-design/) and [large-scale neuroscience](/lessons/large-scale-neuroscience/) lessons build those tools.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The numbers behind the worked problem

The drifts in the worked problem come from one constant: 7.8125 (spikes/s) per second for each percent coherence, which makes 51.2% come out at exactly 400. The simulation uses the same constant.

### When adding up stops helping

The exact √n rule applies to independent samples with the same expected signal and finite noise variance. If the noise drifts slowly, so that one moment's noise resembles the last, adding the two cancels little. The span over which it still resembles the last is the noise's **correlation time**, and over windows shorter than that, waiting barely helps. For steady (stationary) noise with a stable mean signal whose correlations die away fast enough to have a finite integrated autocovariance, adding over windows much longer than the correlation time gives approximately √t growth in reliability, with fewer effective independent samples than raw ones. A long window is an approximation, not a guarantee of independence: slowly decaying correlations, or noise shared across a whole trial, prevent the gain.

### The Kanashiro figures

In Kanashiro and colleagues' reanalysis, mean covariance fell 34.0% while mean single-neuron variance rose 1.61%. Dividing one by the other does not reproduce the mean correlations (0.065 × 0.66 / 1.016 ≈ 0.042, not 0.045), presumably because the paper averaged pair by pair, so do not try to recompute one from the other. The 14% illustration in the main text comes out the same to the nearest percent with or without the small rise in variance (13.7% with it, 14.3% without). Their circuit model predicts that attention acts mainly on inhibitory neurons, a prediction still to be tested.

### Holding a goal: thalamus and prefrontal cortex in mice

In mice running a delayed non-match-to-sample task in a T-maze, Bolkan and colleagues (2017) inhibited pathways selectively. Input from the **mediodorsal thalamus** (a thalamic nucleus, or cluster of neurons, connected with prefrontal cortex) to medial prefrontal cortex supported maintenance during the delay, and the reverse projection supported the later choice. Delay firing in medial prefrontal cortex needed the thalamic input, yet those delay-tuned neurons overlapped minimally with neurons tuned to position in the maze.

### Memories that go quiet

In humans, Rose and colleagues (2016) cued people to set one remembered item aside. That item's category could no longer be decoded from the EEG, but a single TMS pulse briefly made it decodable again, and only while it might still be needed. The authors suggest that short-term synaptic changes could hold such latent items. The synapses were not measured, and because decoding was at the category level, reinstated category context is an alternative to reactivation of the item itself.
