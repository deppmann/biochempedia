    story: >-
      Enterochromaffin cells make most of the body's serotonin, yet they are under one percent of the
      intestinal epithelium, so finding one to record from in the intestine is hard. The paper, co-led by the Julius and Ingraham laboratories,
      solved that by growing mouse intestinal organoids in which the enterochromaffin cells carried
      a genetic tag. With the cells marked, the authors could patch-clamp and image single cells
      and ask the question a sensory physiologist asks of any receptor cell: what does it detect,
      and does it fire? They reported that the cells express chemosensory receptors, including TRPA1

      electrically excitable. Fibres carrying 5-HT3 receptors made synapse-like contact with the cells, and in an ex vivo

      antagonist alosetron blocked. Those recorded fibres were pelvic (spinal) afferents, not shown to be vagal, and the work was done in organoids and ex vivo tissue rather than

      control of the cells to test their role in gut sensitivity in mice.

    story: >-
      Before 2018 the standard account was that the brain learned about the gut only through
      hormones released into tissue and blood. The work from Bohorquez's laboratory challenged it in
      steps. A 2015 paper showed that a cytoplasmic process on enteroendocrine cells, which the
      laboratory had earlier called a neuropod, puts them in direct contact with mucosal nerves. In 2018 a team including Kaelberer asked whether that contact is a synapse: they traced it with modified rabies virus, then recorded currents
      in cocultures and vagal firing in living mice. In 2022 Buchanan and colleagues put an
      optical fibre inside the duodenum of mice and asked whether silencing one cell type
      changed a choice between sugar and a sweetener. The sequence moved from anatomy to
      synaptic physiology to behaviour, and each step had a stated limit: the human data were
      one organoid donor.
  - name: "Charles Zuker"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory published that mice learn to prefer sugar and fat through gut-to-brain routes
      that do not need taste (Tan and colleagues, Nature, 2020; Li and colleagues, Nature, 2022).
    profileSourceAnchor: "Tan et al., Nature (2020), 'The gut-brain axis mediates sugar preference'; Li et al., Nature (2022), 'Gut-brain circuits for fat preference'."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      If a sweet drink is attractive because it tastes sweet, an animal that cannot taste sweetness
      should show no preference for sugar. Tan and colleagues tested that with mice lacking the
      sweet receptor T1R2/T1R3 or the taste-transduction channel TRPM5, offering sugar against a
      non-nutritive sweetener for 48 hours. The mutants still came to prefer sugar. The team then
      imaged vagal ganglion neurons, found populations that respond to sugar delivered to the gut
      but not to sweeteners, and found that silencing the sugar-activated neurons in the brainstem
      prevented the development of learned preference while sparing initial attraction. Li and colleagues repeated the
      logic for fat, using an emulsion against a sweetener, and found that fat had its own
      receptors and its own vagal cluster. The work gave a clean way to separate initial attraction
      in brief tests from what the gut teaches an animal to prefer.

techniques:
  - name: "Intestinal organoids with tagged cells, patch clamp and calcium imaging"
    blurb: >-
      Rare cells are tagged genetically in cultured mini-intestines so that single cells can be
      recorded and imaged (and, in the Kaelberer work, cocultured with nodose neurons). It tests what a cell detects and whether it
      is excitable, in a dish rather than in the intact animal.
  - name: "Monosynaptic rabies tracing"
    blurb: >-
      A modified rabies virus infects chosen starter cells and crosses synapses to the cells
      connected to them, which are then labelled. It shows connectivity, not the transmitter and
      not whether the connection matters for behaviour.
  - name: "Optogenetics with an intraluminal optical fibre"
    blurb: >-
      A light-gated channel or pump is placed in a cell type, and a fibre delivers light to it,
      here inside the intestine. Driving the cells tests sufficiency; silencing them tests
      necessity, for those cells, that light and that behaviour only.
  - name: "Two-bottle choice with lick counters and activity-dependent genetic access"
    blurb: >-
      Mice choose between two drinks while licks are counted electronically, separating initial
      attraction in brief tests from a preference that builds over hours or days. Neurons active during a stimulus
      can be given a genetic silencing or activating tool and the choice tested again.

A mouse that cannot taste sweetness still learns to prefer real sugar over a calorie-free sweetener. [Smell and taste](/lessons/smell-and-taste/) owns the tongue, so taste is only a control here; the sensor sits in the gut.

Three anatomical layers frame everything below. Food moves through the **lumen**, the hollow interior of the intestine, lined by a single-cell **epithelium** containing rare hormone-producing **enteroendocrine cells**. Beneath that lining run two sensory pathways: **vagal afferents** (for the nutrient pathway described here, cell bodies in the **nodose ganglion** and projections to the brainstem **caudal nucleus of the solitary tract**, **cNST**) and **spinal afferents** (including **pelvic** nerves from the colon).

> **The one idea.** In mice, gut epithelial cells sense chemicals and excite sensory nerves rapidly; each circuit link requires its own test.
>
> *Scope:* Apply the evidence rule to every circuit claim. Rapid epithelial-to-nerve excitation was demonstrated in selected mouse preparations, not every enteroendocrine cell, every luminal stimulus, or human behaviour; the rat GLP-1 studies tested other links. Synaptic and hormonal signals can coexist—in Kaelberer and colleagues' mouse sucrose assay, glutamate contributed to the rapid vagal response and CCK to sustained firing—so use route-specific evidence, not speed alone, to distinguish the mechanisms.

## First establish a sensory epithelial cell

**Enterochromaffin (EC) cells**, a serotonin-making subtype of enteroendocrine cell, produce over 90 percent of the body's serotonin yet are under 1 percent of intestinal epithelial cells—both background facts in Bellono and colleagues (Cell, 2017), co-led by the laboratories of David Julius and Holly Ingraham. Because their rarity blocked single-cell recording in intact gut, the authors genetically tagged EC cells in mouse intestinal organoids (cultured mini-intestines) and checked the findings in native tissue.

The tagged EC cells expressed chemosensory receptors—**TRPA1** as an irritant receptor and the odorant receptor **Olfr558** for a microbial metabolite—and were electrically excitable. Nerve fibres carrying **5-HT3 receptors** (fast, ionotropic serotonin receptors) formed synaptic-like contacts with them. In an **ex vivo** colon preparation (isolated living tissue in a bath), applying norepinephrine or the bacterial metabolite isovalerate to the epithelium evoked firing in single mucosal afferent fibres, blocked by the 5-HT3 antagonist alosetron.

**Predict.** Does this result show that EC cells synapse onto the vagus nerve?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The recorded fibres were pelvic, hence spinal, afferents; none was shown to be vagal. EC cells establish the receptor-cell logic. Next come partly different enteroendocrine populations and a direct vagal link.

## What each synapse experiment earns

Before 2018 the prevailing view, as Kaelberer and colleagues put it, was that the brain senses gut stimuli only through passive hormone release. Bohorquez and colleagues (J. Clin. Invest., 2015) showed that a basal cytoplasmic process on enteroendocrine cells, earlier called a **neuropod**, contacts mucosal nerves. Kaelberer and colleagues (Science, 2018) tested whether that contact is a fast synapse and named synaptically connected cells **neuropod cells**.

The first film walked through the five-step argument from anatomy to live-mouse firing; the table below adds the exact drivers, reagents, counts and limits where each inference stops.

| Step | Exact observation | Claim earned—and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Connectivity | Modified rabies virus given by enema to peptide YY (**PYY-Cre**) pups, or by oral gavage to cholecystokinin (**CCK-Cre**) pups, infected enteroendocrine cells and labelled nerves; in the colon with pseudotyped virus, labelling reached vagal nodose neurons in 2 of 5 mice with infected colon nerves. | A physical link reaches the vagus in some animals; tracing identifies neither the transmitter nor behavioural role. |
| Transmitter machinery | Enteroendocrine cells expressed **VGLUT1** (vesicular glutamate transporter 1) transcripts. | Fits glutamate packaging, but does not show a postsynaptic current. |
| Speed | In 9 cocultured enteroendocrine-cell–nodose-neuron pairs, light activation evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents within 60 to 800 ms. | The gut cell rapidly excites a vagal neuron in a dish, not yet in vivo. |
| Pharmacology | Kynurenic acid (3 mM), an ionotropic glutamate-receptor blocker, abolished the currents; washout restored them in 4 neurons. | Ionotropic glutamate receptors carry the current; washout recovery argues against cell damage. |

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The mouse. Cells from one intestine are nested observations, not independent animals. The count shows a minority contact fraction across 3 mice; because Pgp9.5 labels sensory fibres generally, it does not prove every contact is vagal or generalise to all enteroendocrine cells.

Two further limits follow once the synapse model is in place. First, coculture latency (milliseconds), in-vivo vagal firing (seconds) and learned preference (24 to 48 hours) measure different processes and cannot be merged. Second, optogenetic silencing is acute, but with constitutive Cre and a permanent Cre-dependent effector, targeted cells can include cells that expressed the driver earlier—which cells are targeted depends on the driver and labelling strategy, so it is not necessarily pure by mature cell type.

## Sugar without taste: exact genotypes and cell types

The second film separated **initial attraction** (early intake measured in a brief baseline or attraction test, which the film calls "first sip" as shorthand) from **learned preference** (the shift built by gut signals) and left the exact genotypes, sample sizes and cell counts to the text.

Tan and colleagues (Nature, 2020), from Charles Zuker's laboratory, paired sugar against acesulfame K at an equally attractive concentration. In the wild-type time course, sugar intake rose within 24 hours and predominated by 48 hours; mice lacking the sweet receptor **T1R2/T1R3** (n = 5) or taste channel **TRPM5** (n = 7) also shifted toward sugar in their respective assays. Vagal-ganglion imaging found neurons answering gut-delivered sugar but not sweeteners; intestinal phlorizin caused a dramatic loss of glucose responses. Silencing transmitter release in sugar-activated cNST neurons tagged with **TRAP2** (an activity-dependent genetic driver, n = 6 mice) prevented the development of sugar preference while sparing attraction to glucose and acesulfame K in separate tests against water. **Chemogenetic activation** (switching on engineered receptors with a selective drug) of proenkephalin (**Penk-Cre**) cNST neurons created preference for a previously less-preferred stimulus. That separated initial attraction from gut-driven learning while leaving the intestinal cell type open.

Buchanan and colleagues (Nature Neuroscience, 2022) tied CCK-labelled duodenal neuropod cells to the choice, reporting glutamatergic vagal transmission for sugar and **purinergic** (ATP/purine-mediated) transmission for sweetener. Across 132 CCK-GFP small-intestinal cells from 3 mice, 60.1 percent had only *Slc5a1* (encoding SGLT1) transcripts, 19.6 percent had both *Slc5a1* and *Tas1r3* (encoding T1R3) transcripts, 1.2 percent had only *Tas1r3*, and 19.1 percent had neither (mean percentages across the three mice). The tested vagal response to intestinal glucose depends on SGLT1; for sucrose, epithelial SGLT1 can transport glucose released during digestion, and the studies support distinct sensing mechanisms for sucralose and glucose. In Buchanan and colleagues' human experiment, duodenal organoids from one donor released glutamate after sucrose or a glucose analogue, with no detectable increase after sucralose: the authors say it suggests conservation; it is not human vagal transmission or behaviour.

## Sugar and fat can separate

Li and colleagues (Nature, 2022) tracked 48-hour choices between 1.5 percent **Intralipid** (a fat emulsion) and 3 mM acesulfame K with lick counters. Vagal imaging revealed a broad nutrient population (sugar, fat and amino acids) plus a fat-only cluster marked by **TRPA1** (here a vagal-neuron marker, distinct from EC-cell TRPA1).

| Perturbation in Li and colleagues (2022) | Target perturbed | Outcome in mice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| TRPM5 knockout | TRPM5-dependent taste transduction | Immediate attraction to fat was lost in a brief test, while strong learned fat preference still developed |
| SGLT1 knockout | Epithelial sugar transporter | No vagal neurons respond to intestinal sugar; fat responses remain |
| GPR40/GPR120 double or CD36/GPR40/GPR120 triple knockout | Fatty-acid sensors | Only these combined knockouts substantially reduce vagal fat responses |
| **VIP** (vasoactive intestinal peptide) vagal silencing | Vagal nutrient-relay neurons | Learned nutrient preference markedly impaired; immediate attraction to sugar and fat intact |

A **germline deletion** (whole-body knockout present from conception) is neither acute nor cell-specific: the whole-animal double knockout shows that GPR40 and GPR120 jointly contribute to normal fat-evoked responses, without identifying the requisite cell type or establishing that either receptor is individually necessary.

Li and colleagues' VIP-neuron counts also highlight denominators. Across 9 nodose ganglia, 60 of 203 VIP neurons were broad nutrient responders (60 / 203 = 29.6 percent, matching "approximately 30 percent"), answering sugar, fat and amino acids, and about 4 percent of VIP neurons were fat-only—roughly 0.04 × 203, or about 8 additional neurons. That makes fat-only neurons about 8 / (60 + 8), or roughly 12 percent, of the combined responding groups.

## Worked problem: measuring preference above chance

With equally accessible, side-balanced bottles, 50 percent is the equal-intake benchmark (a midpoint, not a floor). Subtract the treated share from the control share for the percentage-point change; to express that drop as a fraction of an above-50-percent control advantage, divide by the control share minus 50 percentage points.

After mice had developed a stable sucrose preference, Buchanan and colleagues used an intraluminal fibre to silence halorhodopsin-expressing CCK-labelled duodenal cells during a one-hour test of sucrose's share of total liquid intake (300 mM sucrose versus 15 mM sucralose). Under the same light, sucrose made up a mean of 90.8 percent of total liquid intake in controls (N = 5) and 58.9 percent in silenced mice (N = 8). In a separate pharmacological experiment, the same four mice consumed a mean of 82.4 percent sucrose by volume under vehicle and 44.0 percent under local duodenal glutamate-receptor blockade (N = 4).

For the silencing cohort, compute the advantage above the 50 percent equal-intake benchmark—the part that could be lost:

1. **Control above 50 percent:** 90.8 − 50 = 40.8 percentage points.
2. **Silenced above 50 percent:** 58.9 − 50 = 8.9 percentage points.
3. **Points lost:** 90.8 − 58.9 = 31.9 percentage points.
4. **Fraction lost:**

> **fraction lost = (control − silenced) / (control − 50) = 31.9 / 40.8 = 0.78, about 78 percent**

Reporting 31.9 points as "32 percent" leaves the drop unscaled; dividing by 90.8 (about 35 percent) folds the 50 percent equal-intake benchmark into the preference; and 8.9 / 40.8 (about 22 percent) is the fraction that remained.

## GLP-1: three routes, not one relay

Apply the guiding rule once more: sharing a molecule's name does not join two cells—each circuit link requires its own test. **GLP-1** (**glucagon-like peptide-1**) and its receptor (**GLP1R**) appear in three distinct places; keep them in separate lanes before drawing arrows.

| Lane | Cells and location | Supported role in the rodent evidence here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peripheral peptide | Enteroendocrine L cells in the gut | Nutrients trigger GLP-1 release, background in a rat study by Krieger and colleagues (Diabetes, 2016). |
| Central peptide | Preproglucagon (PPG) neurons in the brainstem | Brierley and colleagues (Nature Metabolism, 2021) concluded that central and peripheral GLP-1 systems suppress eating through independent gut–brain circuits. |
| Receptor-defined afferent | Vagal neurons expressing GLP1R | Williams and colleagues (Cell, 2016) reported **intraganglionic laminar endings** (stretch-sensing terminals in the gut muscle layers) acting as gastrointestinal mechanoreceptors. |

**Predict.** A student proposes "gut L-cell GLP-1 → vagal GLP1R neuron → brainstem PPG neuron → eating falls." Which rodent findings break that chain?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Oxytocin-receptor vagal afferents provide the predominant vagal input to brainstem PPG neurons, rather than GLP1R afferents. PPG neurons can suppress eating, yet they were dispensable for the eating suppression produced by liraglutide and semaglutide in the mouse tests. Together these results argue against an obligatory serial relay.

Williams and colleagues (2016) sharpened that third lane by contrasting it with **GPR65** vagal neurons, which account for much of the vagal innervation of proximal duodenal **villi** (finger-like projections of the mucosal lining); the tested GPR65 neurons did not respond to GLP-1 or cholecystokinin. Over 95 percent of GLP1R neurons and over 97 percent of GPR65 neurons were slow C fibres (see Going deeper for a note on the paper's 2025 erratum). The [vagal sensory neurons lesson](/lessons/vagal-sensory-neurons/) develops their endings.

## Design problem

A reader says that a gut hormone "tells the brain through the vagus." Design experiments that decide, for one nutrient, whether the message is hormonal or synaptic and where the receptor that matters sits.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use coculture to check for a vesicular-transporter transcript and test for a fast, reversible glutamate-receptor-dependent current with washout recovery. During the same nutrient challenge in vivo, test the glutamate and candidate hormone pathways separately and together while measuring early and sustained vagal responses and behaviour—because perturbing the epithelial cell alone can alter both neurotransmitter and hormone release together. Use receptor deletion restricted to the candidate cell class and rescue to identify where the receptor that matters sits. Acute epithelial cell silencing and activation test whether the labelled cells are necessary for the evoked vagal response and sufficient to raise firing.

State the tool limits: with constitutive Cre and a permanent Cre-dependent effector, optogenetics is acute but not necessarily pure by mature cell type; a germline receptor deletion is neither acute nor cell-specific. Compare a receptor deletion restricted to one neuronal class with gut-afferent interruption on the same behaviour, adding malaise controls and separating initial attraction in brief tests from learned preference. All route evidence here is rodent, whereas human data are one organoid donor and one pharmacokinetic study, so repeat route tests before making claims about people.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Enterochromaffin cells in an intact mouse

In Bellono and colleagues (2017), Olfr558 was also tested in HEK293 cells. In Bayrer and colleagues (Nature, 2023), enhancing EC-cell function in vivo was sufficient to cause hypersensitivity to gut distension in mice; EC-cell output was needed for isovalerate-induced afferent sensitization in ex vivo gut–nerve preparations and for the observed in vivo sensitization to colorectal distension in males, while the EC-to-mucosal-afferent circuit appeared tonically engaged in females.

### GLP-1 drugs do not identify one physiological route

In rats, vagal deafferentation did not block the weight effect of a long-acting GLP-1 receptor agonist given for 14 days: weight gain was about 10 percent lower than controls in both groups (Secher and colleagues, J. Clin. Invest., 2014). In mice, fluorescently labelled liraglutide bound arcuate neurons after peripheral injection (Secher and colleagues, 2014). Deleting GLP1R from glutamatergic (vGlut2) neurons eliminated liraglutide's weight effect, while deletion from GABAergic (vGAT) neurons did not (Adams and colleagues, Diabetes, 2018). None of this is a statement about people.

The one human measurement here is pharmacokinetic, reflecting rapid N-terminal enzymatic breakdown of native peptide in blood: after intravenous GLP-1(7-36) amide, intact peptide was 19.9 percent of the rise in immunoreactivity measured with a COOH-terminal assay (Deacon and colleagues, Diabetes, 1995; n = 8 normal subjects). Vagal GLP-1 receptors still matter physiologically in rodents: knockdown in rat nodose neurons enlarged meals, sped gastric emptying and raised postmeal glucose, with normal long-term energy balance (Krieger and colleagues, 2016). In Williams and colleagues (2016), a 2025 erratum corrects only an EKG trace in Fig. 3A; the reported C-fibre percentages for GLP1R and GPR65 neurons are unaffected.
