scientists:
  - name: "Marc Tessier-Lavigne"
    contribution: >-
      With colleagues in Thomas Jessell's laboratory showed that the floor plate releases something
      that draws commissural axons out of rat spinal-cord explants across collagen, then led the
      laboratory that purified the netrins from embryonic chick brain and showed that DCC is a
      netrin receptor.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Tessier-Lavigne, Placzek, Lumsden, Dodd & Jessell, Nature 336:775 (1988); Serafini et al., Cell 78:409 (1994); Keino-Masu et al., Cell 87:175 (1996)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In 1988 Tessier-Lavigne, Marysia Placzek, Andrew Lumsden, Jane Dodd and Thomas Jessell set
      pieces of embryonic rat spinal cord in a gel of collagen. A piece of floor plate placed near
      a piece of dorsal spinal cord drew bundles of commissural axons out of the dorsal piece,
      toward the floor plate, with no contact between them. Something had crossed the gel. The
      assay then became a detector. In 1994 his laboratory, with Tito Serafini as first author and Timothy Kennedy as second, fractionated membranes from embryonic chick brain and returned each
      fraction to spinal-cord explants to ask whether the activity was still there. Two proteins
      came through, netrin-1 and netrin-2, and their sequences matched UNC-6, a worm guidance
      protein. A companion paper showed that cells secreting netrin attract commissural axons at a
      distance in vitro. In 1996 his laboratory, with Kazuko Keino-Masu and Masayuki Masu, showed
      that DCC binds netrin-1 and that an antibody to DCC blocks netrin-evoked
      outgrowth. The cue and its receptor could now be tested in any system.
  - name: "Edward Hedgecock"
    contribution: >-
      With Joseph Culotti and David Hall, showed in the worm C. elegans that unc-6 is needed for
      cells and axons to migrate both toward and away from the ventral side, unc-40 mainly for
      movement toward it and unc-5 mainly for movement away.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Hedgecock, Culotti & Hall, Neuron 4:61 (1990); Ishii et al., Neuron 9:873 (1992)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The worm C. elegans is transparent, and many of its cells can be named and found again in
      every animal. The unc genes are named for the uncoordinated movement of their mutants. In
      1990 Hedgecock, Culotti and Hall followed pioneer axons, the first axons to grow along a
      route, and mesodermal cells as they migrated around the body, between the ventral and
      dorsal sides, in mutants of three of these genes. In unc-6 mutants, migrations in both
      directions went wrong. In unc-5 mutants, mainly movement away from the ventral side failed;
      in unc-40 mutants, mainly movement toward it. The genetics sorted the migrations into two pathways that
      both needed unc-6, though the 1990 paper itself proposed two opposing gradients. In 1992 Naoaki Ishii, William
      Wadsworth and colleagues working with Culotti and Hedgecock found that UNC-6 is a protein related to laminin. When netrin was purified from chick brain two years later and
      matched UNC-6, the worm genes pointed to where the vertebrate receptors might be found.
  - name: "Mu-ming Poo"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory developed the repeated-pulse growth cone turning assay and, with Guo-li Ming, Hong-jun
      Song and colleagues, showed that frog spinal growth cones attracted by netrin-1 are repelled
      by it when cAMP signalling is lowered or PKA is blocked.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Ming et al., Neuron 19:1225 (1997); Ming et al., Nature 417:411 (2002)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Poo's laboratory studied single growth cones of spinal neurons from embryos of the frog
      Xenopus, grown in a dish. A fine pipette placed near the growth cone released short, regular
      pulses of a test molecule, which set up a gradient across the growth cone, and time-lapse
      imaging showed which way it turned. In 1997 Guo-li Ming, Hong-jun Song and colleagues
      reported that these growth cones turn toward a netrin-1 source. When cAMP signalling was
      blocked, with a cAMP look-alike that blocks protein kinase A or an inhibitor of it, the same growth
      cones turned away. An antibody to DCC abolished both responses. In 2002 Ming and colleagues showed that the
      growth cones adapt to a raised background of netrin. Together the papers turned one cue into
      a readout of the receiving cell's state.

techniques:
  - name: "Collagen-gel explant co-culture"
    blurb: >-
      Small pieces of embryonic tissue are embedded at set distances in a three-dimensional
      collagen gel. If axons leave one piece and grow toward another without touching it, the
      second piece is releasing something that diffuses. The same set-up, with the floor plate
      replaced by a test fraction, became the assay that purified netrin.
  - name: "Activity-guided protein purification"
    blurb: >-
      A tissue extract is separated step by step by chromatography, and after each step every
      fraction is tested in the living assay. The fractions that keep the activity are carried
      forward, so the biological response, not a guess about the molecule, decides what to keep.
  - name: "Growth cone turning assay"
    blurb: >-
      A pipette releases pulses of a cue near one cultured growth cone, off to one side of it, and time-lapse imaging measures the turning angle. Drugs or
      antibodies in the bath show which receptors and second messengers the turn needs.
  - name: "Forward genetics in C. elegans"
    blurb: >-
      Mutants are found by their behaviour, here uncoordinated movement, and then examined cell by
      cell. Comparing which migrations fail in which mutant sorts genes into a pathway before any
      protein is known.

      - "Their axons ignore UNC-6 and keep their normal course"
      - "Their axons grow ventrally toward UNC-6 faster"

    rationale: >-
      Hamelin and colleagues did this experiment in 1993. With UNC-5 the touch-neuron axons grew
      dorsally, away from the ventral UNC-6, and the new course still needed UNC-6. UNC-5 adds a
      response away from the cue; it did not strengthen the pull toward it. The axons did not ignore
      the cue, since their new course depended on UNC-6. They did not collapse and retract either;
      they kept growing, on a new route.
  - stem: "In frog spinal growth cones, netrin-1 caused attraction, and netrin-1 with a PKA inhibitor caused repulsion. Which further result showed that both responses ran through the same receptor?"
    choices:
      - "An antibody to DCC blocked both attraction and the repulsion"
      - "A cAMP analogue that activates PKA made the repulsion stronger"
      - "Laminin in the dish raised the cAMP level of the growth cones"
      - "An antibody to DCC blocked attraction but left repulsion intact"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      If blocking DCC abolishes both the turn toward netrin and the turn away, both turns need DCC,
      so the receptor stays the same and the cell's internal state picks the direction. Blocking
      attraction alone would point to a second receptor for repulsion. Activating PKA would be expected to favour
      attraction rather than strengthening repulsion, since lowering PKA activity is what caused
      the repulsion. Laminin lowers cAMP in frog retinal growth cones (a result covered under Going deeper), and in any case says nothing
      about which receptor carries the turn.
  - stem: "In mice, netrin-1 is deleted only from floor plate cells, and most spinal commissural axons still reach the floor plate. When it is deleted only from ventricular-zone progenitors, many commissural axons stray. What do these results most directly suggest?"
    choices:
      - "Netrin acts only as a long-range gradient spreading from the midline"
      - "Commissural axons reach the floor plate without needing any netrin"
      - "Progenitor netrin laid down near the pia does much of the guiding"
      - "Floor plate netrin is the only netrin commissural axons can read"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Losing progenitor netrin causes errors while losing floor plate netrin causes few, so netrin
      made by ventricular-zone progenitors and deposited near the pial surface, along the axons'
      path, does much of the work, as 2017 studies reported. If floor plate netrin were the only
      source that mattered, its loss would cause the larger defect. The axons do need netrin, since
      the progenitor deletion causes errors. A purely long-range gradient from the midline would
      depend on the floor plate source, which these results do not show. Later work found the
      floor plate source can still contribute.
  - stem: "In the 1994 purification of netrins, what tissue supplied the starting material?"
    choices:
      - "Floor plates dissected from embryonic rat spinal cord"
      - "Retinas dissected from embryonic frog eyes"
      - "Whole adult worms grown in liquid culture"
      - "Membranes prepared from embryonic chick brain"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Netrins were purified from membranes of embryonic chick brain, with rat spinal-cord explants
      as the assay. Rat floor plates were the suspected source but are far too small to purify a
      protein from; they were used in the 1988 assay instead. Worms gave the genetics of UNC-6 but
      were not the source of purified netrin. Frog retinas were used later, in turning assays, not

  - stem: "A bead releasing netrin sits in a collagen gel. At steady state the concentration at distance r from the bead is C = k/r. A growth cone 10 µm wide faces the bead, so it compares concentrations at r and at r + 10 µm. What is the approximate fractional difference in concentration across the growth cone at r = 50 µm and at r = 1,000 µm?"
    choices:
      - "1% at 50 µm and 20% at 1,000 µm"
      - "2% at 50 µm and 0.1% at 1,000 µm"
      - "20% at 50 µm and 20% at 1,000 µm"
      - "20% at 50 µm and 1% at 1,000 µm"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      For C = k/r the fractional difference across a small width w is about w/r: 10/50, or 20% (exactly 17%, still closest to this choice),
      near the bead, and 10/1,000, or 1%, a millimetre away, so the comparison gets much harder
      with distance. The same 20% at both distances would hold only for a gradient whose
      fractional slope is constant, such as an exponential, not for this one. The answer with 1%
      near and 20% far reverses the trend; the gradient is steepest close to the source. The answer
      with 2% and 0.1% divides by ten an extra time.

  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      Netrins are secreted, laminin-related guidance proteins conserved from worms (UNC-6) to
      vertebrates. Netrin-1 was purified from embryonic chick brain by its ability to evoke
      outgrowth of commissural axons from rat spinal-cord explants, and it is made by the floor
      plate and, in the mouse, by ventricular-zone progenitors. DCC (UNC-40 in worms, Frazzled in
      flies) mediates attraction. UNC5 receptors mediate repulsion, in some systems together with DCC. In cultured Xenopus spinal
      neurons, attraction through DCC requires cAMP and PKA activity; lowering either converts the
      turn to repulsion through the same receptor. In Xenopus retinal growth cones, laminin-1
      lowers cAMP and makes netrin repulsive, which may help axons leave the retina at the optic
      disk (optic nerve head).
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      Netrin-1 has separate receptor-binding sites, which structures
      suggest lets one netrin bridge different receptors, such as DCC and UNC5, and so encode
      opposite outputs in the composition of the receptor complex. A second layer is cytosolic:
      in Xenopus spinal growth cones the ratio of cAMP to cGMP sets the direction of netrin-1
      turns, reportedly by regulating calcium channels. Reading the gradient is the third problem. For steady diffusion from a point
      source, the fractional difference across a growth cone of width w at distance r is about
      w/r, so a 10 micrometre growth cone a millimetre away sees about 1 percent. Assays with
      controlled gradients of nerve growth factor report that sensory axons are extremely sensitive, but only
      over a limited concentration range, and frog growth cones desensitize and resensitize in a
      raised background of netrin.


      An open question is how much of netrin's guidance in vivo is diffusible at all. Conditional
      deletions in the mouse in 2017 found that netrin-1 made by ventricular-zone progenitors and
      deposited at the pial surface does much of the guiding of commissural axons in the spinal
      cord and hindbrain, and 2019 studies found that floor plate netrin still contributes. Which
      functions of netrin depend on a gradient, and which on netrin bound to a surface, remain
      under study.

**Put a scrap of floor plate from an embryonic rat spinal cord in a gel near a scrap of dorsal spinal cord, and axons grow out of the dorsal scrap toward the floor plate, though the two never touch.**

Something crossed the gel. That something is netrin, a secreted guidance cue. Yet the same protein draws some axons toward the midline and pushes others away, and in the living embryo much of it does not have to travel far. This lesson asks how netrin was found, how the growth cone (the moving, sensing tip of a growing axon; see [the lesson on the growth cone](/lessons/growth-cone/)) reads it, and what decides which way the growth cone turns.

## The layout

Most arguments in this lesson happen on one map, which the first film draws. Picture the embryonic spinal cord cut crosswise, like a slice through a tube, at the stage when commissural axons are growing.

- **The floor plate** is a strip of cells along the ventral midline, at the bottom of the slice.
- **The ventricular zone** lines the central canal, on the inside of the tube. Its cells are **progenitors**, dividing cells that make neurons and glia. Each sends a long fibre outward to the edge of the cord.
- **The pial surface** is that outer edge, covered by a basement membrane, a thin sheet of extracellular matrix.
- Many **commissural neurons** sit in the dorsal cord. Their axons grow ventrally, close to the pial surface, then turn inward to the floor plate and cross it to the other side.

Ventricular-zone progenitors line the central canal and send fibres to the outer pial surface; the floor plate sits at the ventral midline. A commissural neuron in the dorsal cord sends its axon down near the pial surface, then inward through the floor plate to the other side." caption="The map for this lesson: in the embryonic spinal cord, commissural axons grow ventrally close to the pial surface, then turn inward and cross the floor plate at the ventral midline. Ventricular-zone progenitors line the canal and reach the pial surface with long fibres." />

Two more travellers, each on its own map, complete the picture.

- **Trochlear motor axons** belong to another part of the neural tube, near the junction of midbrain and hindbrain, not to this spinal slice. There they start near the ventral midline and grow dorsally, away from the floor plate, the opposite way from commissural axons.
- **In the worm *C. elegans***, cells and pioneer axons (the first axons to lay down a route) migrate around the body between its ventral and dorsal sides. The worm's netrin, UNC-6, is made on the ventral side.

The map raises three puzzles.

1. **What crosses the gel?** The floor plate releases something. What is it?
2. **How can one cue** pull commissural axons toward the ventral midline and push trochlear axons away from it?
3. **Is the cue a gradient spreading from the midline**, or something laid down along the path the axons travel?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Netrin marks a place; the growth cone decides what to do there. Where netrin is (its source, its slope, the surface it lies on) supplies the *where*. The growth cone supplies the *sign*, attraction or repulsion: its receptors first (DCC usually draws it in; UNC5 receptors add repulsion, sometimes together with DCC), and in some cells its internal signalling state (such as cAMP or calcium), which a matrix protein outside it, laminin, can change.

**What the rule covers.** Netrin's steering of growth cones and migrating cells in the systems used here: the worm's dorsal-ventral migrations, vertebrate commissural and trochlear axons, and frog spinal and retinal growth cones. It has three limits, each met below. It does not say how far the cue spreads (the mouse spinal cord is a case in point). "Decides" means receptors plus internal state, not receptor identity alone. And it says nothing about netrin's other jobs: promoting outgrowth, acting on adhesion, and working through other receptors and partners in some tissues.

## Something crosses the gel

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

In 1988, in Thomas Jessell's laboratory, Marc Tessier-Lavigne and colleagues cut pieces of embryonic rat spinal cord and embedded them in a three-dimensional gel of collagen. Each piece was an **explant**, living tissue cultured outside the embryo. Some explants held dorsal spinal cord, where the commissural neurons sit; others held floor plate.

**Predict.** A dorsal explant sits in the gel on its own, with no floor plate nearby. What should it do?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

On its own, a dorsal explant sent out few or no axons. That is the control. With a floor plate explant nearby, bundles of commissural axons grew out of the dorsal explant toward the floor plate. The explants were apart, so the effect had to cross the gel.

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/netrin-and-dcc/floor-plate-gel-assay.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two collagen-gel dishes seen from above. A dorsal spinal cord explant alone sends out few or no axons; with a floor plate explant nearby but not touching, bundles of axons curve across the gel toward it." caption="Dorsal spinal cord explants in collagen gel send out few or no commissural axons on their own, but with a floor plate explant nearby, commissural axon bundles grow toward it though the two never touch." />

A cue that draws axons toward its source from a distance is a **chemoattractant**, and steering by a diffusing cue is **chemotropism**. That fits one of the four classes of cue you met in [the lesson on guidance cues and pioneers](/lessons/guidance-cues-and-pioneers/): long-range attraction. It does not tell you what the molecule is. Picture smelling bread from down the street. You know there is a bakery, and you know which way to walk, but not the recipe. Unlike bread, though, whether netrin draws you in depends on who is smelling it.

## From an activity to a molecule

To purify a protein you need a lot of tissue, and floor plates are tiny. In 1994 Tessier-Lavigne's laboratory kept the rat assay as a detector and looked for the same activity in a larger source. Membranes from embryonic chick brain had it. Thousands of brains went into the work. The extract was separated over several chromatography columns, and after each step every fraction went back onto rat spinal-cord explants. The axons reported which fractions to keep.

Two proteins came through. Their sequences were related to laminin, a protein of the extracellular matrix, and to UNC-6, the product of a worm gene. The authors named them netrin-1 and netrin-2. Each promoted commissural outgrowth.

**Outgrowth is not direction.** The purification assay scored whether axons grew out of an explant, not which way they pointed, and a cue can promote growth without steering it. A companion paper supplied direction. Floor plate cells make netrin-1 RNA, and cells engineered to secrete netrin attracted commissural axons at a distance in the gel. That answers the first puzzle: netrin is a cue that can cross the gel.

## The worm already knew: one cue, two readers

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Before vertebrate netrin had a name, worm genetics had found its logic. In 1990 Edward Hedgecock and colleagues followed cells and pioneer axons as they migrated around the body of *C. elegans*, toward or away from the ventral side, in mutants of three genes. Keep in mind that UNC-6 is made ventrally.

| Gene lost | Movement toward ventral | Movement away from ventral | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *unc-6* (the cue) | disrupted | disrupted | both directions need the cue |
| *unc-40* | mainly fails | largely normal | the "toward" reader: attraction |
| *unc-5* | largely normal | mainly fails | the "away" reader: repulsion |

The asymmetry matters: *unc-40* mutants have strong defects in movement toward the ventral side and weaker defects in movement away. In the migrations compared here, *unc-5* mutants disrupt movement away from the ventral side while sparing movement toward it. Without UNC-6, cells and axons still move, but many take the wrong route. One cue, two readers, two directions. In 1992 UNC-6 turned out to be a laminin-related protein, and UNC-5 a protein that spans the membrane.

The table links genes to directions. To test the link, change the reader and leave the cue alone.

**Predict.** Worm touch neurons do not normally carry UNC-5, and their axons do not grow dorsally. Force them to make UNC-5. Which way do their axons grow, and does the new route still need UNC-6?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In 1993 Michel Hamelin and colleagues in Joseph Culotti's laboratory did this. With UNC-5, the touch-neuron axons grew dorsally, away from the ventral UNC-6, and this still needed UNC-6. Changing the receptor changed the answer to an unchanged cue.

</details>

## The vertebrate readers: DCC and UNC5

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

UNC-40 turned out to resemble a human protein, DCC (Deleted in Colorectal Cancer), found in 1990 on a stretch of chromosome 18 often lost in colorectal tumours and studied as a candidate tumour suppressor (see [the lesson on oncogenes](/lessons/oncogenes/)). In 1996 Tessier-Lavigne's laboratory, working with rat embryos, spinal explants and cultured cells, showed three things.

1. DCC is on commissural axons.
2. Netrin-1 binds cultured cells displaying DCC.
3. An antibody to DCC selectively blocks netrin-evoked outgrowth from spinal explants.

In the same journal issue, a worm study showed that UNC-40 is needed in the moving cells themselves.

The mouse confirmed both partners. Mice with little or no netrin-1 (Serafini and colleagues, 1996) had misrouted spinal commissural axons and lacked several forebrain commissures, and mice lacking Dcc (Fazeli and colleagues, 1997) had similar defects. Remove the cue or remove the reader, and commissural axons fail in much the same way.

Repulsion has a vertebrate example too. In 1995 Sophia Colamarino and Tessier-Lavigne found that netrin-1 repels trochlear motor axons in explant cultures. So the second puzzle is real in vertebrates, and the worm suggests its answer: different readers, opposite turns. In the netrin-1 mutant, though, trochlear axons were largely normal, so other repellents also guide them.

The fly shows how the repulsive reader works. Its Unc5 receptor is a repulsive netrin receptor: Unc5 alone is enough for short-range repulsion, and long-range repulsion also needs Frazzled, the fly's DCC-family receptor (Keleman and Dickson, 2001). That is the receptor layer of the rule; treat it as a starting point.

## Same receptor, opposite turn

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

Receptors are one layer. In some cells the growth cone's internal state is a second. Mu-ming Poo's laboratory developed a turning assay: a fine pipette puffs short, regular pulses of a cue near one cultured growth cone, off to one side, and time-lapse imaging measures the turn. In 1997 Guo-li Ming and colleagues found that growth cones of frog (*Xenopus*) spinal neurons turn toward netrin-1.

Next they blocked cAMP signalling, in one test with Rp-cAMPS, a cAMP look-alike that blocks protein kinase A (PKA, the enzyme cAMP switches on), and in another with an inhibitor of PKA. (For how cAMP activates PKA, see [the lesson on cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/).)

**Predict.** With PKA blocked, puff netrin-1 beside the growth cone. Does it turn toward, turn away, or not turn? Then add an antibody to DCC. If the receptor is unchanged, what should the antibody block?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The same growth cones turned away. An antibody to DCC stopped both turns, the attraction and the repulsion. The receptor was the same, and the cell's cAMP chose the direction in this system.

</details>

This is one worked example in one system, not a rule for every netrin-responsive axon. Calcium and, in frog retinal growth cones, the matrix protein laminin, which lowers their cAMP, can also change the response (Going deeper follows both).

## Where is the netrin?

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

The gel shows what netrin *can* do from a distance. The third puzzle asks what the embryo relies on. Two questions sit inside it: can a growth cone read a gradient at all, and where is the netrin that the embryo's axons actually use?

### In the dish: reading a shallow gradient

A growth cone steering in a gradient compares the cue on one side with the cue on the other, a few micrometres apart. Work it in three steps.

*Step 1: the concentration difference.* For steady diffusion from a small source in three dimensions, such as a bead in a gel, with no cue lost on the way, concentration falls as 1/r (the same flow of cue spreads over ever larger spheres), and the fractional difference across a growth cone is roughly its width over its distance from the source:

> **fractional difference ≈ w / r** (for C = k / r; w = growth cone width, r = distance)

A 10 micrometre growth cone 100 micrometres away sees about a 10 percent difference. A millimetre away it sees 1 percent.

*Step 2: receptors read occupancy, not concentration.* A growth cone does not measure netrin directly. It measures how many of its receptors are bound. In the simplest binding model the fraction bound is C / (C + Kd), where Kd is the concentration that fills half the receptors.

**Predict.** Give a growth cone the same 10 percent concentration difference across its width, at three background levels: a hundredth of Kd, Kd itself, and a hundred times Kd. At which background is the difference in bound receptors between its two sides largest?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

At Kd. The simple binding model gives these fractions bound.

| Background netrin | Bound, far side | Bound, near side | Difference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a hundredth of Kd | 0.99% | 1.09% | 0.1 points |
| Kd | 50.0% | 52.4% | 2.4 points |
| a hundred times Kd | 99.01% | 99.10% | 0.1 points |

Far below Kd, almost no receptors are bound on either side. Far above it, almost all are. Either way the same 10 percent concentration difference becomes a sliver of occupancy.

</details>

Binding is also noisy, since ligand molecules arrive and leave at random, so one snapshot of occupied receptors cannot be trusted and a difference of a tenth of a point is easily lost.

*Step 3: what adaptation can and cannot do.* Fixed receptors give the most signal in a band of backgrounds around Kd, yet growth cones meet netrin at very different levels, near a source and far from it. A growth cone that resets its baseline, **adapting** to the background, keeps its internal response from sticking at one level, so it can respond again as the background changes. It cannot undo the binding limit: if Kd is unchanged, the occupancy difference is still small and noisy far from Kd. Both halves have been seen, in different systems: sensory axons in controlled gradients of nerve growth factor were extremely sensitive, but only within a narrow range of concentrations (Rosoff and colleagues, 2004), and frog growth cones in a raised background of netrin-1 lost sensitivity and then regained it (Ming and colleagues, 2002).

### In the embryo: which source do the axons need?

Go back to the map. Netrin-1 is made in two of its places: by floor plate cells, and by ventricular-zone progenitors. The progenitors send their netrin-1 out along their fibres, and it builds up at the pial surface, along the stretch the commissural axons travel. That gives two models to test in the embryo: a gradient diffusing from the floor plate, as in the gel, or a path of netrin held on the surface the axons grow along. Guidance by a cue bound to a surface, unlike chemotropism, is called **haptotaxis**.

**Predict.** In 2017 two groups, Dominici and colleagues and Varadarajan and colleagues, deleted the netrin-1 gene from one source at a time in mice, by conditional deletion, which removes a gene only in chosen cells. If a gradient from the floor plate did most of the guiding, which deletion should cause more errors?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The floor plate deletion should, and it did not. Without floor plate netrin, most spinal commissural axons stayed on course and reached the midline. Without progenitor netrin, many commissural axons went off course. A third study found the same pattern in the hindbrain.

</details>

**What this shows, and what it does not.** A deletion shows which source the axons need, not how they read it. A netrin path laid along the pia fits the result, and so does a short-range gradient near it; the experiment does not separate the two. Nor is the floor plate idle. In 2019 two further studies found that floor plate netrin still contributes, working with progenitor netrin and, in one study, with Sonic hedgehog, another floor plate cue.

Netrin dots sit at the floor plate and travel out along progenitor fibres from the cells lining the canal, collecting in a line along the pial surface, where a commissural axon grows down to the floor plate. An inset compares two deletions: without floor plate netrin, three of four axons still reach the floor plate; without progenitor netrin, only one does and three wander." caption="Netrin-1 comes from two sources: the floor plate, and ventricular-zone progenitors whose netrin collects at the pial surface along the commissural axons' path. So, for commissural axons in the mouse spinal cord and hindbrain, much of the netrin that matters seems to act close to where progenitors lay it down, with the floor plate keeping a part in the spinal cord. Elsewhere netrin's range has to be tested place by place. That answers the third puzzle, for this system.

### Design problem

How would you experimentally test whether netrin acts as a long-range diffusible gradient versus a short-range contact cue? Design a simple in vitro experiment.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use commissural explants from the dorsal spinal cord in a three-dimensional collagen gel. In one set, place aggregates of cells that secrete netrin at measured distances, say 100, 300 and 600 micrometres, with a porous filter or a clear gap so that no cell can touch the explant. Score outgrowth and turning toward the source at each distance. In a second set, offer netrin bound to the substrate in a stripe or spot, with no secreting cells, and ask whether axons grow only on the netrin or turn toward it from off the stripe.

Turning at a distance, falling off as the source moves away, supports a diffusible cue. Growth only on bound netrin, with no turning from a distance, supports a contact cue. A cue could pass both tests, and netrin can act in both forms in vitro.

</details>

## Putting it together

The three puzzles share one answer: netrin marks a place; the growth cone decides what to do there. The gel showed the cue can travel; the worm showed that changing the reader changes the sign, and vertebrate studies named the readers; the frog growth cone showed the reader's state can flip it; and the mouse showed where the netrin that matters lies.

Commissural axons pose one more puzzle. Once across the floor plate, they do not turn back toward it. What changes in the reader after it crosses, with Slit and Robo signalling, is the subject of [crossing the midline](/lessons/crossing-the-midline/).

## Going deeper

### Binding sites and a retracted paper

A 2014 crystal structure showed that one netrin can bind DCC at two sites, a DCC-specific one and a second, generic one, and that UNC5A can take DCC's place at the generic site, which the authors proposed switches the response from attraction to repulsion (Finci and colleagues). A 1999 frog-neuron paper that had reported this switch with chimeric receptors (Hong and colleagues) was retracted in 2023, so this lesson does not rely on it.

### Calcium, laminin and the retinal exit

Calcium is part of the readout. In 2003 Makoto Nishiyama and colleagues reported that the ratio of cAMP to cGMP in these growth cones sets the direction of the netrin-1 turn by changing the activity of L-type calcium channels (see [the lesson on calcium signaling](/lessons/calcium-signaling/)). The eye shows why a flip might be useful. Retinal axons run along the inner surface of the retina to the optic disk (optic nerve head), the exit, where netrin-1 is concentrated. In mice lacking netrin-1 or DCC the axons still reach the optic disk but fail to leave through it, so netrin acts locally there. In 1999 Vivian Höpker and colleagues found that laminin-1 turns netrin's attraction into repulsion in frog retinal growth cones, and that laminin-1 lowers their cAMP. Laminin-1 lies on the retinal surface. Their model: where laminin and netrin overlap, netrin repels, and this pushes axons down into the exit, where there is netrin without laminin.

### Real gradients are not ideal

Departures from the assumptions of Step 1 change the profile. The floor plate is a strip, not a point. In a long-line approximation with an outer boundary R, C is proportional to ln(R/r) and the fractional difference is about w / [r ln(R/r)], which is shallower than w/r once R is more than about 2.7 times r. Netrin also binds the extracellular matrix. Reversible binding holds a bound pool and slows how fast the profile forms, but does not by itself steepen it. Returning to a steady small point source in homogeneous three-dimensional space, suppose netrin is irreversibly removed at a uniform first-order rate kC, where k has units of inverse time. The concentration outside the source is then C ∝ e^(−r/λ)/r, with λ = √(D/k). For w much smaller than both r and λ, the fractional difference is approximately w(1/r + 1/λ), so far from the source it does not fall as low as w/r. These equations describe the point-source model; a line or sheet source has a different profile.

For timing, if netrin's effective diffusion coefficient D is near 70 µm²/s (an illustrative value, not a measurement), the diffusion time scale r²/D over half a millimetre is about an hour. That is a time scale, not the time to a steady gradient: for a source switched on in an open gel, the concentration 500 µm out is only about half its final value after an hour and takes more than a day to reach 90 percent of it. Binding slows this further.

How much noise matters depends on numbers that are not measured for netrin. In an illustrative model with 10,000 independent receptors split between the two halves of a growth cone, the gradient whose signal just equals one standard deviation of the snapshot noise is about 4 percent at Kd and about 20 percent at a hundredth or a hundred times Kd. Reliable detection needs a larger signal than that. Averaging M independent snapshots over time cuts the noise by about √M.
