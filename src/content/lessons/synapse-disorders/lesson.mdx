    blurb: >-
      A blocking sequence flanked by loxP sites is placed in a gene so that the gene is silent.
      The animal also carries Cre recombinase fused to a modified oestrogen receptor, which enters
      the nucleus only after tamoxifen is given. Cre then removes the block, so you choose when
      the gene comes back on.
  - name: "Genetic dosage rescue"
    blurb: >-
      A mutant mouse is bred with a mouse carrying one broken copy of a second gene. If halving
      the second gene moves the mutant's traits toward typical, the two genes interact: the second
      gene's product may be part of the cause, or may compensate through another route. Fragile X mice with half the mGluR5 tested the mGluR theory.
  - name: "Long-term depression in hippocampal slices"
    blurb: >-
      Living slices of hippocampus are kept in oxygenated fluid, and synaptic responses are
      recorded before and after a drug that activates group I mGluRs. The lasting fall in response
      size measures synaptic weakening, and blocking translation shows whether it needs new protein.
  - name: "Mouse behaviour assays"
    blurb: >-
      Standard tests score how long a mouse spends near a stranger mouse in a three-chamber box,
      how many ultrasonic calls it makes, and how many marbles it buries or how long it
      grooms. They measure mouse behaviour, not autism, and are read as rough parallels.

**About a hundred genes now have strong evidence for a role in autism, and they are not a random list. Many build or run synapses, the junctions where neurons pass signals. Many others control which genes a neuron expresses. Different genetic routes seem to converge on a few shared jobs in neurons.**

That convergence raises a developmental question: where might these developmental mechanisms fail in autism spectrum disorders? Neurons must gather in the right places, send axons to the right targets, win trophic support, and then build, prune and maintain their synapses. The genes found so far cluster around building synapses, making protein at them, and setting gene expression in neurons. Changes there can shift how strongly a circuit is excited or held back. Autism itself is still diagnosed from behaviour, not from a gene, blood or scan test. So this lesson takes two single-gene conditions in which the gene is known, fragile X syndrome and Rett syndrome, and asks which step fails, how that changes a circuit, and what mouse experiments can and cannot show about people.

A note on words. Many autistic people prefer identity-first language ("autistic people"), and some prefer "people with autism"; this lesson mostly uses the first. Autism is a difference in how a brain develops, and the biology below is about mechanism, not about fixing people.

## The main idea: many genetic routes converge on the synapse

Twin studies estimate heritability at roughly 60 to 90 percent, meaning that most of the variation in who is diagnosed tracks genetic variation. No single gene explains it. Most of the genetic contribution is thought to come from many common variants, each with a tiny effect, while rare new mutations with large effects explain a smaller share of cases. Large sequencing studies have now named about a hundred genes with strong evidence. Two groups stand out: genes for synaptic proteins and genes that regulate chromatin and transcription.

The synaptic group fits the developmental question. Neurexins on the sending side bind neuroligins on the receiving side and help specify a synapse (see [CNS synapses](/lessons/cns-synapses/)); rare variants in *NRXN1*, *NLGN3* and *NLGN4X* have been found in some autistic people. SHANK3 is a scaffold protein that organizes receptors in the postsynaptic density of excitatory synapses. Mice lacking *Shank3* show altered synapses in the striatum and groom themselves excessively. Other syndromes act on growth signalling at synapses: *TSC1*, *TSC2* and *PTEN* mutations all raise mTOR activity (see [mTOR and AMPK](/lessons/mtor-and-ampk/)), and each carries a higher chance of autism. Pruning may matter too, through microglia and complement (see [microglia and complement](/lessons/microglia-complement-pruning/)). mTOR is a master regulator of protein synthesis, so these syndromes and fragile X, below, share a third theme: how much protein a synapse makes. Reviews of autism genetics describe similar points of convergence: synapse-related protein synthesis, synapse structure and signalling, and the regulation of chromatin and transcription.

The diagnosis itself rests on behaviour. Under DSM-5, published in 2013, autism spectrum disorder is defined by two areas of behaviour: differences in social communication and interaction, and restricted or repetitive behaviours and interests, present from early childhood. "Spectrum" means that support needs range widely, from people who need help every day to people who need little. Identified autism has become more common in US surveillance, and much of the rise is generally attributed to broader criteria, more screening and better access to diagnosis.

## From synapse to circuit: excitation and inhibition

A synapse either excites the next neuron, pushing it toward firing, or inhibits it, holding it back. In the cortex most inhibition comes from interneurons, local neurons that release GABA and make up about a fifth of all neurons. A working circuit keeps excitation and inhibition in balance. That balance is set during development, as synapses form and mature, and then has to be maintained.

The genes in the previous section can all move that balance. A change in synapse number, in the receptors at a synapse, in how much protein a synapse makes or in a neuron's own firing properties changes how strongly a circuit responds. It can do this in excitatory neurons or in interneurons. Reviews of fragile X syndrome, Rett syndrome and autism models keep returning to this bridge: altered excitation and inhibition links the molecular defect to the circuit.

John Rubenstein and Michael Merzenich proposed the idea for autism in 2003, partly because epilepsy is common in autistic people. It needs care. The change is not always toward more excitation. Its direction differs between brain regions, cell types and ages, and between models, and no single imbalance explains autism. The two syndromes below show both directions.

## Fragile X: a synapse that makes too much protein

Fragile X syndrome is the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability, and many people with it, more often males, are also autistic. Its cause is a repeat of three bases, CGG, near the start of the *FMR1* gene on the X chromosome. Most people have fewer than about 45 copies. Between about 55 and 200 copies is a premutation, which can grow when a mother passes it on. Above about 200 copies, the gene's control region usually becomes methylated and the gene is silenced, so little or no FMRP is made.

FMRP binds RNA. In neurons it travels with hundreds of mRNAs, many encoding synaptic proteins, and holds back their translation. Dendrites carry their own ribosomes, so synapses can make protein locally within minutes of a signal. FMRP is a brake on that local synthesis.

One signal that releases the brake comes through mGluR5, a metabotropic glutamate receptor (a G protein-coupled receptor; see [GPCRs and G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/)). In the hippocampus, activating group I mGluRs, the class that includes mGluR5, makes synapses weaken for hours, a long-term depression (LTD) that needs new protein. In 2002 Kimberly Huber, Mark Bear, Stephen Warren and colleagues found this mGluR-LTD exaggerated in *Fmr1* knockout mice.

In 2004 Bear, Huber and Warren proposed the mGluR theory: without FMRP, mGluR5 signalling drives too much protein synthesis, and many features of the syndrome follow. In 2007 Gül Dölen and Bear tested it by breeding fragile X mice with only one working copy of the mGluR5 gene. Several traits moved toward those of typical mice, including protein synthesis, spine density (spine findings vary with age and brain region) and seizures triggered by loud sound.

Loss of FMRP also shows up at the level of circuits. In several brain regions of *Fmr1* knockout mice, neurons and networks are more excitable than normal, partly because the neurons' own firing properties change. One of the most reliable traits of these mice is a low threshold for seizures triggered by loud sound. Drugs that activate GABA receptors, which strengthen inhibition, reduce those seizures. Many people with fragile X syndrome are unusually sensitive to sound and touch, and some have seizures in childhood, which fits the same picture of circuits that respond too strongly.

Drugs that block mGluR5 then went into people. Placebo-controlled trials of two such drugs, mavoglurant (reported in 2016) and basimglurant (2018), in adolescents and adults with fragile X syndrome did not improve the main behavioural measures. A later trial of mavoglurant in young children also found no benefit over placebo. Why the trials failed is still argued, and the candidate explanations are listed under Going deeper. ## Rett syndrome: a circuit that needs MeCP2 to keep running

Rett syndrome shows the gene-regulation arm of autism genetics. In 1999 the laboratory of Huda Zoghbi at Baylor College of Medicine, with Uta Francke's group, found that mutations in *MECP2* cause most cases. The condition had been described in 1966 by the Austrian paediatrician Andreas Rett: girls who seemed to develop typically for many months and then lost purposeful hand use and speech, made repetitive hand movements, and had slowed head growth. Seizures, breathing irregularities, scoliosis and sleep problems are common.

*MECP2* encodes methyl-CpG-binding protein 2, MeCP2, which binds DNA where cytosines carry methyl groups. It is abundant in neurons and is thought to help set the level at which many genes are expressed, often by recruiting a co-repressor complex.

The gene is on the X chromosome, which shapes who is diagnosed. In each cell of a female embryo one X is switched off at random, so a girl with one mutant copy is a mosaic: some neurons use the working copy and others the mutant one. Most mutations arise new, most often in the father's sperm, and a father passes his X only to daughters. Boys with a severe mutation have no working copy and usually have a different, severe course from early infancy. Too much MeCP2 is also harmful: duplication of *MECP2* causes a separate syndrome in males.

Mice lacking *Mecp2* appear typical for several weeks and then develop tremor, breathing changes and reduced movement. Their neurons do not die in large numbers. They are smaller and more tightly packed, with simpler dendrites.

Circuits in these mice are changed too, and here the imbalance runs both ways. In parts of the cortex of mice lacking *Mecp2*, excitatory neurons have fewer excitatory synapses, and the forebrain as a whole tends to be underactive. The hindbrain tends to be overactive, and in the brainstem, which runs breathing, the balance tilts toward excitation. In these mice, drugs that strengthen GABA signalling reduced pauses in breathing. One gene, opposite imbalances, depending on the region.

### Design problem

Suppose Rett syndrome comes from circuits built wrongly during development. Using mice in which *Mecp2* is silent but can be switched on with a drug, what should happen when MeCP2 is restored in adult mice that already have symptoms? What result would show instead that the circuit is built but needs MeCP2 to keep working?

If the defect is a permanent error made during building, adult restoration should do little. Wrong connections made months earlier would stay wrong, much as a misrouted axon does not reroute because its guidance receptor returns.

If MeCP2 is needed to maintain function, restoring it should improve symptoms even in adults, and the improvement should depend on the gene coming back, not on the drug.

In 2007 Jacky Guy, Adrian Bird and colleagues did this experiment. A blocking sequence flanked by loxP sites silenced *Mecp2*, and the mice carried Cre recombinase that enters the nucleus only after tamoxifen. Treating symptomatic adults restored MeCP2 and reversed many symptoms, in young adult and in older adult mice. Tamoxifen did nothing in mice without the silenced allele. So in mice the circuits were not irreversibly miswired: they could work once MeCP2 returned. In 2011 Zoghbi's laboratory showed the other half by deleting *Mecp2* in adult mice, which then developed symptoms like those of mice that never had it. MeCP2 is needed continuously.

## What mouse models can and cannot show

Most of the causal evidence in this lesson comes from mice. A mouse rescue shows what a gene does in a mouse circuit. It does not by itself show that the same step causes autism in a person, or that a drug will help, and the fragile X trials are the clearest example. Drugs that strengthen inhibition also improve symptoms in several animal models, but no trial of a drug targeting GABA receptors has yet succeeded in fragile X syndrome or autism.

Behaviour is the hardest part to model, which raises one question: how can we recapitulate and evaluate complex behaviors? In mice the usual tests score time spent near a stranger in a three-chamber box, ultrasonic calls, marble burying and grooming. They measure mouse behaviour. A mouse that buries marbles is not autistic.

Maternal immune activation is a smaller line of evidence that shows both the value and the limit of such models. Genes are not the whole story: some infections during pregnancy, such as rubella, are associated with autism in the children. These are associations, and most children of mothers with infections are not autistic. They concern infection, not vaccines: vaccines do not cause autism, and rubella vaccination prevents congenital rubella. In mice, poly(I:C), a synthetic double-stranded RNA, mimics a viral infection and sets off an immune response with no virus present. In 2007 Stephen Smith, Paul Patterson and colleagues gave it to pregnant mice and found that the offspring behaved differently as adults; a single dose of the cytokine IL-6 had a similar effect, and blocking IL-6 prevented it. In 2016 Gloria Choi, Jun Huh and colleagues traced a step further: maternal T cells making IL-17a were required, and offspring had patches of disorganized cortex. Recent reviews judge the effect of maternal immune activation in people to be modest. A cytokine that changes a mouse's behaviour has not been shown to cause autism in a child. What these models can do is identify a mechanism, such as a maternal cytokine acting on the developing cortex during a window of pregnancy, that human studies can then look for.

## Going deeper

These details support the main line above. None of them changes it.

### Counting autism

DSM-5 merged autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder and related categories into one diagnosis, autism spectrum disorder. Signs are often noticed in the second year of life. A CDC report in 2014 gave 1 in 68 for children who were eight in 2010. For children who were eight in 2022, the report published in 2025 estimated that about 1 in 31 in the communities it monitors had been identified as autistic, and no blood test, scan or gene test made a single one of those diagnoses. Boys were identified more than three times as often as girls in the 2022 data.

### Fragile X spines

In brain tissue from affected people, dendritic spines are often long and thin, like immature spines. Spines in *Fmr1* knockout mice are abnormal too, but how varies with age and brain region, and the most consistent change may be in how spines mature and respond to activity.

### Why the fragile X trials may have failed

The reasons are still argued: behaviour scales may change too slowly, tolerance may develop, and compensation may differ between species. Age at treatment was one proposed reason, but the later mavoglurant trial, in children aged about 3 to 6 and combined with language-learning therapy, also found no benefit over placebo, which makes age a weaker explanation.

### More on circuit balance

Seizures occur in roughly 10 to 20 percent of people with fragile X syndrome, usually as a benign focal epilepsy of childhood that is controlled by medication and often resolves. In a small PET study, GABA-A receptor availability was about 10 percent lower across the brain in people with fragile X syndrome. In Rett models the cell type matters as much as the region: mice lacking MeCP2 only in somatostatin interneurons develop seizures but have typical memory and social behaviour, while mice lacking it only in parvalbumin interneurons do not have seizures but show cognitive deficits.

### Maternal infection in people

The rubella association comes mainly from the epidemic of the mid-1960s. In a Danish registry study, hospital admission for a viral infection in the first trimester was associated with a roughly threefold higher rate of autism diagnosis in the child, though the estimate was imprecise and infection across pregnancy as a whole showed no link.

### Monkeys and maternal antibodies

Monkeys bring the model closer to people. In 2014 researchers at the University of California, Davis gave pregnant rhesus monkeys a stabilized form of poly(I:C) in the first or second trimester. As the offspring grew, they showed more repetitive behaviour and fewer affiliative calls than controls, and some approached unfamiliar monkeys in unusual ways. An earlier study from the same centre tested a different idea, that antibodies in some mothers act on the fetal brain: pregnant monkeys given antibodies purified from mothers of autistic children had offspring with more repetitive movement and hyperactivity. These studies used small numbers of animals and need cautious reading.
