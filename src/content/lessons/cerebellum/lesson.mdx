    story: >-
      By the late 1960s anatomists had drawn the cerebellar cortex in detail: granule cells
      beyond counting, parallel fibres crossing the flat Purkinje dendrites, and one climbing
      fibre wrapped around each Purkinje cell. Marr asked what such a
      circuit could compute. His answer, published in the Journal of Physiology in 1969, treated

      sign turned out to be wrong; later experiments found depression. The structure of the
      idea survived: a cortex with an input that carries context, an input that carries
      instruction, and a synapse where the two meet. The theory gave experimenters a specific
      synapse to test, and it remains the reference point for models of cerebellar learning.

techniques:
  - name: "Single-unit extracellular recording"
    blurb: >-
      A fine electrode placed near one Purkinje cell picks up its spikes without entering it.
      Simple spikes and complex spikes have different shapes, so one record gives you both the
      cell's ongoing output and each climbing-fibre event it received.
  - name: "Motor adaptation paradigms"
    blurb: >-
      You perturb a movement in a controlled way (prism goggles, a rotated cursor, treadmill belts
      at two speeds) and measure the error trial by trial, then remove the perturbation and
      measure the after-effect. The after-effect is evidence that the mapping between sensing and moving was
      recalibrated.
  - name: "Optogenetic stimulation"
    blurb: >-
      Neurons made to express a light-gated ion channel are driven with light at chosen moments.
      Pairing climbing-fibre activation with a movement tests whether that input is sufficient to
      cause learning in the tested protocol; it does not show that the input is necessary.
  - name: "Clinical lesion studies"
    blurb: >-
      People with damage confined to known parts of the cerebellum are examined and tested
      against healthy controls. These studies show what the damaged tissue is needed for, within
      the limits of lesions that are rarely clean and of a small number of patients.

**In one careful count of four adult male brains, about 69 billion of the roughly 86 billion (mean 86.1, standard deviation 8.1) neurons sat in the cerebellum (Azevedo and colleagues, 2009, using the isotropic fractionator). Damage the cerebellum and a person is not paralysed. They move, and they move badly.**

The reaches overshoot, the hand shakes as it nears the cup, and walking widens and wanders. The cerebellum does not usually start movements on its own, though in trained mice its output through the thalamus is needed to trigger a cued movement; it makes them accurate, and it learns to keep them accurate when the body or the world changes.

## One circuit, repeated

The cerebellum sits behind the brainstem, under the back of the cerebral hemispheres. Its cortex is a thin, deeply folded sheet, and the same small circuit repeats across it. Regions differ most obviously in where the inputs come from and where the output goes, and also in cell types, firing rates and plasticity.

Two kinds of input arrive. **Mossy fibres** come from many sources, among them the pontine nuclei (which relay signals from the cerebral cortex), the spinal cord and the vestibular nuclei. A nucleus, in the brain, is a cluster of neuron cell bodies. Mossy fibres excite **granule cells**, small neurons packed into the deepest layer of the cortex; most cerebellar neurons are granule cells. Each granule cell sends its axon up to the outer, molecular layer, where it splits and runs along the fold as a **parallel fibre**. Think of parallel fibres as telephone wires strung through a row of flat trees: they pass through the fan-shaped dendrites of many **Purkinje cells** in turn, and each Purkinje cell is contacted by a very large number of them.

The second input is the **climbing fibre**. Climbing fibres come from the inferior olive in the medulla, and they cross the midline, so each olive supplies the opposite half of the cerebellum. Each adult Purkinje cell receives one climbing fibre, which winds up its dendrite and makes many contacts along it. Young Purkinje cells start with several and keep one, as [synapse elimination](/lessons/synapse-elimination/) explains; how the layers form in the first place belongs to [reelin and reeler](/lessons/reelin-and-reeler/).

**Purkinje cells are the only output cells of the cerebellar cortex, and they release GABA.** They inhibit neurons of the deep cerebellar nuclei: in humans four pairs, the fastigial, globose, emboliform and dentate nuclei, with the globose and emboliform together called the interposed nuclei. In the vestibulocerebellum some Purkinje axons skip the deep nuclei and inhibit the vestibular nuclei of the brainstem directly. The nuclear neurons also receive excitatory collaterals from mossy and climbing fibres on their way in. So the nuclei carry the output, and the cortex sculpts it by timed inhibition. Local interneurons add more inhibition: Golgi cells inhibit granule cells, and basket and stellate cells inhibit Purkinje cells. In mouse slices, Purkinje cells also inhibit a subset of interneurons in the inner molecular layer (16% of 371 sampled cells; Halverson and colleagues, 2022), so the cortex is not a strictly one-way chain.

Regions map onto jobs. The midline vermis and the fastigial nuclei serve posture, the trunk and gait. The intermediate zone and the interposed nuclei serve the limbs. The lateral hemispheres and the dentate nuclei project through the thalamus to motor and premotor cortex. The flocculonodular lobe works with the vestibular nuclei on balance and eye movements.

## Two kinds of spike, two kinds of input

An electrode placed beside a Purkinje cell picks up two waveforms. **Simple spikes** are ordinary action potentials, fired continuously, with a rate set by the cell's intrinsic excitability and by its excitatory and inhibitory inputs. In awake resting mice, Zhou and colleagues (2014) reported pooled mean simple-spike rates of 58.3 Hz in zebrin-positive Purkinje cells and 92.9 Hz in zebrin-negative ones. Zebrin is a protein marker that divides the cortex into stripes. **Complex spikes** are large spikes followed by smaller wavelets, one for each climbing-fibre event, at around one per second. After each complex spike the simple spikes pause; in adult mice the mean pause was 20.0 ms in zebrin-negative and 34.5 ms in zebrin-positive cells (Beekhof and colleagues, 2021).

Read a made-up recording. In 2 s you count 130 simple spikes and 2 complex spikes, each followed by a 25 ms gap.

> **rate = count / time**

Simple spikes: 130 / 2 s = 65 Hz. Complex spikes: 2 / 2 s = 1 Hz. Time paused: 2 × 25 ms = 50 ms, which is 50 / 2000 = 2.5% of the record. The simple spikes carry most of the cell's ongoing output; each complex spike marks when its climbing fibre fired, and it also sends a short burst of spikes down the axon.

## Learning from error: Marr-Albus-Ito and its tests

Marr (1969) and Albus (1971) proposed that the climbing fibre is a teacher. When it fires together with a set of parallel fibres, those parallel-fibre synapses change. Albus chose weakening, and in 1982 Ito and Kano reported that pairing parallel-fibre and climbing-fibre stimulation in rabbits produced a long-lasting depression of parallel-fibre to Purkinje transmission: cerebellar **long-term depression (LTD)**. Its calcium and protein kinase C machinery belongs to [calcium signaling](/lessons/calcium-signaling/) and [PLC, IP3, DAG and PKC](/lessons/plc-ip3-dag-pkc/); LTD as a general form of plasticity is in [synaptic plasticity](/lessons/synaptic-plasticity/).

Follow the sign. If the parallel fibres active just before an error are depressed, the Purkinje cell fires less in that context next time, so it inhibits its nuclear target less at that moment, and the nuclear output rises in a well-timed way. The vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR), which turns the eyes against a head turn (see [hearing and balance](/lessons/hearing-and-balance/)), is the classic test bed. Its gain is eye-speed amplitude divided by head-speed amplitude: a mouse whose eyes reach 8 degrees/s for a 10 degrees/s head turn has a gain of 0.8.

The evidence supports the idea and also limits it. Gilbert and Thach (1977) reported that complex spikes became more frequent while monkeys adapted to a changed load on a handle, with a lasting fall in simple-spike responses. Kimpo and colleagues (2014) found in two monkeys that a complex spike predicted the next trial's output change during VOR gain-increase learning but not gain-decrease learning, and in mice that optogenetic climbing-fibre activation paired with head rotation was enough to drive gain increases only. The teacher's effect is gated. Climbing fibres in mice trained on a rewarded task also signalled reward expectation, delivery and omission (Kostadinov and colleagues, 2019), so they carry more than movement errors. Three mouse lines with no LTD in standard slice tests learned VOR adaptation, eyeblink conditioning and a stepping task normally (Schonewille and colleagues, 2011), though modified protocols induced LTD in slices from two of these lines (Yamaguchi and colleagues, 2016), and plasticity is now thought to occur at several sites, including the nuclei. Where the VOR memory is stored, in the flocculus or in the vestibular nuclei it controls, was argued for years. Marr-Albus-Ito remains a model.

## Adaptation in people

Prism goggles shift what you see to one side. Your first throws miss, later throws hit, and when the goggles come off you miss the other way. That opposite miss, the **after-effect**, shows that the mapping between seeing and moving was recalibrated. The change can include shifts in where you see and feel your arm, not only in the motor command. Martin and colleagues (1996) used this throwing task and found adaptation impaired in people with damage to the olivocerebellar system.

**Worked problem.** This thrower is made up; the task follows Martin and colleagues. Prisms make the first throw land 20 cm right of target. Assume each throw corrects a fixed fraction k = 0.30 of the remaining error and that nothing else changes.

> **error after n corrections = E0 × (1 − k)^n**

*Step 1.* With E0 = 20 cm, the error after 1 to 4 corrections is 20 × 0.7 = 14.0, then 9.8, 6.86 and 4.80 cm.

*Step 2.* To get below 2 cm, you need 0.7^n < 0.1. Take logs: n > ln 0.1 / ln 0.7 = (−2.303) / (−0.357) = 6.46, so 7 corrections, leaving 20 × 0.7^7 = 1.65 cm.

*Step 3.* The learned correction is 20 − 1.65 = 18.35 cm leftward. Remove the prisms and that correction is applied to an unshifted world, so the first throw lands about 18.4 cm left. That is the after-effect in this model. In real throwers the after-effect is usually smaller than the full correction, because part of the correction is a deliberate aiming strategy that people drop once the prisms are off.

*Step 4.* Now suppose an illustrative patient with k = 0.05. After 7 corrections the error is 20 × 0.95^7 = 20 × 0.698 = 14.0 cm, and the after-effect is only 20 − 14.0 = 6.0 cm.

Split-belt walking separates two timescales. Morton and Bastian (2006) drove one leg faster than the other. People with cerebellar damage kept the quick, reactive adjustments within each step but showed disrupted predictive, feedforward adaptation, the kind that leaves an after-effect when the belts are equalized. In this task, the cerebellum seemed needed for predicting more than for reacting.

### Design problem

Does cerebellum-dependent adaptation learn from a **sensory prediction error** (the mismatch between the predicted and the observed result of a command) or from having made a corrective movement? Design conditions that tell the two apart.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Rotate the cursor that shows the hand, say by 30 degrees. In a **shooting** condition, people make fast reaches that pass through the target with no chance to correct during the movement; they see the error but make no correction. In a **pointing** condition, they may correct online. If making a correction is what teaches, shooting should adapt less. If prediction error teaches, both should adapt.

Test healthy people and people with cerebellar damage. Match the size and timing of the visual error across conditions, and measure after-effects with the rotation removed and feedback withheld, so the learned change is not confused with online steering.

Tseng and colleagues (2007) ran this contrast. Healthy people adapted in both conditions, and people with hereditary cerebellar ataxia adapted less than controls in both, which supports prediction error as the signal. Limits: this is behaviour in one patient group; it does not identify the synapse or show that every task uses the same signal.

## Predicting a lesion: same side, by region

Ipsilateral means on the same side; contralateral means on the opposite side. Cerebellar signs are typically ipsilateral, and you can work out why. Output from the left dentate and interposed nuclei leaves through the superior cerebellar peduncle and crosses in the midbrain to the right red nucleus and the right ventrolateral thalamus, which relays to right motor and premotor cortex. The corticospinal tract from the right cortex crosses back at the pyramidal decussation in the caudal medulla to the left side of the spinal cord. Two crossings: a left cerebellar lesion disturbs the left limbs. (The olivary climbing fibres also cross, so the right inferior olive teaches the left cerebellum.)

Then use the region. Damage to an intermediate or lateral hemisphere can produce **limb ataxia** (deficient coordination of movement), **dysmetria** (overshooting or undershooting a target), **dysdiadochokinesia** (difficulty with fast alternating movements), **intention tremor** (a tremor that worsens as the limb nears its endpoint), decomposition of movement, and slurred speech (dysarthria). Damage to the vermis or flocculonodular lobe can produce an unsteady, wide-based **gait ataxia** and **nystagmus**, rhythmic involuntary eye movements.

These lesions cause incoordination, not paralysis. And intention tremor is not the rest tremor of Parkinson's disease, which is seen when the limb is still and relaxed and starts with the [basal ganglia](/lessons/basal-ganglia/), though it also involves a cerebellum-thalamus-cortex loop. Cerebellar damage is not purely motor either: in 20 patients with disease confined to the cerebellum, Schmahmann and Sherman (1998) described executive, visual-spatial and affective changes, most marked with lesions of the posterior lobe and vermis. Eyeblink conditioning, a cerebellum-dependent form of learning, returns in [memory systems](/lessons/memory-systems/).
