techniques:
  - name: "Solution culture with omission and add-back"
    blurb: >-
      Grow plants in a complete nutrient solution made from purified salts, and in matching
      solutions each lacking one element, then add the element back to some deficient plants. A
      symptom that appears on omission and disappears on add-back ties the symptom to that element
      in that species.
  - name: "Tissue elemental analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Dry and dissolve leaf or grain tissue and measure its elements, today often by inductively
      coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). It tests a diagnosis made from symptoms, since
      different deficiencies can look alike.
  - name: "Stable-isotope pulse-chase"
    blurb: >-
      Supply a compound labelled with a heavy isotope, such as nitrogen-15 in amino acids, and
      measure where the label turns up over the following hours or days. With controls, such as two labels on one molecule, it can show which route a
      nutrient took into a plant or between partners.
  - name: "Tracer dye exclusion at the endodermis"
    blurb: >-
      Bathe roots in a dye such as propidium iodide that travels in cell walls but does not cross
      intact membranes, and look under the microscope for whether it reaches the central cylinder.
      An intact Casparian strip stops it at the endodermis.

The first film showed why a root must reach for some nutrients; here is what it left out.

> **The one idea.** A green plant builds its organic compounds from carbon dioxide, water and mineral nutrients, but it cannot make an element. How it gets each mineral nutrient depends on three things: the form the element is in, how far that form moves, and who can take it up, the plant or a partner.
>
> *Scope:* green vascular plants that make their own food, and the conventional 17-element list. Most land plants take the 14 mineral nutrients chiefly from soil, though partners, prey, hosts and leaves can supply some. How far an ion moves in soil varies with moisture, texture and how tightly the soil binds it. The root's checkpoint holds where the Casparian strip is complete. Leaf-age patterns are tendencies, not a key for every species.

## What a plant must get from the soil

The first film sorted the 17 essential elements by source. (Why you must eat amino acids a plant can make: [digestive systems](/lessons/digestive-systems/); how the leaf fixes carbon: [the Calvin cycle](/lessons/calvin-cycle/).) What earns an element its place?

An element is **essential** if, without it, a plant cannot complete its life cycle from seed to seed. Daniel Arnon and Perry Stout added two more conditions in 1939: no other element can stand in for it, and it acts directly in the plant rather than by changing the soil or its microbes.

The conventional list for vascular plants has 17 essential elements:

- **From air and water:** carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.
- **Macronutrients** (required in larger amounts): nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulfur.
- **Micronutrients** (required in trace amounts): chlorine, boron, iron, manganese, copper, zinc, nickel and molybdenum.

Some lists add further elements, called **beneficial**: they improve growth in some species but are not universal requirements. Sodium helps some plants with C4 or CAM photosynthesis, silicon strengthens grasses such as rice, and cobalt is used by the nitrogen-fixing bacteria in legume nodules.

The list was built in water culture. Dennis Hoagland and Arnon published a standard nutrient solution in 1938, and leaving one element out of it, then putting it back, is still the basic test.

### Design problem

Brown, Welch and Cary (Plant Physiology, 1987) asked whether nickel is essential for barley (Hordeum vulgare, cultivar Onda). Nickel is needed in such small amounts that contamination from salts, water and the seed itself can hide a requirement. Design the test.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Grow barley in solution culture made with purified salts and clean water, so the solution is depleted of nickel. Give one group no added nickel and a second group the same solution plus nickel, and keep a third group of deficient plants for later add-back. Run it for the whole life cycle and into the next generation, because a seed carries nickel from its mother, and a first-generation plant may live on that store. Measure grain nickel and test whether the grain germinates. In the published experiment, adding nickel completely relieved the deficiency symptoms in the mother plants, and grain with less than 30 ng of nickel per gram of dry weight was not viable. Failure without nickel and rescue with it meet the Arnon and Stout criterion of a life cycle that cannot be completed without the element. The limits: this was one cereal in one preparation, so it does not give a threshold for all plants or any fertiliser rate.

</details>

## Reading a sick leaf

The first film showed how **mobility**, whether the plant can move an element out of old tissue through the **phloem** to growing tissue, predicts which leaves show a shortage first. It left out what the symptoms look like, and why calcium misses young leaves.

On the left, the three oldest lower leaves are yellow, the two young top leaves are green, and a gold arrow runs up the stem to the tip. On the right, the two young top leaves are yellow and the old leaves are green." caption="Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and magnesium are mobile, so a shortage tends to show first in old, lower leaves; iron, calcium and, in most species, boron stay largely where they were deposited, so a shortage tends to show first in young leaves. For **immobile elements**, new leaves and growing tips depend mainly on what arrives from the roots. The symptom itself differs. Without nitrogen the whole leaf yellows, **chlorosis**, from lost chlorophyll; without phosphorus, Japanese mint blades turned dark green with purple margins (Going deeper).

Why can't a young leaf get more calcium from the roots? The **xylem** carries water up from the roots, and calcium rides in that water, so it goes mostly where water leaves fastest by **transpiration**, evaporation from the leaves.

**Predict.** A plant is transpiring hard and carrying fruit. Which parts get most of the calcium coming up from the roots: the large mature leaves, or the young leaves and the fruit?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The large mature leaves, which lose water fastest. Much of their calcium becomes bound in cell walls and is not readily moved again. Young leaves and fruit lose much less water, so they receive less, and calcium moves only poorly in the phloem, which cannot make up the difference.

</details>

Different deficiencies can look alike, and drought, disease or cold can mimic them, so a symptom is a hypothesis. **Tissue analysis**, measuring the elements in the leaf, tests it; **add-back**, supplying the suspected element and watching new growth recover, confirms it.

## Into the root: hairs, two routes and a checkpoint

How much surface do the first film's **root hairs** and mycorrhizal **hyphae** add past the **depletion zone**, and at what cost in material?

**Worked problem.** These are illustrative numbers, not measurements from one species. Treat every structure as a cylinder and ignore the ends.

> **side area of a cylinder = pi x d x L**
> **surface per volume of a cylinder = 4 / d**

1. *The root.* A 10 mm segment, 0.5 mm in diameter: pi x 0.5 mm x 10 mm = 15.7 mm2.
2. *The hairs.* Suppose 1,000 hairs on that segment, each 0.5 mm long and 0.010 mm wide. One hair: pi x 0.010 mm x 0.5 mm = 0.0157 mm2. All of them: 1,000 x 0.0157 = 15.7 mm2. The hairs double the surface, to 31.4 mm2.
3. *The fungus.* Suppose 1 m (1,000 mm) of mycorrhizal hyphae, each 0.004 mm wide, attached to the segment: pi x 0.004 mm x 1,000 mm = 12.6 mm2. The total is now 15.7 + 15.7 + 12.6 = 44.0 mm2.
4. *Surface per material.* With 4 / d, the root gives 4 / 0.5 = 8 mm2 of surface per mm3 of material, a hair 4 / 0.010 = 400 and a hypha 4 / 0.004 = 1,000. So the hyphae add more than three quarters of the root's own surface for under one per cent of its volume (the volume check is in Going deeper).

Reaching an ion is half the job; it still has to cross the **cortex**, a thick layer of cells, to the xylem in the root's centre, by two routes it can switch between.

- The **apoplast** is the cell walls and the spaces between them, outside the living membranes: a connected wet pathway through the outer root that water and ions seep along without entering a cell.
- The **symplast** is the living cytoplasm, joined from cell to cell by **plasmodesmata**, narrow channels through the walls. An ion enters it by crossing a plasma membrane once, through a transporter or channel, and can then pass from cell to cell without crossing another.

(Water can also cross from cell to cell through membranes.)

Both routes reach the **endodermis**, a single ring of cells around the central cylinder, the root's core that holds the xylem. In the walls between endodermal cells runs the **Casparian strip**, a band that seals the wall route. Where the strip is complete, an ion travelling in the walls must cross a plasma membrane into a living cell, while an ion already in the symplast carries on through the endodermal cells. So the endodermis is the root's checkpoint: whatever reaches the xylem has passed through a living cell, whose transporters and channels could have kept it out. (Its make-up and a second barrier: Going deeper.)

Crossing a membrane often costs energy. The inside of a root cell is negative, so an **anion**, a negative ion such as nitrate or phosphate, is pushed back by the voltage, and often by its concentration gradient too. A **proton pump**, an ATP-driven H+-ATPase in the plasma membrane, moves protons out of root cells. Transporters then let a proton flow back in, down its gradient, carrying nitrate or another ion with it: **cotransport** (see [membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/)).

## Borrowing nitrogen and phosphorus: nodules and mycorrhizae

Nitrate and ammonium are the main nitrogen forms a root takes up. Yet the air around every leaf is mostly nitrogen gas, which no plant enzyme can use. Here the problem is form, not distance; the second film follows the pots that showed how a pea gets around it.

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The 1888 pots did not identify the living agent behind the **nodules**, the root swellings; in the same year Martinus Beijerinck reported isolating bacteria from nodules, the rhizobia. Barley, like oats, has no nodules and depends mainly on nitrate and ammonium in the soil.

The nodule hides a contradiction. Nitrogenase is damaged by oxygen, yet the bacteroids that make it respire. A plant protein, **leghemoglobin**, binds oxygen and buffers it at a low, steady level; it does not exclude oxygen. Silencing it stops fixation (Going deeper). The ecosystem side of fixation is in [nutrient cycling](/lessons/nutrient-cycling/).

A related trade is made with fungi: the plant pays carbon, and the fungus extends its reach beyond the depletion zone, though the root still takes up phosphate itself (hyphae: [microbes, protists and fungi](/lessons/microbes-protists-fungi/)).

- **Arbuscular mycorrhizae** form in most land plants. Hyphae push through the walls of cortex cells and branch into **arbuscules**, tree-like structures wrapped in the plant's folded cell membrane, where nutrients are exchanged. The fungus delivers minerals taken from soil, especially phosphate. The plant pays in sugars, and in Lotus japonicus with the fungus Rhizophagus irregularis, Keymer and colleagues (2017) showed with isotope labelling and plant mutants that it pays in lipids as well.
- **Ectomycorrhizae** form on trees such as pines and oaks, and help supply the trees with nitrogen. The fungus wraps the root tip in a sheath and grows a network between root cells without entering them: among the epidermal cells in flowering trees such as oaks, and deeper, between cortex cells, in pines.

Partners differ in quality, and hosts can respond to that, as [mutualism, parasitism and coevolution](/lessons/mutualism-parasitism-coevolution/) explains.

## Plants that eat animals and plants that tap plants

Some plants also take in ready-made organic compounds. The purple pitcher plant, Sarracenia purpurea, grows in nitrogen-poor bogs. It photosynthesizes like any green plant. Insects drown in rainwater held in the pitchers, where bacteria, protists and larvae break them down, releasing mineral nitrogen and also competing with the plant for it. Karagatzides, Butler and Ellison (2009) added labelled amino acids to pitchers in Ontario and Massachusetts and found data consistent with direct amino-acid uptake by the plant, clearest for phenylalanine.

Dodder (Cuscuta), a twining stem parasite, takes a different route: it forms **haustoria**, organs that grow into the host stem and connect to its xylem and phloem, drawing water, nutrients and sugars.

A pea borrows from bacteria, a pine from fungi, a pitcher plant from insects and a food web, and dodder from another plant. The one idea again: a plant cannot make an element, so how it gets each one depends on its form, how far that form moves, and who can take it up.

How plants sense and respond to what they find, through hormones, comes next in [plant signals](/lessons/plant-signals/).

## Going deeper

### The Japanese mint deficiency study

In Japanese mint (Mentha arvensis), grown in greenhouse hydroponics with one nutrient left out of Hoagland's solution at a time, the whole blade of old leaves yellowed first without nitrogen, and young leaves yellowed without iron. Symptoms in plants without nitrogen were first seen 15 days after transplanting. Without potassium, old leaves developed yellow spots; the phosphorus result is in the main text. As a general pattern, not a result of this study, many plants short of magnesium yellow between the veins of older leaves while the veins stay green. **Necrosis** means patches of dead tissue.

### Surface per volume: the volume check

Check by volume: the hyphae occupy pi x (0.002 mm)2 x 1,000 mm = 0.013 mm3, against 2.0 mm3 for the root segment.

### What the Casparian strip is made of, and a second barrier

In young Arabidopsis roots the strip is lignin, not suberin as older textbooks say; suberin is laid down later. Many species, though not Arabidopsis, also have an **exodermis**, an outer layer under the epidermis with Casparian bands of its own.

### The proton pump and a nitrate transporter

The proton pump is an H+-ATPase of the P type. In Arabidopsis the NRT1.1 nitrate transporter is a cotransporter that lets a proton flow in with nitrate.

### Silencing leghemoglobin

Ott and colleagues (2005) silenced the leghemoglobin genes in Lotus japonicus. Free oxygen in the nodules rose, fixation stopped, and the plants grew normally only when given mineral nitrogen, so leghemoglobin is needed for fixation, not for growth itself.

### Pitcher and phyllodium

The purple pitcher plant's flat leaves, phyllodia, photosynthesize more efficiently than its pitchers.
