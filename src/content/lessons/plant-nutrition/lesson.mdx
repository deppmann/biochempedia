techniques:
  - name: "Solution culture with omission and add-back"
    blurb: >-
      Grow plants in a complete nutrient solution made from purified salts, and in matching
      solutions each lacking one element, then add the element back to some deficient plants. A
      symptom that appears on omission and disappears on add-back ties the symptom to that element
      in that species.
  - name: "Tissue elemental analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Dry and dissolve leaf or grain tissue and measure its elements, today often by inductively
      coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). It tests a diagnosis made from symptoms, since
      different deficiencies can look alike.
  - name: "Stable-isotope pulse-chase"
    blurb: >-
      Supply a compound labelled with a heavy isotope, such as nitrogen-15 in amino acids, and
      measure where the label turns up over the following hours or days. With controls, such as two labels on one molecule, it can show which route a
      nutrient took into a plant or between partners.
  - name: "Tracer dye exclusion at the endodermis"
    blurb: >-
      Bathe roots in a dye such as propidium iodide that travels in cell walls but does not cross
      intact membranes, and look under the microscope for whether it reaches the central cylinder.
      An intact Casparian strip stops it at the endodermis.

**Peas grown in sterilised sand, given a little soil extract and watered with a solution that held no nitrogen, ended up with many times more nitrogen than their seeds contained.** Oats in the next pot starved. Nitrogen gas makes up most of the air around both plants, so why could one use it and the other not? Answering that takes you through what a plant takes from the soil, how a root lets it in, and the partners some plants rely on.

## What a plant must get from the soil

Start with a contrast to animals. You must eat certain amino acids and vitamins because your cells cannot make them, as [digestive systems](/lessons/digestive-systems/) explains. A green plant can build every organic molecule it needs, every amino acid, lipid and nucleotide, from carbon dioxide, water and a short list of mineral elements. Some plants also take in ready-made organic compounds, as the pitcher plant and dodder later in this lesson show. Photosynthesis fixes the carbon in the leaf; the chemistry of that belongs to [the Calvin cycle](/lessons/calvin-cycle/). What the plant cannot make is an element. It has to take each one in.

An element is **essential** if, without it, a plant cannot complete its life cycle from seed to seed. Daniel Arnon and Perry Stout added two more conditions in 1939: no other element can stand in for it, and it acts directly in the plant rather than by changing the soil or its microbes.

The conventional list for vascular plants has 17 essential elements:

- **From air and water:** carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.
- **Macronutrients** (required in larger amounts): nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulfur.
- **Micronutrients** (required in trace amounts): chlorine, boron, iron, manganese, copper, zinc, nickel and molybdenum.

Some lists add further elements, called **beneficial**: they improve growth in some species but are not universal requirements. Sodium helps some plants with C4 or CAM photosynthesis, silicon strengthens grasses such as rice, and cobalt is used by the nitrogen-fixing bacteria in legume nodules.

The list was built in water culture. Dennis Hoagland and Arnon published a standard nutrient solution in 1938, and leaving one element out of it, then putting it back, is still the basic test.

## Reading a sick leaf

A deficiency shows on the leaves, and where it shows first is a clue. The key is **mobility**: whether the plant can move an element out of old tissue and through the phloem to growing tissue.

On the left, the three oldest lower leaves are yellow, the two young top leaves are green, and a gold arrow runs up the stem to the tip. On the right, the two young top leaves are yellow and the old leaves are green." caption="Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and magnesium are mobile, so a shortage shows first in old, lower leaves; iron, calcium and, in most species, boron stay largely where they were deposited, so a shortage shows first in young leaves. Treat these as examples, not a key for every species." />

- **Mobile elements** (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium) are withdrawn from old leaves when supply runs short. Symptoms appear first in **old, lower leaves**.
- **Immobile elements** (iron and calcium, and boron in most species) stay largely where they were deposited. New leaves depend mainly on what arrives from the roots, so symptoms appear first in **young leaves** and growing tips.

Here **chlorosis** means yellowing from lost chlorophyll, and **necrosis** means patches of dead tissue.

A study of Japanese mint (Mentha arvensis), grown in greenhouse hydroponics with one nutrient left out of Hoagland's solution at a time, shows the pattern. Without nitrogen, the whole blade of old leaves yellowed first, with symptoms first seen 15 days after transplanting. Without iron, young leaves yellowed. Without potassium, old leaves developed yellow spots. Without phosphorus, blades turned dark green with purple margins. Without magnesium, many plants yellow between the veins of older leaves while the veins stay green.

Treat these as examples, not a key for every species. Different deficiencies can look alike, and drought, disease or cold can mimic them. A symptom is a hypothesis. **Tissue analysis**, measuring the elements in the leaf, tests it, and **add-back**, supplying the suspected element and watching new growth recover, confirms it.

## Into the root: hairs, two routes and a gatekeeper

Much uptake happens in the young root a little behind the growing tip, where **root hairs**, tubular outgrowths of single epidermal cells, push into the spaces between soil particles and enlarge the absorbing surface.

**Worked problem: how much surface do hairs and hyphae add?** These are illustrative numbers, not measurements from one species. Treat every structure as a cylinder and ignore the ends.

> **side area of a cylinder = pi x d x L**
> **surface per volume of a cylinder = 4 / d**

1. *The root.* A 10 mm segment, 0.5 mm in diameter: pi x 0.5 mm x 10 mm = 15.7 mm2.
2. *The hairs.* Suppose 1,000 hairs on that segment, each 0.5 mm long and 0.010 mm wide. One hair: pi x 0.010 mm x 0.5 mm = 0.0157 mm2. All of them: 1,000 x 0.0157 = 15.7 mm2. The hairs double the surface, to 31.4 mm2.
3. *The fungus.* Suppose 1 m (1,000 mm) of mycorrhizal hyphae, each 0.004 mm wide, attached to the segment: pi x 0.004 mm x 1,000 mm = 12.6 mm2. The total is now 15.7 + 15.7 + 12.6 = 44.0 mm2.
4. *Surface per material.* With 4 / d, the root gives 4 / 0.5 = 8 per mm, a hair 4 / 0.010 = 400 per mm and a hypha 4 / 0.004 = 1,000 per mm. Check by volume: the hyphae occupy pi x (0.002 mm)2 x 1,000 mm = 0.013 mm3, against 2.0 mm3 for the root segment. They add more than three quarters of the root's own surface for under one per cent of its volume.

Surface matters most for ions that move slowly through soil. Phosphate diffuses so slowly that a root soon depletes the available phosphate right around it, and thin hyphae reach past that depletion zone. Hairs help with water too, but not equally everywhere: in barley, root hairs aided water extraction by rapidly transpiring plants in drying soil, while a hairless maize mutant took up water no differently in sand or loam. ([Moving water and sugar in plants](/lessons/plant-transport/) covers water potential and the pull of transpiration.)

Inside the root, water and dissolved ions cross the cortex by two main routes, and can switch between them. The **apoplast** is the continuous network of cell walls and spaces outside the membranes. The **symplast** is the cytoplasm of cells joined by plasmodesmata, channels through the walls. Water can also pass from cell to cell across their membranes. Both routes reach the **endodermis**, the innermost cortical layer around the central cylinder. Many species, though not Arabidopsis, also have an **exodermis**, an outer layer with its own Casparian bands. In the walls between endodermal cells runs the **Casparian strip**, a band that blocks the wall route. In young Arabidopsis roots that band is lignin, not suberin as older textbooks say; suberin is laid down later. Past the strip, anything heading for the xylem has to cross a living membrane, so the endodermis acts as a gatekeeper on what enters.

Taking up many nutrient ions takes energy, because they often move against their gradients. A **proton pump**, an ATP-driven H+-ATPase of the P type in the plasma membrane, moves protons out of root cells. Transporters then let a proton flow back in with nitrate or another ion, **cotransport**. In Arabidopsis the NRT1.1 nitrate transporter works this way. How such transporters work is in [membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/).

## Borrowing nitrogen and phosphorus: nodules and mycorrhizae

Back to the peas. At the Bernburg experiment station, Hermann Hellriegel and Hermann Wilfarth grew legumes, including peas, and cereals, including oats, in sterilised sand with a nitrogen-free nutrient solution, and published the work in 1888. If every plant relied on nitrogen compounds in soil, none should end with more nitrogen than its seed held. The cereals grew only as far as the nitrate they were given allowed. Peas given a little extract of field soil formed **nodules**, swellings on the roots, and gained much nitrogen from the air. Without the extract, or with extract that had been heated, they formed no nodules and did not. A living agent from soil, tied to the nodules, was required. The experiment did not identify the agent; in the same year Martinus Beijerinck reported isolating bacteria from nodules.

Those bacteria are **rhizobia**. They enter the root, and inside nodule cells they become **bacteroids** whose enzyme, **nitrogenase**, reduces nitrogen gas to ammonia. The root makes no nitrogenase. In return the plant supplies carbon: sucrose from the leaves is converted to malate, which fuels the bacteroids. Nitrogenase is damaged by oxygen, yet the bacteroids respire. A plant protein, **leghemoglobin**, binds oxygen and buffers it at a low, steady level. It does not exclude oxygen. In Lotus japonicus, Ott and colleagues (2005) silenced leghemoglobin: free oxygen in nodules rose, fixation stopped, and the plants grew normally only when given mineral nitrogen. Oats and barley have no nodules and depend mainly on nitrate and ammonium in the soil. The ecosystem side of fixation is in [nutrient cycling](/lessons/nutrient-cycling/).

Many plants also borrow from fungi, especially for phosphorus. A **mycorrhiza** is an association of a root with a fungus (fungal hyphae appear in [microbes, protists and fungi](/lessons/microbes-protists-fungi/)).

- **Arbuscular mycorrhizae** form in most land plants. Hyphae push through the walls of cortex cells and branch into **arbuscules**, tree-like structures wrapped in the plant's folded cell membrane, where nutrients are exchanged. In Lotus japonicus with the fungus Rhizophagus irregularis, the fungus delivers minerals taken from soil, especially phosphate. Keymer and colleagues (2017) showed with isotope labelling and plant mutants that the plant pays in lipids as well as sugars.
- **Ectomycorrhizae** form on trees such as pines and oaks, and help supply the trees with nitrogen. The fungus wraps the root tip in a sheath and grows a network between root cells without entering them: among the epidermal cells in flowering trees such as oaks, and deeper, between cortex cells, in pines.

Partners differ in quality, and hosts can respond to that, as [mutualism, parasitism and coevolution](/lessons/mutualism-parasitism-coevolution/) explains.

## Plants that eat animals and plants that tap plants

The purple pitcher plant, Sarracenia purpurea, grows in nitrogen-poor bogs. It photosynthesizes like any green plant, and its flat leaves, phyllodia, photosynthesize more efficiently than its pitchers. Insects drown in rainwater held in the pitchers, where bacteria, protists and larvae break them down, releasing mineral nitrogen and also competing with the plant for it. Karagatzides, Butler and Ellison (2009) added labelled amino acids to pitchers in Ontario and Massachusetts and found data consistent with direct amino-acid uptake by the plant, clearest for phenylalanine.

Dodder (Cuscuta) takes a different route. This twining stem parasite forms **haustoria**, organs that grow into the host stem and connect to its xylem and phloem, drawing water, nutrients and sugars.

A pea borrows from bacteria, a pine from fungi, a pitcher plant from insects and a food web, and dodder from another plant. In those pots, oats had only the nitrate their solution held.

### Design problem

Brown, Welch and Cary (Plant Physiology, 1987) asked whether nickel is essential for barley (Hordeum vulgare, cultivar Onda). Nickel is needed in such small amounts that contamination from salts, water and the seed itself can hide a requirement. Design the test.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Grow barley in solution culture made with purified salts and clean water, so the solution is depleted of nickel. Give one group no added nickel and a second group the same solution plus nickel, and keep a third group of deficient plants for later add-back. Run the experiment for the whole life cycle and into the next generation, because a seed carries nickel from its mother, and a first-generation plant may live on that store. Measure grain nickel and test whether the grain germinates. In the published experiment, adding nickel completely relieved the deficiency symptoms in the mother plants, and grain with less than 30 ng of nickel per gram of dry weight was not viable. Failure without nickel and rescue with it meet the Arnon and Stout criterion of a life cycle that cannot be completed without the element. The limits: this was one cereal in one preparation, so it does not give a threshold for all plants or any fertiliser rate.

</details>
