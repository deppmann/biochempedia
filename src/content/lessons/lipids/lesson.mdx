summary: "Proteins, nucleic acids and polysaccharides are polymers; lipids are not. They are mixed and matched from a handful of modular parts, and the only thing that unites them is solubility in organic solvent. Lipids do two big jobs: their amphipathic shape lets them assemble into the bilayer barriers around every cell, and their reduced, dry storage makes fat the body's densest fuel. This lesson runs on one packing rule: straight tails lie together and melt high, and a single cis double bond, the first kink most of all, can turn a solid fat into a liquid oil. The same rule explains why cold-adapted membranes stay fluid and why cholesterol acts like a thermostat. Two other facts have their own reasons: the hydrophobic effect drives bilayer assembly, and fat outstores hydrated glycogen about sevenfold because its carbon is more reduced and it is stored dry. Structure predicts properties, properties predict function, and function predicts where in the body you find the molecule."

techniques:
  - name: "Reading geometry off a melting point"
    blurb: >-
      A fat's melting temperature is a structural readout. Each added CH₂ raises Tm by
      a few degrees Celsius through van der Waals contact; the first cis double bond drops it sharply by
      kinking the chain so it cannot pack tightly, and in the 18-carbon series the second and third lower it by less.
      Measure the Tm and you learn how well the chains pack.
  - name: "Saponification"
    blurb: >-
      Alkaline hydrolysis, which uses up hydroxide, of the ester bonds in a triacylglycerol releases glycerol
      and fatty-acid salts: soap. It is esterification run backward, cutting the same
      ester bonds pancreatic lipase hydrolyzes when it digests dietary fat at the sn-1 and
      sn-3 positions.
  - name: "The extraction test for membrane proteins"
    blurb: >-
      How a protein comes off a membrane separates peripheral proteins from membrane-attached
      ones. A salt or pH change frees a peripheral protein; detergent is needed for an integral
      one buried in the hydrophobic core, and for most lipid-anchored proteins too, since their
      covalent fatty acid, isoprenoid or GPI anchor sits in the bilayer (a GPI-anchored protein
      can also be cut loose by a specific phospholipase). The anchor itself is identified chemically.

  - stem: "Stearate is 18:0 and oleate is 18:1 cis-Δ9. Both have 18 carbons, yet stearate melts far higher. The best explanation is that:"
    choices:
      - "Oleate is charged at physiological pH and stearate is not."
      - "Oleate has fewer carbons available for van der Waals contact."
      - "The cis double bond kinks oleate's chain so it cannot pack tightly, lowering the melting point."
      - "The double bond in oleate is weaker, so the molecule comes apart at lower temperature."
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      A cis double bond introduces a fixed kink that prevents tight packing, and poorly
      packed solids melt at lower temperatures. The two fatty acids have the same 18-carbon
      length, so carbon count is not the difference, and both ionize at pH 7.4, so charge
      does not distinguish them. The "weaker bond" choice states a common misconception:
      double bonds are stronger than single bonds, and bond strength is not what sets the
      melting point here — geometry is.

    rationale: >-
      A phospholipid has a water-loving head and water-fearing tails, so in water the tails cluster together and the heads face the water on both sides, forming a bilayer. Lipids are not polymers, a one-tailed cone curls into a micelle rather than a bilayer, and the steroid ring belongs to cholesterol, which inserts into a bilayer but does not build one.

  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: |
      Lipids are different from proteins and DNA: those are long chains of repeating units, but
      lipids are mixed and matched from a few separate parts. What groups them together is simply
      that they dissolve in oil, not water.

      A fatty acid is a long carbon tail with an acid head. If the tail is straight (saturated),
      the molecules pack tightly and the fat is solid, like lard. If the tail has a cis double
      bond, it gets a kink, the molecules cannot pack, and the fat is liquid, like olive oil.
      Longer tails melt at higher temperatures; more kinks melt lower. Trans fats have a double
      bond that leaves the tail nearly straight, so they pack much better than kinked ones and the fat is firmer, and eating them raises the 'bad' LDL cholesterol in blood,
      which is why the FDA took them out of most foods.

      Fat is the body's main energy store because it packs about twice the calories per gram of
      carbohydrate, and because it is stored dry while glycogen is stored soaked in water.
      Membranes are built from phospholipids, which have a water-loving head and two water-fearing
      tails, so they line up automatically into a double layer.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: |
      Lipids are defined operationally by solubility in organic solvent, not by a common monomer.
      They are built combinatorially from fatty acids, glycerol, sphingosine, and sterols, and
      they split into storage versus structural/signaling roles.

      Fatty-acid melting follows one packing rule with two dials: each CH₂ raises Tm by a few °C
      (van der Waals surface), and a cis double bond lowers it, the first one most (kink geometry
      blocks packing, not bond weakness). At pH 7.4 the carboxyl (pKa about 4.8) is ionized, hence palmitate, not palmitic
      acid. Human desaturases cannot work beyond Δ9 toward the methyl end, making linoleate and α-linolenate essential; arachidonate
      feeds cyclooxygenase to make prostaglandins, the NSAID target. Energetically, fat yields
      about 9 kcal/g versus carbohydrate's 4; once you also count glycogen's bound water, the
      effective storage advantage is about sevenfold. Per molecule is a separate comparison:
      palmitate yields about 106 ATP to glucose's 30-32.

      Storage fat is triacylglycerol, three fatty acids on glycerol; natural fats are mixed TAGs
      (in many plant oils saturated at sn-1/sn-3 and unsaturated at sn-2; lard differs). Membranes solve barrier, fluidity, and
      identity with three lipid classes. Phospholipid head-group identity sets charge, flippases and floppases set
      leaflet, and together they set function: PC (outer, neutral), PS (inner, negative, the apoptotic eat-me signal
      when externalized), PI (inner, PIP₂ → DAG + IP₃), cardiolipin (almost only mitochondria).
      Glycerophospholipids (ester, glycerol) versus sphingolipids (amide, sphingosine) is the
      structural diagnostic. Cholesterol buffers fluidity in both directions and is the
      steroid-hormone precursor.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: |
      The lipid class is unified only by partition behavior, which is why its members share no
      common biosynthetic backbone. Melting transitions reflect the cooperative packing of acyl
      chains: chain elongation increases the enthalpy of the gel-to-fluid transition via
      cumulative van der Waals contacts (a few °C per methylene), while a cis unsaturation
      introduces a roughly 30-degree kink that disrupts lattice registration and depresses Tm far
      more per unit than the dispersion gain from added carbons. Trans isomers retain near-linear
      geometry and pack better than cis chains: elaidate melts about midway between oleate and
      stearate, and in bilayers trans chains sit closer to cis than to saturated (DEPC about 12 °C
      vs DOPC −17 °C and DSPC 55 °C); their dietary harm runs through plasma lipoproteins, raising LDL and lowering HDL.

      Membrane organization is governed by spontaneous curvature and lateral phase behavior.
      Head-group identity dictates net charge and thus electrostatic partitioning of peripheral
      proteins; PS asymmetry is ATP-dependent (P-type flippases, with scramblases collapsing
      asymmetry during apoptosis to display PS for efferocytosis). PIP₂ is both a structural anchor
      and the PLC substrate generating DAG and IP₃. Cardiolipin's four acyl chains and conical
      geometry stabilize cristae curvature and respiratory supercomplexes, and its oxidation
      participates in cytochrome c release.

      Cholesterol's condensing and ordering effects are bidirectional: above Tm it reduces
      acyl-chain trans-gauche isomerization (increasing order), below Tm it disrupts gel packing
      (increasing fluidity), broadening rather than merely shifting the transition, and it underlies
      liquid-ordered raft phases. Homeoviscous adaptation tunes the unsaturation index to hold the
      in-vivo membrane in the liquid-crystalline state. Transmembrane segments resolve the
      backbone-amide desolvation penalty either intramolecularly (α-helix, about 20 apolar residues
      across about 30 Å) or intermolecularly (β-barrel inter-strand hydrogen bonding), the two folds
      the translocon and the outer-membrane assembly machinery respectively select for.

**Lard is solid on the counter and olive oil pours, and the difference starts with one bend in a carbon chain.** Lard carries more straight, saturated chains such as palmitate and stearate. Olive oil is mostly oleate, which has stearate's 18 carbons but one fixed bend in the middle.

Why should one bend matter so much? The answer is a rule about how lipid tails pack, and the same rule decides whether the membrane around each of your cells is stiff or fluid. Building bilayers at all and storing fuel have their own reasons.

## Lipids break the rules

Proteins are polymers of amino acids. DNA is a polymer of nucleotides. Lipids are neither. They are assembled combinatorially from a small kit of modular parts: fatty acids, glycerol, sphingosine and sterols, mixed and matched. The only property that unites them is solubility in organic solvent, a definition by behavior rather than by structure. The same handful of parts builds energy stores, membranes and signals.

## Fatty acids: why one bend melts a fat

A **fatty acid** is a hydrocarbon tail, a chain of CH₂ groups, with a carboxylic acid at one end. That acid group has a pKa of about 4.8, so at pH 7.4 it has almost entirely lost its proton, and biochemists name the ion: palmitate for palmitic acid, oleate for oleic acid. The film above uses the acid names; this page uses the ion names, but its melting points are measured on the pure acids.

> **Reading the shorthand.** **16:0** means 16 carbons and no double bonds; the chain is *saturated* (it holds as many hydrogens as it can). **18:1** means 18 carbons and one double bond. Carbons are counted from the acid end, so **Δ9** means the double bond starts at carbon 9. In a **cis** double bond, the two parts of the chain leave from the same side, so the chain bends by about 30°. In a **trans** double bond they leave from opposite sides, and the chain stays nearly straight.

Start with data. Here are the melting points of four saturated fatty acids.

| Fatty acid (free acid) | Shorthand | Melts at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Laurate | 12:0 | 44 °C |
| Myristate | 14:0 | 54 °C |
| Palmitate | 16:0 | 63 °C |
| Stearate | 18:0 | 70 °C |

Longer chains melt higher, by about 5 °C per added CH₂ at first, falling to about 3.5 °C by stearate. Picture a solid fatty acid as pencils laid side by side in a box, each touching its neighbors along its whole length by weak van der Waals attraction. A longer pencil has more length in contact, so it takes more heat to shake it free.

**Predict.** Oleate (18:1 cis-Δ9) is stearate with one change: a cis double bond at carbon 9, a bend in the middle of the chain. Does it melt above or below stearate's 70 °C, and by a little or a lot?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Far below: oleate melts near 13 °C, about 57 degrees lower than stearate, so it is liquid at room temperature. The bend did not weaken any bond (a double bond is stronger than a single one); it changed the shape. A bent pencil cannot lie flat against straight ones, so much less of its length is in contact, and there is much less to shake apart.

</details>

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Tails that lie side by side, neatly lined up, stick to each other by van der Waals contact, and melting is the temperature at which heat shakes that ordered packing loose. Anything that adds contact (a longer, straight tail) raises the melting point, and anything that removes it (a cis kink, the first kink most of all) lowers it. The same rule decides whether a membrane is a stiff gel or a working fluid.

The rule ranks two lipids that differ in one dial (two more carbons, or one more cis bond). It does not give exact temperatures, and it cannot settle a trade-off between a longer chain and an extra kink.

Notice how much the first kink does. Add a second cis bond (linoleate, 18:2) and the melting point falls only about 18 degrees more, to about −5 °C. A third bond (α-linolenate, 18:3) lowers it about 6 degrees more. Once one bend has broken up the packing, each further bend removes less (in the 18-carbon series, at least up to three double bonds). Linoleate and α-linolenate are also the two polyunsaturated fatty acids you must eat, because your own enzymes cannot make them.

**Predict.** A *trans* double bond at carbon 9 leaves the chain nearly straight. Where should elaidate, the trans twin of oleate, melt: near oleate, near stearate, or between them?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Between them, a little nearer stearate: about 45 °C, close to halfway. A nearly straight chain packs much better than a bent one, but well short of a saturated chain. (A trans fat has the same molecular formula and the same number of double bonds as its cis twin; only the geometry differs.) This is why partly hydrogenating a vegetable oil, which turns some cis bonds trans and saturates others, makes a liquid oil spreadable. In the diet, industrial trans fats raise LDL cholesterol, lower HDL cholesterol and raise the risk of heart disease. In 2018 the FDA ended most uses of partially hydrogenated oils, the main source of artificial trans fats.

</details>

## Triacylglycerols: three tails on glycerol

Stored fat is a **triacylglycerol**: three fatty acids joined by ester bonds to a glycerol backbone. The packing rule carries over. Tripalmitin, three saturated palmitates on one glycerol, is a high-melting solid. Triolein, three oleates, stays liquid even at room temperature. Natural fats mix saturated and unsaturated chains, which gives them an intermediate melting point.

A fat's melting behavior is set mostly by *which* fatty acids it carries, and to a lesser degree by *where* they sit on glycerol, the smaller effect the film describes as cells "choosing which fatty acid goes to which position."

## Why lipids make bilayers (a separate rule)

The packing rule says how tails behave once they are together. A different rule explains why they come together at all: the **hydrophobic effect**.

Most membrane lipids are phospholipids, and a phospholipid has four parts: two hydrocarbon tails, a platform (glycerol or sphingosine), a phosphate, and a variable head alcohol. The head is polar and the tails are not, so the molecule is **amphipathic**. "Water-fearing" is a nickname, not a force: water molecules next to an exposed tail are forced into ordered cages. Tucking the tails together frees that water to tumble again, and that gain in water's entropy, its disorder, drives assembly. No bond between tails is needed to start it, though van der Waals contact then holds them. So a phospholipid in water forms a bilayer with no ATP and no help from proteins.

What it forms depends on its overall shape. In the demo below, pick a head-to-tail shape: a one-tailed cone curls into a micelle, and a two-tailed cylinder, the phospholipid shape, lies flat as a bilayer.

## Bilayers melt too: gel and fluid

A bilayer is two sheets of packed tails, so the rule applies to it too: a simple bilayer has a melting, or transition, temperature, **Tm**. Below Tm the tails are straight and packed in order, and the membrane is a stiff **gel**. Above Tm the tails flex and slide past each other, and the membrane is a **fluid**, the state in which its proteins can move and work.

| Phosphatidylcholine | Its two tails | Tm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DPPC | 16:0 and 16:0 | 41 °C |
| POPC | 16:0 and 18:1 cis | −2 °C |
| DOPC | 18:1 cis and 18:1 cis | −17 °C |

Swapping one saturated tail of DPPC for oleate drops Tm by 43 degrees. Making the second tail oleate as well lowers it only 15 more. The first kink is again doing most of the work. (Each swap also lengthens that tail from 16 to 18 carbons, which alone would raise Tm, so the kink's own effect is if anything larger.) Real membranes are mixtures, and the trend carries over: more saturated tails, a higher and broader transition.

**Predict.** E. coli growing at a warm temperature is moved to the cold. Its membrane is now closer to its Tm and starting to stiffen. What should it change about its membrane lipids?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Make more unsaturated fatty acids, as E. coli does. More cis kinks mean less contact, so Tm falls below the new, colder temperature and the membrane stays fluid.

</details>

Tuning composition to hold viscosity constant as the environment changes is **homeoviscous adaptation**. A trout moved to cold water remodels its membrane phospholipids toward more unsaturated forms, while a mammal holding a steady 37 °C has much less need to retune. Unsaturation is the main dial: the packing rule, run by the cell.

## Cholesterol, the buffer

Cholesterol is a rigid tetracyclic steroid about as long as a 16-carbon fatty acid, spanning a single leaflet. Its hydroxyl hydrogen-bonds at the head-group interface, its ring system pins the upper part of neighboring chains, and its tail reaches into the core.

**Predict.** Above Tm, in a fluid membrane, adding cholesterol makes the membrane more ordered. What does adding it do to a membrane below Tm, in the gel state?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It makes the gel more fluid. In a fluid membrane, cholesterol's flat, rigid rings lie against neighboring tails and stop them flexing. In a gel, the same bulky rings sit between tails that would otherwise line up and crystallize.

</details>

Cholesterol is a thermostat, not a heater or an air conditioner: rather than just shifting the melting point, it broadens the whole transition. It is also the precursor to every steroid hormone you make.

On the bench below, lengthen the chains and watch Tm climb, add cis double bonds and watch it fall, then add cholesterol and watch the sharp gel-to-fluid step flatten into a gentle slope.

## The other job: fuel (a separate rule)

Fat's value as fuel has nothing to do with packing. First, fat's carbon is more reduced. Its carbons are almost all CH₂, while each carbon of glucose already carries an oxygen, so glucose is partly burned before you start. Per gram, fat carries roughly 9 kcal against carbohydrate's 4. Second, fat is stored nearly anhydrous (without water), while glycogen is stored hydrated, binding about 2 grams of water per gram or more (estimates vary).

**Predict.** A 70 kg person stores about 135,000 kcal as fat but only about 1,600 usable kcal as glycogen. If all that fat energy were stored as hydrated glycogen instead, roughly how much more would you carry?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

> **Worked numbers.** As fat: 135,000 kcal ÷ 9 kcal/g ≈ 15,000 g, about 15 kg.
> As glycogen: take 2 g of water with each gram, so 3 g of wet store holds 4 kcal, about 1.33 kcal per wet gram. 135,000 ÷ 1.33 ≈ 100,000 g, about 100 kg.
> Difference: about 85 extra kilograms, more than a second body.

Per wet gram, fat holds 9 ÷ 1.33 ≈ 6.75 times as much energy, about sevenfold, and more if glycogen holds more water.

</details>

Fat is the savings account: dense, slow, patient. Glycogen is the checking account: fast to draw on but heavy with water. A migrating bird crossing an ocean runs mainly on fat, not glycogen, for the same reason you store it.

## Membrane identity: head groups and leaflets

A membrane must be a barrier, stay fluid, and carry an identity the cell and its neighbors can read. Phospholipids, glycolipids and cholesterol share those jobs; identity lives in the head groups.

Head-group identity sets a lipid's charge. Pumps set which of the bilayer's two sheets, or **leaflets**, it sits in: **flippases** are ATP-driven pumps that move particular lipids to the inner leaflet, and **floppases** move lipids outward, also using ATP. **Scramblases**, when switched on, let lipids cross both ways and erase the difference. Charge and location together set what the lipid does.

- **Phosphatidylcholine (PC)** carries no net charge and is enriched in the outer leaflet, as is sphingomyelin.
- **Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE)** is mostly inner.
- **Phosphatidylserine (PS)** is negative and is held on the inner leaflet, where positively charged cytoplasmic proteins meet it.
- **Phosphatidylinositol (PI)** is also inner and is the signaling lipid: its phosphorylated form, PIP₂ (phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate), is cleaved by phospholipase C into two messengers, DAG (diacylglycerol) and IP₃ (inositol trisphosphate).
- **Cardiolipin**, with two phosphates and four tails, lives almost only in the mitochondrion.

The most striking case is the death flag. When a cell undergoes apoptosis, its flippases shut off and scramblases switch on, PS reaches the outer surface, and a macrophage reads externalized PS as an "eat me" signal, engulfing the dying cell before it bursts and inflames the tissue.

One structural diagnostic is worth memorizing. Glycerophospholipids hang their tails off a three-carbon glycerol, usually by ester bonds (plasmalogens use an ether bond for one tail). Sphingolipids use an 18-carbon sphingosine backbone with one amide bond. Ester versus amide tells the two families apart. Sphingomyelin, a sphingolipid, is enriched in the myelin that insulates nerves.

Glycolipids are the cell's name tag. Their sugars face outward only, advertising the cell's identity to the immune system.

## Proteins at the membrane

Proteins associate with membranes in three ways. **Integral** proteins sit in the hydrophobic core. **Peripheral** proteins cling to the surface by ionic and hydrogen bonds. **Lipid-anchored** proteins are tethered by a covalently attached lipid: a fatty acid such as palmitate, an isoprenoid such as farnesyl, or a GPI anchor. The anchor dips into the bilayer, but the protein itself stays outside the core.

The extraction test separates peripheral proteins from membrane-attached ones. A salt or pH change frees a peripheral protein. An integral protein comes off only with detergent, and so do most lipid-anchored proteins, because of that buried anchor; a GPI-anchored protein can also be cut loose by a specific phospholipase. The lipid anchor itself is identified chemically.

An integral protein's transmembrane segment has a problem to solve: its backbone amides need hydrogen-bond partners, and the lipid core offers none. An alpha-helix solves this by satisfying its own backbone internally, taking about 20 hydrophobic residues to cross the roughly 30-angstrom core; a beta-barrel solves it by hydrogen-bonding strand to neighboring strand.

## Going deeper

The main line above does not depend on these details.

### Where the kink sits, and what melting measures

A kink near the middle of the chain disrupts packing most. Petroselinate, with its cis bond at Δ6, melts near 30 °C; oleate, with its cis bond at Δ9, melts near 13 °C. Crystal form also shifts measured melting points by several degrees.

In a bilayer, trans chains sit closer to cis than they do in a crystal. A phosphatidylcholine with two trans 18:1 tails (DEPC) has a Tm near 12 °C, closer to the cis version (DOPC, −17 °C) than to the saturated one (DSPC, 55 °C).

"Less contact" is the main cause of a kink's effect, but not the whole story. The heat needed to melt stearic acid is about 57 kJ/mol; for the α crystal of oleic acid, the form that melts near 13 °C, it is about 40 kJ/mol, roughly 30% less, so there really is less contact to break. The entropy gained on melting falls too, which partly offsets this. These values depend on crystal form (another oleic acid form needs about 52 kJ/mol).

### The acid group in a membrane

In a membrane, the apparent pKa of a long-chain fatty acid is higher than an isolated carboxyl's 4.8, so a real fraction keeps its proton at pH 7.4.

### Omega numbering and essential fatty acids

Δ numbering counts from the carboxyl end, where reactions happen; nutritionists count from the methyl, or omega, end. The omega convention earns its keep because our desaturases, the enzymes that put double bonds into chains, cannot place one between carbon 10 and the methyl end; we lack Δ12 and Δ15 desaturases. In an 18-carbon chain, an omega-3 double bond sits at Δ15 and an omega-6 at Δ12, both out of reach. We cannot make omega-3 or omega-6 fatty acids from scratch, which is why linoleate (omega-6) and α-linolenate (omega-3) are dietary essentials. Our Δ5 and Δ6 desaturases, which act on the carboxyl side of Δ9, can then turn them into longer chains such as arachidonate.

### Prostaglandins and NSAIDs

Arachidonate, an omega-6, feeds into cyclooxygenase to make prostaglandins, the signaling molecules behind inflammation, fever and pain. NSAIDs block that enzyme, which is also why they irritate the stomach: the same prostaglandins (made by COX-1) protect the gastric lining.

### Fuel per molecule and per carbon

Per molecule, palmitate yields about 106 ATP and glucose about 30–32. That comparison flatters fat, because palmitate has 16 carbons to glucose's 6. Per carbon it is about 6.6 ATP against about 5.2, and the gap is the oxygen glucose's carbons already carry. (The ATP accounting comes with β-oxidation.)

### Lipid droplets

The lipid droplets that hold stored fat were drawn for decades as inert yellow blobs. In fact they are perilipin-coated organelles, and perilipin gates a lipase's access to the stored fuel. When that regulation fails, the consequences are clinical: perilipin mutations cause lipodystrophy, and droplet dysfunction contributes to fatty liver disease and type 2 diabetes.

In tissues such as heart, muscle and brown fat, droplets also make close contacts with mitochondria; some contacts seem to help burn the fat and others to build it.

### Where the fatty acids sit on a triacylglycerol

Glycerol's three carbons are labeled sn-1, sn-2, and sn-3 (sn for stereospecific numbering); sn-2 is the middle carbon, a chiral center whenever the sn-1 and sn-3 chains differ. In many plant oils, saturated chains tend to occupy the sn-1 and sn-3 positions and an unsaturated chain the sn-2, though pig fat, the source of lard, puts palmitate at sn-2. Pancreatic lipase cleaves at sn-1 and sn-3 to release fat for absorption.

### Niemann-Pick disease

When the enzyme that breaks sphingomyelin down, acid sphingomyelinase, is missing, sphingomyelin accumulates and produces Niemann-Pick disease types A and B (type C is a separate lipid-trafficking disorder). The severe infant form, type A, erodes mental and motor abilities. Disrupt lipid catabolism and you can wreck the nervous system.

### Blood groups

Because glycolipid sugars face outward, they can serve as antigens. The A and B blood-group antigens are carried on cell-surface glycolipids and glycoproteins, and differ by a single terminal sugar — N-acetylgalactosamine for A, galactose for B — which is why transfusion matching can be a matter of life and death.

### A qualified clinical example: ATP11C

ATP11C is one of the P-type flippases that keep PS on the inner leaflet, here in red cells. In a few reported patients, mutations in the red-cell flippase ATP11C cause an inherited hemolytic anemia; how much PS their cells expose, and whether that drives their clearance, is still being worked out.

### Prenylation: Ras and progeria

Lipid anchors matter in cancer and in a premature-aging disease. The Ras oncoprotein must be prenylated, normally by farnesylation, to reach the membrane, which made farnesyltransferase a cancer drug target; K-Ras escaped the drugs by taking a different prenyl group, geranylgeranyl, instead. And in Hutchinson-Gilford progeria, a lamin A mutation leaves a farnesyl group stuck in place, anchoring the protein in the nuclear membrane, deforming the nucleus, and driving premature aging. Geometry, again, deciding fate.
