techniques:
  - name: "Electron-microscope stereology"
    blurb: >-
      Thin sections of fixed cells are photographed in the electron microscope, and the area each
      compartment covers in many random sections is measured. Area fractions in sections estimate
      volume fractions in the whole cell, the kind of measurement used to apportion the volume of
      V79 hamster cells among nucleus and mitochondria.
  - name: "Fluorescent envelope marker with Nomarski optics"
    blurb: >-
      A fluorescent protein fused to a nuclear-envelope protein outlines the nucleus, while
      Nomarski (differential interference contrast) imaging outlines the unstained cell. Measuring
      both in the same cell gives the nuclear-to-cell volume ratio, as in fission yeast with
      Cut11-GFP.
  - name: "Electronic cell sizing (Coulter counter)"
    blurb: >-
      Cells in salt solution pass one at a time through a small aperture, and each one changes the
      electrical resistance in proportion to its volume. Thousands of cells give a volume
      distribution in minutes, which is how yeast volumes of 65 to 600 femtolitres were followed.
  - name: "Complementation by a human ortholog"
    blurb: >-
      An essential yeast gene is removed or shut off, and a human ortholog is supplied on a plasmid.
      If the cells grow, the human gene supplies the function yeast needs for growth; if not, the
      result alone does not say why.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: 'Fission yeast behaves as a sizer: a cell divides once it reaches a set size. Two newborn cells of the same strain differ, one born smaller than usual and one larger. What do you expect by their next division?'
    choices:
    - Both add the same volume, so the larger cell stays larger
    - The larger-born cell grows more, to stay ahead
    - The smaller-born cell grows more, and both divide at a similar size
    - Both divide after the same time, so their sizes stay apart
    answer: 2
    rationale: Under a sizer, a cell divides on reaching a set size, so a cell born small has to grow more and a cell born large grows less, and both divide at a similar size. Adding the same volume each cycle is an adder, which shrinks a size difference only over several generations. Dividing after a fixed time is a timer, which does not correct size differences on its own. The larger-born cell growing more has the correction backwards.
  - stem: In budding yeast, every newborn cell receives about the same amount of the cell-cycle inhibitor Whi5, which is diluted as the cell grows in G1. Compared with a cell born large, what does a cell born small do?
    choices:
    - It stays longer in G1 and grows more before committing to division
    - It commits to division sooner, since it holds less Whi5
    - It commits at the same time, since both cells hold the same amount
    - It never commits, since its Whi5 stays at its birth concentration
    answer: 0
    rationale: 'The same amount of Whi5 in a smaller volume gives a higher concentration, so a cell born small must grow more to dilute it to the point where commitment becomes likely; it stays longer in G1. It does not hold less Whi5: both cells start with about the same amount, and what differs is the concentration. The same amount does not mean the same timing, because it is the concentration that falls as each cell grows. Growth dilutes Whi5 in every cell, so a small cell does commit, only later.'
  - stem: Budding-yeast cells are held in G1 so that they keep growing without dividing, until they are many times their normal volume. Their macromolecule synthesis does not keep pace with the growth. What happens to their cytoplasm?
    choices:
    - It becomes more crowded, as protein builds up faster than volume
    - It stays the same, since total protein always scales with volume
    - It shrinks, as a vacuole takes up most of the new volume
    - It becomes diluted, with macromolecules at lower concentration
    answer: 3
    rationale: If volume rises faster than RNA and protein are made, their concentration falls, so the cytoplasm becomes diluted; this is what the enlarged-yeast experiment found. More crowding would need synthesis to outrun growth, the opposite of what the stem says. Total protein rises roughly in step with volume only within a normal range, and here synthesis falls behind. Putting new volume into a vacuole is how a growing plant root cell limits its cytoplasm, a different case; here the cytoplasm does not shrink, its contents thin out.

    choices:
    - About 0.42
    - About 0.053
    - About 0.53
    - About 0.27
    answer: 1
    rationale: 37.5 divided by 710 is 0.053, about 5% of the cell. About 0.27 is the nuclear fraction in the same cells (190/710), a mix-up of compartments. About 0.53 slips the decimal point by a factor of ten. About 0.42 is 37.5 divided by 90, the average volume of one counted mitochondrion in µm³, which is a volume and not a fraction of the cell.
  - stem: In a made-up data set, a young plant cell has a volume of 1,000 µm³, of which the vacuole fills 35%. After elongation the cell is 10,000 µm³ and the vacuole fills 85%. By about what factor did the volume outside the vacuole grow?
    choices:
    - About 10-fold
    - About 2.3-fold
    - About 0.4-fold
    - About 24-fold
    answer: 1
    rationale: Outside the vacuole the cell held 650 µm³ before (65% of 1,000) and 1,500 µm³ after (15% of 10,000), a rise of about 2.3-fold. Which conclusion do these results best support, in this yeast?

    - Nuclear volume is set mainly by how much DNA the nucleus holds
    - Nuclear volume tracks cell volume rather than DNA content

    answer: 3
    rationale: Growth with the human gene but not the empty plasmid shows that the human gene supplies the function yeast needs to grow.   - stem: A model cell is a smooth sphere. Its membrane can take up a nutrient at no more than 15 molecules per µm² per second, and its cytoplasm uses 9 molecules per µm³ per second, evenly throughout. Assume uptake over the whole surface and no other limit. Above what radius can uptake no longer meet demand?
    choices:
    - A radius of 5 µm
    - A radius of 1.7 µm
    - A radius of 1.8 µm
    - A radius of 45 µm
    answer: 0
    rationale: Supply meets demand when J times 4πr² is at least q times 4πr³/3, which simplifies to r ≤ 3J/q = 3 × 15 / 9 = 5 µm. A radius of 1.7 µm is J/q, which drops the factor of 3 that comes from A/V = 3/r. A radius of 1.8 µm is 3q/J, the ratio turned upside down. A radius of 45 µm is 3J with the demand left out, as if consumption did not depend on volume.

**Growing eukaryotic cells hold their size: they couple growth to division, so division depends on how much a cell has grown.**

Size matters because it sets how much a cell can make and how much room each part gets. Most of a cell's machinery grows in step with its volume, and so do many of its organelles, including the nucleus. This lesson follows that idea in order. How do cells control their size? What happens when a cell outgrows its own synthesis? How do the parts of animal, plant and fungal cells share the space, in measured cells? What physical cost does size carry? And which organism should you study to answer a given question?

## Cells control their size by coupling growth to division

A cell that kept growing without dividing would get ever bigger; one that divided without growing would shrink. Proliferating cells avoid both by linking the decision to divide to their size. Fission yeast, a rod that grows at its ends, divides only once it reaches a set size. A cell born small grows more before it divides, and a cell born large grows less, so sizes at division come out similar. A rule like this is called a sizer. Mutants that divide too early or too late are the size mutants used later in this lesson; the genes behind them are covered in [cell-cycle control](/lessons/cell-cycle-control/).

To follow such a rule, a cell has to sense its own size. One way is dilution. In budding yeast, every newborn cell gets about the same amount of a cell-cycle inhibitor called Whi5, and Whi5 is diluted as the cell grows in G1. A big cell dilutes it sooner, so it is more likely to commit to division; a cell born small stays longer in G1 and grows more first.

Growth rate feeds in too. In bacteria, yeasts and mammalian cells alike, the faster cells grow, the larger they are.

Geometry, which comes later in this lesson, sets a cost on being big. It does not choose a cell's size. The coupling of growth to division does.

## Size sets how much a cell can make

The rate of a reaction depends on the concentration of the molecules that carry it out. So as a cell grows, it has to make more RNA and protein to keep their concentrations steady. In most cells it does: total RNA and protein rise roughly in step with volume, because larger cells transcribe and translate faster.

That scaling has a limit. When budding-yeast cells were held in G1 so that they kept growing without dividing, they reached many times their normal volume, and macromolecule synthesis failed to keep pace. Their cytoplasm became diluted. In enlarged human fibroblasts the same study could not measure protein and RNA directly, but found a sign of reduced crowding that fits dilution. Recent work suggests that excess size itself helps drive senescence, the permanent exit from division. A cell can be too big for its own synthesis machinery, not only for its membrane.

## The parts scale with the cell: a set of measured compartments

A eukaryotic cell keeps most of its DNA inside a membrane-bounded nucleus; mitochondria, and plastids in plants, carry small genomes of their own. Its interior is divided into compartments, regions enclosed by a membrane. The space inside an organelle is its lumen; the fluid outside the organelles but inside the plasma membrane is the cytosol. Think of a floor plan. A sketch tells you there is a kitchen and a bedroom; a floor plan with square metres tells you how the house is used.

Animal, plant and fungal cells share a plasma membrane, a nucleus and a cytoskeleton, the network of protein filaments that shapes the cell and moves things within it. All three have an endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus, which sort and transport proteins headed for secretion, and mitochondria, which carry out [oxidative phosphorylation](/lessons/oxidative-phosphorylation/). The bilayer that bounds all of these is covered in [membrane structure and function](/lessons/membrane-structure-function/), and the broad picture of the cell in [the introduction to biochemistry and cell biology](/lessons/intro-biochemistry-cell-biology/).

The differences are where students slip:

| Part | Animal cell | Plant cell | Fungal cell (yeast) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wall | none | cellulose fibres in a matrix of hemicelluloses and pectin | β-glucans, chitin and mannoproteins (*S. cerevisiae*) |
| Plastids | none | chloroplasts in photosynthetic cells; in nonphotosynthetic tissues, leucoplasts without pigment or, in many fruits and flowers, pigmented chromoplasts | none |
| Vacuole | small or absent | often large, can fill most of the cell | acidic, with degradative and storage roles |

Chloroplasts carry out the [light reactions](/lessons/light-reactions/), but only in cells that photosynthesize. A root cell deep in soil is a plant cell without them. Yeasts are unicellular fungi, and budding yeast has the same core organelles as your cells, which is why it is so useful.

A textbook drawing of "the animal cell" gives you the parts but not the shares. Each number below belongs to one cell type. Carry the label with the number.

- **Chinese hamster V79-S171 cells, log phase, in culture:** mean volume 710 µm³, nucleus 190 µm³, total mitochondria 37.5 µm³, about 90 mitochondria per cell (an estimate from EM sections that depends on how a mitochondrion is counted; see Going deeper). In 1972 D. W. Ross and H. C. Mel took whole-cell volume from electronic cell sizing and apportioned it among nucleus, cytoplasm and mitochondria by electron microscopy.
- **Wild-type fission yeast, growing:** nuclear-to-cell volume ratio 0.080 ± 0.013, measured by Frank Neumann and Paul Nurse with a fluorescent nuclear-envelope marker.
- **Arabidopsis leaf mesophyll, wild type (Wassilewskija):** a mean of 83 chloroplasts per cell. That is one tissue of one plant, not every plant cell.

Now read the hamster data as you would a results table.

**Worked problem, part 1: reading a volume table.** Using the V79 means:

> nuclear fraction = 190 / 710 = 0.27;  mitochondrial fraction = 37.5 / 710 = 0.053

So the nucleus is a little more than a quarter of the cell, about 27%, and mitochondria about 5%. If you treat the cell as a sphere of the same volume, r³ = 3 × 710 / (4π) = 169.5 µm³, so r = 5.5 µm and the sphere-equivalent diameter is about 11.1 µm. That is a calculation, not a measured diameter; cells spread on a dish are flatter than that.

Compartment shares differ between cell types: 0.27 of a V79 cell is nucleus, against 0.08 of a fission yeast cell. Each value is real for its own cell, so there is no universal nuclear fraction. Within one cell type, though, the nucleus grows with the cell, and many other organelles also scale in size or number with the cell, in ways that depend on the cell type. The design problem below shows how the nuclear result was tested. How these volumes are measured, and what limits each method, comes in [microscopy and quantitative images](/lessons/microscopy-and-images/).

### Design problem

Does DNA content or cell size set the size of the nucleus? You work with fission yeast and have size mutants that divide at very different sizes, a way to raise DNA content without division (switching off the mitotic cyclin Cdc13 so the genome is copied repeatedly), a fluorescent nuclear-envelope marker, and images that outline each cell. Design the experiment and say what each model predicts before you look.

<summary>One way to do it</summary>

First, measure nuclear and cell volume in each cell, across the widest size range you can get from the mutants. If cell size sets nuclear size, the nuclear-to-cell ratio stays constant as cells get bigger. If DNA alone sets it, nuclear volume stays roughly fixed in cells with the same DNA content, so the ratio falls as cells grow.

Second, change DNA content without changing the rule for cell size. Switch off Cdc13 so the genome is copied several times, and compare cells with 2C and with much higher DNA content. The DNA model predicts a relatively larger nucleus in the high-DNA cells; the cell-size model predicts the same ratio.

Controls: measure wild-type cells in the same conditions, use the same marker and thresholds for every strain, and measure enough cells per strain to see the spread.

Neumann and Nurse did this in 2007. The ratio stayed roughly constant, near 0.08, across a 35-fold range of average cell sizes, and a 16-fold difference in DNA content (2C against 32C) left relative nuclear size unchanged. In cells with several nuclei, a nucleus surrounded by more cytoplasm grew faster. The answer in fission yeast is cell size, probably through something in the cytoplasm.

## Plant cells grow by filling a vacuole

A plant cell can grow large without making much new cytoplasm, because it puts most of its new volume into the vacuole. In Arabidopsis root epidermis, the vacuole fills about 30 to 40% of young cells and 80 to 90% after elongation. Across that comparison the cells grew about 14-fold in volume, while the space between vacuole and cell boundary grew only about two- to threefold.

The vacuole cannot do this alone. The cell wall has to loosen so the cell can stretch, and vacuole expansion and wall loosening must be coordinated. Which one leads, and exactly what the vacuole contributes, is still being worked out.

## A physical cost: surface, volume and supply

Everything a cell takes in crosses its surface, while everything it uses is spread through its volume. Picture a city fed by trucks entering at its border: double the city's width and the border doubles, but the area to feed goes up fourfold.

**Worked problem, part 2: geometry.** Take a hypothetical smooth spherical cell that grows from radius 5 µm to 10 µm.

> A = 4πr²    V = 4πr³/3    A/V = 3/r

At r = 5 µm: A = 314.2 µm², V = 523.6 µm³, A/V = 0.6 µm⁻¹.
At r = 10 µm: A = 1256.6 µm², V = 4188.8 µm³, A/V = 0.3 µm⁻¹.

The radius doubles, the area rises fourfold, the volume eightfold, and the ratio halves.

**Worked problem, part 3: supply against demand.** Now invent two rates; they are made-up inputs, not measurements. Suppose the membrane can take up a nutrient at no more than J = 20 molecules µm⁻² s⁻¹, and the cell uses q = 6 molecules µm⁻³ s⁻¹ evenly through its volume. Assume the whole surface takes up nutrient and nothing else limits the cell.

> supply meets demand when J × A ≥ q × V, that is, 3/r ≥ q/J, so r_max = 3J/q

Here r_max = 3 × 20 / 6 = 10 µm. The ratio of capacity to demand is (J/q) × (3/r) = 10/r: at 5 µm it is 2 (supply 6,283 molecules per second against demand of 3,142), and at 10 µm it is exactly 1. Change J, q or the shape and the answer changes.

So the arithmetic gives a scaling penalty, not a universal maximum. It leaves out several things.

**Shape.** A flat or long thin cell keeps much more surface per volume than a sphere. Diffusion time grows with the square of distance, which also favours cells where no point is far from the surface.

**Vacuoles.** A vacuole-filled plant cell grows its cytoplasm far less than the cell, as the previous section showed, so surface per unit of cytoplasm falls more slowly than the sphere model says.

**Biosynthetic scaling.** A cell can outgrow its own synthesis machinery before it outgrows its membrane, as the enlarged-yeast experiment above showed.

**The size guide itself.** "Eukaryotic cells are 10 to 100 µm across" is a guide, not a bound. A 65 fL yeast cell has a sphere-equivalent diameter of about 5 µm.

## Model systems and the limits of transfer

Choose the organism for the question, and remember what a result in it does and does not show.

**Budding yeast** (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) is fast and easy to manipulate genetically; how genes are deleted is covered in [CRISPR and functional genomics](/lessons/crispr-and-functional-genomics/). Many of its genes still do their job when swapped for the human version: when human orthologs were tested in place of 414 essential yeast genes, 176 (43%) restored growth. That shows a conserved function in those genes. It does not show that the rest are unconserved, since failure can have several causes, and growth rescue says nothing about tissue roles.

**Fission yeast** (*Schizosaccharomyces pombe*) is a rod that grows at its ends, which makes size easy to measure; its size control and nuclear scaling appear earlier in this lesson.

**Cultured mammalian lines** give precise numbers, like the V79 volumes, but a line is not an animal, and a line's name is not a guarantee: strains of the breast cancer line MCF7 have diverged genetically and can respond very differently to the same drugs. Report the strain and conditions.

**Flies** (*Drosophila*): many human disease genes have apparent fly counterparts (see Going deeper for the tallies), but conservation is gene by gene. No fly counterpart of α-synuclein was found, yet a missing gene can sometimes be added back: flies made to express human α-synuclein are used to model Parkinson's disease.

**Worms** (*C. elegans*): the worm is transparent, so its cells can be watched in a living animal, and most worms fertilize themselves, so a mutation breeds true without a mate. Sydney Brenner's mutant screens showed how to go from a visible defect in a whole animal to a mapped gene. Human effects need more evidence.

**Plants** (*Arabidopsis*): the *arc6* mutant, with one to three chloroplasts per mesophyll cell against 83 in wild type, and the root vacuole measurements show control of chloroplast number and vacuole size that animal cells do not offer. Leaf and root data are not interchangeable.

## Going deeper

### Sizers, adders and timers

A sizer, as in fission yeast, divides at a set size, so it can correct a size error within one cycle. An adder adds about the same volume each cycle whatever the birth size, so a size error shrinks only over several generations. A timer keeps cycle time constant; on its own it cannot correct size in cells that grow exponentially, though it can hold the sizes a sizer has set. Real cells mix these. Budding yeast shows a weak sizer in G1 and a timer for the rest of the cycle, so its whole cycle behaves roughly like an adder; fission yeast behaves as a sizer overall. Many cultured mammalian cell lines behave as adders, partly because cancer mutations disrupt their G1/S control. Size control is also noisy: budding-yeast cells pass the commitment point over a range of sizes, but the chance of passing rises as they grow. Whi5 works much like the mammalian brake Rb, and plants use dilution too: in Arabidopsis the inhibitor KRP4 is diluted with growth. Fission yeast reads several inputs at once, among them the activator Cdc25, which is made in step with cell volume.

### The enlarged-yeast experiment

In 2019 Gabriel Neurohr, Angelika Amon and colleagues held budding-yeast cells in G1 of the cell cycle with a temperature-sensitive *cdc28-13* allele. The cells kept growing, from about 65 fL to about 600 fL in six hours. When macromolecule synthesis failed to keep pace with volume, the cytoplasm became diluted. At very large sizes the nuclear DNA itself appears to become limiting for transcription. In primary human fibroblasts (IMR90) enlarged by senescence induction, the evidence was faster diffusion of tracer nanoparticles, the indirect sign of reduced crowding mentioned above.

### Counting organelles

Counts need a definition too. The figure of about 90 mitochondria per V79 cell is an estimate from EM sections, and mammalian mitochondria can also join into branched networks. In budding yeast, image analysis shows that mitochondria can form one interconnected network or many separate tubules, so the count depends on the rule: counting each connected structure as one object makes a single network one mitochondrion. This lesson gives no census of organelle numbers across animals, plants and fungi.

### Gene tallies in flies and worms

A 2000 survey by Fortini and colleagues found apparent fly counterparts for 178 of 287 human disease genes, but none for α-synuclein; a larger 2001 search by Reiter and colleagues matched 714 of 929 disease genes (77%) to fly sequences. Sydney Brenner's 1974 paper linked about 300 EMS-induced mutants to about 100 genes, establishing gene-to-phenotype links in a whole animal.

### Humanized yeast and cell-line strains

The humanization test was reported in 2015 by Aashiq Kachroo, Edward Marcotte and colleagues. The 43% comes from their own assays, judged by rescue of growth; adding earlier published cases brought the rate to 47%. The MCF7 comparison is from Uri Ben-David and colleagues, who characterized 27 strains of the line in 2018. They found rapid genetic diversification, and at least 75% of the compounds that strongly inhibited some strains were completely inactive in others.
