techniques:
  - name: "Electron-microscope stereology"
    blurb: >-
      Thin sections of fixed cells are photographed in the electron microscope, and the area each
      compartment covers in many random sections is measured. Area fractions in sections estimate
      volume fractions in the whole cell, the kind of measurement used to apportion the volume of
      V79 hamster cells among nucleus and mitochondria.
  - name: "Fluorescent envelope marker with Nomarski optics"
    blurb: >-
      A fluorescent protein fused to a nuclear-envelope protein outlines the nucleus, while
      Nomarski (differential interference contrast) imaging outlines the unstained cell. Measuring
      both in the same cell gives the nuclear-to-cell volume ratio, as in fission yeast with
      Cut11-GFP.
  - name: "Electronic cell sizing (Coulter counter)"
    blurb: >-
      Cells in salt solution pass one at a time through a small aperture, and each one changes the
      electrical resistance in proportion to its volume. Thousands of cells give a volume
      distribution in minutes, which is how yeast volumes of 65 to 600 femtolitres were followed.
  - name: "Complementation by a human ortholog"
    blurb: >-
      An essential yeast gene is removed or shut off, and a human ortholog is supplied on a plasmid.
      If the cells grow, the human gene supplies the function yeast needs for growth; if not, the
      result alone does not say why.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: 'Fission yeast behaves as a sizer: a cell divides once it reaches a set size. Two newborn cells of the same strain differ, one born smaller than usual and one larger. What do you expect by their next division?'
    choices:
    - Both add the same volume, so the larger cell stays larger
    - The larger-born cell grows more, to stay ahead
    - The smaller-born cell grows more, and both divide at a similar size
    - Both divide after the same time, so their sizes stay apart
    answer: 2
    rationale: Under a sizer, a cell divides on reaching a set size, so a cell born small has to grow more and a cell born large grows less, and both divide at a similar size. Adding the same volume each cycle is an adder, which shrinks a size difference only over several generations. Dividing after a fixed time is a timer, which does not correct size differences on its own. The larger-born cell growing more has the correction backwards.
  - stem: Under the leading, debated Whi5-dilution model, two budding-yeast daughters begin G1 with about the same amount of Whi5. Compared with a daughter born large, what does a daughter born small tend to do?
    choices:
    - It stays longer in G1 and grows more before committing to division
    - It commits to division sooner, since it holds less Whi5
    - It commits at the same time, since both cells hold the same amount
    - It never commits, since its Whi5 stays at its birth concentration
    answer: 0
    rationale: 'The same amount of Whi5 in a smaller volume gives a higher concentration, so a cell born small must grow more to dilute it to the point where commitment becomes likely; it stays longer in G1. It does not hold less Whi5: both cells start with about the same amount, and what differs is the concentration. The same amount does not mean the same timing, because it is the concentration that falls as each cell grows. Growth dilutes Whi5 in every cell, so a small cell does commit, only later.'
  - stem: Budding-yeast cells are held in G1 so that they keep growing without dividing, until they are many times their normal volume. Their macromolecule synthesis does not keep pace with the growth. What happens to their cytoplasm?
    choices:
    - It becomes more crowded, as protein builds up faster than volume
    - It stays the same, since total protein always scales with volume
    - It shrinks, as a vacuole takes up most of the new volume
    - It becomes diluted, with macromolecules at lower concentration
    answer: 3
    rationale: If volume rises faster than RNA and protein are made, their concentration falls, so the cytoplasm becomes diluted; this is what the enlarged-yeast experiment found. More crowding would need synthesis to outrun growth, the opposite of what the stem says. Total protein rises roughly in step with volume only within a normal range, and here synthesis falls behind. Putting new volume into a vacuole is how a growing plant root cell limits its cytoplasm, a different case; here the cytoplasm does not shrink, its contents thin out.
  - stem: In log-phase Chinese hamster V79-S171 cells, the mean cell volume was 710 µm³ and the mean total mitochondrial volume was 37.5 µm³. What fraction of cell volume did mitochondria occupy?
    choices:
    - About 0.42
    - About 0.053
    - About 0.53
    - About 0.27
    answer: 1
    rationale: 37.5 divided by 710 is 0.053, about 5% of the cell. About 0.27 is the nuclear fraction in the same cells (190/710), a mix-up of compartments. About 0.53 slips the decimal point by a factor of ten. About 0.42 is 37.5 divided by 90, the average volume of one counted mitochondrion in µm³, which is a volume and not a fraction of the cell.
  - stem: In a made-up data set, a young plant cell has a volume of 1,000 µm³, of which the vacuole fills 35%. After elongation the cell is 10,000 µm³ and the vacuole fills 85%. By about what factor did the volume outside the vacuole grow?
    choices:
    - About 10-fold
    - About 2.3-fold
    - About 0.4-fold
    - About 24-fold
    answer: 1
    rationale: Outside the vacuole the cell held 650 µm³ before (65% of 1,000) and 1,500 µm³ after (15% of 10,000), a rise of about 2.3-fold. About 10-fold assumes the cytoplasm grows with the whole cell, which is exactly what a vacuole-dominated cell does not do. About 24-fold is the growth of the vacuole itself (350 to 8,500 µm³). About 0.4-fold is the ratio of the vacuole fractions, 35 over 85, which is not a volume change at all.

In a second experiment, a cyclin is switched off so that DNA is copied again and again without division; cells carrying 2C and 32C DNA show the same nuclear-to-cell volume ratio. Which conclusion do these results best support, in this yeast?
    choices:
    - Nuclear volume is fixed and stays the same as the cell grows
    - Nuclear volume is set mainly by how much DNA the nucleus holds
    - Nuclear volume tracks cell volume rather than DNA content
    - Nuclear volume tracks DNA content rather than cell volume
    answer: 2
    rationale: The ratio stays constant while cell size changes 35-fold, so the nucleus grows with the cell, and a 16-fold change in DNA leaves the ratio unchanged, so DNA content is not what sets it. The two choices that make DNA the controller predict a much larger relative nucleus at 32C, which was not seen. A fixed nuclear volume would make the ratio fall as cells grew, but it stayed near 0.08. The result is scoped to fission yeast; it does not give a universal nuclear fraction, since hamster V79 cells have about 0.27.
  - stem: A budding-yeast strain lacks an essential gene and survives only on a plasmid copy that can be shut off. A second plasmid carries either the human ortholog or nothing. With the yeast copy shut off, cells carrying the human gene grow almost normally and cells with the empty plasmid do not grow. A second essential gene tested in the same way gives no growth with its human ortholog. Which interpretation is best supported?
    choices:
    - The first human gene does in yeast all it does in human tissue
    - The second human gene shares no function with its yeast ortholog
    - The first yeast gene is not essential in the conditions used
    - The first human gene supplies the function yeast needs to grow
    answer: 3
    rationale: Growth with the human gene but not the empty plasmid shows that the human gene supplies the function yeast needs to grow. The yeast gene is essential here, since the empty-plasmid cells fail to grow. Rescue of growth says nothing about the many functions the gene may have in human tissue. Failure of the second gene has several possible causes, such as poor expression or a missing partner protein, so it does not show that the two genes share no function.
  - stem: A model cell is a smooth sphere. Its membrane can take up a nutrient at no more than 15 molecules per µm² per second, and its cytoplasm uses 9 molecules per µm³ per second, evenly throughout. Assume uptake over the whole surface and no other limit. Above what radius can uptake no longer meet demand?
    choices:
    - A radius of 5 µm
    - A radius of 1.7 µm
    - A radius of 1.8 µm
    - A radius of 45 µm
    answer: 0
    rationale: Supply meets demand when J times 4πr² is at least q times 4πr³/3, which simplifies to r ≤ 3J/q = 3 × 15 / 9 = 5 µm. A radius of 1.7 µm is J/q, which drops the factor of 3 that comes from A/V = 3/r. A radius of 1.8 µm is 3q/J, the ratio turned upside down. A radius of 45 µm is 3J with the demand left out, as if consumption did not depend on volume.

**In 1977 Leland Hartwell and Michael Unger followed budding yeast as it divided unequally, into a large mother cell and a small daughter. The daughter waited longer before starting its next cycle, as if it first had to reach a size.**

The first film above followed one ratio, amount over volume; it leaves out the evidence behind each step, and where a cell's volume goes. The sections below give the evidence for the Whi5 ruler, for contents keeping pace, and for the two costs, the genome and the surface; the second film then follows the compartments that share the volume, how root cells add vacuolar volume, and a short reference to animal, plant and fungal parts.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Many of a cell's processes depend on concentrations: amount divided by volume. Growth raises the volume, so anything whose amount does not keep pace is diluted. In the leading model, budding yeast reads its size from one such dilution, of a brake called Whi5. Most other contents a growing cell must make in step with its volume. Two things can fall behind and set costs of being big: a single genome, which appears to serve only so much cytoplasm, and the surface, which grows more slowly than the volume when a cell keeps its shape.

A note on units: cell volumes are given in femtolitres (fL) or cubic micrometres (µm³), and the two are the same, 1 fL = 1 µm³.

## Reading size: the brake that growth dilutes

The first film's ruler needs a few terms, then its evidence. The budding-yeast cycle runs through G1 (growth before the DNA is copied), S (DNA synthesis), G2 and M (mitosis). **Start**, the first film's green line, is the point in G1 where the cell commits to a full cycle of copying its DNA and dividing (the mammalian version, the restriction point, is in [cell-cycle control](/lessons/cell-cycle-control/)). Whi5 is a protein that holds back the transcription factor (a gene-switching protein) that turns on the genes for Start; as Whi5's concentration falls, the brake weakens. A rule under which cells divide at about the same size, whatever size they were born, is a **sizer**.

The first film's red dots stand for a measurement. In 2015 Kurt Schmoller, Jan Skotheim and colleagues tagged Whi5 with a fluorescent protein and measured it in single living cells. Whi5 was made mostly in S, G2 and M, in an amount that hardly depended on the cell's size. A small daughter therefore began G1 with about as many Whi5 molecules as a larger cell, at a higher concentration. That premise does all the work: if the dose at birth grew in proportion to birth size, every cell would start at the same concentration, and dilution would tell it nothing about its size.

**Predict.** A strain carries two copies of the *WHI5* gene instead of one, so each newborn cell gets about twice the usual dose. Do these cells pass Start at their normal size, a smaller size, or a larger one? And what would deleting *WHI5* do?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Larger. In the idealized model, if the Whi5 dose stays fixed during G1 and Start occurs at the same Whi5 concentration, doubling the dose doubles the volume at that threshold. Experimentally, adding *WHI5* copies made cells larger, though not by a guaranteed twofold amount. Cells lacking Whi5 pass Start small.

</details>

The first film used the simplest case, a hard line that every cell crosses at the same size; here is the fuller picture. The budding-yeast sizer is weak and noisy: cells pass Start over a range of sizes, though the chance rises as they grow, so a cell born small makes up only part of its shortfall in one G1. And how much of size control Whi5 dilution explains is still argued (Going deeper).

Fission yeast, a rod that grows at its ends, reads size another way, before mitosis. It divides only once it reaches a set size, so a cell born small grows more first, a cell born large grows less, and sizes at division come out similar. Its mutants that divide too early or too late are the size mutants in the design problem below.

## Keeping pace: RNA and protein

The ruler works because Whi5 does not keep pace; nearly everything else has to. The rate of a reaction depends on the concentration of the molecules that carry it out, so a growing cell has to make more RNA and protein to keep their concentrations steady. In most cells it does: total RNA and protein rise roughly in step with volume, because larger cells transcribe and translate faster. The first film's flat orange gauge showed concentration only: the total amount still grows with the cell, but the concentration carries no size signal.

## What can fall behind: the genome

The first film's first cost, the genome, ended on a question. Its swelling cells come from a 2019 study by Gabriel Neurohr, Angelika Amon and colleagues: held in G1, budding-yeast cells grew from about 65 fL to about 600 fL in six hours, and their cytoplasm was diluted.

**Predict.** After six hours these cells are both much bigger and much longer arrested. How could you tell whether the dilution comes from their size or from the time spent arrested?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Hold some cells for the same time but let them grow less. The team did this by limiting glucose during the arrest, so these cells spent as long arrested but stayed smaller. If dilution comes from time, they should be diluted too; if from size, they should be spared. The comparison pointed to size: it was the enlarged cells that failed to scale up synthesis.

</details>

Why the genome? RNA is copied from DNA, so a cell with one genome copy has a fixed set of templates. At very large sizes the nuclear DNA itself appears to become limiting for transcription; if so, one genome can serve only so much cytoplasm. Cells that copy their genome without dividing, or carry extra genome sets, tend to be larger, which fits that idea. In enlarged human fibroblasts (connective-tissue cells) the same study could not measure protein and RNA directly, but found a sign of reduced crowding that fits dilution. Recent work suggests that excess size itself helps drive senescence, the permanent exit from division. A cell can be too big for its own synthesis machinery, not only for its membrane.

## What falls behind: the surface

## Where the volume goes: compartments and their shares

The first film's purple block was DNA alone; the second film adds the nuclear envelope around it. The second film left one question open: does the nucleus follow its DNA or its cell? Many other organelles also scale in size or number with the cell, in ways that depend on the organelle and cell type, but the nucleus is where this was tested most cleanly.

### Design problem

Does DNA content or cell size set the size of the nucleus? You work with fission yeast and have size mutants that divide at very different sizes, a way to raise DNA content without division (switching off Cdc13, the cyclin the cell needs to enter mitosis, so the genome is copied again and again), a fluorescent nuclear-envelope marker, and images that outline each cell. Design the experiment and say what each model predicts before you look.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

First, measure nuclear and cell volume in each cell, across the widest size range you can get from the mutants. If cell size sets nuclear size, the nuclear-to-cell ratio stays constant as cells get bigger. If DNA alone sets it, nuclear volume stays roughly fixed in cells with the same DNA content, so the ratio falls as cells grow.

Second, change DNA content without changing the rule for cell size. Switch off Cdc13 so the genome is copied several times, and compare cells with 2C and with much higher DNA content (C is the DNA of one haploid genome). The DNA model predicts a relatively larger nucleus in the high-DNA cells; the cell-size model predicts the same ratio.

Controls: measure wild-type cells in the same conditions, use the same marker and thresholds for every strain, and measure enough cells per strain to see the spread.

Neumann and Nurse did this in 2007. The ratio stayed roughly constant, near 0.08, across a 35-fold range of average cell sizes, and a 16-fold difference in DNA content (2C against 32C) left relative nuclear size unchanged. In cells with several nuclei, a nucleus surrounded by more cytoplasm grew faster. The answer in fission yeast is cell size, probably through something in the cytoplasm.

</details>

Here are the two measured shares behind the second film, each with its cell type and method.

- **Chinese hamster V79-S171 cells, log phase, in culture:** mean volume 710 µm³, nucleus 190 µm³, total mitochondria 37.5 µm³, about 90 mitochondria per cell (an estimate from electron-microscope sections that depends on how a mitochondrion is counted; see Going deeper). In 1972 D. W. Ross and H. C. Mel took whole-cell volume from electronic cell sizing and apportioned it among nucleus, cytoplasm and mitochondria by electron microscopy.
- **Wild-type fission yeast, growing:** nuclear-to-cell volume ratio 0.080 ± 0.013, measured by Frank Neumann and Paul Nurse with a fluorescent nuclear-envelope marker.

**Reading a volume table.** From the V79 means:

> nuclear fraction = 190 / 710 = 0.27;  mitochondrial fraction = 37.5 / 710 = 0.053

So in fission yeast the nuclear share holds as the cell grows, while the hamster and yeast measurements show that shares differ between cell types. Carry the cell type with each number.

Treated as a sphere of the same volume, a V79 cell has r³ = 3 × 710 / (4π) = 169.5 µm³, so r = 5.5 µm and the diameter is about 11.1 µm. That is a calculation, not a measured diameter; cells spread on a dish are flatter. How these volumes are measured comes in [microscopy and quantitative images](/lessons/microscopy-and-images/).

## Same parts, different shares

This last section is a reference tool, not a new step: the shares above only mean something if you know which parts each kind of cell has.

A eukaryotic cell keeps most of its DNA inside a membrane-bounded nucleus; mitochondria, and plastids in plants, carry small genomes of their own. Its interior is divided into compartments, regions enclosed by a membrane. The space inside an organelle is its lumen; the fluid outside the organelles but inside the plasma membrane is the cytosol.

Animal, plant and fungal cells share a plasma membrane, a nucleus and a cytoskeleton, the network of protein filaments that shapes the cell and moves things within it. All three have an endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus, which sort and transport proteins headed for secretion, and mitochondria, which carry out [oxidative phosphorylation](/lessons/oxidative-phosphorylation/). The bilayer that bounds all of these is covered in [membrane structure and function](/lessons/membrane-structure-function/), and the broad picture of the cell in [the introduction to biochemistry and cell biology](/lessons/intro-biochemistry-cell-biology/).

The differences are where students slip:

| Part | Animal cell | Plant cell | Fungal cell (yeast) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wall | none | cellulose fibres in a matrix of hemicelluloses and pectin | β-glucans, chitin and mannoproteins (*S. cerevisiae*) |
| Plastids | none | chloroplasts in photosynthetic cells; in nonphotosynthetic tissues, leucoplasts without pigment or, in many fruits and flowers, pigmented chromoplasts | none |
| Vacuole | small or absent | often large, can fill most of the cell | acidic, with degradative and storage roles |

Chloroplasts carry out the [light reactions](/lessons/light-reactions/), but only in cells that photosynthesize. A root cell deep in soil usually lacks chloroplasts, though it still contains other plastids. Their number is a measured share too: wild-type Arabidopsis leaf mesophyll (Wassilewskija) holds a mean of 83 chloroplasts per cell, a figure for one tissue of one plant. Yeasts are unicellular fungi, and budding yeast has the same core organelles as your cells, which is why it is so useful.

## Going deeper

### Sizers, adders and timers

A sizer can correct a size error within one cycle. An adder adds about the same volume each cycle whatever the birth size, so a size error shrinks only over several generations. A timer keeps cycle time constant; on its own it cannot correct size in cells that grow exponentially, though it can hold the sizes a sizer has set. Budding yeast shows a weak sizer in G1 and a timer for the rest of the cycle, so its whole cycle behaves roughly like an adder; fission yeast behaves as a sizer overall. Many cultured mammalian cell lines behave close to adders; one proposal, not settled, is that cancer mutations disrupt their G1/S control. Whi5 works much like the mammalian brake Rb, and plants use dilution too: in Arabidopsis the inhibitor KRP4 is diluted with growth.

### Debates over what is measured

Whi5 dilution is the leading model, not a closed case: later measurements disagree about how far Whi5 concentration falls during G1 and how much of size control it explains (for example, Litsios and colleagues in 2019 and Barber, Amir and Murray in 2020, with follow-ups from the Schmoller and Skotheim groups). Fission yeast reads several inputs at once. One is the activator Cdc25, whose concentration rises as the cell grows, the mirror image of Whi5. Whether it measures its length, surface area or volume is debated; a sensor at the cell cortex built around the protein Cdr2 has been proposed to read surface area.

Growth rate feeds in too. In bacteria and yeasts, cells that grow faster, in richer media, are larger; a similar trend is reported in mammalian cells, but it is weaker and depends on how growth is changed.

### The enlarged-yeast experiment

The arrest used a temperature-sensitive *cdc28-13* allele. In primary human fibroblasts (IMR90) enlarged by senescence induction, the evidence was faster diffusion of tracer nanoparticles, the indirect sign of reduced crowding mentioned above.

### Counting organelles

The count of about 90 mitochondria per V79 cell depends on the counting rule, since mammalian mitochondria can also join into branched networks. In budding yeast, image analysis shows that mitochondria can form one interconnected network or many separate tubules, so the count depends on the rule: counting each connected structure as one object makes a single network one mitochondrion. This lesson gives no census of organelle numbers across animals, plants and fungi.

### Model organisms and the limits of transfer

This lesson's numbers come from two yeasts, a hamster line and Arabidopsis; each model answers some questions and not others.

**Budding yeast** (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) is fast and easy to manipulate genetically ([CRISPR and functional genomics](/lessons/crispr-and-functional-genomics/)). In 2015 Aashiq Kachroo, Edward Marcotte and colleagues tested human orthologs (the matching human genes) in place of 414 essential yeast genes, and 176 (43%) restored growth in their own assays; adding earlier published cases brought the rate to 47%. That shows a conserved function in those genes. It does not show that the rest are unconserved, since failure can have several causes, and growth rescue says nothing about tissue roles.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/measured-eukaryotic-cell/human-ortholog-rescue-test.webp" width={1200} height={807} alt="AI-generated illustration: two side-by-side frames of budding yeast whose own copy of an essential gene is shut off. With an empty plasmid a single cell does not grow; with a plasmid carrying the human ortholog the cells bud and grow." caption="With the yeast copy of an essential gene shut off, cells with an empty plasmid cannot grow, while cells given the human ortholog can. Growth rescue shows a conserved function; failure alone does not say why." />

**Fission yeast** (*Schizosaccharomyces pombe*) grows at its ends, which makes size easy to measure.

**Cultured mammalian lines** give precise numbers, like the V79 volumes, but a line is not an animal, and a line's name is not a guarantee. Uri Ben-David and colleagues characterized 27 strains of the breast cancer line MCF7 in 2018 and found rapid genetic diversification: at least 75% of the compounds that strongly inhibited some strains were completely inactive in others. Report the strain and conditions.

**Flies** (*Drosophila*): a 2000 survey by Fortini and colleagues found apparent fly counterparts for 178 of 287 human disease genes, and a larger 2001 search by Reiter and colleagues matched 714 of 929 (77%). Conservation is gene by gene: no fly counterpart of α-synuclein was found, yet flies made to express human α-synuclein are used to model Parkinson's disease.

**Worms** (*C. elegans*) are transparent and mostly self-fertilizing, so cells can be watched in a living animal and a mutation breeds true without a mate. Sydney Brenner's 1974 paper linked about 300 EMS-induced mutants to about 100 genes, from visible defect to mapped gene. Human effects need more evidence.

**Plants** (*Arabidopsis*): the *arc6* mutant, with one to three chloroplasts per mesophyll cell against 83 in wild type, and the root vacuole measurements show control of chloroplast number and vacuole size that animal cells do not offer. Leaf and root data are not interchangeable.
