## Explain: a twisted ladder with a four-letter code

<WordBank terms={['DNA', 'double helix', 'base', 'base pair', 'sequence', 'chromosome', 'gene', 'protein', 'trait']} />

**DNA** is the molecule that carries the instructions for building proteins. Those proteins shape what a living thing is like and keep it alive.

Take a rope ladder and twist it along its length. That is the shape of DNA. A **double helix** is this shape, a ladder twisted around itself.

The two sides of the ladder are made of sugar and phosphate. These sides are the same in every living thing. A strawberry and a person build them from the same parts.

The code sits in the rungs. A **base** is one of four small units in the middle of the ladder. Scientists name them with letters: A, T, C and G. A **base pair** is two bases joined in the middle to make one rung. A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G. So if one side reads A C G T, the other side reads T G C A.

The **sequence** is the order of the bases along the DNA. The sequence is the code. A different order gives different instructions. That is why scientists write DNA as letters only, such as ATTGCAC. The sides are the same everywhere, so writing them down would add nothing.

Think of the bases like letters in a word. The same letters in a new order spell a new word. The comparison breaks here: a cell does not understand words. Its molecules simply follow the bases in order.

People worked this out step by step, over many years. In the early 1950s, X-ray photos of DNA and careful model building showed the double helix. The model was published in 1953.

## Explain: from chromosome to gene to protein to trait

DNA is very long and very thin. In plants, animals and fungi, most of it sits in the nucleus, which you met in [Inside a Cell](/lessons/inside-a-cell/).

A **chromosome** is a strand of DNA wound up tightly with proteins. Before a cell divides, chromosomes pack down even more. Then they are thick enough to see with a microscope.

A **gene** is a section of a chromosome that carries a code. Many genes carry the code for a particular protein. Others carry code the cell uses in other ways. One chromosome carries many genes.

A **protein** is a molecule that builds cells, tissues and organs and does most of the work in cells. Some proteins form hair. Others carry oxygen in blood or speed up reactions in cells.

A **trait** is a feature of a living thing, such as flower colour or eye colour. Many genes control the making of certain proteins, and those proteins affect traits.

> chromosome → gene → protein → trait

How does a cell turn a gene into a protein? It reads the bases in order, and the order decides which protein is built. You learn how in high school Biology.

Here is an example. In pea plants, purple flowers need a working protein from one gene. A change in that gene can stop the protein from working, and the flowers come out white. Changes in the code are called mutations, as you see in [Mutations and Variation](/lessons/mutations-and-variation/). Many traits depend on several genes, and on the surroundings too.

Almost every cell in your body carries the same DNA. So why does a skin cell look so different from a muscle cell? Each kind of cell uses a different set of genes. So each makes a different mix of proteins. How chromosomes pass from parents to offspring comes in [Reproduction and Meiosis](/lessons/reproduction-and-meiosis/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Many genes on a chromosome carry the code for a protein, and proteins build the body and do its work, which shapes traits.</Support>

### Design problem

In the strawberry activity, does the amount of dish soap change how much white clump you collect? Plan an investigation.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does more dish soap give a bigger clump from the same amount of strawberry?
- **Independent variable:** dish soap in the mix: 0 mL, 5 mL and 10 mL.
- **Dependent variable:** the mass of the wet clump in grams, on a balance.
- **Constants:** 50 g of strawberry, the same salt, water, mashing time, filter and amount of cold alcohol.
- **Control:** the cup with 0 mL of soap.
- **Repeated trials:** three cups for each amount of soap. Find the mean mass for each.
- **Safety:** wear goggles. Rubbing alcohol catches fire easily, so keep it away from any flame or heat. Do not taste anything. Wash your hands after.

If the clump grows as soap goes up, that fits the idea that soap helps open more cells, but it does not prove it. The clump is not pure DNA, so its mass is only a rough measure.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The white clump held DNA from strawberry cells. Strawberries, mushrooms, bacteria and people all build DNA from the same sugar, phosphate and four bases. They differ because the order of the bases differs, so their genes code for different proteins and are used in different ways. More DNA does not make a living thing more complex.
