techniques:
  - name: "Cell-free filtration"
    blurb: >-
      Grind up a tissue and pass it through a filter with channels too narrow for cells or
      bacteria. If the filtrate still causes the disease, the agent is smaller than a bacterium.
      The test gives a size limit and says nothing about what the agent is.
  - name: "Hybridisation with a radioactive probe"
    blurb: >-
      Make a labelled single strand of DNA for one gene, mix it with single-stranded DNA from a
      sample, and measure how much pairs. Matching strands pair and unrelated ones do not. The
      temperature at which the pairs come apart also reports how similar the two sequences are.
  - name: "Chromosome banding"
    blurb: >-
      Stain dividing cells so that each chromosome shows its own pattern of light and dark bands,
      then compare the chromosomes one by one. Banding let Janet Rowley see that the small
      chromosome of chronic myeloid leukaemia was a chromosome 22 whose missing piece was attached
      to chromosome 9.
  - name: "Focus assay for transformation"
    blurb: >-
      Put a gene into normal cells growing as a flat single layer and look for piles of cells,
      called foci, that keep dividing when their neighbours have stopped. Counting foci measures
      how well a gene, or a pair of genes, transforms cells.

      proteins that switch on and then off again. Cancer often starts when a mutation jams one of
      these proteins on. A gene jammed in this way is called an oncogene. The surprise of the

  - level: "college"

    text: >-
      A proto-oncogene is a normal gene, usually for a signalling protein, that becomes an
      oncogene when a change makes its product active without input, or makes far too much of it. The changes fall into a
      short list: a point mutation that blocks the off-step (Ras G12V hydrolyses GTP only slowly, even

**In 1911 Peyton Rous passed a ground-up hen tumour through a filter that stops bacteria and injected the clear fluid into healthy hens. New tumours grew where the needle went in.** Something smaller than a bacterium had carried the cancer from bird to bird. Sixty-five years later the gene this agent uses to cause cancer turned up somewhere surprising: in the DNA of healthy birds, as one of the birds' own genes. This lesson gathers what earlier lessons said about cancer into one argument: how a signalling switch gets stuck on, and why one stuck switch is seldom enough to make a tumour.

## The story: the cancer gene was the chicken's own

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The hen's tumour was a sarcoma, a cancer of connective tissue. Before Rous trusted his filter he tested it against a bacterium, and it held the bacterium back. So the agent was smaller than a bacterium, the size of a virus, and that was all the filter could say. By Rous's own account his result was doubted for about fifteen years.

The agent is now called Rous sarcoma virus. By the early 1970s Peter Vogt and others had isolated mutants of it that still multiplied but could no longer turn a normal cell into a tumour cell, and Peter Duesberg and his colleagues found that their RNA was missing a piece. The missing piece held a gene, named *src* (say "sark") for sarcoma. A gene that can turn a cell cancerous is an **oncogene**.

Where did a virus get such a gene? In the laboratory that Michael Bishop and Harold Varmus shared in San Francisco, a French postdoctoral fellow, Dominique Stehelin, made a radioactive strand of DNA that matched *src* and no other part of the virus. A single strand of DNA sticks to its matching sequence, so this probe could ask whether any sample of DNA held a relative of *src*. It stuck to DNA from chickens that had never met the virus.

That alone proved little. A leading idea of the day held that old infections had left viral genes behind in chicken chromosomes. So Stehelin tried duck, quail and emu, a flightless bird at a great evolutionary distance from the chicken. The probe stuck to all of them, and the matches were close enough that Varmus judged the sequence had been conserved for at least 100 million years. A sequence shared across that distance is older than any infection of chickens. It was a gene of the birds themselves, and later work found it in mammals, people included. The paper appeared in *Nature* in March 1976 under four names: Stehelin, Varmus, Bishop and Vogt.

A normal gene that can become an oncogene when it is altered is a **proto-oncogene**. The virus had picked up the cell's gene, *c-src*, by accident, and what it carries now is an altered copy, *v-src*.

What does the gene make? In 1978 two groups reported that the Src protein is a kinase: Marc Collett and Ray Erikson, and Art Levinson with colleagues in the Bishop and Varmus laboratory. Two years later Tony Hunter and Bart Sefton showed that it puts its phosphate on tyrosine, a story told in [the phosphorylation lesson](/lessons/phosphorylation-switch). And what has v-Src lost? [The lesson on interaction modules](/lessons/protein-interaction-modules) showed how c-Src holds itself shut: a tyrosine near its tail end (tyrosine 527 in the chicken protein) carries a phosphate, the protein's own SH2 domain grips it, and the kinase is clamped. In v-Src the tail has been replaced and that tyrosine is gone. Nothing closes the clamp, so the kinase runs all the time, and a few changes elsewhere in the protein raise its activity further.

## The idea: five ways to stick a switch

Every pathway in this course has an on-step and an off-step. Jam an on-step or remove an off-step, and a cell that should divide only when told can start to divide on its own, unless its checkpoints stop it. Many kinases have been linked to disease, and the ways a kinase or any other switch goes wrong fall into a short list.

| What changes | Example | Why the switch sticks | Pathway taught in |

Rows one and two you have met. In row three, roughly 15 to 20 percent of breast cancers have the HER2 gene copied many times over. The protein is normal, but there is far too much of it. HER2 has no growth factor of its own. Normally it pairs with a relative such as EGFR after that partner has bound its growth factor. With far too many HER2 receptors in the membrane, they pair with each other and with their partners unaided.

Row four began with a small chromosome, seen in 1960 by Peter Nowell and David Hungerford in the blood cells of people with chronic myeloid leukaemia. In 1973 Janet Rowley showed that the missing piece of this chromosome 22 was attached to chromosome 9. Later work found that the join fuses two genes, BCR and ABL. ABL makes a tyrosine kinase related to Src. The BCR part makes the fusion proteins stick to each other, so their kinase domains sit side by side and switch each other on, as paired receptors do.

"Gain of function mutations in RTKs" could head a table of its own: EGFR and its relatives, KIT, MET and RET are each found stuck on in particular human tumours.

Row five is different in kind. PTEN removes the phosphate that PI 3-kinase adds to make PIP3, neurofibromin is a GAP for Ras, and APC is part of the complex that marks β-catenin for destruction. No on-step is jammed here. An off-step is missing. A gene whose loss lets cells grow when they should not is a **tumour suppressor**.

## One copy or two: gain acts alone, loss needs both

You carry two copies of nearly every gene, one from each parent. For the first four rows a change at one copy is usually enough. The altered protein, or the extra protein made from an amplified gene, signals without being told to, and the normal protein from the other copy does not switch it off. The normal copy is not always irrelevant: in mice, normal Kras restrains tumours driven by mutant Kras, and such tumours often lose the normal copy later. This is a gain of function, and it is dominant: it shows even when the other copy is normal.

For row five, one good copy usually makes enough protein to do the job, so both copies must go before the off-step fails. Alfred Knudson reached this idea in 1971 from the ages at which children were diagnosed with retinoblastoma, a tumour of the eye. The inherited form, often in both eyes, appeared on a schedule that fitted one chance event, and the form that is not inherited fitted two. He proposed that the tumour takes two "hits", and that children with the inherited form are born with the first in every cell. The two hits turned out to be the two copies of one gene.

There are many ways to break a protein and few ways to jam it on. That is why Ras changes in tumours cluster at positions 12, 13 and 61, while changes in a tumour suppressor are scattered along the gene. A broken protein can still act through one copy if it gets in the way: the laboratory tool Ras S17N stays in its GDP form and, by one model, ties up the exchange factor that normal Ras needs.

## Why one stuck switch is not enough

A normal cell treats a growth signal that never stops as a fault. In 1983 Robert Weinberg's laboratory put an always-on *ras* gene into cells taken fresh from rat embryos. The cells did not become tumour cells. Nor did they with *myc* alone, a gene for a transcription factor that drives growth. With both genes together they did.

What stops a cell with one stuck switch? Checkpoints do. In normal cells p53, from [the stress lesson](/lessons/stress-signals), answers excess growth signalling much as it answers damaged DNA, and it halts the cell or kills it. A cell that gets past p53 still needs the survival signals of [the cell death lesson](/lessons/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases), or it dies by apoptosis. So a tumour collects changes: a stuck on-switch, a lost off-switch, a broken checkpoint, a block on death. In the colon a common order is known, with loss of APC early, an always-on Ras later and loss of p53 later still.

## A tumour that needs its oncogene

How can we exploit this to treat cancer? A cell with several changes sounds like a cell with no single weak point. The surprise is that many tumours stay dependent on one of their stuck switches. In mice built so that an oncogene can be turned off at will, tumours that grew with the oncogene on have shrunk when it was turned off. The other mutations were still there, yet the cells stopped dividing or died.

Why should that be? This dependence is called oncogene addiction, and its cause is still debated. One idea uses the negative feedback of [the circuits lesson](/lessons/signaling-circuits): a pathway stuck on keeps its own feedback inhibitors high, and those inhibitors may quiet the routes beside it, so that nothing is ready to take over when the stuck signal is removed. Another idea is that survival signals fade faster than death signals once the oncogene is switched off. The ideas are not exclusive, and which one matters may differ from tumour to tumour. Whatever the cause, the dependence is why a drug aimed at one protein can sometimes shrink a tumour that carries many mutations. Imatinib against BCR-ABL, from the biochemistry course, is the best-known case. The next lesson is about those drugs.
