techniques:
  - name: "Cell-free filtration"
    blurb: >-
      Grind up a tissue and pass it through a filter with channels too narrow for cells or
      bacteria. If the filtrate still causes the disease, the agent is smaller than a bacterium.
      The test gives a size limit and says nothing about what the agent is.
  - name: "Hybridisation with a radioactive probe"
    blurb: >-
      Make a labelled single strand of DNA for one gene, mix it with single-stranded DNA from a
      sample, and measure how much pairs. Matching strands pair and unrelated ones do not. The
      temperature at which the pairs come apart also reports how similar the two sequences are.
  - name: "Chromosome banding"
    blurb: >-
      Stain dividing cells so that each chromosome shows its own pattern of light and dark bands,
      then compare the chromosomes one by one. Banding let Janet Rowley see that the small
      chromosome of chronic myeloid leukaemia was a chromosome 22 whose missing piece was attached
      to chromosome 9.
  - name: "Focus assay for transformation"
    blurb: >-
      Put a gene into normal cells growing as a flat single layer and look for piles of cells,
      called foci, that keep dividing when their neighbours have stopped. Counting foci measures
      how well a gene, or a pair of genes, transforms cells.

  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Cells divide when growth signals tell them to, and the signal passes along a chain of
      proteins that switch on and then off again. Cancer often starts when a mutation jams one of
      these proteins on. A gene jammed in this way is called an oncogene. The surprise of the
      1970s was that the oncogene carried by a chicken tumour virus is an altered copy of a gene
      that every normal bird already has. A second kind of cancer gene, a tumour suppressor, is an
      off-switch that causes trouble only when it is lost. You have two copies of most genes. One
      jammed on-switch is enough to cause trouble, but both copies of an off-switch usually have
      to be lost. One change is rarely enough for a tumour, because cells have checkpoints that
      stop or kill a cell whose growth signals are stuck on.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      A proto-oncogene is a normal gene, usually for a signalling protein, that becomes an
      oncogene when a change makes its product active without input, or makes far too much of it. The changes fall into a
      short list: a point mutation that blocks the off-step (Ras G12V hydrolyses GTP only slowly, even
      with a GAP), loss of an autoinhibitory piece (v-Src lacks the tail tyrosine that its SH2
      domain binds), gene amplification (HER2), and gene fusion (BCR-ABL, in which the BCR part
      holds ABL kinase domains together). These are gains of function and act as dominants, so one
      altered copy is enough. Tumour suppressors such as PTEN, neurofibromin and APC are the
      off-steps of their pathways. Their loss is usually recessive in the cell, so both copies
      must go, as Knudson's two-hit analysis of retinoblastoma proposed. One oncogene seldom
      transforms a normal cell, because checkpoints such as p53 answer excess growth signalling
      with arrest or apoptosis, and tumours therefore carry several cooperating changes. Many
      tumours still depend on one activated oncogene, which is why inhibiting that one protein can
      shrink them.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      Activating lesions are constrained by mechanism, which is why they recur at a few

## The story: the cancer gene was the chicken's own

In 1911 Peyton Rous ground up a sarcoma (a cancer of connective tissue) from a hen's breast and passed it through a filter he had first tested against a bacterium. The filter stopped the bacterium, yet injecting the clear filtrate into healthy hens grew new sarcomas at the needle site. Something smaller than a bacterium, the size of a virus, had carried the cancer from bird to bird—all the filter could say—and by Rous's account the result was doubted for about fifteen years.

The agent is now called Rous sarcoma virus. By the early 1970s Peter Vogt and others had isolated mutants that still multiplied without transforming normal cells, and Peter Duesberg and his colleagues found their RNA was missing a piece carrying a gene named *src* (say "sark") for sarcoma. A gene that can turn a cell cancerous is an **oncogene**.

Sixty-five years after Rous's paper, in Michael Bishop and Harold Varmus's San Francisco laboratory, French postdoctoral fellow Dominique Stehelin built a radioactive single-stranded DNA probe matching *src* and no other part of the virus. Because a single strand sticks to its matching sequence, it could ask whether normal DNA held a *src* relative. It stuck to DNA from uninfected chickens—yet that alone fit two competing hypotheses: an ancient viral infection left behind in chicken chromosomes (the leading idea of the day), or a normal bird gene the virus had picked up.

An ancient chicken infection predicts poor matching in a distant bird such as the emu, just as for the virus's other genes. A cellular gene predicts that the *src* probe should still match emu DNA closely while the rest of the virus matches poorly outside chickens.

</details>

The *src* probe stuck closely to duck, quail and emu DNA while the virus's other genes did not. From the match, Varmus judged the sequence had been conserved for at least 100 million years—older than any chicken infection. It was a gene of the birds themselves, later found in mammals, people included. A normal gene that can become an oncogene when altered is a **proto-oncogene**. The virus had picked up the cell's *c-src* by accident and now carries an altered copy, *v-src*.

Src is a tyrosine kinase ([the phosphorylation lesson](/lessons/phosphorylation-switch)). As [the interaction-modules lesson](/lessons/protein-interaction-modules) showed, c-Src clamps itself shut when its SH2 domain grips a phosphorylated tail tyrosine (tyrosine 527 in chicken). In v-Src the tail is replaced and that tyrosine is gone, so nothing closes the clamp, and a few changes elsewhere raise its activity further.

In c-Src the SH2 domain grips a phosphate on the tail and clamps the kinase shut; in v-Src the tail is replaced, nothing is gripped, and the kinase is open and running." caption="Replacing c-Src's phosphorylated tail tyrosine leaves its SH2 domain nothing to grip, so the clamp never closes and v-Src runs all the time." />

> **The one idea.** One altered copy jams a switch, while off-switch loss usually needs both; tumours usually need several changes, yet often depend on one stuck switch. **Scope:** The copy-number rule assumes diploid cells in which one working copy of an off-switch is enough to restrain the pathway; dosage effects and mutants that interfere with the normal copy are exceptions covered under Going deeper.

## The idea: five ways to stick a switch

Jamming an on-step or removing an off-step—pushing a cell to divide unless its checkpoints stop it—falls into five molecular routes across this course:

| What changes | Example | Why the switch sticks | Pathway taught in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One amino acid, breaking the off-step | Ras G12V or Q61 | Ras hydrolyses its GTP only slowly, even with a GAP | [GTPase switches](/lessons/gtpase-switches), [Ras to MAPK](/lessons/ras-to-mapk) |
| The piece that holds a kinase shut is lost | v-Src | No tail tyrosine for the SH2 domain to grip | [Interaction modules](/lessons/protein-interaction-modules) |
| Too many copies of a normal gene | HER2 | HER2 needs no growth factor of its own, and crowded receptors pair unaided | [Receptor tyrosine kinases](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases) |
| Two genes joined | BCR-ABL | The BCR part holds ABL kinases together | [Receptor tyrosine kinases](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases), [the phosphorylation switch](/lessons/phosphorylation-switch) |
| The off-protein is lost | PTEN, neurofibromin, APC | PIP3, Ras-GTP or β-catenin is not cleared | [PI 3-kinase and Akt](/lessons/pi3k-akt-insulin), [GTPase switches](/lessons/gtpase-switches), [Wnt signalling](/lessons/wnt-signaling) |

Rows one and two alter the protein's own off-mechanism. Rows three and four activate a tyrosine kinase without mutating its catalytic site.

**Predict.** In roughly 15 to 20 percent of breast cancers the *HER2* gene is copied many times over with its normal sequence; in chronic myeloid leukaemia *BCR* is fused to the *ABL* kinase. How can extra normal HER2 receptors signal without a growth factor, and what receptor step does BCR imitate?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Receptor tyrosine kinases switch on when two receptors pair and phosphorylate each other. HER2 has no growth factor of its own—normally it pairs with a relative such as EGFR after that partner binds ligand—so crowding too many HER2 receptors into the membrane lets them pair unaided. In BCR-ABL, the BCR segment makes the fusion proteins stick together, imitating ligand-driven pairing.

</details>

That BCR-ABL fusion arises in chronic myeloid leukaemia when chromosomes 9 and 22 swap tips: a piece of chromosome 22 attaches to chromosome 9 while *ABL* (encoding a tyrosine kinase related to Src) moves from chromosome 9 onto chromosome 22 beside *BCR*, fusing them on the shortened chromosome 22 so their kinase domains sit side by side and switch each other on. (Other receptor tyrosine kinases stuck on in human tumours appear under Going deeper.)

<LessonFigure src="/figures/oncogenes/bcr-abl-fusion.webp" width={1200} height={571} alt="AI-generated illustration: one continuous BCR–ABL fusion protein contains a BCR-derived part and an ABL kinase domain. A schematic pair associates through its BCR parts, bringing the ABL kinase domains together; arrows from each kinase to the other show activation by phosphorylation." caption="Fusing BCR to ABL holds the kinase domains side by side so they phosphorylate and activate each other without a growth signal." />

Row five is different in kind: PTEN removes the phosphate PI 3-kinase adds to make PIP3, neurofibromin is a GAP for Ras, and APC helps mark β-catenin for destruction. Here an off-step is missing. A gene whose loss lets cells grow when they should not is a **tumour suppressor**.

## One copy or two: gain acts alone, loss needs both

You carry two copies of nearly every gene, one from each parent. For rows one to four, one altered or amplified copy signals without input and the normal copy cannot shut it off—a **dominant gain of function**. For row five, one working copy usually supplies enough off-protein, so loss is usually **recessive** inside the cell:

| Cell genotype | Pathway state (no growth factor) | Cell-level effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 normal *Ras*, 2 normal *NF1* | Off | Wild type |
| 1 altered *Ras* + 1 normal *Ras* | Stuck on | Dominant gain |
| 1 lost *NF1* + 1 normal *NF1* | Usually off | Silent in most cells |
| 2 lost *NF1* | Stuck on (Ras-GTP not cleared) | Recessive loss |

Although off-switch loss is recessive inside each individual cell, inheriting one broken copy still raises cancer risk across a body of billions of cells.

**Predict.** Retinoblastoma, an eye tumour, occurs in an inherited form (often in both eyes) and a non-inherited form (one eye). If a retinal cell loses control only when both copies of a tumour suppressor are lost, which form should appear earlier and follow a single-chance-event schedule?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The inherited form. A child born with one broken copy already has the first hit in every retinal cell, so only one later chance event is needed in a cell to lose the second copy, whereas a child born with two good copies needs two independent hits in the same cell.

</details>

Alfred Knudson deduced this in 1971 from children's diagnosis ages: the inherited form fitted one chance event and the non-inherited form fitted two, and his two "hits" proved to be the two copies of one gene. The same asymmetry shapes where mutations fall: because few changes jam a protein on while many break it, tumour mutations in Ras cluster at positions 12, 13 and 61, whereas tumour suppressor mutations scatter along the gene (exceptions sit under Going deeper).

A single altered gene copy can jam a switch on—yet most cells with one stuck switch never become a tumour, and a multi-mutation tumour can still collapse when just one switch stops.

## Why one stuck switch is not enough

The second film showed why a nonstop growth signal trips a normal cell's alarms; here are the experiments that showed a tumour usually needs cooperating changes.

In 1983 Robert Weinberg's laboratory (Hartmut Land, Luis Parada and Weinberg) put an always-on *ras* gene and *myc*—a nuclear transcription factor that drives growth—into fresh primary rat embryo cells.

**Predict.** What happened in fresh rat embryo cells given always-on *ras* alone, *myc* alone, or both together?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Neither *ras* alone nor *myc* alone transformed the fresh embryo cells; only both together did, because transformation here needs two cooperating changes.

</details>

In normal cells, **p53** ([the stress lesson](/lessons/stress-signals)) treats excess growth signalling like damaged DNA, halting or killing the cell, and cells escaping p53 still need survival signals ([the cell death lesson](/lessons/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases)) or die by apoptosis. Tumours therefore accumulate cooperating changes; in the colon a common order is loss of **APC** early, always-on **Ras** later and loss of **p53** later still.

## A tumour that needs its oncogene

In mice where an engineered oncogene can be switched off at will, tumours that grew with it on have shrunk when it was turned off, although their other mutations were unchanged—the cells stopped dividing or died. This dependence, **oncogene addiction**, explains why a drug targeting one protein—above all **imatinib** against BCR-ABL (from the biochemistry course; the next lesson covers such drugs)—can sometimes shrink a multi-mutation tumour.

Its cause is still debated between two non-exclusive ideas: chronic negative feedback ([the circuits lesson](/lessons/signaling-circuits)), in which a stuck pathway keeps feedback inhibitors high and may quiet parallel routes, or survival signals fading faster than death signals once the oncogene shuts off. Which matters may differ from tumour to tumour.

**Predict.** If chronic negative feedback keeps a tumour addicted to one oncogene, what should be true of parallel growth pathways *before* a drug is given—and how might that hint at later resistance?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Before the drug arrives, high feedback inhibitors should quiet parallel growth pathways. After the drug shuts the oncogene off, those inhibitors can fall, so surviving cells—or cells gaining a parallel mutation—may wake another pathway and resume growth.

</details>

Together, both halves of our rule link molecular switches to therapy: one altered copy jams a switch while off-switch loss usually needs both, and though tumours usually need several changes, they often depend on one stuck switch.

### Design problem

Take the chain receptor, Ras, Raf, MEK, ERK, with an off-switch at each step. List every kind of single change you can think of that would leave ERK on with no growth factor present. Sort your list into changes that work when one copy of a gene is altered and changes that need both copies lost. Then say which kind you would expect to find more often as an inherited risk for cancer, and why.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**One altered copy is enough (gain).** A receptor that pairs without growth factor, because of a point mutation, a missing outer part, too many copies, or fusion to a partner that pairs it. A Ras that a GAP cannot turn off. A Raf or a MEK fixed in its active shape (BRAF V600E, common in melanoma, is one).

**Both copies usually must be lost (loss).** The GAP for Ras, neurofibromin. The phosphatases that strip phosphate from the receptor, MEK or ERK. The ligase that tags an active receptor for removal.

**Inherited risk.** Expect the loss kind more often. An inherited change is in every cell from the first division. A strongly stuck on-switch in every cell disturbs the embryo, and the milder inherited activating changes in this pathway cause developmental syndromes called RASopathies. One lost copy of an off-switch is mostly silent while the second copy works, so most such people are born without tumours. Every cell is then one hit away from having no off-switch, and in a body of many billions of cells a second hit somewhere becomes likely. Neurofibromatosis type 1, one inherited bad copy of the neurofibromin gene, works this way. There are exceptions: inherited activating changes in the receptor RET cause a cancer syndrome.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How src was found, with names and dates

The 1976 hybridisation paper appeared in *Nature* in March 1976 under Stehelin, Varmus, Bishop and Vogt. In 1978 Marc Collett and Ray Erikson, and Art Levinson with colleagues in the Bishop and Varmus laboratory, showed Src is a kinase; two years later Tony Hunter and Bart Sefton showed it phosphorylates tyrosine.

### How the BCR-ABL join was found

Peter Nowell and David Hungerford reported a small chromosome in chronic myeloid leukaemia blood cells in 1960. In 1973 Janet Rowley showed the missing piece of chromosome 22 was attached to chromosome 9; later work showed the reciprocal swap places *ABL* from chromosome 9 beside *BCR* on chromosome 22.

### Other receptor tyrosine kinases stuck on in cancer

HER2 sits within a wider group of receptor tyrosine kinases found stuck on by gain-of-function alterations in particular human tumours, including EGFR and its relatives, KIT, MET and RET.

### Exceptions to the one-copy rules

The normal copy is not always irrelevant: in mice, normal Kras restrains tumours driven by mutant Kras, and such tumours often lose the normal copy later. A broken protein can also act through one copy if it interferes with the normal copy: Ras S17N stays in its GDP form and, by one model, ties up the exchange factor normal Ras needs, and many p53 missense mutants poison the tetramer they form with wild-type subunits as dominant negatives. Finally, two-hit kinetics assume one copy supplies enough protein: PTEN shows dosage effects in mice, and a single lost copy of neurofibromin can have milder effects in some cells.
