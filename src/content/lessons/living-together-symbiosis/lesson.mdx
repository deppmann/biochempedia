practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "What is symbiosis?"
    choices:
      - "A hunt in which a predator catches and eats its prey"
      - "A close relationship of two species that live together"
      - "A relationship in which both partners are always helped"
      - "A group of the same species sharing one home together"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Symbiosis is a close relationship between individuals of different species living together.
      A predator catching prey is a short meeting, not living together. Saying both partners are
      always helped describes only mutualism; commensalism and parasitism are symbiosis too. Members
      of the same species sharing a home is not symbiosis, which needs two different species.
  - stem: "Which Virginia pair is the best example of commensalism?"
    choices:
      - "A blacklegged tick feeding on the blood of a deer"
      - "The fungus and the alga that together form a lichen"
      - "Fungi on tree roots trading minerals for plant food"
      - "A lichen growing on bark of a tree it does not harm"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      A lichen on bark gains a place to live, and the tree seems to be neither helped nor harmed
      much, so it fits commensalism. The tick gains blood and the deer is harmed, which is
      parasitism. The fungus and alga inside a lichen both gain, and so do root fungi and trees, so
      those two pairs are mutualism.
  - stem: "Which statement about parasites is best supported by evidence?"
    choices:
      - "A parasite can feed on a living host for days or more"
      - "Parasites always kill their hosts quickly after they attach"
      - "A parasite gives its host food in return for a place to live"
      - "A parasite and its host are always the very same species"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      A parasite does not have to kill its host quickly; a tick, for example, can stay attached and
      feed on a living host for days. The idea that parasites always kill hosts quickly is a common
      mistake. Giving the host food in return would make it mutualism. Symbiosis is between
      different species, so a parasite and its host are not the same species.
  - stem: "In a lichen, the alga makes sugar that the fungus uses, and the fungus shelters the alga and holds water. Which kind of symbiosis is this?"
    choices:
      - "Commensalism, because only the fungus gains anything"
      - "Parasitism, because the fungus takes some of the sugar"
      - "Mutualism, because the fungus and the alga both gain"
      - "Not symbiosis, because the lichen looks like one body"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Both partners gain: the alga gets shelter and water, and the fungus gets food, so this is
      mutualism. It is not commensalism, because the alga gains too. Taking sugar is not harm here,
      because the alga is not left worse off overall. A lichen looks like one body, but it is a close
      relationship between different species, which is symbiosis.
  - stem: "A classmate says, “Fungi help tree roots because they are being kind.” Which reply fits the science best?"
    choices:
      - "Right; living things choose to help the partners they like"
      - "Wrong; root fungi always harm the trees that they grow on"
      - "Partnerships last because partners that lived this way survived"
      - "Right; only kind living things can be part of a symbiosis"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Scientists explain partnerships by survival and reproduction: partners that lived this way
      survived and reproduced, so the relationship carried on over many generations. Saying the
      fungus chooses to help a partner it likes adds a feeling the evidence does not show. Root fungi
      usually trade minerals for plant food rather than harm trees. Kindness is not needed for any
      symbiosis.
  - stem: "A hawk catches a mouse and eats it within minutes. A tick feeds on a mouse for several days while the mouse lives. Which statement is correct?"
    choices:
      - "The tick is a parasite, and the hawk is a predator"
      - "Both are parasites, because both feed on the mouse"
      - "The hawk is a parasite, because it harms the mouse most"
      - "Both show mutualism, because each one gets some food"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      A predator kills and eats its prey quickly, as the hawk does. A parasite lives on or in a host
      and feeds over time, as the tick does. Feeding on the mouse is not enough to make the hawk a
      parasite, and causing more harm does not make it one either. The mouse gains nothing, so
      neither is mutualism.
  - stem: "In a greenhouse trial, marigolds watered to 75% of what the soil can hold had a mean shoot dry mass of 4.12 g with a root fungus and 3.28 g without it. How much more was the mass with the fungus?"
    choices:
      - "7.40 g"
      - "0.48 g"
      - "1.24 g"
      - "0.84 g"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Subtract the mass without the fungus from the mass with it: 4.12 g minus 3.28 g equals 0.84 g.
      Adding the two masses gives 7.40 g, which is a total, not a difference. 0.48 g comes from
      swapping digits while subtracting. 1.24 g is too large and does not match either subtraction
      of these two values.
  - stem: "Suppose a class measured trunk growth of 10 trees with lichen and 10 without. Both groups grew a mean of 2.0 cm in a year, and the lichens grew. Which kind of symbiosis do these made-up data fit best?"
    choices:

      - "Commensalism, because the lichen gains and the tree does not change"

    rationale: >-
      The lichen gains a place to grow, and the trees with and without lichen grew the same, so the

anecdotes:

    source: "Spribille and colleagues, Science, 2016 (doi 10.1126/science.aaf8287)."
    text: >-
      For about 150 years, textbooks described a lichen as one fungus and one alga. In 2016 Toby
      Spribille and colleagues studied 15 lichens collected in western Montana. They read the RNA
      in each sample and then used glowing dyes to find cells in the lichen. They found yeast cells,
      another kind of fungus, in the lichen’s outer layer. What the yeast does there is still an
      open question. The two-partner picture is a good model, but it leaves some members out.
  - title: "Same fungus, different results"
    source: "Thirkell and colleagues, Food and Energy Security, 2022 (doi 10.1002/fes3.370)."
    text: >-
      Tom Thirkell and colleagues grew 99 kinds of wheat with a mix of five root fungi. For a
      control, they used the same fungus mix after killing it. After five weeks, some kinds of
      wheat grew more with the living fungi, and others grew less. The change ranged from 34% less
      to 89% more shoot growth. The study showed that a partner’s effect has to be measured, not
      assumed.

## Explain: what symbiosis means

<WordBank terms={['symbiosis', 'lichen', 'mutualism', 'commensalism', 'parasitism', 'host', 'parasite']} />

**Symbiosis** is a close relationship between individuals of different species living together. The partners stay together for a long time. A bird that eats one seed and flies off is not in a symbiosis with the plant.

A **lichen** is a living thing made of a fungus and an alga or a cyanobacterium living as one body. They stay two different species, but they share one body. Here is what each partner gives:

- The fungus forms the outside of the lichen. It shelters its partner and helps keep it moist.
- The lichen takes in water from rain and from the air.
- The alga or cyanobacterium makes sugar by photosynthesis. That food feeds both partners.

{/* DIAGRAM: a cut-open lichen on tree bark, with 1 the fungus threads on the outside, 2 the layer of green alga cells, 3 an arrow of sugar from the alga to the fungus, 4 rain and water from the air soaking in, 5 the tree bark underneath */}

This two-partner picture is a model. In 2016, scientists found yeast cells, another kind of fungus, living inside some lichens too. What the yeast does there is still being studied.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** symbiosis is a close relationship between individuals of different species that live together for a long time.</Support>

## Explain: three kinds of symbiosis

Scientists sort symbiosis by asking what each partner gains or loses. There are three kinds.

- **Mutualism** is symbiosis in which all the partners benefit.
- **Commensalism** is symbiosis in which one partner benefits and the others are unaffected.
- **Parasitism** is symbiosis in which one partner benefits and the others are harmed.

In parasitism, the **host** is the living thing that the other partner lives on or in. The **parasite** is the partner that benefits by feeding on its host.

| Virginia example | Partner A | Partner B | Kind |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fungus and alga in a lichen | fungus gets food | alga gets shelter and water | mutualism |
| Fungi on tree roots | fungus gets food from the tree | tree gets minerals from the soil | mutualism |
| Bacteria in soybean root bumps | bacteria get food from the plant | plant gets nitrogen it can use | mutualism |
| Lichen on tree bark | lichen gets a place to live | tree seems unaffected | commensalism |
| Blacklegged tick on a deer | tick gets blood | deer loses blood and gets sore skin | parasitism |

Look at the two lichen rows. The fungus and alga inside the lichen are one relationship. The lichen and the tree are a different one. Experts say ordinary lichens on bark do not harm the tree.

The soybean bacteria turn nitrogen from the air into a form the plant can use. You met them in [Nitrogen for Life](/lessons/nitrogen-for-life/).

Blacklegged ticks live in Virginia. A tick holds onto its host and drinks blood for days. Bites can make skin red, itchy and swollen.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** in mutualism all partners benefit, in commensalism one benefits and the others are unaffected, and in parasitism one benefits and the others are harmed.</Support>

## Explain: why partnerships last

A parasite is not the same as a predator. A predator kills and eats its prey quickly. A parasite usually lives on or in its host and feeds over a long time, often while the host stays alive. You can compare them in [Predators and Prey](/lessons/predators-and-prey/).

Partners do not help each other on purpose. A fungus cannot decide to be kind. Over many generations, partners that lived together survived and reproduced better than ones that did not. Their offspring kept living the same way, so the relationship carried on.

That is how symbiosis supports the survival of different species. A lichen can grow on bare rock, where the alga alone might dry out and the fungus alone would have no food.

## Elaborate: does a root fungus help a plant grow?

Many plants grow with a fungus on their roots. The fungus brings minerals from the soil into the roots. The plant passes food it made to the fungus. Does the plant really grow more with this partner?

|---|---|---|
| 100% | present | 5.29 |
| 100% | absent | 4.44 |
| 75% | present | 4.12 |
| 75% | absent | 3.28 |
| 50% | present | 3.06 |
| 50% | absent | 2.89 |
| 25% | present | 2.62 |
| 25% | absent | 2.01 |

These are means from a pot experiment by Asrar and Elhindi, published in 2011. They grew Jubilee marigolds in a greenhouse at King Saud University in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Seeds were sown in September 2009. The shoots were dried and weighed six weeks after the seedlings were moved into pots. The table shows only plant growth, not how many plants lived.

1. At 25% water, how many grams more did plants with the fungus have? What percent is that of the mass without the fungus?
2. As the water drops from 100% to 25%, what happens to shoot mass in both groups?
3. At which water level is the gap between the two groups smallest?

### Design problem

Plan an investigation to test whether a root fungus helps bean plants grow. Name your variables, your control, what you keep the same, how many plants you use, what you measure in metric units, and how you keep it safe.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Question: do bean plants grow more shoot mass with a root fungus than without one?
- Independent variable: root fungus added (a garden-store root fungus powder) or not added.
- Dependent variable: dry shoot mass in grams after six weeks. Cut the shoots, dry them in paper bags for a week, then weigh them on a balance.
- Control: pots with the same potting mix and no fungus powder.
- Constants: the same seeds, pot size, amount of potting mix, water, light and temperature.
- Trials: at least 10 pots per group, mixed together on the shelf so neither group gets better light. Find the mean for each group.
- Safety: wear gloves and avoid breathing the powder, follow the label, wash your hands afterward, and never taste anything.
- What results would mean: a higher mean mass with the fungus supports a benefit to the plant in these conditions. If a teacher has a microscope, check stained roots to see if the fungus grew.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

Those gray-green crusty patches can be lichens: a fungus and an alga or cyanobacterium living as one body. Both partners gain, so that is mutualism. Living close together does not always help, though. A lichen on bark leaves the tree unaffected, and a tick harms the deer it feeds on.

**Science practices you used:** predicting and testing with a model, sorting examples into groups, reading a data table and finding a difference and a percent, planning a fair test with a control and many plants, and naming the limits of one study.
