    contribution: >-
      Built the compartmented culture that separates a neuron's cell body from its distal axons,
      and used it with rat sympathetic neurons to show that NGF acts locally on the axons it bathes
      and that NGF at distal axons can support cell bodies that have none.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Campenot, PNAS 74:4516 (1977); MacInnis & Campenot, Science 295:1536 (2002)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Campenot's problem was a practical one. Neurons in a dish sit in one pool of medium, so
      every part of the cell sees the same NGF, and you cannot ask where the factor is needed. His
      answer, published in 1977, was a barrier sealed onto a culture dish, making
      separate chambers whose fluids did not mix. Sympathetic neurons from rats were plated in one
      chamber, and their axons grew under the seal into the others. Now NGF could be given to the
      axons and not the cell bodies, or the reverse. The result was that axons grew only where NGF
      was, and stopped and later degenerated where it was taken away, even with the cell bodies in
      NGF. Cell bodies without NGF of their own could survive on what their axons collected. The
      design spread through the field and is still used. Campenot later used it to argue, with
      Bronwyn MacInnis in 2002, that survival can be supported from the axon without NGF itself
      being carried back, which kept the question of what travels open.
  - name: "David Ginty"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory showed that NGF applied only to the distal axons of cultured sympathetic
      neurons activates the transcription factor CREB in the nucleus, and characterized the NGF and
      phosphorylated TrkA complex that is carried back to the cell body.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Riccio, Pierchala, Ciarallo & Ginty, Science 277:1097 (1997); Tsui-Pierchala & Ginty, J. Neurosci. 19:8207 (1999)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the 1990s it was known that labeled NGF travels from target to ganglion, but not whether
      a signal that changes gene expression went with it. Antonella Riccio and colleagues in
      Ginty's laboratory put sympathetic neurons into compartmented cultures and gave NGF to the
      distal axons only. CREB, a transcription factor that binds DNA and switches genes on, became
      phosphorylated in the nuclei. Later work from the same group, with Brian Tsui-Pierchala, described a
      complex of NGF with phosphorylated TrkA that moves retrogradely, and a 2003 paper with Rejji Kuruvilla,
      Larry Zweifel and others was titled "evidence in support of" signaling
      endosome-based retrograde survival. The laboratory's work turned retrograde signaling from an
      anatomical observation into a set of testable steps: receptor activation at the tip, a
      carried complex, and a nuclear response that keeps the neuron alive.
  - name: "Rejji Kuruvilla"
    contribution: >-
      As first author of a 2004 Cell paper from the Ginty laboratory, showed that NT-3 and NGF both
      activate TrkA on sympathetic axons, but only NGF-TrkA supports retrograde survival
      signaling, so the intermediate target's factor guides growth and the final target's factor
      keeps the neuron alive.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Kuruvilla, Zweifel, Glebova, Lonze, Valdez, Ye & Ginty, Cell 118:243 (2004)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Sympathetic axons in the mouse grow along blood vessels before they reach the organs they
      will innervate. The vessels make NT-3; the final targets make NGF. Both factors can activate
      TrkA, so why does a neuron need two? Kuruvilla and colleagues found that NT-3 given to distal
      axons in compartmented culture promoted local axon growth through TrkA but did not support
      the cell bodies, while NGF did both. The difference lay in trafficking: NGF-TrkA complexes
      were internalized and carried back, and NT-3-TrkA complexes were not carried back. In mice lacking NT-3,
      sympathetic axon growth and target innervation were impaired. The paper also proposed a
      handoff, in which NGF signaling raises p75 expression and so reduces the neuron's response to
      NT-3 once the final target is reached. In her own laboratory she went on to study how TrkA
      receptors are delivered to axons, the other half of the round trip.

  - name: "Compartmented (Campenot) culture"
    blurb: >-
      Neurons are plated in one chamber and their axons grow under a sealed barrier into others,
      so cell bodies and distal axons sit in separate fluids. You can give a factor to one part of
      the neuron and measure survival, growth or gene expression in another.
  - name: "Retrograde transport of a radiolabeled ligand"
    blurb: >-
      A radioactive form of a factor such as iodinated NGF is placed in a target, and after some
      hours the ganglia are sectioned and the label located by autoradiography or counting. It
      shows that the label moved toward cell bodies along a specific route, not that the
      molecule arrived intact or active.
  - name: "Knockout mice"
    blurb: >-
      A knockout is an animal in which a gene has been deliberately inactivated. Comparing NT-3
      or NGF knockouts, often on a Bax-null background that prevents neuron death, separates
      effects on axon growth from effects on survival.

**A sympathetic neuron's cell body can sit centimetres away from the axon terminals where it collects NGF. The terminals receive the signal. The nucleus decides whether the cell lives.**

You met the neurotrophic hypothesis in [that lesson](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/): neurons are overproduced, targets make a limited amount of a survival factor, and neurons that do not get enough die. Stated that way, the hypothesis skips a step. NGF is released near the axon terminals, but in these young neurons, the death that follows loss of NGF needs new genes switched on in the nucleus ([the lesson on death machinery in neurons](/lessons/death-machinery-in-neurons/)), so a survival signal from the terminal has to be able to act on the cell body. Something has to cross the distance between them. This lesson asks what crosses, how we know, and why it matters for which target factor keeps a neuron alive.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A target keeps a neuron alive only if its signal makes the trip home and is able to signal when it gets there. Binding at the tip is not the test. The sharpest case comes near the end: two factors switch on the same receptor at the tip, and only one keeps the neuron alive.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which neurons.** Target-dependent neurons with long axons, during their death window: sympathetic neurons (autonomic neurons whose axons run from ganglia out to organs), and the sensory neurons that carry TrkA, the receptor NGF binds. Much of the mechanistic evidence comes from rat and mouse sympathetic neurons in compartmented culture.
- **Which effect.** Survival supported from the axon terminal. Local effects, such as axon growth where the factor is, do not need the trip, and a factor given straight to the cell body bypasses it.
- **What it does not say.** What carries the signal: whether NGF itself has to travel, and whether a faster signal also contributes, are still open. Nor does it say that every signal that arrives is enough.

## Could it just drift?

The simplest idea is that something made at the tip diffuses to the cell body. The typical time to diffuse a distance grows with the square of that distance.

> **time ≈ (distance)² / (2 × D)**

D, the diffusion coefficient, says how fast a molecule spreads. A soluble protein in cytoplasm has a D on the order of 10 square micrometres per second.

**Predict.** A sympathetic axon is 1 cm long, which is 10,000 micrometres. Roughly how long would a protein take to diffuse from the terminal to the cell body: seconds, hours, or much longer?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Much longer. 10,000 squared is 10⁸, and 10⁸ / 20 is about 5 million seconds: roughly two months. Over a few centimetres, it would take many months.

</details>

Worse, the signal would be switched off on the way. Many signals inside a cell are proteins carrying an added phosphate, and enzymes called phosphatases keep removing those phosphates.

How far does an active signal get? Take a simple model: steady diffusion from the tip, with phosphates removed at a constant rate. Suppose a phosphate lasts about ten seconds on average. The active signal then fades over a distance of about √(D × lifetime) = √(10 × 10) = 10 micrometres: every 10 micrometres from the tip, the active fraction falls to about a third. A centimetre is a thousand such steps, so effectively nothing active arrives. With a phosphatase a hundred times slower, the fade distance is 100 micrometres, and the active fraction at 1 cm is still only about 10⁻⁴⁴. These rates are illustrative, not measurements for TrkA pathways. Protein molecules may still drift in, but inactive: the signal does not arrive late, it arrives switched off.

## What a long-distance signal needs

So the signal needs a vehicle or a relay, and it needs two properties.

- **It must still be able to signal when it arrives.** A carried complex need not be active every second of the trip, but it must reach the cell body intact and able to signal. A relay must switch each stretch on in turn.
- **It should keep the target's message separate from signals the neuron picks up along the way.** Keeping the ligand bound to its receptor is one way to do this. Whether the cell body reads which factor started a signal is not shown here. Whether NGF itself must arrive is unsettled: the bead result in [Going deeper](#a-faster-signal) hints that survival may not need it, but Ye and colleagues found NGF inside the cell bodies was needed ([below](#courier-or-relay)).

A membrane vesicle holding the ligand still bound to its active receptor fits both. That is why the signaling endosome became the favoured model.

Here is the model. NGF binds TrkA at the axon terminal. TrkA is a receptor tyrosine kinase: an NGF dimer holds two TrkA molecules together, their kinases add phosphates to each other's tyrosines, and the phosphorylated tails recruit signaling proteins. The ligand and the active receptor are pulled into the cell together in a small membrane vesicle, the **signaling endosome**. NGF stays bound inside the vesicle, while TrkA's kinase tails face the cytoplasm outside it, so the complex can keep signaling while it is carried (the cell biology is in [the lesson on endocytosis and signaling endosomes](/lessons/endocytosis-and-signaling-endosomes/)). The motor protein **dynein** drags it along microtubules toward the cell body. Once there, kinase pathways reach transcription factors such as CREB, which binds DNA and switches genes on.

Dynein-driven endosomes move at roughly a micrometre per second, so a centimetre takes about three hours. That makes the first need sharp. Take an illustrative model in which the carried complex loses its ability to signal at a steady rate, for good, with nothing switching it back on. If it halves every hour, a three-hour trip is three halvings: (1/2)³ = 1/8 arrive able to signal.

| If the carried complex's ability to signal halves every | Fraction still able to signal after a three-hour trip |
| --- | --- |
| 5 minutes | essentially none (36 halvings) |
| 1 hour | one in eight |
| 10 hours | about four in five |

Carrying alone is not enough. And favoured is not proven: the next two sections ask what the evidence shows.

## The evidence that something travels

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The first evidence was anatomical. In 1974, Hendry, Stöckel, Thoenen and Iversen injected radioactive, iodine-labeled NGF into the eye of mice and found the label building up in the superior cervical ganglion on the same side, the sympathetic ganglion whose axons innervate the eye. In 1979, Judy Brunso-Bechtold and Viktor Hamburger did the embryonic version. They implanted a small gel pellet soaked in radioactive NGF under the leg skin of chick embryos at about ten days of incubation. Eight hours later, the lumbar dorsal root ganglia on the implanted side were labeled. The lateral motor columns, ventral roots and dorsal roots were not labeled, though motor axons ran into the same leg.

That selectivity is the point. Label leaking into the blood would reach both sides; uptake by any nerve would label the motor column. Instead it went to the sensory neurons, which respond to NGF. Autoradiography locates radioactivity. It cannot tell you whether NGF arrived intact, whether it was active, or whether its arrival had anything to do with survival.

Robert Campenot's dish answered the survival question. In 1977 he described a culture in which a divider, sealed to the dish, separated a central chamber from side chambers. Rat sympathetic neurons were plated in the centre, and their axons grew under the seal into the side chambers. The fluids did not mix. Now NGF could be given to the **distal axons**, the far ends near the terminals, and not to the cell bodies, or the reverse.

**Predict.** Two tests. First, take NGF away from one side chamber while the cell bodies stay in NGF. What happens to the axons in that chamber? Second, give the cell bodies no NGF, but let their axons reach a chamber that has it. Do the cell bodies live?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

When NGF was removed from a side chamber, the axons there stopped growing and later degenerated, even though their cell bodies sat in NGF. An axon depends on NGF in its own surroundings. And when cell bodies had no NGF, many still survived, provided their axons reached a chamber that had it. A signal from the distal axon can keep the cell body alive.

</details>

Does that signal reach the genome? Antonella Riccio and colleagues in David Ginty's laboratory gave NGF only to the distal axons of cultured rat sympathetic neurons and found CREB phosphorylated in the nuclei. The same study found that NGF and TrkA had to be internalized and transported to the cell body for this to happen. And TrkA kinase activity was needed in the cell body, not only at the tip. Was that active TrkA the same receptor, carried from the tip?

## Courier or relay?

Two simple models of how an active signal could get from terminal to cell body make different predictions, though they are not the only possibilities.

- **Courier.** The NGF-TrkA complex itself is carried the whole way and signals when it arrives.
- **Relay.** Each stretch of axon switches on the next, like a row of dominoes in which each one tips its neighbour. No one molecule makes the whole trip.

Heerssen, Pazyra and Segal reported in 2004 that dynein transports activated Trk receptors and that this transport is needed for the survival of target-dependent neurons. That fits a courier.

**Predict.** Does it rule out a relay?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Suppose each local step of a relay needed dynein for its own reasons, to bring up a protein the next stretch uses, say. Blocking dynein would stop the relay just as completely. A dynein block, by itself, cannot tell a courier from a relay.

</details>

What can tell them apart?

| Test | Courier predicts | Relay predicts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Watch receptors and NGF in the axon | Labeled complexes move all the way from tip to cell body | Moving complexes may exist, but they would not be what carries the signal |
| Block TrkA's kinase only in the middle stretch of the axon, with an inhibitor applied only to that stretch (a courier test needs it not to stop transport) | The signal still arrives: the complex is carried through and can signal again once past the inhibitor | A relay that needs TrkA kinase in each stretch stops there |
| Stop NGF from being taken into the cell | Survival fails: there is nothing to carry | A relay could still start from the surface |

**Predict.** Which test separates the models most cleanly, and which result would favour the courier?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The middle-segment block. In 2003, Ye, Kuruvilla, Zweifel and Ginty did it: blocking TrkA kinase only in the middle stretch of the axon did not stop the signal reaching the cell bodies or the neurons' survival. That is evidence against a relay built from TrkA kinase, and for a courier, though not against every kind of relay.

The other tests fit a courier. Heerssen and colleagues imaged fluorescent Trk receptors moving from axon terminals to cell bodies, and in 2007 Cui and colleagues tracked single NGF-containing endosomes moving back along axons. Imaging shows that complexes are carried, not that they are what keeps the neuron alive. Ye and colleagues also found that NGF had to be internalized, and that NGF inside the cell bodies was needed for survival.

</details>

For survival, most of the evidence favours the carried endosome. Two results keep the question from being closed: TrkA became phosphorylated in cell bodies within a minute of NGF reaching distal axons, far faster than dynein-driven endosomes move, and NGF fixed to beads supported survival with little or no NGF transport ([Going deeper](#a-faster-signal)). Whether a faster, propagated signal also contributes is not settled.

## NGF versus NT-3: one signal makes the trip

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Development supplies the sharpest test. Sympathetic axons in the mouse grow out along blood vessels before reaching the organs they innervate. The vessels, an intermediate target, make NT-3. The final targets make NGF. Both factors can activate TrkA. So why does a neuron need two?

**Predict.** Kuruvilla, Ginty and colleagues gave NGF or NT-3 to the distal axons only of sympathetic neurons in compartmented cultures, with no factor at the cell bodies, and measured growth, transport and survival in separate assays. Fill in each cell with yes or no.

| Factor on distal axons only | Local axon growth | Factor-TrkA complexes carried back to the cell body | Cell bodies survive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NGF | ? | ? | ? |
| NT-3 | ? | ? | ? |

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

| Factor on distal axons only | Local axon growth | Factor-TrkA complexes carried back to the cell body | Cell bodies survive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NGF | yes | yes | yes |
| NT-3 | yes | no (not stably) | no |

Both factors activate TrkA at the axon, so local signaling for growth works in both cases. The difference was in trafficking. NGF-TrkA complexes were internalized into signaling endosomes and carried back to the cell body by dynein. NT-3-TrkA complexes were not stably carried back. To test that explanation further, look for phosphorylated TrkA arriving in cell bodies, and for nuclear CREB phosphorylation, with each factor on the axons: both with NGF, neither with NT-3.

</details>

NT-3 is not useless. In mice lacking NT-3, sympathetic axon growth and target innervation were impaired, so NT-3 matters for the journey to the target.

The paper also proposed a handoff. Retrograde NGF signaling raises the neuron's level of p75, a second neurotrophin receptor that binds every neurotrophin ([the lesson on Trk and p75 receptors](/lessons/trk-and-p75/)). As p75 rises, the neuron's response to NT-3 falls, so the neuron becomes less responsive to the intermediate target once it reaches the final one.

This is the guiding idea at its sharpest: both factors bind TrkA at the tip, but only NGF's signal makes the trip, and of the two, only NGF keeps these neurons alive from the axon tip. A factor that acts only locally can steer an axon, and so decide which target it reaches, but it cannot by itself keep the neuron alive. Competition among neurons for the final target, the subject of [that lesson](/lessons/competition-for-targets/), therefore depends on the trafficking of the receptor, not only on binding.

The experiments trace a shift, though not strictly in date order: from evidence that something moves, to evidence that a signal from the axon keeps the cell body alive, to evidence of what makes the trip and why only one of two factors can use it.

### Design problem

A new factor Y activates TrkA at the distal axons of sympathetic neurons in compartmented culture, with no factor at the cell bodies. Labeled Y is found in the cell bodies, yet the neurons die. Name two places the signal could fail, one before it reaches the cell body and one after, and an experiment for each.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

First, remember what label shows. Finding labeled Y in the cell bodies says that Y, or its label, moved. It does not say that Y arrived bound to an active receptor.

1. **Y never delivers a receptor that can still signal.** TrkA might be taken up with Y into endosomes that are not carried home, as with NT-3, or not taken up at all, or it might lose Y or its phosphates on the way. Test: follow TrkA from the distal axons and measure its phosphorylation along the axon and in the cell bodies, compared with NGF. No carried receptor points to uptake or sorting; carried receptor that fades points to switching off on the way.
2. **Active receptor arrives, but the cell body's response falls short.** Test: measure nuclear CREB phosphorylation, as one readout, with Y on the axons. Active TrkA in the cell bodies without phospho-CREB points here. Normal phospho-CREB alone would not prove the survival response is complete: the rule does not promise that every signal that arrives is enough.

A useful control for both: give Y directly to the cell bodies at a matched dose. If the neurons then survive, they can use Y, so the failure lies somewhere between the axon tip and a sufficient response in the cell body. The measurements above say where.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Where the decision is made

The nucleus is not the only place where death machinery sits. The Bcl-2 family proteins act at mitochondria, and mitochondria run along the axon too; the machinery that dismantles an axon locally is the subject of [the lesson on axon pruning](/lessons/axon-pruning/). What is confined to the cell body is the nucleus, whose new transcription the death program in these neurons needs.

### A faster signal?

Senger and Campenot (1997) saw the cell-body TrkA phosphorylation before any labeled NGF was detected there. Even across one millimetre, arriving within a minute would need about 17 micrometres per second, far above the roughly one micrometre per second measured for dynein transport.

In the bead experiment, MacInnis and Campenot (2002) supplied the beads to the distal axons of rat sympathetic neurons, and they kept over 80% of the neurons alive for 30 hours. Compare Riccio and colleagues, who found that internalization and transport were needed for nuclear CREB phosphorylation. The two studies measured different things, a nuclear transcription factor against survival, and how they fit together is part of the open question.

### The cell biology of the trip

How dynein moves cargo along an axon is covered in [the lesson on long-distance signaling in neurons](/lessons/long-distance-signaling-in-neurons/), and how kinase signals reach transcription factors in [the lesson on signaling to the nucleus](/lessons/signaling-to-the-nucleus/).

### Does a single flavor of endosome mediate all of these functions?

NGF arriving at the cell body does more than prevent death. It regulates dendrite growth, transmitter phenotype, cell size and, through the dendrites, synapse formation. One carrier, or several?

Work from David Ginty's laboratory, where Deppmann was a postdoctoral fellow, and later from Deppmann's own laboratory addresses this. Sharma, Deppmann, Harrington and colleagues in the Ginty laboratory (Neuron, 2010) found that in sympathetic neurons, NGF-TrkA signaling endosomes travel from distal axons to cell bodies and dendrites and promote assembly of the postsynaptic specialization, a long-distance relationship between target NGF and the synapses a neuron receives.

Suo and colleagues (Nature Neuroscience, 2014) identified Coronin-1, a protein whose expression NGF induces, as an endosomal effector that keeps NGF-TrkA endosomes from fusing with lysosomes, and found that it is required for developmental competition for survival. Together these results suggest that biasing an endosome's maturation changes which function it serves, so that distinct endosome pools may govern distinct developmental processes. The review by Barford, Deppmann and Winckler (Developmental Neurobiology, 2017) sets out this view. Whether every message rides in its own kind of endosome is still open.

### Retrograde transport in disease models

In disease models, dynein and dynactin mutations that impair retrograde transport are linked to motor neuron degeneration, and a mouse model of Down syndrome shows failed NGF transport with degeneration of cholinergic neurons.
