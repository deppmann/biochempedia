    story: >-
      Mouse genetics cannot be done in people, so the human touch classes rest on a different
      method. In microneurography a fine tungsten electrode is inserted through the skin into a
      peripheral nerve of a conscious subject, close enough to record the impulses of a single
      afferent. Roland Johansson and Åke Vallbo used it in the median nerve to study 334
      low-threshold mechanoreceptive units from the glabrous skin of the hand, published in the
      Journal of Physiology in 1979. They classified the units into four groups by how they
      adapted and by their receptive fields: RA, PC, SA I and SA II. The classes were defined by
      firing, not by finding each unit's ending under a microscope, which is why the SA II to
      Ruffini pairing remains an inference. Vallbo and colleagues later used the same method to
      record unmyelinated afferents in forearm hairy skin that respond to gentle deformation. The
      method made it possible to compare human afferent classes with the genetically defined
      mouse classes on equal physiological terms.

techniques:
  - name: "Genetic labelling with Cre and CreER drivers"
    blurb: >-
      A Cre driver is a mouse line that expresses Cre recombinase under one gene's control, so a
      reporter marks the cells that expressed that gene at some time. CreER adds a tamoxifen
      switch, so the induction window can be chosen. The label is a handle on a population, not
      proof of a pure type until physiology checks it.
  - name: "Intracellular recording in an ex vivo skin-nerve-DRG-spinal cord preparation"
    blurb: >-
      Skin, nerve, dorsal root ganglion and cord are dissected out together, and a sharp electrode
      records a labelled cell body while the skin is touched or the nerve stimulated. It gives
      conduction velocity, threshold and adaptation for a neuron whose genetic identity and ending
      can be confirmed afterwards.
  - name: "Whole-mount immunostaining and confocal microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      A sheet of fixed skin is stained intact and imaged in three dimensions, so each labelled
      ending can be assigned to the follicle and hair type it surrounds.
  - name: "Human microneurography"
    blurb: >-
      A tungsten microelectrode is inserted through the skin into a peripheral nerve of a conscious
      person to record single afferents. Units are classified by firing and receptive field; their
      endings are not seen.

practiceQuestions:
- stem: A probe is pressed into the skin and held still. Which LTMR class in the lesson's table is best suited to report the held press and the probe's edges?
  choices:
  - Aβ RA2 afferents ending in Pacinian corpuscles
  - Aδ-LTMRs with lanceolate endings at hairs
  - Aβ SA1 afferents at Merkel cell-neurite complexes
  - Aβ RA1 afferents ending in Meissner corpuscles
  answer: 2
  rationale: Aβ SA1 afferents keep firing during a held step and are best driven by indentation, points and edges, so they report the steady press. Pacinian (RA2) afferents fire at onset and offset and are most sensitive to vibration near 100 to 300 Hz, so they mark only the start and end of the press. Aδ-LTMRs adapt rapidly and are tuned to tiny hair deflections. Aβ RA1 afferents fire at onset, sometimes at offset, and report skin motion. Each class is most sensitive to a different part of the same touch.
- stem: A neuron is classed as a low-threshold mechanoreceptor. What does the term specify?
  choices:
  - It cannot fire to a noxious mechanical force
  - Its axon conducts in the Aβ velocity range
  - It responds to innocuous mechanical stimuli
  - It adapts rapidly to a maintained indentation
  answer: 2
  rationale: 'LTMR is a sensitivity designation: the neuron is activated by innocuous mechanical stimulation. It does not mean the neuron cannot fire to strong force; mouse Aβ field-LTMRs fired more as indentation entered the noxious range. LTMRs include Aδ and C classes, so the term says nothing about Aβ speed, and SA1 and C-LTMRs are LTMRs that do not adapt rapidly.'
- stem: In an ex vivo mouse skin-nerve-DRG preparation, an identified afferent is stimulated 20 mm from the ganglion and its spike arrives 3.4 ms later. Assume constant velocity and ignore utilization time (the brief delay between the stimulus and the start of the spike). Which measured mouse class mean (Li et al., 2011, or Bai et al., 2015) does it match best?
  choices:
  - Aβ RA-LTMR, near 13.6 m/s
  - Aδ-LTMR, near 5.9 m/s
  - C-LTMR, near 0.6 m/s
  - Aβ field-LTMR, near 17 m/s
  answer: 1
  rationale: 'Velocity is distance over time: 0.020 m divided by 0.0034 s is 5.9 m/s, close to Li et al.''s Aδ-LTMR sample mean of 5.8 m/s. The C-LTMR sample conducted at about 0.58 m/s, ten times slower. The hairy-skin Aβ RA sample averaged 13.6 m/s and the field-LTMR sample (Bai et al., 2015) 17.0 m/s, both more than twice as fast. Velocity narrows the class but genetic identity or ending is still needed to confirm it.'
- stem: In the mouse trunk skin studied by Li et al. (2011), which lanceolate endings innervated virtually all zigzag hair follicles?
  choices:
  - Aδ-LTMR and C-LTMR lanceolate endings
  - Aβ RA, Aδ and C-LTMR lanceolate endings
  - Aβ RA-LTMR lanceolate endings only
  - Aβ RA-LTMR and Aβ SA1 Merkel endings
  answer: 0
  rationale: Li et al. found that virtually all zigzag follicles received both Aδ- and C-LTMR lanceolate endings. Aβ RA plus Merkel-associated SA1 endings is the guard hair combination. Aβ RA, Aδ and C lanceolate endings together is the awl/auchene combination. Aβ RA lanceolate endings alone is wrong too, as the paper reports very few Aβ RA associations with zigzag follicles. Li did not label field-LTMRs, whose circumferential endings also wrap most awl/auchene and zigzag follicles (Bai et al., 2015).
- stem: Microneurography in human forearm hairy skin finds slowly conducting, unmyelinated afferents that respond to slow, gentle stroking. Which statement about these C-tactile afferents fits the reviews cited in the lesson?
  choices:
  - They are nociceptors that respond to light pressure
  - They support affective touch, but not all of it
  - They are the only route to pleasant touch
  - They carry the fine detail of discriminative touch
  answer: 1
  rationale: Reviews conclude that CT afferents support gentle, affective touch, but that not every affective touch relies on CTs (Schirmer and colleagues, 2023). They are low-threshold mechanoreceptors, activated by innocuous touch, so calling them nociceptors confuses a slow fibre with a high threshold. The only-route claim is the overstatement that the review rejects. Discriminative touch is served by fast myelinated Aβ afferents, and a slow C fibre is poorly suited to it.

Unit 1 conducts at 14 m/s and fires at onset and offset. Unit 2 conducts at 6 m/s and fires a brief burst at onset. Unit 3 conducts at 0.6 m/s and keeps firing for part of the hold. Which conclusion do these results best support?
  choices:
  - Only unit 1 signals the deflection, as it is fastest
  - The three units are one class with different axons
  - One hair movement is reported by several classes
  - Unit 3 is a nociceptor, as it conducts so slowly
  answer: 2
  rationale: 'The units differ in speed and time course, and their speeds sit close to Li et al.''s means for Aβ RA-LTMRs (13.6 m/s), Aδ-LTMRs (5.8 m/s) and C-LTMRs (0.58 m/s), the three classes whose lanceolate endings share awl/auchene follicles. So one deflection is reported in parallel by three classes, and their signals reach the spinal cord at different times. All three units fire, so the fastest is not the only signal. - stem: In adult mice, optogenetic activation of C-LTMRs in hairy skin evoked whole-body wet dog shakes. Which conclusion do these results best support?
  choices:
  - C-LTMR activity is not needed for shakes
  - C-LTMRs signal only pleasant touch in mice
  - C-LTMRs are the only route to the shake
  - C-LTMRs contribute to the shake response
  answer: 3
  rationale: 'Activation shows that C-LTMR activity is sufficient to evoke shakes, and a partial loss after ablation shows that they contribute to the normal response. - stem: In a microneurography recording from the median nerve of a conscious volunteer, a single afferent from the palm adapts slowly, has a large receptive field with poorly defined borders and fires to skin stretch. The investigator classes it as SA II. Can she conclude that this unit ends in a Ruffini corpuscle?
  choices:
  - No, as human glabrous skin lacks slowly adapting units
  - Yes, as the mouse SA2 ending is a Ruffini corpuscle
  - Yes, as SA II units always end in Ruffini corpuscles
  - No, as the recording does not identify the ending
  answer: 3
  rationale: Microneurography classifies units by firing and receptive field; it does not see the ending, and the SA2 to Ruffini pairing remains controversial in the 2021 review. Saying SA II units always end in Ruffini corpuscles states that inference as fact. Human glabrous skin does have slowly adapting units, both SA I and SA II in Johansson and Vallbo's sample. **Bend one awl/auchene hair on a mouse's back and three classes of touch neuron can answer, because all three have endings along its follicle. Each class is most sensitive to a different part of the movement, and their reports travel at different speeds.**

[Touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) sorted the four touch afferents of the human hand into a two-by-two table, by how fast they adapt and how big their receptive field is, and sorted axons into Aβ, Aδ and C by speed using a latency problem. That table was built from firing alone. This lesson asks what lies behind each box, which ending and which neuron, and what hairy skin adds. It also answers the question the table raises: why split one touch into several signals at all?

## One press, several reports

Start with the simplest touch there is. A smooth probe presses into a fingertip, holds still for a second, then lifts. Record from each kind of touch afferent in turn, and line up its spikes under the probe's position.

- A **slowly adapting** afferent ending at Merkel cells (SA I) fires as the probe goes in and keeps firing while it is held. It reports that something is there, and where its edges are.
- A **rapidly adapting** afferent ending in a Meissner corpuscle (RA) fires while the skin moves, as the probe goes in and again as it lifts, and is quiet during the hold. It reports that something is changing.
- A **Pacinian** afferent (PC) is silent during the hold. It gives at most a brief burst as the probe goes in and as it lifts, and with a smooth, slow ramp even that may be weak.

**Predict.** Now make the probe vibrate fast, at 250 Hz, with tiny movements. Which afferent detects the smallest such vibration?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The Pacinian afferent. It is the most sensitive of the classes to fast vibration, best near 100 to 300 Hz, while the slowly adapting afferent hardly responds. Each class has a band in which it is most sensitive. These are bands of best sensitivity, not cutoffs: a strong enough vibration drives other classes too.

</details>

Same skin, same probe, different records; each is one part of the touch.

Why not one all-purpose ending? The experiments here do not test that. They show that the recorded classes are complementary: SA I afferents keep signalling a held press, while Pacinian afferents detect fast vibrations too small to drive the others much. Together they give the nervous system several views of the same contact.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Each class of low-threshold mechanoreceptor is most sensitive to a different part of a touch, much as overlapping filters each favour one band of a sound: a held press, skin motion, fast vibration, a stroke over a wide area, the slightest bend of a hair, a slow stroke. Its end organ, together with the neuron's own channels and excitability, shapes which part. Its axon's diameter and myelin set how fast the report travels, and the fibre groups Aβ, Aδ and C summarize those speeds. No class reports the whole touch, so the nervous system reads the pattern across the classes that share the skin.

This extends a rule from Touch and pain, that the ending helps decide what can activate a line, to the parts of one sense. It has limits, and the end of the lesson lists them.

Two terms, now that you have seen them work. A **low-threshold mechanoreceptor (LTMR)** is a cutaneous sensory neuron activated by touch too light to hurt (Abraira and Ginty, 2013). "Low threshold" describes the bottom of its range, not the top. In mice, Aβ field-LTMRs respond vigorously to a gentle stroke, and their firing kept increasing as indentation entered the noxious range (Bai and colleagues, 2015). An LTMR can fire to a pinch; what makes it an LTMR is that it also fires to a touch too light to hurt.

**RA** (rapidly adapting) and **SA** (slowly adapting) are names for the response to one test, the held step you just watched: RA afferents fire at onset and sometimes at offset, SA afferents keep firing (Handler and Ginty, 2021). A step response does not tell you everything a neuron reports during natural movement. [Sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/) treats adaptation in general.

A note on names. This lesson uses Roman numerals for human microneurography classes (SA I, SA II, with RA and PC) and Arabic numerals for mouse genetic classes (Aβ SA1-LTMR, Aβ RA1); the literature mixes both. SA I, RA and PC match SA1, RA1 and RA2 by firing; whether SA II matches a mouse SA2 class is open.

## Hairy skin: two endings and the class table

Two kinds of ending recur in hairy skin. A **lanceolate ending** is an axon branch that runs lengthwise along a hair follicle, parallel to the shaft, like one picket in a fence around the hair. A **circumferential ending** wraps around the follicle instead. A **terminal Schwann cell** is a non-neuronal cell whose processes interleave with lanceolate endings.

## The key experiment: label, record, map

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

For decades, classes in hairy skin were defined by recording, and the ending behind each was inferred. What changed was genetic access. A **Cre driver** is a mouse line that expresses Cre recombinase from one gene's regulatory DNA, so that a reporter marks cells that expressed that gene; **CreER** adds a tamoxifen switch so the experimenter picks the labelling window ([tools for development](/lessons/tools-for-development/) covers how). A label is a handle, not a class, until physiology confirms it. Transcriptomic atlases, covered in [the atlas lesson](/lessons/sensory-neuron-atlas/), now offer many more handles, and each still needs this step.

Mouse trunk skin has three main hair types: long guard hairs, awl/auchene hairs, and the short, bent zigzag hairs. Li and colleagues (2011) labelled the C, Aδ and Aβ RA classes genetically and checked each label by recording. They did not label field-LTMRs, which return below. No mouse line for SA1 neurons existed yet, so they marked SA1 endings through the Merkel cells they contact. They then imaged fixed whole mounts of trunk skin to see which follicles each label surrounded. Here is what each handle gave them.

| Genetic handle | Recording showed | Ending | Follicles |
|---|---|---|---|
| TrkB | Aδ-LTMR: 5.8 m/s, rapidly adapting, threshold below 0.07 mN | Lanceolate | Awl/auchene, zigzag |
| TH | C-LTMR: 0.58 m/s, intermediate adaptation | Lanceolate | Awl/auchene, zigzag |
| Npy2r-GFP, a subset of the early Ret-expressing neurons | Aβ RA-LTMR: 13.6 m/s, rapidly adapting | Lanceolate | Guard, awl/auchene |
| None for SA1 neurons; Merkel cells stained instead | Not recorded | Touch dome | Guard |

In their recordings, adult TrkB-expressing dorsal root ganglion neurons behaved as Aδ-LTMRs, and the cutaneous C-fibre neurons with C-LTMR properties stained TH-positive. That is the check that turns a handle into a class.

"Stereotyped" keeps its exceptions: the paper reports very few Aβ RA associations with zigzag follicles. The three hair types occurred in an approximate 1:23:76 ratio (guard to awl/auchene to zigzag), and recurring clusters of them led the authors to describe **peripheral LTMR units**. The field-LTMRs Li did not label add circumferential endings around most awl/auchene and zigzag follicles (Bai and colleagues, 2015), making four LTMR classes at an awl/auchene follicle and three at a zigzag follicle. Li and Ginty (2014) later found that a single terminal Schwann cell can host endings of more than one LTMR type.

This is what the map means for coding. One movement of an awl/auchene hair can be reported in parallel by three classes, at three speeds and with three time courses. One warning when you count: a single afferent branches to many follicles, so a count of endings at hairs is not a count of neurons. The same handles let later studies ablate, silence or activate one class, as in the C-LTMR experiment under "Where the idea stops".

## Your own skin: human touch classes and C-tactile afferents

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Mouse genetics cannot be done in people, so the human classes rest on another method. In **microneurography**, a tungsten microelectrode inserted through the skin into a peripheral nerve records single afferents in a conscious person. Recordings from the glabrous skin of the hand sorted human LTMRs into four classes, RA, PC, SA I and SA II (Johansson and Vallbo, 1979). By their firing, RA, PC and SA I line up with the Aβ RA1, RA2 and SA1 rows of the table. The human classes rest on firing and receptive fields; no ending was seen. That is why SA II, slowly adapting units with large receptive fields that fire to skin stretch, is not yet tied to an ending in either species.

Hairy skin adds another class. Vallbo and colleagues (1999) recorded unmyelinated afferents in human forearm hairy skin that respond to gentle deformation, the **C-tactile (CT) afferents**, physiologically similar to mouse C-LTMRs.

**Predict.** A soft brush strokes the forearm at speeds from very slow to fast. Which speed drives a CT afferent hardest? Will a fast myelinated Aβ afferent follow the same curve?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not the fastest. In microneurography by Löken and colleagues (2009), CT firing followed an inverted U across brushing velocities, peaking at about 1 to 10 cm/s, the slow, gentle stroking commonly felt as pleasant. The volunteers' pleasantness ratings correlated with CT firing. Aβ afferents behaved differently: their firing kept rising with velocity. This inverted U is the CT's characteristic response, as the held step is for SA and RA afferents.

</details>

Reviews of the field conclude that CTs support gentle, affective touch, the social side of touch, while fast myelinated Aβ afferents serve discriminative touch (Schirmer and colleagues, 2023; McGlone and colleagues, 2026). The link has a limit: not every affective touch relies on CTs, and affective touch need not be pleasant (Schirmer and colleagues, 2023). The human CT ending is unknown, and the awl/auchene and zigzag map cannot be transplanted onto human hair. [Hairy skin and affective touch](/lessons/hairy-skin-and-affective-touch/) takes CTs further.

## How a tuning is built: direction

One class shows that what a neuron is most sensitive to can be built into its wiring during development. Aδ-LTMRs are among the most sensitive hair afferents in the mouse, and in adult mouse trunk skin they prefer one direction: they fire more to hair deflection toward the head (caudal-to-rostral) than to an equal deflection the other way (Rutlin and colleagues, 2014). Their lanceolate endings are enriched on the caudal, tail side of each follicle, where BDNF in the follicle epithelium is also enriched.

Two things here are easy to confuse. Think of a weathervane: where a vane is mounted is not the way it points. The endings are mounted on the tail side; the neuron's preferred movement is the hair tip swinging toward the head. How one leads to the other mechanically is unknown. BDNF signalling through TrkB is covered in [Trk and p75](/lessons/trk-and-p75/).

## Design problem

A transcriptomic atlas offers a new marker gene for mouse afferents that may be the missing SA2-like class. Design the experiments that would establish these neurons as an LTMR class: what they respond to, how fast they conduct, and where they end. State what the design could and could not conclude.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Get genetic access. Make a Cre or CreER driver from the marker gene and cross it to a reporter. CreER lets you choose the labelling window, which matters if the gene is also expressed by other neurons at other times.

Check the label with physiology. Record labelled neurons in an ex vivo skin, nerve, ganglion and spinal cord preparation. Measure conduction velocity, threshold, the response to a held step indentation and the response to skin stretch. An SA2-like class should fire to innocuous force, keep firing during a held stimulus and respond to stretch.

Find the ending. Image whole mounts of labelled skin, and confirm the ending of recorded neurons afterwards, to see whether the endings are Ruffini-like or something else.

Test what the class does. With genetic access you can ablate, silence or activate the class and ask which behaviour changes, as was done for C-LTMRs and the wet dog shake. A partial loss would show that the class contributes, not that it is the only route.

Conclude, at most, that mice have a genetically defined SA2-like class with a known response and ending. The design cannot show what human SA II units end in, because microneurography does not see the ending.

</details>

## Where the idea stops

The one idea says "most sensitive to", not "reports only", and gives the ending a share in tuning, not all of it. Here is why.

- **Classes overlap.** In a small human study that changed surface friction during touch, frictional transients evoked time-locked spikes across the sampled afferent classes (Dione and colleagues, 2021).
- **Same ending, different tuning.** Aβ RA, Aδ and C-LTMRs all make lanceolate endings at the same awl/auchene follicle, yet differ in threshold, adaptation and speed. The ending type alone does not set the tuning.
- **A C-LTMR is not only a line for gentle stroking.** In mice, optogenetic activation of C-LTMRs evoked wet dog shakes, and adult ablation cut oil-droplet-evoked shakes by about half (Zhang and colleagues, 2024).
- **Some endings are unknown.** SA II physiology is secure, but its ending is unidentified in both species, and the human CT ending is unknown too, so the end organ's share in their tuning cannot yet be checked.
- **The idea stops at the skin.** How the spinal cord combines these reports is the subject of [dorsal horn touch](/lessons/dorsal-horn-touch/).

## Going deeper

The details below support the main line without changing it.

### Where the class-table numbers come from

The Li velocities come from intracellular recordings in an ex vivo skin, nerve, ganglion and spinal cord preparation from 2 to 3 month old mice, with five C-LTMRs, 19 Aδ-LTMRs from seven animals and 18 Aβ RA-LTMRs from five animals. The often-quoted 15 to 100 m/s for Aβ is a review range pooled across species; the mouse hairy-skin Aβ RA mean sits below it. The Li whole mounts were of trunk skin from 3 to 10 week old mice, and the SA1 endings were found through their Merkel cells with anti-Troma1 staining.

What the ± values can tell you. The main text of Li et al. gives them without saying whether they are standard deviations or standard errors, and the size of a value is no guide to which. Either way, treat the values as summaries of these samples, not as boundaries between classes. If both Aβ values are standard deviations, the mean ± 1 SD intervals, 10.9 to 16.3 m/s for Aβ RA and 15.0 to 19.0 m/s for field-LTMRs, already overlap; if they are standard errors, single neurons spread more widely still.

The Meissner band in the table is a review value; [Touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) describes the same afferents as most sensitive to low-frequency vibration, the range of flutter and slip.

### The dorsal horn columns

The dorsal horn columns came from separate experiments in which cholera toxin B (CTB), a tracer taken up by axons, was injected into small patches of back skin at postnatal day 14 to 25. They showed that the central endings of afferents serving one patch line up in columns in the dorsal horn. That alignment is anatomy; how the columns combine their inputs is the subject of [dorsal horn touch](/lessons/dorsal-horn-touch/).

### Multi-afferent end organs

The awl/auchene follicle is not the only end organ with more than one afferent type. The 2021 review by Handler and Ginty adds that mouse Meissner corpuscles receive two afferent populations, some of them more slowly adapting, a result from Neubarth and colleagues (2020) that [the corpuscle lesson](/lessons/vibration-corpuscles/) takes up. Handler and colleagues (2023) located tagged Piezo2 in the sensory axon membrane of lanceolate, Meissner and Pacinian endings ([Piezo channels](/lessons/mechanotransduction-channels/) covers the channel). Merkel cells get [their own lesson](/lessons/merkel-cells/), and field-LTMRs and C-LTMRs return in [hairy skin and affective touch](/lessons/hairy-skin-and-affective-touch/).

### Counting follicle associations is not counting neurons

**Worked problem: associations versus neurons.** Take an idealized patch of exactly 100 mouse trunk follicles in Li's ratio: 1 guard, 23 awl/auchene, 76 zigzag. Use only the dominant map above, count each class once per follicle it innervates, and leave out field-LTMRs and the rare exceptions.

1. Aβ RA: guard plus awl/auchene = 1 + 23 = 24 associations.
2. Aβ SA1: guard only = 1.
3. Aδ-LTMR: awl/auchene plus zigzag = 23 + 76 = 99.
4. C-LTMR: awl/auchene plus zigzag = 23 + 76 = 99.
5. Total: 24 + 1 + 99 + 99 = 223.
6. Check by follicle: (1 × 2) + (23 × 3) + (76 × 2) = 2 + 69 + 152 = 223.

> **associations = sum over follicle types of (number of follicles × classes per follicle)**

Is 223 a neuron count? No. One afferent contacts many follicles; a single mouse field-LTMR arbor reached 20 to 180 follicles (Bai and colleagues, 2015). And 1:23:76 is a ratio of hairs, not of neurons. The calculation tells you how often each class has endings at a hair.

### SA II and the Ruffini question

Johansson and Vallbo (1979) recorded 334 low-threshold units through the median nerve; their SA II class is defined by firing, so its Ruffini pairing is an inference. The 2021 review by Handler and Ginty keeps that pairing controversial, and SA2-like responses have been recorded in mice without a corresponding Ruffini ending being identified. Three claims go too far. "Mice lack SA2" goes beyond the evidence, whether mice have a distinct SA2 class is still open, and "SA2 is the Ruffini afferent" is unproven in both species. The experiment that would settle it in mice is the one in the design problem.

### Small human samples: palm CTs and friction

CTs, long thought to be confined to hairy skin, have been found, sparsely, in the glabrous skin of the hand. Watkins and colleagues (2021) reported three CT afferents there, at roughly one per 80 myelinated afferents encountered. The authors used that ratio to estimate a CT density about seven times lower than in hairy skin, an estimate that rests on electrode sampling. Dione and colleagues (2021) changed surface friction with an ultrasonic device while recording from eight afferents in five women aged 21 to 28.

### Direction tuning in detail

In the ex vivo recordings, genetically identified Aδ-LTMRs fired, on average, more than three times as many spikes to caudal-to-rostral deflection as to an equal deflection the other way. The caudal BDNF was shown by reporter histology. The mutants were K5Cre;Bdnf conditional mutants: an epithelial driver avoids the problem that a germline Bdnf knockout would affect many neurons and the animal's health. Rutlin and colleagues recorded from TrkB-GFP neurons in these mutants. A TrkB-GFP knock-in labels Aδ-LTMRs without recombining Bdnf; a tamoxifen-induced TrkB-CreER line would also excise the floxed Bdnf allele in the labelled neurons. TrkB is expressed by other neurons too, so identity should be confirmed by conduction velocity and threshold. Separating development from adult function would need an adult, inducible deletion, and touch-evoked release would still have to be measured. The experiment says nothing directly about human hair afferents.

#### How to test it

The three columns of the prediction table map onto three measurements in mutants and littermate controls: ending polarity (the fraction of Aδ endings on the caudal side of each follicle, in whole mounts), tuning (spikes to equal rostral and caudal deflections, as a direction index, in ex vivo recordings) and survival (counts of labelled DRG neurons).

**Worked problem: a direction index.** Suppose, as illustration, an Aδ-LTMR fires 12 spikes to a caudal-to-rostral deflection (H = 12) and 3 to an equal rostral-to-caudal one (T = 3).

> **direction index = (H − T) / (H + T), for H + T > 0**

1. Ratio: 12 / 3 = 4, in the range of the paper's over-3-fold average.
2. Index: (12 − 3) / (12 + 3) = 9 / 15 = 0.60.
3. Check: the head direction is fixed by the body axis, so a neuron tuned the other way gets a negative index; 1 means no response to the opposite direction. A ratio of 3 gives (3 − 1) / (3 + 1) = 0.50, so 0.60 is a plausible illustrative value; the paper reports an average over tested spots, not a per-neuron floor.
