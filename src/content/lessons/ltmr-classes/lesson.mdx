    story: >-
      Mouse genetics cannot be done in people, so the human touch classes rest on a different
      method. In microneurography a fine tungsten electrode is inserted through the skin into a
      peripheral nerve of a conscious subject, close enough to record the impulses of a single
      afferent. Roland Johansson and Åke Vallbo used it in the median nerve to study 334
      low-threshold mechanoreceptive units from the glabrous skin of the hand, published in the
      Journal of Physiology in 1979. They classified the units into four groups by how they
      adapted and by their receptive fields: RA, PC, SA I and SA II. The classes were defined by
      firing, not by finding each unit's ending under a microscope, which is why the SA II to
      Ruffini pairing remains an inference. Vallbo and colleagues later used the same method to
      record unmyelinated afferents in forearm hairy skin that respond to gentle deformation. The
      method made it possible to compare human afferent classes with the genetically defined
      mouse classes on equal physiological terms.

techniques:
  - name: "Genetic labelling with Cre and CreER drivers"
    blurb: >-
      A Cre driver is a mouse line that expresses Cre recombinase under one gene's control, so a
      reporter marks the cells that expressed that gene at some time. CreER adds a tamoxifen
      switch, so the induction window can be chosen. The label is a handle on a population, not
      proof of a pure type until physiology checks it.
  - name: "Intracellular recording in an ex vivo skin-nerve-DRG-spinal cord preparation"
    blurb: >-
      Skin, nerve, dorsal root ganglion and cord are dissected out together, and a sharp electrode
      records a labelled cell body while the skin is touched or the nerve stimulated. It gives
      conduction velocity, threshold and adaptation for a neuron whose genetic identity and ending
      can be confirmed afterwards.
  - name: "Whole-mount immunostaining and confocal microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      A sheet of fixed skin is stained intact and imaged in three dimensions, so each labelled
      ending can be assigned to the follicle and hair type it surrounds.
  - name: "Human microneurography"
    blurb: >-
      A tungsten microelectrode is inserted through the skin into a peripheral nerve of a conscious
      person to record single afferents. Units are classified by firing and receptive field; their
      endings are not seen.

- stem: Microneurography in human forearm hairy skin finds slowly conducting, unmyelinated afferents that respond to slow, gentle stroking. Which statement about these C-tactile afferents fits the reviews cited in the lesson?
  choices:

  - They support affective touch, but not all of it
  - They are the only route to pleasant touch
  - They carry the fine detail of discriminative touch
  answer: 1
  rationale: Reviews conclude that CT afferents support gentle, affective touch, but that not every affective touch relies on CTs (Schirmer and colleagues, 2023). **Gentle touch is split at the skin into separate signals. Each class of low-threshold mechanoreceptor (LTMR) has its own ending, its own speed and its own time course, and so reports a different feature of the same touch: skin motion, a steady press and its edges, vibration, a stroke over a wide area, the slightest bend of a hair, or a gentle stroke.** No class reports the whole touch; the nervous system reads them together. Mouse hairy skin shows this most clearly. An awl/auchene hair follicle is fenced by the lanceolate endings of three different kinds of touch neuron, and nearly every zigzag follicle by two. Most follicles of both types are also wrapped by the circumferential ending of one more kind, the field-LTMR. This lesson asks what the classes are and what each one reports, how anyone found out which endings belong to which neurons, and how far the mouse map carries over to your own skin.

[Touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) introduced the four classical receptors of the human hand by adaptation and receptive field, and sorted axons into Aβ, Aδ and C by speed with a latency problem. This lesson starts where that one stops: genetic access to each class, the end organs of hairy skin, and why one end organ can hold more than one afferent type.

## Each class reports a different feature of gentle touch

A **low-threshold mechanoreceptor (LTMR)** is a cutaneous sensory neuron activated by innocuous mechanical stimulation (Abraira and Ginty, 2013). The word is a statement about sensitivity, not a limit. In mice, Aβ field-LTMRs respond vigorously to a gentle stroke, and their firing kept increasing as indentation entered the noxious range (Bai and colleagues, 2015). An LTMR can fire to a pinch. What makes it an LTMR is that it also fires to a touch too light to hurt.

The labels RA (rapidly adapting) and SA (slowly adapting) are also test results. They describe the response to one stimulus, a held step indentation: RA afferents fire at onset and sometimes at offset, SA afferents keep firing (Handler and Ginty, 2021). [Sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/) treats adaptation in general. Here the point is narrower: a step response does not tell you everything a neuron reports during natural movement.

Two kinds of ending recur in hairy skin. A **lanceolate ending** is an axon branch that runs lengthwise along a hair follicle, parallel to the shaft, like one picket in a fence around the hair. A **circumferential ending** wraps around the follicle instead. A **terminal Schwann cell** is a non-neuronal cell whose processes interleave with lanceolate endings.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| C-LTMR | Lanceolate endings at awl/auchene and zigzag hairs (TH) | 0.58 ± 0.02 m/s (Li 2011) | Intermediate | Gentle deformation of hairy skin |

Read the table by its last column. Each class is tuned to a different part of a touch. Aβ SA1 afferents keep firing through a held press and signal points and edges. Aβ RA1 afferents fire as the skin moves. Pacinian (RA2) afferents are most sensitive to fast vibration. Field-LTMRs respond to a stroke over a wide area. Aδ-LTMRs respond to the tiniest hair deflections. C-LTMRs respond to gentle deformation of hairy skin, and in the mouse they conduct about ten times more slowly than Aδ-LTMRs. A real touch drives several classes at once, and their impulses reach the spinal cord at different times. Abraira and Ginty (2013) argue that touch is read from these ensembles of impulses from physiologically distinct LTMRs, not from one labelled line per sensation.

Three readings keep the table honest. First, the mouse velocities are means from one preparation in one study each, not universal boundaries for Aβ, Aδ and C fibres; the cohorts and the review range for Aβ are in Going deeper. Second, RA1 is not a synonym for Meissner: in mouse hairy skin, the Aβ RA endings are lanceolate. Third, the frequency bands are bands of best sensitivity from reviews, not cutoffs.

## How it was shown: a genetic label, checked by recording

For decades, classes in hairy skin were defined by recording, and the ending behind each was inferred. What changed was genetic access. A **Cre driver** is a mouse line that expresses Cre recombinase from one gene's regulatory DNA, so that a reporter marks cells that expressed that gene; **CreER** adds a tamoxifen switch so the experimenter picks the labelling window ([tools for development](/lessons/tools-for-development/) covers how). A label is a handle, not a class, until physiology confirms it. Li and colleagues (2011) did that check: in their recordings, adult TrkB-expressing dorsal root ganglion neurons behaved as Aδ-LTMRs, and the cutaneous C-fibre neurons with C-LTMR properties stained TH-positive. Transcriptomic atlases, covered in [the atlas lesson](/lessons/sensory-neuron-atlas/), now offer many more handles, and each still needs this step. The same handles let later studies ablate, silence or activate one class and ask what it does, as the C-LTMR experiments later in this lesson show.

## One follicle, several neurons: the Li 2011 map

Mouse trunk skin has three main hair types: long guard hairs, awl/auchene hairs, and the short, bent zigzag hairs. Li and colleagues labelled the C, Aδ and Aβ RA classes genetically and confirmed their identity by recording. No mouse line for SA1 neurons existed yet, so they marked SA1 endings through the Merkel cells they contact. They then imaged fixed whole mounts of trunk skin to see which follicles each label surrounded. The combinations were:

- **Guard:** Aβ RA lanceolate endings, plus Merkel-associated Aβ SA1 endings in the touch dome beside the hair.
- **Awl/auchene:** Aβ RA, Aδ and C lanceolate endings, interdigitated.
- **Zigzag:** Aδ and C lanceolate endings, in virtually all follicles examined.

"Stereotyped" keeps its exceptions: the paper reports very few Aβ RA associations with zigzag follicles. The three hair types occurred in an approximate 1:23:76 ratio (guard to awl/auchene to zigzag), and recurring clusters of them led the authors to describe **peripheral LTMR units**. In separate experiments, a tracer placed in a small patch of back skin showed that the central endings of afferents serving one patch line up in columns in the dorsal horn. That alignment is anatomy; how the columns combine their inputs is the subject of [dorsal horn touch](/lessons/dorsal-horn-touch/).

This is what the map means for coding. One movement of an awl/auchene hair is reported at the same moment by three classes, with three speeds and three time courses. In the model of Abraira and Ginty (2013), a poke at one spot drives mostly the SA1 afferents of a guard hair's touch dome, while a stroke across the fur drives a different mix of classes. Which classes fire depends on the stimulus: SA1 touch-dome afferents respond to indentation, not to deflection of the hair alone. One warning when you count: a single afferent branches to many follicles, so a count of endings at hairs is not a count of neurons. Going deeper works through the arithmetic.

One end organ can hold more than one afferent type. The awl/auchene follicle is the clearest case, with three classes interdigitated around it, and Li and Ginty (2014) showed that a single terminal Schwann cell can host endings of more than one LTMR type. The 2021 review by Handler and Ginty adds that mouse Meissner corpuscles receive two afferent populations, some of them more slowly adapting, a result from Neubarth and colleagues (2020) that [the corpuscle lesson](/lessons/vibration-corpuscles/) takes up. Handler and colleagues (2023) located tagged Piezo2 in the sensory axon membrane of lanceolate, Meissner and Pacinian endings ([Piezo channels](/lessons/mechanotransduction-channels/) covers the channel). Merkel cells get [their own lesson](/lessons/merkel-cells/), and field-LTMRs and C-LTMRs return in [hairy skin and affective touch](/lessons/hairy-skin-and-affective-touch/).

## Your own skin: human touch classes and C-tactile afferents

In **microneurography**, a tungsten microelectrode inserted through the skin into a peripheral nerve records single afferents in a conscious person. Recordings from the glabrous skin of the hand sorted human LTMRs into four classes, RA, PC, SA I and SA II (Johansson and Vallbo, 1979). By their firing, RA, PC and SA I line up with the Aβ RA1, RA2 and SA1 rows of the table. The human classes rest on firing and receptive fields; no ending was seen.

Hairy skin adds another class. Vallbo and colleagues (1999) recorded unmyelinated afferents in human forearm hairy skin that respond to gentle deformation, the C-tactile (CT) afferents, physiologically similar to mouse C-LTMRs. CTs respond best to slow, gentle stroking of the kind commonly felt as pleasant. Reviews of the field conclude that CTs support gentle, affective touch, the social side of touch, while fast myelinated Aβ afferents serve discriminative touch (Schirmer and colleagues, 2023; McGlone and colleagues, 2026). The link has a limit: not every affective touch relies on CTs, and affective touch need not be pleasant (Schirmer and colleagues, 2023). The human CT ending is unknown, and the awl/auchene and zigzag map cannot be transplanted onto human hair. [Hairy skin and affective touch](/lessons/hairy-skin-and-affective-touch/) takes CTs further.

Two matches remain open. Human SA II physiology is secure: slowly adapting units with large receptive fields that fire to skin stretch. The 2021 review keeps the SA2-Ruffini pairing controversial, and SA2-like responses have been recorded in mice without a corresponding Ruffini ending being identified, so SA II is not yet tied to an ending in either species. And CTs, long thought to be confined to hairy skin, have been found, sparsely, in the glabrous skin of the hand (Watkins and colleagues, 2021).

Human recordings also warn against one-class, one-feature thinking: in a small study that changed surface friction during touch, frictional transients evoked time-locked spikes across the sampled afferent classes (Dione and colleagues, 2021). In mice, C-LTMRs are not only about gentle stroking either: optogenetic activation evoked wet dog shakes, and adult ablation cut oil-droplet-evoked shakes by about half (Zhang and colleagues, 2024).

## A specialized case: direction tuning set during development

One class shows that a feature can be built into the wiring during development. Aδ-LTMRs are the most sensitive hair afferents in the mouse, and in adult mouse trunk skin they prefer one direction: they fire more to hair deflection toward the head (caudal-to-rostral) than to an equal deflection the other way (Rutlin and colleagues, 2014). Their lanceolate endings are enriched on the caudal, tail side of each follicle, where BDNF in the follicle epithelium is also enriched. Keep two variables apart: the endings sit on the tail side, and the preferred movement is toward the head. BDNF signalling through TrkB is covered in [Trk and p75](/lessons/trk-and-p75/).

Deleting Bdnf from the epithelium disrupted the caudal polarity of the Aδ endings and randomized the neurons' preferred direction relative to the body axis, while Aδ-LTMR survival and general maturation were retained. Because the deletion acted through development, the result shows that epithelial BDNF is required to organize the endings, not that BDNF is released during touch. Treat this as a specialized example from mouse trunk skin; the size of the preference, a direction index to compute, and the details of the experiment are in Going deeper.

### Design problem

A transcriptomic atlas offers a new marker gene for mouse afferents that may be the missing SA2-like class. Design the experiments that would establish these neurons as an LTMR class: what they respond to, how fast they conduct, and where they end. State what the design could and could not conclude.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Get genetic access. Make a Cre or CreER driver from the marker gene and cross it to a reporter. CreER lets you choose the labelling window, which matters if the gene is also expressed by other neurons at other times.

Check the label with physiology. Record labelled neurons in an ex vivo skin, nerve, ganglion and spinal cord preparation. Measure conduction velocity, threshold, the response to a held step indentation and the response to skin stretch. An SA2-like class should fire to innocuous force, keep firing during a held stimulus and respond to stretch. A label is a handle, not a class, until this step confirms it.

Find the ending. Image whole mounts of labelled skin, and confirm the ending of recorded neurons afterwards, to see whether the endings are Ruffini-like or something else.

Test what the class does. With genetic access you can ablate, silence or activate the class and ask which behaviour changes, as was done for C-LTMRs and the wet dog shake. A partial loss would show that the class contributes, not that it is the only route.

Conclude, at most, that mice have a genetically defined SA2-like class with a known response and ending. The design cannot show what human SA II units end in, because microneurography does not see the ending.

## Going deeper

The details below support the main line. None of them changes the main points.

### Where the class-table numbers come from

The Li velocities come from intracellular recordings in an ex vivo skin, nerve, ganglion and spinal cord preparation from 2 to 3 month old mice, with five C-LTMRs, 19 Aδ-LTMRs from seven animals and 18 Aβ RA-LTMRs from five animals. The often-quoted 15 to 100 m/s for Aβ is a review range pooled across species; the mouse hairy-skin Aβ RA mean sits below it. The Li whole mounts were of trunk skin from 3 to 10 week old mice, and the SA1 endings were found through their Merkel cells with anti-Troma1 staining. The dorsal horn columns came from separate experiments in which cholera toxin B (CTB), a tracer taken up by axons, was injected into small patches of back skin at postnatal day 14 to 25.

### Counting follicle associations is not counting neurons

**Worked problem: associations versus neurons.** Take an idealized patch of exactly 100 mouse trunk follicles in Li's ratio: 1 guard, 23 awl/auchene, 76 zigzag. Use only the dominant map above, count each class once per follicle it innervates, and leave out field-LTMRs and the rare exceptions.

1. Aβ RA: guard plus awl/auchene = 1 + 23 = 24 associations.
2. Aβ SA1: guard only = 1.
3. Aδ-LTMR: awl/auchene plus zigzag = 23 + 76 = 99.
4. C-LTMR: awl/auchene plus zigzag = 23 + 76 = 99.
5. Total: 24 + 1 + 99 + 99 = 223.
6. Check by follicle: (1 × 2) + (23 × 3) + (76 × 2) = 2 + 69 + 152 = 223.

> **associations = sum over follicle types of (number of follicles × classes per follicle)**

Is 223 a neuron count? No. One afferent contacts many follicles; a single mouse field-LTMR arbor reached 20 to 180 follicles (Bai and colleagues, 2015). And 1:23:76 is a ratio of hairs, not of neurons. The calculation tells you how often each class has endings at a hair.

### SA II and the Ruffini question

Johansson and Vallbo (1979) recorded 334 low-threshold units from the glabrous skin of the hand through the median nerve. Their SA II class is defined by firing, so its pairing with the Ruffini ending is an inference. Three claims go too far. "Mice lack SA2" goes beyond the evidence, whether mice have a distinct SA2 class is still open, and "SA2 is the Ruffini afferent" is unproven in both species. The experiment that would settle it in mice is the one Li used for hair: genetic access to SA2-like afferents, recording to confirm the response, and reconstruction of the ending. The design problem below lays it out.

### Small human samples: palm CTs and friction

Watkins and colleagues (2021) reported three CT afferents in human glabrous hand skin, at roughly one per 80 myelinated afferents encountered. The authors used that ratio to estimate a CT density about seven times lower than in hairy skin, an estimate that rests on electrode sampling. Dione and colleagues (2021) changed surface friction with an ultrasonic device while recording from eight afferents in five women aged 21 to 28.

### Direction tuning in detail

In the ex vivo recordings, genetically identified Aδ-LTMRs fired, on average, more than three times as many spikes to caudal-to-rostral deflection (hair tip moving toward the head) as to an equal deflection the other way. A weathervane analogy helps with the anatomy: where the vane is mounted is not the direction it points. The caudal BDNF was shown by reporter histology. The mutants were K5Cre;Bdnf conditional mutants: an epithelial driver avoids the problem that a germline Bdnf knockout would affect many neurons and the animal's health. Rutlin and colleagues recorded from TrkB-GFP neurons in these mutants. A TrkB-GFP knock-in labels Aδ-LTMRs without recombining Bdnf; a tamoxifen-induced TrkB-CreER line would also excise the floxed Bdnf allele in the labelled neurons. TrkB is expressed by other neurons too, so identity should be confirmed by conduction velocity and threshold. Two questions stay open. How a caudally placed ending converts into a preference for rostral deflection is a mechanical question that this experiment does not answer. And an adult, inducible deletion would be needed to separate development from adult function; touch-evoked release would still have to be measured. The experiment says nothing directly about human hair afferents.

Compare mutants with littermate controls.

Measure two outcomes in the same line: ending polarity (fraction of Aδ endings on the caudal side of each follicle, in whole mounts) and tuning (spikes to equal rostral and caudal deflections, as a direction index, in ex vivo recordings).

Control for survival. Count labelled DRG neurons in mutants and controls; Rutlin found survival and maturation retained, so tuning that no longer lines up with the body axis is not explained by lost neurons.

**Worked problem: a direction index.** Suppose, as illustration, an Aδ-LTMR fires 12 spikes to a caudal-to-rostral deflection and 3 spikes to an equal rostral-to-caudal one.

> **direction index = (preferred − opposite) / (preferred + opposite)**

1. Ratio: 12 / 3 = 4, in the range of the paper's report that average responses to caudal-to-rostral deflection were over 3-fold larger than to the opposite direction.
2. Index: (12 − 3) / (12 + 3) = 9 / 15 = 0.60.
3. Check: here "preferred" always means caudal-to-rostral, the direction set by the body axis, so a neuron tuned the other way gets a negative index. An index of 0 means no preference along that axis; 1 means no response to the opposite direction. A ratio of 3 gives (3 − 1) / (3 + 1) = 0.50, so 0.60 is a plausible illustrative value; the paper reports an average over tested spots, not a per-neuron floor.
