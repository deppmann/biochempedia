**A rat is born with most of its muscle fibers wired to two or more motor neurons. Within two to three weeks, each fiber listens to one.**

Nothing is wrong with the extra inputs. They release acetylcholine, each evokes an endplate potential in the muscle fiber, and they work. Then they are removed. The general version fits on one line: functional synapses are eliminated. This lesson asks how anyone counted the inputs, where the lost ones go, and what decides which axon keeps the fiber. The answer to the last question turns out to be, in large part, relative activity read locally: an input that is less active than its neighbours at the same junction is more likely to go, and glial cells help clear the retreating branches. The molecules behind it are still being worked out.

## The idea: refinement removes working connections

Think of wiring a mature circuit as neural circuit refinement, a change in three quantities: the number of neurons, the number of synapses, and the strength of synapses. Earlier lessons covered the first. Neurons that fail to get enough target-derived factor die, as you saw in [competition for targets](/lessons/competition-for-targets/). This lesson is about the second and third, on neurons that stay alive.

The best place to study it is the neuromuscular junction, the synapse between a motor axon and a skeletal muscle fiber. Its assembly, with the receptor patch that agrin and its partners build, is covered in [the neuromuscular junction](/lessons/neuromuscular-junction/). Here what matters is that a typical adult mammalian muscle fiber has one junction and one axon. The postsynaptic site is called the endplate.

A useful measure is convergence, the number of axons that innervate one postsynaptic cell. In many developing circuits where it has been measured, convergence falls during a particular period, a change called refinement of convergence. At the neonatal rodent junction it falls from two or more to one. In their review of synapse elimination at the mammalian NMJ, Lichtman and Colman point out why muscle allows it: the fibers of one muscle are nearly interchangeable targets, so a motor neuron can first diverge onto many equally suitable fibers and then sort out which it keeps. The diffuse, redundant early wiring becomes a specific one.

## Counting inputs and counting neurons

Anatomists had described several axons on one young endplate long before, starting with Tello, a student of Cajal. But in a simple stain of a junction with several terminals tangled together, it is hard to tell the axons apart or count them. The functional count was electrical. In 1970 P. A. Redfern recorded from fibers of newborn rat diaphragm with an electrode inside the cell. He increased the strength of the stimulus to the phrenic nerve in small steps. The endplate potential grew in discrete jumps, each with its own threshold and latency, because each jump was another axon reaching threshold. This method is called stepwise recruitment. Most responses at birth were complex, built from two to four steps. By 16 to 18 days they were single steps.

That count left an obvious question. Were the extra axons lost because their motor neurons died? Michael Brown, Jan Jansen and David Van Essen answered it in rat soleus muscle in 1976. Fibers lost their extra inputs by the end of the second week, and the force produced by each motor unit (one motor neuron and all the fibers it supplies) fell sharply as a share of the muscle's twitch. Yet the number of motor neurons supplying the soleus did not change significantly. The only way both are true is that each surviving neuron withdrew some of its terminals. Synapse elimination is branch loss, the subject of [axon pruning](/lessons/axon-pruning/), not neuron death.

Fluorescent proteins let later work see the branches. In 2001 Cynthia Keller-Peck, Jeff Lichtman and colleagues used transgenic mice in which only a small, random subset of motor axons glowed, and traced every branch of single axons. Each axon was losing branches, and its branches withdrew at different times, at different junctions, without an obvious spatial pattern. If the neuron were running one program for its whole arbor, the branches would go together. They did not, which points to a separate contest at every junction.

### Watching the contest

In 2003 Mark Walsh and Lichtman watched one. They used mice in which two motor axons carried different colours of fluorescent protein, labelled the receptors with fluorescent alpha-bungarotoxin, and imaged the same living junction again and again over days.

Typically, one axon withdrew from the receptor sites while the other extended into the sites it left, so the movie looks like a takeover. In rare cases the winner did not move into a vacated site, and the receptors there quickly disappeared. Two findings were surprising. The relative areas shifted in favour of one axon and then the other, so competition was not always a steady retreat. And the outcome could not be reliably predicted from how much territory each axon held at the start.

## Activity biases the contest

If size does not decide, what does? Experiments in neonatal rat muscle gave the first clue. Reducing activity, for example by blocking nerve impulses with tetrodotoxin, slowed elimination. Chronic electrical stimulation sped elimination up, although some patterns of stimulation did so and others did not. So activity drives the process. But all of these experiments changed activity everywhere at once, and they could not say whether inactivity marks the loser or whether the loser is the input that is less active than its neighbour.

Brown, Jansen and Van Essen had already found a hint that the contest is local: in their experiments with a foreign nerve implanted into a muscle, endplates on one fiber that sat more than about 1 mm apart could both persist. Elimination seemed to act only between synapses close together.

Mu-ming Poo's group then reduced the contest to three cells. In 1991 Yi-Jun Lo and Poo made Xenopus cultures in which one embryonic muscle cell was innervated by two spinal neurons. A brief burst of stimulation to one neuron immediately suppressed the synapse made by the other. When both neurons were stimulated together, the suppression was largely absent. This is heterosynaptic depression: activity at one synapse weakens an inactive neighbour on the same cell.

In living mice, blocking the receptors on only part of an endplate led to loss of that patch, whereas blocking the whole endplate did not (Rita Balice-Gordon and Lichtman, 1994), so active sites can destabilize inactive neighbours within one junction. The activity rule was not always accepted: an experiment in neonatal rabbit soleus, in which a few motor neurons were silenced, found the reverse, but a genetic test in mice later favoured it, with the more powerful of two inputs to the same fibers strongly favoured. Relative activity biases the contest. It is not the only factor: firing patterns, muscle fiber type and the terminal Schwann cells also matter. Terminal Schwann cells engulf the tips of retreating axon branches. Whether they take part in choosing the winner is not known.

## How the contest is carried out: candidate signals

Several candidate signals have good evidence, from culture and from developing rodents, but how they fit together is unclear. One summary ties them together: when the winning axon's transmitter activates the muscle, the muscle generates two kinds of signal, one that punishes nearby inactive sites and one that protects the active site. Two candidates fit this picture. In compartmented nerve-muscle cultures, protein kinase C (PKC) mediated heterosynaptic loss of the less active input and protein kinase A (PKA) stabilized the stimulated input, both through the number of acetylcholine receptors at the synapse. And the neurotrophin BDNF: in Xenopus nerve-muscle cultures, proBDNF acting through p75 promoted retraction of the less active terminal, while mature BDNF acting through TrkB stabilized the active one. Mice lacking BDNF or p75, however, eliminate synapses on a normal schedule, so whether this pathway is required is still open. This is a working model. What remains unknown is how these signals are confined to one patch, and how they relate to the cytoskeletal steps inside a retreating branch and to the Schwann cells that engulf its tips.

## A similar contest in the brain

The neuromuscular junction is not unusual. In the rodent cerebellum, each Purkinje cell is innervated by several climbing fibers, the axons from the inferior olive, in the first postnatal week, and by about the end of the third week most have one. Recordings of stepwise climbing-fiber responses, the same counting method Redfern used, track the change, and that literature, from groups such as Masanobu Kano's, is separate from the muscle work. The detailed mechanisms differ, but glia take part in both places. At the muscle these are the terminal Schwann cells described above. In the brain, astrocytes and microglia remove synapses, as [microglia and complement](/lessons/microglia-complement-pruning/) describes. The logic of starting with several working inputs and removing some of them recurs in many developing circuits, though the number kept varies: a Purkinje cell usually keeps one climbing fiber, while a thalamic relay neuron keeps a few retinal inputs.

### Design problem

You can apply alpha-bungarotoxin, which binds acetylcholine receptors and blocks them, to part of one mouse endplate or to the whole endplate, and then image the junction over weeks. Predict what happens to the receptors and to the overlying nerve terminal in each case, and say what each result would tell you about the rule.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Write down the two rules you are trying to separate. Rule one: a silent synapse is removed. Rule two: a synapse is removed when it is less active than synapses near it.

Rule one predicts loss under both treatments. Rule two predicts loss only under partial block, because only then is a silent patch next to active patches. Under full block, every site is equally silent and nothing is less active than its neighbour.

Rita Balice-Gordon and Lichtman did this in living mice and reported the result in 1994. Focal blockade caused long-lasting synapse elimination at the blocked site: receptors were lost there and the terminal withdrew from that patch. Blockade of the whole junction did not cause elimination. Rule two wins. Active sites can destabilize inactive synapses nearby, over a short distance within one junction. The signal itself was not identified.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The imaging preparation

Walsh and Lichtman imaged the junction in the sternomastoid muscle of an anaesthetised pup, let it recover, and found the junction again days later.

### Kinases in the muscle

Phillip Nelson's laboratory at the NIH built compartmented cultures in which two groups of neurons innervated the same myotubes. Stimulating one group weakened the other's input, and that heterosynaptic loss depended on protein kinase C (PKC), the kinase family whose conventional members are switched on by diacylglycerol and calcium, which you met in [the PLC lesson](/lessons/plc-ip3-dag-pkc/). An activity-dependent action of protein kinase A (PKA), the cAMP-driven kinase of [cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/), stabilized the stimulated input. Both worked through the number of acetylcholine receptors at the synapse. In rat muscle in vivo, other work has linked PKC activity to the normal pace of elimination.

### A neurotrophin with two faces

BDNF is made as a pro-form, proBDNF, which can be cut to mature BDNF. As [Trk and p75](/lessons/trk-and-p75/) explains, the pro-form signals mainly through p75, with its partner sortilin, and the mature form mainly through TrkB. Bai Lu's group, working at the NIH, found in Xenopus nerve-muscle cultures that muscle stimulation releases proBDNF, which depresses synapses and then causes the presynaptic terminal to retract, through p75 on the nerve. Cutting proBDNF to mature BDNF at the synapse turned the same molecule into a signal that potentiates. With two neurons on one muscle cell, proBDNF and p75 promoted retraction of the less active terminal, while mature BDNF and TrkB stabilized the active one. In developing mouse junctions, adding proBDNF promoted elimination, infusing mature BDNF delayed it, and blocking the cutting step sped it up through p75. The group called the two forms "punishment" and "reward" signals. Mice lacking BDNF or p75, however, eliminate synapses on a normal schedule, so whether the pathway is required is still open.

Heterosynaptic depression itself, the effect described in the Xenopus co-cultures, is reported in culture to need a rise of calcium in the postsynaptic cell, whichever signal carries it.

### A rabbit result that went the other way

In 1987 Callaway, Soha and Van Essen silenced a small fraction of the motor neurons to neonatal rabbit soleus for four days and found that the inactive neurons had a competitive advantage, the reverse of the prediction. In 2003 Buffelli, Sanes, Lichtman and colleagues blocked transmitter release from a subset of motor axons, so that one of two inputs to the same fibers was weaker, and the more powerful inputs were strongly favoured.
