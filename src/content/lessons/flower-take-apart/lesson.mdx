## The big idea

In 30 minutes at the kitchen table, your child can discover where seeds begin. Take a flower apart ring by ring, then use coloured sugar to see how a bee moves pollen. Your child practises looking closely, sorting the pieces and checking a guess.

> **Say it together:** "A flower is the part of a plant that makes seeds. Its powdery dust is pollen, carried by wind or animals. After pollen arrives, seeds can start to grow in the very middle."

Each ring has a job. The outer ring protects the bud. Petals often draw in animals with colour and scent. Thin stalks make **pollen**, a fine powder that must reach the central part before seeds can form. Wind, water or animals move pollen from flower to flower—a step called **pollination**. Afterwards, seeds grow inside the central part, and the part around them swells into a **fruit**.

<SafetyNote>
- **Supervision, cutting, glass and choking:** Stay with your child the whole time. You do all cutting with scissors or a craft knife, and put them away afterwards. Hold any glass hand lens yourself. Never point the hand lens at the sun. Keep cotton swabs, pipe cleaners, sugar, small flower bits and any loose peas, beans or seeds away from children under 4. Keep them out of mouths, noses and ears. **Call 911 if choking or struggling to breathe.**
- **Never taste and wash hands:** Never taste flowers, leaves, pods, seeds or sugar. Shop flowers are often sprayed on the farm, and raw green beans are not for eating. Keep flowers and fingers out of mouths, and wash hands with soap and water when finished and before eating. If a child swallows plant parts, call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222 (free, any hour).
- **Pets:** True lilies (Easter, stargazer, tiger, Asiatic) and daylilies can kill a cat—even a bite, pollen licked off fur, or vase water is enough. Do not bring them in if a cat lives or visits. Skip tulips with a cat or dog, as they can upset a pet’s stomach. If a cat may have eaten any, call your vet or ASPCA Poison Control, (888) 426-4435, right away.

<details>
<summary>More safety: allergies, food dyes and garden flowers</summary>

- **Allergies and dyes:** Pollen can bother children with hay fever or asthma, so the pretend pollen is plain coloured sugar instead of dusty chalk. Check the sugar label for food or dye allergies. Work on a tray, use a small spoonful, do not blow on it, throw it away afterwards (not for eating), and skip the bee game if anyone sneezes or wheezes. Tulips and alstroemeria can make some skin itch or redden; wash and stop handling if that happens.
- **Toxic plants and garden picking:** Foxglove, oleander and lily of the valley are poisonous to people and pets. If you use a garden flower, pick it yourself while staying with your child. Pick only in your own yard or with permission, away from roads. Stay out of strong sun, skip picking in bad weather, and check for ticks afterwards. Skip sprayed flowers. Check for insects before picking and leave them where you find them. Never handle insects that can sting or bite.

</details>
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Look first and guess.** Give your child a whole flower. Let them use a plastic hand lens or hold a glass lens for them. Have them draw it and count the petals. Ask, "What do you think is hiding in the very middle?"
2. **Peel the outer ring.** Peel one outer piece together so your child sees how gently to pull near the base, then let them finish the ring. These are **sepals**, which wrapped the bud. In many flowers, the sepals are small and green. In alstroemeria and tulips, the two outer rings look like petals. Botanists call both sets tepals. Pull off the outer three pieces, just outside the inner three. Tape them in an outer ring.
3. **Peel the petals.** Pull off the petals and tape them inside the first ring. In a petunia the petals join into one trumpet, so you slit it open and tape it flat. Ask, "Why might a flower be this colour? Who is it showing off for?"
4. **Find the pollen.** Your child will notice thin stalks with knobs on top. Touch a knob to a fingertip or dark paper. Ask, "What rubbed off?" That fine powder is pollen. Tape the stalks in a smaller ring.
5. **Predict, then open the middle.** One part remains in the centre. Before cutting, ask, "What will we find inside this thick base? Where do seeds grow?" You slice across the swollen base and tape the cut pieces in the centre. In alstroemeria, the base is the green knob just under the petals. Use the hand lens to look for tiny pale beads. Say, "Pollen lands on the tip of the middle part. The tiny beads inside the base can become seeds."
6. **Set up pretend flowers.** On a tray, you put a small spoonful of each sugar colour in a separate cupcake liner, leaving the third empty. Throw the sugar away afterwards (not for eating). Say, "These liners are flowers, and the sugar is pollen."
7. **Predict, then be a bee.** Your child is the bee, and the swab or folded pipe cleaner is a hairy bee body. Ask, "What will the empty flower look like after the bee visits?" Dab the first liner, the second, then the third. Check each liner with the lens. Look for specks that moved from one liner to the next, including both colours in the last liner.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder how many petals this flower has. Will the next one have the same number?"
- "What do you think the green parts on the outside were for, back when this was a bud?"
- "I wonder what this dust is for. What would happen if it never moved?"
- "What do you think will happen to the empty flower when the bee visits?"
- "If a flower were plain green with no smell, how might its pollen travel?"
- "Where do you think the seeds in an apple or a bean pod came from?"

### Two-question wrap-up

1. "Point to your sheet: where do seeds start to grow?" *(The middle part, where they saw the tiny pale beads.)*
2. "How does pollen travel between flowers?" *(Bees, butterflies, birds or wind carry it, like the swab carried sugar.)*

Read the "Say it together" lines again as your child points to each ring.

## Why it works

A typical flower is a short stem with rings of changed leaves. From the outside in, they are sepals, petals, **stamens** (the stalks that make pollen) and the **pistil** (the central part). The base of the pistil, the ovary, holds the ovules—the tiny beads your child saw. When a pollen grain lands on the sticky tip of the pistil, it grows a thin tube down to an ovule and delivers two sperm cells. One joins the egg and starts the baby plant. The other helps make the seed’s food store. The fertilized ovule becomes a seed, and the ovary becomes the fruit.

Flowers that use animals often have bright colours, scent and nectar, and sticky pollen. Flowers that use wind tend to be small and plain, and make large amounts of light pollen. Grass and ragweed are examples of plants that use wind to move their pollen. The swab demo shows the idea, not the details: real pollen sticks to fine hairs and must land on a flower of the same kind.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use one large flower and skip the technical names. Call the rings "outside covers," "petals," "pollen sticks" and "the middle." Your child can count and sort pieces by colour, then play bee with just two liners. Let them draw the taped flower instead of writing, and colour each ring to match the real parts.

### For older children (ages 8 to 10)
Take apart two different flowers and compare counts in a table. Many flowers have parts in threes or in fives. Add the names sepal, petal, stamen and pistil. Then compare a wind flower (grass heads or corn tassels) with an animal flower. Your child lists colour, scent, size and amount of pollen for each, and writes how each one moves its pollen.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

Florists sometimes pull the pollen knobs off lilies and other flowers to keep them from staining. If you see no pollen, try a flower from a garden or a fresh bunch. If the middle part is too small to slice, look at the base with a hand lens, or open a pea pod or green bean to show where seeds sit inside a fruit (raw green beans are not for eating).
