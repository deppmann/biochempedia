## The big idea

Most children see a flower as something pretty. Few have looked inside one. When your child pulls a flower apart, ring by ring, the pieces turn out to have different jobs.

A flower is the part of a plant that makes seeds. The outer ring protects the bud. The petals often draw in animals with colour and scent. Thin stalks in the middle make **pollen**, a fine powder that has to reach the central part of a flower before seeds can form. Wind, water or animals move pollen from flower to flower. This step is called **pollination**. Afterwards, seeds grow inside the central part, and the part around them swells into a **fruit**.

Virginia’s grade 4 standard asks children to describe how pollen moves and how colour and scent attract animals. It says they are not responsible for naming the male and female parts of a flower. The names stamen and pistil are an extra for older children who enjoy them.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time. You do all the cutting with scissors or a craft knife, and put them away afterwards. Keep cotton swabs, sugar and small flower bits away from children under 4, and out of mouths, noses and ears. Never point the hand lens at the sun.
- True lilies (Easter, stargazer, tiger, Asiatic) and daylilies can kill a cat. A small bite, pollen licked off fur, or vase water is enough. If a cat lives in or visits your home, do not bring these flowers in at all. If a cat may have eaten any, call your vet or ASPCA Poison Control, (888) 426-4435, right away.
- Never taste flowers, leaves, pods or seeds. Foxglove, oleander and lily of the valley are poisonous to people and pets. Tulips can upset a pet’s stomach. If a child is choking or struggling to breathe, call 911. If a child swallows plant parts, call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222 (free, any hour).
- Pollen can bother children with hay fever or asthma, and dusty chalk can too, so the pretend pollen is plain coloured sugar. Work on a tray, use only a small spoonful, don’t blow on it, throw it away afterwards (it is not for eating), and skip the bee game if anyone starts sneezing or wheezing.
- If you pick a garden flower, you pick it, look it over for bees and other insects, and skip any that were sprayed with chemicals.
- Wash hands with soap and water when you finish, and before eating. Tulips and alstroemeria can make some people’s skin itch or turn red; if that happens, wash and stop handling them.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Look first.** Give your child a whole flower and a hand lens. Ask them to draw it and count the petals. Ask, "What do you think is in the very middle?"
2. **Peel the outer ring.** Gently pull off the outermost parts, the **sepals**, which wrapped the flower when it was a bud. In many flowers they are small and green. In alstroemeria and tulips they look like a second set of petals. Tape them in a ring near the edge of the paper.
3. **Peel the petals.** Pull off the petals and tape them in a ring inside the first one. Ask, "Why might a flower be this colour? Who is it showing off for?"
4. **Find the pollen.** Inside are thin stalks with knobs on top. Touch a knob to a fingertip or dark paper and look for powder. That is pollen. Tape the stalks in a smaller ring.
5. **Open the middle.** One part is left in the centre. Now ask, "Where do you think seeds will grow?" You slice across the swollen base of that part, then tape the pieces in the middle of the paper. In alstroemeria, this is the green knob just under the petals. With the hand lens, look for tiny pale beads. Each one could become a seed if pollen reaches it.
6. **Make the pollen.** You put a small spoonful of each colour of sugar in a separate cupcake liner. Leave the third liner empty. Throw the sugar away afterwards, because it is not for eating. These are three "flowers."
7. **Be a bee.** Your child is the bee and the cotton swab or pipe cleaner is a hairy bee body. Before starting, ask, "What will the empty flower look like after the bee visits?" Dab the swab in the first flower, then the second, then the third. Look at each one with the hand lens. Colour moves from flower to flower, and the last one ends up with both.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder how many petals this flower has. Will the next one have the same number?"
- "What do you think the green parts on the outside were for, back when this was a bud?"
- "I wonder what this dust is for. What would happen if it never moved?"
- "What do you think will happen to the empty flower when the bee visits?"
- "If a flower were plain green with no smell, how might its pollen travel?"
- "Where do you think the seeds in an apple or a bean pod came from?"

## Why it works

A typical flower is a short stem with rings of changed leaves. From the outside in, they are sepals, petals, **stamens** (the stalks that make pollen) and the **pistil** (the central part). The base of the pistil, the ovary, holds the ovules, the tiny beads your child saw. When a pollen grain lands on the sticky tip of the pistil, it grows a thin tube down to an ovule and delivers a sperm cell. The fertilized ovule becomes a seed, and the ovary becomes the fruit.

Flowers that use animals often have bright colours, scent and nectar, and their pollen is sticky. Flowers that use wind tend to be small and plain, and make large amounts of light pollen. That is why grass and ragweed bother people with hay fever more than roses do. The swab demo shows the idea, not the details: real pollen sticks to fine hairs and must land on a flower of the same kind.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use one large flower and skip the names. Call the rings "outside covers," "petals," "pollen sticks" and "the middle." Your child can count and sort pieces by colour, then play bee with just two liners. Let them draw the taped flower instead of writing, and colour each ring to match the real parts.

### Ages 8 to 10
Take apart two different flowers and compare counts in a table. Many flowers have parts in threes or in fives. Add the names sepal, petal, stamen and pistil. Then compare a wind flower (grass heads or corn tassels) with an animal flower. Your child lists colour, scent, size and amount of pollen for each and writes which way each one moves its pollen.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

Florists sometimes pull the pollen knobs off lilies and other flowers to keep them from staining. If you see no pollen, try a flower from a garden or a fresh bunch. If the middle part is too small to slice, use a hand lens on the base, or open a pea pod or a green bean to show where seeds sit inside a fruit (raw green beans are not for eating).
