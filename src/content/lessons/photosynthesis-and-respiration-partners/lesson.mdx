## Explain: two processes in one leaf cell

<WordBank terms={['photosynthesis', 'cellular respiration', 'chloroplast', 'mitochondria', 'reactant', 'product', 'interdependent', 'dissolved oxygen']} />

You met both processes in [Photosynthesis Feeds the World](/lessons/photosynthesis-feeds-the-world/) and [Releasing Energy from Food](/lessons/releasing-energy-from-food/). Now put them in the same cell.

**Photosynthesis** uses radiant energy to make sugar (glucose) and oxygen from carbon dioxide and water. It happens in the **chloroplast**, a green part of the cell that takes in light.

> carbon dioxide + water + radiant energy → sugar (glucose) + oxygen

**Cellular respiration** joins sugar with oxygen and releases energy the cell can use. Most of it happens in the **mitochondria**, small parts found in almost all plant and animal cells.

> sugar (glucose) + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy for the cell

A green leaf cell has both organelles. An animal cell has mitochondria but no chloroplasts. Not every plant cell is green, though. Root cells usually have no chloroplasts. They get sugar sent down from the leaves.

| | Photosynthesis | Cellular respiration |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Chloroplast | Mostly in mitochondria |
| Goes in | Carbon dioxide, water, radiant energy | Sugar (glucose), oxygen |
| Comes out | Sugar (glucose), oxygen | Carbon dioxide, water, usable energy |
| When | Only in light | Day and night |

## Explain: what one makes, the other uses

A **reactant** is a substance that goes into a chemical process. A **product** is a substance that comes out. Look at the two word equations again. The products of photosynthesis, sugar and oxygen, are the reactants of respiration. The products of respiration, carbon dioxide and water, are the reactants of photosynthesis. Processes that depend on each other like this are **interdependent**. Each one needs the other.

Matter and energy move differently through this pair:
- **Matter is used again.** The same carbon, oxygen and hydrogen go around and around.
- **Energy flows one way.** Light from the sun becomes chemical energy in sugar. Respiration releases that energy for life processes. Some of it leaves as heat. Energy is never made or used up. It changes form.

Producers, like the Bay’s algae and underwater grasses, make the food and oxygen that almost every food web needs.

Each process also has things that change its speed:
- Photosynthesis: light, carbon dioxide and temperature.
- Respiration: temperature, oxygen and sugar (glucose).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** The products of photosynthesis are the reactants of respiration, and the other way around, so matter cycles while energy flows through.</Support>

## Explain: why creek oxygen rises and falls

**Dissolved oxygen** is oxygen gas mixed into water. Fish, crabs, plants and microbes can use it there. Scientists measure it in milligrams per liter (mg/L).

In daylight, algae and underwater grasses photosynthesize and add oxygen to the water. At the same time, nearly every living thing in the creek respires and takes oxygen out. On a bright day, photosynthesis often adds oxygen faster than respiration takes it. The level climbs. After dark, photosynthesis stops, but respiration does not. The level tends to fall through the night.

Think of a bathtub with the tap running and the drain open. Sunlight turns the tap on. The drain never closes. The comparison breaks because a real creek is open. Tides bring in new water. Air at the surface can add oxygen, or take some away when the water holds extra. Warm water holds less oxygen than cold water.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/photosynthesis-and-respiration-partners/bathtub-tap-and-drain.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two bathtubs side by side. In the night tub the tap is off, the drain is still open, and the water is lower." caption="Sunlight turns the tap on, and the drain never closes. When oxygen drops very low over a large area, many animals can die. You will study these dead zones in [Big Changes](/lessons/eutrophication-and-big-changes/).

### Design problem

Plan an experiment to test whether a water plant uses oxygen in the dark and adds it in the light. You can buy water plants and a dissolved oxygen probe for school use.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does light change whether a water plant adds or removes dissolved oxygen?
- **Independent variable:** light or dark (a lamp or a closed cabinet).
- **Dependent variable:** change in dissolved oxygen over 4 hours, in mg/L.
- **Constants:** same jar size, 500 mL of the same water, about 2 grams of plant per jar, same water temperature (check it with a thermometer, and use a lamp that does not heat the jars).
- **Control:** jars with water and no plant, in light and in dark. They show how much oxygen changes without the plant.
- **Trials:** three jars of each kind. Report the mean change.
- **Safety:** keep lamps and cords away from water, wash your hands after, and never release store-bought plants into a creek or pond.

If oxygen rises in the lit jars and falls in the dark jars, while the plain water barely changes, the plant added oxygen in light and used it in darkness.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

Taskinas Creek is a messy example, and that is worth knowing. In the brightest hours, from 9 a.m. to 3 p.m., the oxygen at the sensor barely rose. Then, soon after the tide turned and began to flow in, oxygen jumped from 4.7 to 9.5 mg/L in 15 minutes. Photosynthesis cannot add that much that fast in one spot. The tide carried in water that already held extra oxygen, most likely made by algae photosynthesizing in sunlit water somewhere else. After dark, nearly every living thing in the creek, plants included, kept using oxygen for respiration, with no photosynthesis to replace it, and some of the extra oxygen escaped to the air. So photosynthesis and respiration explain where the oxygen comes from and where it goes, but in this creek the tide decides when it reaches the sensor. That is why the real record is bumpier than the tank.

**Science practices you used:** making and testing predictions with a model, reading a real data table, finding a pattern and other possible causes, and making a claim backed by evidence and reasoning.
