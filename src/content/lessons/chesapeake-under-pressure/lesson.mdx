## Explain: how extra nutrients use up oxygen

<WordBank terms={['watershed', 'runoff', 'nutrient', 'eutrophication', 'dissolved oxygen', 'decomposer', 'hypoxia', 'riparian forest buffer', 'submerged aquatic vegetation', 'restoration', 'criteria', 'constraints']} />

A **watershed** is all the land that drains into one river, lake or bay. The Bay’s watershed covers parts of six states and Washington, D.C. In Virginia, the Potomac (fed by the Shenandoah), Rappahannock, York and James drain to the Bay.

**Runoff** is rainwater that flows over the ground instead of soaking in, carrying what it picks up. A **nutrient** is a substance living things need to grow; here, that means nitrogen and phosphorus. **Dissolved oxygen** is oxygen gas mixed into water, measured in milligrams per liter (mg/L). Fish, crabs and oysters take it in through their gills.

Here is the chain, step by step:

1. Runoff, wastewater and air pollution carry extra nutrients into the Bay.
2. Algae grow fast. A sudden, thick growth of algae is an **algal bloom**.
3. The green water blocks sunlight from underwater grasses.
4. The algae die and sink to the bottom.
5. A **decomposer** is a living thing that breaks down dead matter. Bacteria decompose the dead algae and use oxygen as they do.
6. Bottom water runs low on oxygen, a state called **hypoxia**. Animals that can swim away leave; others may sicken or die.

**Eutrophication** is the enrichment of water with nutrients, which feeds too much algae growth and can lead to oxygen loss.

> extra nutrients → algal bloom → algae die and sink → bacteria decompose them and use oxygen → low-oxygen bottom water

The fertilizer never takes oxygen itself; living things use it.

Why the deep channel, and why summer? Oxygen enters water from the air and from photosynthesis in sunlit water. In summer, fresh river water floats on saltier, heavier seawater, and the layers mix poorly, so little new oxygen reaches the bottom. Warm water also holds less dissolved oxygen. Shallow, well-stirred water rarely runs as low.

Picture the bottom water as a closed room full of breathers. The analogy breaks because storms can open the door: they mix the layers and bring new oxygen down.

## Explain: people and nature both change ecosystems

An ecosystem’s capacity to support life depends on habitat, light, oxygen and food. People change these on land, in the ocean and in the air, and so do natural events. Large disruptions include eutrophication, climate shifts and catastrophes.

| Change | Cause | One effect |
|---|---|---|
| Clearing forest for farms, roads and houses | People | More runoff and soil reach streams |
| Intensive farming with fertilizer and manure | People | Extra nutrients, some stored in soil and leaking out years later |
| Releasing chemicals in wastewater and from industry | People | Nutrients; some contaminants can harm wildlife reproduction |
| Burning fuel in cars and power plants | People | Nitrogen falls from the air onto land and water |
| Wildfire | Nature, sometimes people | Storms afterward wash ash and soil into streams |
| Drought or flood | Nature | Changes how much fresh water and nutrient reach an estuary |
| Earthquake | Nature | The 2002 Denali Fault earthquake in Alaska sloshed a Nevada desert pool, disturbing spawning areas of the Devils Hole pupfish |
| Climate shift (warming) | Nature and people | Warmer water holds less oxygen |

In one computer-model study of the Bay, warming cancelled roughly 6 to 34 percent of the oxygen improvement from cutting nitrogen.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** human activities and natural events both change habitat, light and oxygen, which can lower how much life an ecosystem supports.</Support>

## Explain: fixing a watershed by design

Human activity is not always harmful. **Restoration** is returning a damaged ecosystem toward health.

In 2010, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency set a limit on the nitrogen, phosphorus and sediment entering the Bay, the Chesapeake Bay TMDL (total maximum daily load). Each state wrote a Watershed Implementation Plan for its share. Tools include:

- A **riparian forest buffer** is a strip of trees and shrubs along a stream. It catches runoff before it reaches the water, and roots hold the bank soil in place.
- Cover crops, planted on fields in winter, hold soil and take up leftover nitrogen.
- Wastewater plant upgrades remove more nitrogen. A 2022 study linked large drops in city nutrient pollution to them, and found that nitrogen falling from the air declined across the watershed from 1985 to 2019.
- Oyster reefs: Virginia has rebuilt reefs in the Lafayette, Lynnhaven and Piankatank rivers. Oysters filter particles from water and reefs shelter other animals, but reefs do not replace cutting nutrients.

**Submerged aquatic vegetation** is underwater grass, usually rooted in shallow bottoms. It shelters young crabs and needs light. A 2018 study found that as nitrogen in Bay water fell, underwater grass regained about 17,000 hectares (roughly 42,000 acres).

<LessonFigure src="/figures/chesapeake-under-pressure/watershed-fixes.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a winter field covered with low green cover crops slopes to a stream lined by a strip of trees and shrubs whose roots grip the bank. An inset shows a shallow Bay bottom with an oyster reef and a bed of underwater grass where a young blue crab hides." caption="Three fixes you can see on the ground: cover crops and a forest buffer hold soil and nutrients on land, and oyster reefs and underwater grass give animals shelter, though reefs do not replace cutting nutrients." />

Fixing a watershed is an engineering problem. **Criteria** are what a solution must achieve, such as cutting a set amount of nitrogen. **Constraints** are the limits it must work within, such as money, land and time. Nitrogen stored in soil can keep leaking out for years, so you build, measure, compare with a place that got no fix, and refine.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** cutting nutrients and rebuilding habitat can help an ecosystem recover, and good fixes meet their criteria within real constraints.</Support>

### Design problem

Does a strip of plants between a field and a stream reduce the nitrogen in runoff? Plan a fair test with trays of soil, and say how you would refine it if the result is unclear.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Set up six identical trays of the same soil, tilted to the same angle. Three get a strip of grass sod at the low end, the buffer. Three stay bare, the control.

- Independent variable: buffer strip or no buffer strip.
- Dependent variables: nitrate in the runoff (mg/L, from aquarium test strips) and runoff volume. Together they show how much nitrate left each tray.
- Constants: soil, slope, the amount of dilute plant fertilizer on each tray, the water poured and the waiting time.
- Repeated trials: three trays of each kind, repeated on a second day. Compare means.
- Safety: wear gloves and goggles, never taste the water, wash your hands, and dispose of runoff as your teacher directs.

Lower mean nitrate and runoff from buffer trays on both days supports the buffer. If results overlap, refine: widen the strip, add trays, or try another plant. Trays are a model, not a stream, and a real buffer has a constraint: the farmland it takes.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The summer dead zone starts with nutrients, not a chemical that grabs oxygen. Nutrients from farms, lawns, wastewater and air feed algae; the algae die and sink, and bacteria use up oxygen decomposing them, while layered summer water keeps new oxygen from the bottom. People add many of those nutrients, so people can cut them, and Virginia’s buffers, upgraded plants, reefs and returning grass show that recovery is possible, though slow.
