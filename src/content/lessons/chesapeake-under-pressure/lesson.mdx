## From a watershed to bottom water

<WordBank terms={['watershed', 'runoff', 'nutrient', 'eutrophication', 'dissolved oxygen', 'decomposer', 'hypoxia', 'riparian forest buffer', 'submerged aquatic vegetation', 'restoration', 'criteria', 'constraints']} />

The first film followed nutrients from a field to bacteria on the Bay floor. Its terms:

- A **watershed** is all the land that drains into one river, lake or bay. The Bay’s covers parts of six states and Washington, D.C. In Virginia, the Potomac, fed by the Shenandoah, and the Rappahannock, York and James drain to the Bay.
- **Runoff** is rainwater that flows over the ground instead of soaking in, carrying what it picks up. A **nutrient** is a substance living things need to grow; here, nitrogen and phosphorus.
- **Dissolved oxygen** is oxygen gas mixed into water, measured in milligrams per liter (mg/L). Fish, crabs and oysters take it in through their gills. Water too low in oxygen for most animals, usually below 2 mg/L, is **hypoxia**; in the Bay it forms mostly in bottom water.
- An **algal bloom** is a sudden, thick growth of algae. A **decomposer** is a living thing that breaks down dead matter.
- **Eutrophication** is the enrichment of water with nutrients, which feeds too much algae growth and can lead to oxygen loss.

> extra nutrients → algal bloom → algae die and sink → bacteria decompose them and use oxygen → low-oxygen bottom water

Animals that can swim away leave; others may sicken or die. Because the chain takes months, scientists forecast each summer’s dead zone from the nutrients rivers carried in spring.

## People and nature both change ecosystems

An ecosystem’s capacity to support life depends on habitat, light, oxygen and food. Large disruptions include eutrophication, climate shifts and catastrophes.

| Change | Cause | One effect |
|---|---|---|
| Clearing forest for farms, roads and houses | People | More runoff and soil reach streams |
| Intensive farming with fertilizer and manure | People | Extra nutrients, some stored in soil and leaking out years later |
| Releasing chemicals in wastewater and from industry | People | Nutrients; some contaminants can harm wildlife reproduction |
| Burning fuel in cars and power plants, and ammonia gas from manure and fertilizer | People | Nitrogen falls from the air onto land and water |
| Wildfire | Nature, sometimes people | Storms afterward wash ash and soil into streams |
| Drought or flood | Nature | Changes how much fresh water and nutrient reach an estuary |
| Climate shift (warming) | Mostly people today; nature in the past | Warmer water holds less oxygen and speeds oxygen use |

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** human activities and natural events both change habitat, light and oxygen, which can lower how much life an ecosystem supports.</Support>

## Fixing a watershed by design

Human activity is not always harmful. In the second film’s terms, **restoration** is returning a damaged ecosystem toward health. **Criteria** are what a solution must achieve, such as cutting a set amount of nitrogen. **Constraints** are limits such as money, land and time. What the film left out:

- A **riparian forest buffer** also holds the bank with its roots. Its plants and soil microbes remove nitrate from water seeping through the ground. How much nitrogen it stops depends on its width, soil and water flow.
- Cover crops are planted after the fall harvest and grow through winter.
- A 2022 study linked large drops in city nutrient pollution to wastewater plant upgrades. It also found that nitrogen falling from the air declined across the watershed from 1985 to 2019.
- Virginia has rebuilt oyster reefs in the Lafayette, Lynnhaven and Piankatank rivers.

The habitat these tools aim to bring back includes **submerged aquatic vegetation**, underwater grass usually rooted in shallow bottoms. It shelters young crabs and needs light. A 2018 study found that as nitrogen in Bay water fell, underwater grass regained about 17,000 hectares, roughly 42,000 acres.

### Design problem: test a buffer

Does a strip of plants between a field and a stream reduce the nitrogen in runoff? Plan a fair test with trays of soil, and say how you would refine it if the result is unclear.

**Predict.** Buffer and control runoff have the same nitrate concentration, but the buffer tray makes less runoff. Did the buffer reduce the nitrate leaving the tray?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes. The nitrate leaving equals concentration times runoff volume, so the same mg/L in less water means less nitrate left the tray.

</details>

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Set up six identical trays of the same soil, tilted to the same angle. Three get a strip of grass sod at the low end, the buffer. Three stay bare, the control.

- Independent variable: buffer strip or no buffer strip.
- Dependent variables: nitrate in the runoff, measured in mg/L with aquarium test strips, and runoff volume.
- Constants: soil, slope, the amount of dilute plant fertilizer containing nitrate (check the label) on each tray, the water poured and the waiting time.
- Repeated trials: three trays of each kind, repeated on a second day. Compare means.
- Safety: wear gloves and goggles, never taste the water, wash your hands, and dispose of runoff as your teacher directs.

Lower mean nitrate and runoff from buffer trays on both days supports the buffer. If results overlap, widen the strip, add trays or try another plant. Trays are a model, not a stream; they test only water flowing over the surface. A real buffer also works underground, and it has a constraint: the farmland it takes.

</details>

## Going deeper

### A law, a model and a distant disturbance

In 2010, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency set a limit on nitrogen, phosphorus and sediment entering the Bay: the Chesapeake Bay TMDL, or total maximum daily load. Each state wrote a Watershed Implementation Plan for its share.

In one computer-model study of the Bay, warming cancelled roughly 6 to 34 percent of the oxygen gain from cutting nitrogen. The story “A Bay that never happened,” under Stories & asides, tells what else that study found.

Natural disturbances can also arrive from far away. The 2002 Denali Fault earthquake in Alaska sloshed a Nevada desert pool and disturbed spawning areas of the Devils Hole pupfish.

## Check your thinking

The one idea again: extra nutrients feed algae; bacteria use oxygen while poor mixing limits replacement; effective fixes cut nutrients first, rebuild habitat, and track recovery over time. Judge them over years, because weather makes single years bounce.
