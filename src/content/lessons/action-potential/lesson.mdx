  - name: "Alan Hodgkin"
    contribution: >-
      With Andrew Huxley, recorded from inside the squid giant axon and found that the action
      potential overshoots zero, then used the voltage clamp to separate the sodium and potassium
      currents and to build the conductance model of the impulse.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Hodgkin & Huxley, Nature 144:710–711 (1939); Hodgkin & Huxley, J. Physiol. 117:500–544 (1952); Hodgkin, Nobel Lecture, 11 December 1963."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In the 1930s the leading account of the nerve impulse held that the membrane briefly lost
      its selectivity, so the voltage across it should fall toward zero and stop. The squid giant
      axon, about half a millimetre across, was wide enough to test this from the inside. With
      Andrew Huxley, Hodgkin threaded a fine electrode down the length of the axon and recorded the
      impulse directly. The inside did not stop at zero: at the peak it became positive. They
      reported the overshoot in Nature in 1939, and the war then interrupted the work. Afterwards,
      with Bernard Katz, Hodgkin showed that the overshoot depended on external sodium, which
      pointed to a membrane that becomes selectively permeable to sodium during the spike. With
      Huxley and Katz he then held the axon's voltage fixed and measured the currents, and the
      five papers that followed in the Journal of Physiology in 1952 ended with a set of equations
      that computed an action potential. Hodgkin and Huxley shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in
      Physiology or Medicine with John Eccles. The work turned excitability into currents that
      could be measured, modelled and later assigned to channel proteins.

    story: >-
      Huxley joined Hodgkin to work on squid giant axons in 1939, by his own later account at
      the Plymouth laboratory of the Marine Biological Association. After the war he and Hodgkin
      faced a practical problem: the clamp records showed how the sodium and potassium
      conductances changed at each fixed voltage, but would those changes, left free, produce an
      action potential? They fitted each conductance with variables that rise and fall with
      first-order kinetics, the m, h and n of the 1952 model, and then solved the equations
      numerically for an axon that was not clamped. Huxley did that computation with a hand-operated
      desk calculator. The calculated impulse had the right shape and refractoriness, and for
      one fibre the predicted speed, 18.8 m/s, came close to the 21.2 m/s measured. In 1949, with
      Robert Stämpfli, he had recorded single myelinated fibres from the frog and found evidence
      that the impulse is regenerated at the nodes of Ranvier. He later turned to muscle, and with
      Rolf Niedergerke published one of the two 1954 papers that proposed sliding filaments. His equations remain the template for
      conductance-based models of neurons.
  - name: "Hugh Bostock"
    contribution: >-
      With Tom Sears, recorded longitudinal action currents along experimentally demyelinated
      fibres in rat ventral roots and found that some bare internodes conduct continuously and

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      A myelinated axon fires at its nodes, so stripping the myelin from an internode was
      expected to leave the next node short of current. Earlier recordings had shown block in
      some fibres and slow, delayed jumps from node to node in others. Hugh Bostock and Tom Sears
      asked whether the bare internode itself could carry the impulse. They produced segmental demyelination in the ventral roots of rats and recorded from
      single fibres while the roots were perfused, using a method that measures the current
      flowing along the fibre at many points, so they could map where inward current, the sign of
      active membrane, appeared. In some demyelinated fibres the inward current did not stay at
      the old nodes. It moved smoothly along the bare internode, as it does along an unmyelinated
      axon, at 1.1 to 2.3 m/s. Their 1978 paper in the Journal of Physiology showed that
      internodal axon membrane could become excitable and carry continuous conduction. The result
      added a third outcome to block and delayed saltation: continuous conduction, a mechanism
      that recovery could use.

techniques:
  - name: "Voltage clamp"
    blurb: >-
      A feedback amplifier holds the membrane at a voltage you command and supplies whatever
      current that takes, which equals the current crossing the membrane. Fixing the voltage
      breaks the loop between voltage and channel opening, so you can measure how each
      conductance changes with time at one voltage.
  - name: "Ion substitution and channel blockers"
    blurb: >-
      Replacing an ion in the bathing solution (sodium with choline, for example) or adding a
      blocker such as tetrodotoxin or tetraethylammonium changes one current while you repeat
      the same clamp steps. A component that reverses or disappears, while the others stay, is
      assigned to that ion or channel.
  - name: "Two-site stimulation and longitudinal current recording"
    blurb: >-
      Stimulating a nerve at two points and dividing the distance between them by the
      difference in latency gives a conduction velocity. Recording the current that flows along a
      single fibre at many points shows where inward current appears, which separates
      conduction at nodes from continuous conduction.

    choices:
      - "Charging of the membrane capacitance as voltage changes"
      - "Potassium ions leaving before any sodium channel opens"
      - "Leak current through the bilayer at the new voltage"
      - "Sodium ions entering through the first channels to open"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Capacitive current equals Cm × dV/dt, so it flows only while the clamp is changing the
      voltage and vanishes once the voltage is steady. The sodium current develops after this
      transient, as sodium channels open with a short delay. Potassium current is the delayed component, not
      the first. Leak current flows for as long as the voltage is held, so it cannot explain a
      brief surge that dies away.
  - stem: "In the squid axon, replacing most external Na+ with choline reverses the early current of a voltage-clamp step but barely changes the delayed outward current. What does this establish?"
    choices:
      - "The early current depends on external sodium"
      - "The early current is capacitive and not ionic"
      - "The delayed current is carried by choline ions"
      - "Both currents flow through a single channel type"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Lowering external sodium moves E_Na below the clamp voltage, so the early current reverses,
      while a current that ignores the change does not depend on sodium. Choline was chosen
      because the membrane passes it poorly, so it carries neither current. Ion substitution alone does not prove
      separate channels; selective blockers such as TEA and TTX later showed that. A capacitive transient does not
      depend on which ions are outside, so its reversal could not come from changing sodium.
  - stem: "In the Hodgkin-Huxley model of the squid axon, E_Na is +50 mV, yet the action potential peaks below that. Why?"
    choices:
      - "The sodium pump removes sodium as quickly as it can enter the axon"
      - "The capacitive current cancels the sodium current at the very peak"
      - "Sodium inactivation and potassium activation begin before the peak"
      - "Sodium channels shut as soon as the inside becomes positive at all"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      At the peak the net ionic current is zero, so Vm settles where inward sodium current is
      balanced by outward potassium and leak current. As Vm climbs toward E_Na, the driving force
      on sodium shrinks, and during the rise h is already falling and n rising, so the balance
      point stays short of E_Na. The model has no pump and still
      peaks and repolarizes, so the pump cannot be the reason. Sodium channels do not close at
      zero millivolts; the model's sodium conductance stays large well above zero. At the peak
      dV/dt is zero, so the capacitive current is zero there and cannot cancel anything.
  - stem: "Immediately after an action potential, no stimulus, however strong, can produce a second one. What is mainly responsible?"
    choices:
      - "Depletion of sodium ions from the fluid outside"
      - "Inactivated sodium channels not yet recovered"
      - "The pump pausing while it restores gradients"
      - "Potassium channels holding the membrane at E_K"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The absolute refractory period reflects sodium inactivation: with h low, too few sodium
      channels are available for any stimulus to drive a regenerative inward current. Raised
      potassium conductance adds to the relative refractory period, when a stronger stimulus can
      succeed, but alone it would only raise threshold. One spike changes the external sodium
      concentration very little. The pump works on a slower time scale and does not set
      refractoriness.
  - stem: "An unmyelinated axon is replaced by one of the same kind with four times the diameter. Roughly how does its conduction velocity change?"
    choices:
      - "Quadruples, rising in proportion to the diameter"
      - "Stays the same, since only myelin sets velocity"
      - "Rises sixteenfold, with the square of diameter"
      - "Doubles, rising with the square root of diameter"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      In unmyelinated axons velocity rises roughly with the square root of diameter, and the square
      root of 4 is 2. Proportional scaling holds roughly for myelinated fibres, not bare ones, so
      quadrupling applies the wrong rule. Velocity with the square of diameter is no standard rule.
      Diameter matters without myelin because a wider core has lower internal resistance and
      spreads current farther, which is why the squid giant axon is so wide.

**In 1939 Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley pushed a fine electrode down the inside of a squid giant axon and found that, at the peak of the action potential, the inside became positive.**

The leading theory held that an impulse was the membrane briefly losing its selectivity, so the voltage should have stopped near zero. It overshot. Something let positive charge in faster than it could leak out, then reversed. What that something is explains threshold, refractoriness and speed.

## The spike and its puzzles

Start with the thing to be explained. An **action potential** is a brief, stereotyped swing in the membrane potential. It is also called a **spike**; this lesson uses both words for the same event.

Picture a recording from Hodgkin and Huxley's model of the squid giant axon. Throughout the lesson, numbers marked "in the model" come from this standard Hodgkin-Huxley squid model at 6.3 °C. Membrane potential runs up the vertical axis, and time in milliseconds runs along the bottom. Read the trace from left to right.

- It starts flat at **rest**, about −65 mV. The [resting membrane](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/) lesson explains why.
- A stimulus nudges the voltage up. Small nudges die away. A big enough nudge reaches **threshold**, the point past which the voltage keeps rising on its own.
- In the **rising phase** the voltage shoots up in a fraction of a millisecond, crosses zero and keeps going.
- At the **peak** the inside is positive, about +40 mV in the model. Going past zero is the **overshoot**.
- In the **falling phase** the voltage drops back down past rest. This return toward the resting level is called **repolarization**.
- In the **undershoot** it dips below rest for several milliseconds, then settles back.

In the model the whole spike lasts a few milliseconds.

The trace raises five puzzles, and each has a name.

1. **Overshoot.** Why does the inside go positive instead of stopping at zero?
2. **Threshold.** Why do small nudges fade while a slightly larger one sets off a full spike?
3. **All-or-none.** Why is every spike from a resting membrane essentially the same size, whether a brief stimulus was just enough or far more than enough?
4. **Refractoriness.** Why can't the membrane fire a second spike right after the first?
5. **Travel.** Why does the spike move along the axon at a set speed, away from where it started, without turning back? And why are some axons about a hundred times faster than others?

One rule, three gates and a little cable theory answer all five. The rule comes first, and it needs two terms. An ion's **equilibrium potential** is the voltage at which that ion has no net push in or out. Its **conductance** says how open its path through the membrane is, which mostly means how many of its channels are open.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Once a stimulus is over, with no current injected, the voltage at each moment moves toward a balance point: the average of the ions' equilibrium potentials, weighted by their conductances. Every phase of the spike comes from this rule plus channels that open and close with voltage and time.

## The tug of war: where the voltage goes

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

You met the rule for ionic current in [the resting membrane and passive signals](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/):

> **I_ion = g_ion × (Vm − E_ion)**

- **E_ion** is the ion's equilibrium potential, defined above. It comes from the Nernst equation, derived in [transport across membranes](/lessons/membrane-transport/). In the squid model E_Na is +50 mV and E_K is −77 mV.
- The **driving force**, Vm − E_ion, is the gap between the membrane potential and the ion's equilibrium potential. It says which way the ion is pushed and how hard.
- **g_ion** is the ion's conductance, defined above.

With g in mS/cm² and the driving force in mV, the current comes out in µA/cm², because a millisiemens times a millivolt is a microampere (mS × mV = µA). This lesson takes outward current as positive, so inward current is negative. The 1952 papers flipped the signs of both voltage and current; the anecdote "Read the signs before the traces" gives the conversion.

**Predict.** The membrane sits at 0 mV and sodium channels are open. Which way does sodium move, and what does that do to the voltage?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The driving force is 0 − (+50) = −50 mV. The current is negative, so it is inward. Sodium flows in and makes the inside more positive, so sodium's contribution pulls Vm up toward +50 mV. Whether Vm actually rises depends on the sum of all the currents, which is where the balance point comes in.

</details>

Notice what that current did: it pulled Vm toward E_Na. The same holds for every ion: open channels pull the voltage toward that ion's equilibrium potential.

Picture a voltage scale running from E_K at −77 mV on the left to E_Na at +50 mV on the right. A marker on the scale shows Vm. A rope for each ion pulls the marker toward that ion's end. How hard a rope pulls is that ion's current, g × (Vm − E): its conductance times the marker's distance from the ion's own end. So a rope pulls harder the more open its channels are and the farther the marker sits from its end.

The marker settles where the pulls balance. Write I = g × (Vm − E) for each ion, set the sum to zero, and solve for Vm. It comes out as an average of the equilibrium potentials, each weighted by its conductance:

> **Vm = (g_K × E_K + g_Na × E_Na + g_leak × E_leak) / (g_K + g_Na + g_leak)**

A quick example, ignoring leak: with g_K = 10 and g_Na = 1 mS/cm², the balance point is (10 × −77 + 1 × 50) / 11, about −65 mV. It sits close to E_K because potassium has ten times the conductance.

Here **leak** is a conductance that is always open, with an effective equilibrium potential, E_leak, of about −54 mV in the squid model. The formula holds for a patch of membrane with no current injected and none flowing along the axon. It is a conductance-model formula, not the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz permeability equation.

When the conductances change, the balance point moves, and Vm chases it. That is the rule: **with no stimulus or other current injected, Vm moves toward the balance point, and when it gets there the currents cancel and the voltage holds still.** Apply it to three cases.

- **At rest**, potassium has the largest conductance, so the balance point sits near E_K. It sits about 12 mV above E_K, at about −65 mV, mainly because of leak. In the squid model the leak conductance, 0.3 mS/cm², is nearly as large as the resting potassium conductance, about 0.37 mS/cm², and E_leak lies well above E_K. A very few open sodium channels add a little more. At rest the leak current is inward: with the small sodium current, it balances the outward potassium current.
- **If sodium conductance rose far enough**, the balance point would slide toward E_Na. To pass zero, sodium must outweigh potassium: ignoring leak, g_Na must exceed about 1.5 times g_K, because 50 × g_Na must beat 77 × g_K. If sodium conductance stayed that high long enough, the inside would turn positive.
- **If potassium conductance rose above its resting level**, the balance point would move closer to E_K at −77 mV, and Vm would drop below rest.

Now return to the opening puzzle. In this squid model, a large, brief rise in sodium conductance produces the overshoot, and potassium conductance staying above its resting level produces the undershoot. So the conductances change, fast and only for a moment. Two questions follow. What makes them change? And why only for a moment? To answer, Hodgkin and Huxley had to watch conductances change. That took a trick.

## Holding the voltage still: the voltage clamp

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Measuring a free spike runs into a loop. Depolarization opens sodium channels. Their current depolarizes the membrane further, which opens more channels. During a free spike, voltage and channel opening change together, so their effects are hard to separate.

The **voltage clamp** breaks the loop. It pins the membrane at a voltage you choose and holds it there.

An analogy shows how it also measures current. Suppose you must keep the water in a leaky bathtub at exactly one level. You watch the level and open the tap just enough to hold it steady. If the drain opens wider, you must run the tap faster. If water starts pouring in through a crack instead, your tap must run backwards, like a pump, and remove it just as fast. Either way, the flow through your tap is a direct readout of how fast water is leaving or entering.

In the clamp, a feedback amplifier plays your part. It holds the membrane at a commanded voltage and supplies whatever current that takes. Why does that current equal the current crossing the membrane? The bilayer is a capacitor (see [membrane structure and function](/lessons/membrane-structure-function/)). Current goes into charging it only while the voltage changes, as **capacitive current**, Cm × dV/dt. At a fixed voltage dV/dt is zero, so no current goes into the capacitance, and the amplifier's current must match the current through the channels. When the net ionic current is outward, the amplifier supplies charge to replace what leaves. When it is inward, as during the early sodium current, the amplifier withdraws the charge that enters.

Hodgkin, Huxley and Bernard Katz built a clamp for the squid giant axon and published their records in the Journal of Physiology in 1952.

### What the record looks like

**Predict.** Step the voltage from rest to a depolarized level and hold it there. If every conductance stayed fixed, what would the current do after the step?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It would jump to a new value and stay flat. With the voltage held, the driving force is constant, and with g fixed, I = g × (Vm − E) cannot change.

</details>

The real record does not stay flat. Picture the current trace drawn under the voltage step, with inward current plotted downward. It has three parts.

1. **A brief blip** right at the step. This is capacitive current charging the membrane. It flows only while the voltage is changing, and it is gone once the voltage is steady. Set it aside: what remains is ionic current.
2. **An early inward current.** It grows fast, then dies away, even though the voltage is still held.
3. **A delayed outward current.** It rises slowly and lasts as long as the step does.

In the squid axon, depolarizing steps of about 15 to 110 mV gave this pattern.

> **The key inference.** The voltage is fixed, so the driving force (Vm − E) is fixed. In I = g × (Vm − E), the only thing left that can change is g. So when a current grows and then shrinks at one steady voltage, the conductance itself is opening and then closing. The conductances change with voltage and with time. Measuring how they change is what Hodgkin and Huxley set out to do.

## Which ions carry the two currents?

A current trace does not say which ion carries it. Hodgkin and Huxley used two tests.

The first is the **reversal potential**, the voltage at which a current changes sign. For a current carried by one ion, it sits at that ion's equilibrium potential. Picture a graph of peak early current against the voltage of the step. In normal sea water the early current is inward for modest steps. It shrinks as the step nears +50 mV and turns outward above it. The line crosses zero near E_Na.

The second test is **ion substitution**. They replaced most of the sodium in the sea water with choline, a cation the membrane passes poorly.

**Predict.** If the early current is carried by sodium, which way should its zero crossing move when external sodium falls? If it were carried by potassium, would the crossing move at all?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Less sodium outside lowers E_Na, so a sodium current's crossing should slide to more negative voltages. A potassium current would not care about sodium, so its crossing would stay put.

</details>

In the experiment the early current reversed, while the delayed outward current was little affected. The reversal potential of the early current moved with external sodium, as expected for a current carried by sodium. So the early current was assigned to Na+.

Sodium independence alone could not name the carrier of the delayed current. It was assigned to K+ on other evidence, including tracer measurements of potassium leaving stimulated axons.

**Worked problem.** Take a model squid axon under an ideal clamp, outward current positive, E_Na = +50 mV and E_K = −77 mV (the Hodgkin-Huxley model constants once rest is placed at −65 mV). The conductances are illustrative round numbers, not readings from a published figure. Step from −65 mV to 0 mV.

*Step 1: the early current.* Suppose g_Na peaks at 20 mS/cm². The driving force is 0 − 50 = −50 mV, so

> I_Na = 20 mS/cm² × (−50 mV) = −1000 µA/cm², inward

*Step 2: the late current.* Suppose that at 5 ms g_K is 15 mS/cm². The driving force is 0 − (−77) = +77 mV, so

> I_K = 15 mS/cm² × 77 mV = +1155 µA/cm², outward

*Step 3: the choline prediction in numbers.* Cut external Na+ to 10% with choline, at 6.3 °C. A tenfold change in concentration shifts an equilibrium potential by about 58 mV near 20 °C and about 55 mV in the cold squid preparation, so E_Na drops from +50 mV to about −5 mV. Assume, as a simplification, that g_Na is unchanged. Then

> I_Na ≈ 20 mS/cm² × (0 − (−5) mV) ≈ +100 µA/cm²

The early current is now outward, because 0 mV lies above the new E_Na. Treat the size as rough. The sign is the point.

*Step 4: what the amplifier does.* On the plateau dV/dt = 0, so the amplifier's current matches the net ionic current: it withdraws charge while the inward sodium current of Step 1 dominates, and supplies charge once the outward potassium current of Step 2 dominates.

## How three gates make one spike

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

The clamp records showed two behaviours. Sodium conductance switched on fast and then switched itself off, even though the voltage was held steady. Potassium conductance switched on slowly and stayed on for as long as the step lasted. That takes three processes: fast sodium activation, slower sodium inactivation and slow potassium activation. A cartoon of gates pictures them.

Picture each sodium channel as a pore with **two gates in series**, like two doors in one hallway.

- The **activation gate** is the fast gate. It is shut at rest and swings open within a fraction of a millisecond when the membrane depolarizes.
- The **inactivation gate** is the slow gate. It is open at rest. It swings shut when the membrane depolarizes, but several times more slowly. A channel with this gate shut is **inactivated**.

Sodium can pass only while both gates are open. So after a depolarization there is a brief window: the fast gate has opened and the slow gate has not yet shut.

The squid's delayed potassium channel behaves as if it has **one slow gate**. It opens slowly when the membrane depolarizes and closes slowly when the voltage falls again.

Hodgkin and Huxley's equations track the three processes, not physical gates, with variables that run from 0 to 1. **m** tracks sodium activation, **h** the fraction of sodium channels not inactivated, and **n** potassium activation. Depolarization drives m up fast, h down more slowly, and n up slowly. Sodium conductance follows m³h and potassium conductance n⁴. The exponents were fitted to the S-shaped onset of the currents; they are not counts of gates or subunits.

Hodgkin and Huxley did not know what a channel looked like, and their equations describe conductances without proving how a gate works. Still, the gate picture predicts the right behaviour, and voltage sensors and inactivation machinery were later found in channel proteins; see [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/).

**Inactivation is not deactivation.** Two different processes can close a sodium channel, and they leave it in different states.

- **Deactivation** is activation reversing as the voltage falls: the fast gate swings shut. If the channel has not inactivated, it is closed but ready to open again. If it has inactivated, deactivation alone does not make it available; it must also recover from inactivation.
- **Inactivation** means the slow gate is shut. The channel is closed and not ready until that gate reopens at negative voltage.

Repolarize early in a clamp step, before much inactivation, and the sodium current shuts off within a fraction of a millisecond as m falls, leaving the channels ready to reopen. Inactivated channels stay unavailable until h recovers, which paired pulses at varying intervals reveal.

### Four moments of one spike

Now take the clamp away and let the axon run free. Remember the rule: Vm moves toward the balance point set by the conductances. It chases that point with a slight lag, because changing the voltage means moving charge onto or off the membrane capacitance, and that takes current and time. A stimulus pushes Vm from rest past threshold. Follow four moments.

1. **Rising phase.** Sodium activation gates open, sodium flows in, and the inside becomes more positive. That opens more activation gates, which lets in more sodium. This is **positive feedback**: each step makes the next one bigger, and it is why the rise is so steep. Within a fraction of a millisecond sodium conductance dwarfs potassium conductance, so the balance point leaps toward E_Na at +50 mV and Vm races after it. Even during the rise, the slow processes have begun: inactivation is already developing, and potassium gates are starting to open.

2. **Peak.** Vm stops short of +50 mV, at about +40 mV in the model. It stops where it meets the balance point: there the inward sodium current equals the outward potassium and leak current, and the voltage holds still for an instant. Sodium still has much the larger conductance at the peak, about 12 times g_K in the model. Two things hold the balance point below E_Na. Potassium and leak conductances stay open, so the average is never pure sodium. And during the rise, inactivation lowers g_Na while potassium activation raises g_K, which pulls the balance point down toward the rising Vm. In the model, the risen potassium conductance does the most to keep the peak short of E_Na. Sodium's large conductance can be balanced at the peak because Vm is close to E_Na, so each open sodium channel pushes only weakly, while potassium, far from E_K, pushes hard.

3. **Falling phase.** Inactivation now closes more and more sodium channels, so sodium conductance collapses, while potassium conductance keeps rising. The balance point drops below Vm, so Vm starts to fall. At first sodium may still have the larger conductance, but, as at the peak, its pull is weak and potassium's is strong. Soon potassium becomes the largest conductance, and the balance point sinks toward E_K. This is **negative feedback**: the same depolarization that started the spike also ends it, a little later.

4. **Undershoot.** As Vm falls, the potassium gates begin to close, but slowly. For several milliseconds potassium conductance stays higher than at rest, while sodium conductance is close to zero. With potassium dominating even more than usual, the balance point sits below rest, near E_K at −77 mV, and Vm dips toward it. As the extra potassium gates close, Vm settles back to rest.

| Moment | Sodium activation gate | Sodium inactivation gate | Potassium gate | Largest conductance | Vm and the balance point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rest | almost all shut | more than half open | a few open | potassium, with leak close behind | Vm sits at it: about 12 mV above E_K |
| Rising phase | opening fast | inactivation already developing | starting to open | sodium | point leaps toward E_Na; Vm chases it |
| Peak | mostly open | partly inactivated | partly open | sodium (falling) | meets the balance point: inward current = outward current |
| Falling phase | open, then closing | mostly shut | open | sodium early, then potassium | point below Vm, sinking toward E_K |
| Undershoot | shut | shut, slowly reopening | open, slowly closing | potassium, more than at rest | point below rest, near E_K |

So the spike is a race between one fast process, sodium activation, and two slow ones, sodium inactivation and potassium activation. The fast process wins first, which gives the rise. The slow processes catch up, which gives the fall.

Notice what is missing: the Na+/K+ pump. The model has no pump, yet it repolarizes. In a large axon like the squid's, one spike moves so few ions that the concentrations barely change. The pump restores the gradients over many spikes.

The squid axon is one design among many: some axons repolarize with almost no potassium current (see Going deeper).

The equations passed tests they were not fitted to. They reproduced the squid action potential's shape and refractoriness with fair accuracy. For one fibre at 18.5 °C they predicted conduction at 18.8 m/s against 21.2 m/s measured. That speed belongs to an axon about half a millimetre across.

## Threshold, all-or-none and refractoriness

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

### Threshold: sodium feedback that sustains itself

**Threshold** is the voltage past which the extra sodium current from newly opened sodium channels grows faster than potassium and leak current can oppose it, so depolarization becomes self-sustaining. The test is whether the inward excess keeps growing. A tally makes this concrete; the numbers are illustrative, made up for teaching, in arbitrary units.

- **Nudge the membrane 5 mV, from −65 to −60 mV.** A few sodium channels open: say 2 units of inward current. Potassium current is outward. Leak current is still inward at −60 mV, because that is below E_leak. Say potassium and leak together give 3 units outward. Outward wins, so there is no inward excess to grow: the voltage drifts back and nothing happens.
- **Nudge it 12 mV, to −53 mV.** Now say 6 units in and 4 out. The voltage rises, and each millivolt opens more sodium channels, so the inward excess grows faster than potassium and leak can add outward current. The rise feeds itself and runs away.
- **Threshold lies between the two**, where the sodium feedback first sustains itself. A momentary excess of inward current is not enough on its own: if the opposing currents grow faster, the voltage still turns back.

**All-or-none follows.** Once depolarization is self-sustaining, the spike runs its course. Its size is set by the channels and the equilibrium potentials, not by the stimulus. With brief stimuli, a bigger push gets there sooner, and the spike is essentially the same size. This holds for a membrane that starts from rest. Just after a spike the rules change, as the next part shows.

**Threshold moves.** Threshold is not a fixed voltage. If a depolarization creeps up slowly, sodium channels inactivate along the way and potassium channels open, so threshold moves higher. In conductance-based models, threshold can depend on the recent history of stimulation through sodium inactivation (Platkiewicz and Brette, 2010). A figure such as −55 mV belongs to one cell under one protocol.

### Refractoriness comes from inactivation

After a spike most sodium channels are inactivated. Their inactivation gates reopen only once the membrane is back at negative voltage, and that takes time.

Picture a paired-pulse experiment. Fire one spike. Then give a second stimulus after a chosen interval, and repeat with different intervals. Plot the height of the second spike against the interval.

- **At the shortest intervals there is no second spike.** Turning the stimulus up does not help. This is the **absolute refractory period**. No stimulus, however strong, triggers a second spike, because too few sodium channels are available to drive a runaway inward current.
- **At intermediate intervals a stronger stimulus can fire a spike**, often a smaller one. This is the **relative refractory period**. A spike needs a stronger stimulus than at rest, because some sodium channels are still inactivated and g_K is still raised.
- **At long intervals the second spike is full size again.**

How long recovery takes depends on the cell and its temperature.

Sodium conductance shoots up and falls back first. Potassium conductance rises later and more slowly, and it is still raised during the undershoot. - **Threshold.** Lower the stimulus until the spike fails, then raise it in small steps. Near threshold, a small change switches the response from almost nothing to a nearly full spike. A much larger stimulus gives a spike of about the same size, only sooner.
- **Refractoriness.** Turn on the second pulse and shorten the interval until the second spike fails. Then raise the stimulus. In the relative refractory period a stronger pulse brings the second spike back; in the absolute refractory period no increase will.
- **Blockers.** Reduce g_Na (as with tetrodotoxin) or g_K (as with TEA). Predict the result first; the drill near the end of the lesson has the answers.

## Moving down the axon

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

### Spread ahead, refractory behind

A spike at one patch of axon does not stay there. Its inward current spreads along the axon's core and brings the next stretch of membrane to threshold. That stretch fires, and its current spreads on to the next. How far the current reaches is set by the **length constant**, the distance over which a steady voltage change decays to about 37% of its starting size. The longer it is, the farther ahead the spreading current can bring membrane toward threshold, which matters for speed below.

Why doesn't the spike turn back? The membrane just behind it has just fired, and its sodium channels are inactivated. That membrane is refractory, so it cannot be re-excited. The spike can only move into fresh membrane ahead. The axon itself has no preferred direction: a stimulus applied mid-axon sends spikes both ways. A spike travels away from wherever it started.

### Diameter: a wider pipe

A wider axon has lower internal resistance, so current spreads farther along it before leaking away. Water behaves the same way: it carries farther down a wide pipe than down a narrow, leaky one. Current that spreads farther brings membrane farther ahead to threshold, so the spike moves faster. In unmyelinated axons velocity rises roughly with the square root of diameter. That is why the squid giant axon is so wide.

### Myelin: a hose wrapped in tape

**Myelin** is glial membrane wrapped around the axon ([myelination](/lessons/myelination/) covers how it is made). Think of a leaky garden hose wrapped in tape.

- Myelin raises the resistance of each **internode**, the wrapped stretch between gaps, so less current leaks out.
- It also lowers the internode's capacitance, so less charge is needed. Think of capacitance as the width of a water tank: pour in the same amount, and a narrow tank's level rises more than a wide one's. The voltage rises as charge arrives, and with less capacitance the same charge gives a bigger rise.

So current from one active gap reaches the next gap fast and still strong.

The gaps are the **nodes of Ranvier**. Sodium channels there regenerate the spike, so the nodes act as boost stations. This is **saltatory conduction**, from the Latin for "leap". Huxley and Robert Stämpfli found evidence for it in single frog fibres in 1949. Excitation skips the internode, but current does not: it flows continuously through the axon from each firing node to the next, which fires in turn. Going deeper adds that the internode is not perfectly sealed.

In myelinated fibres velocity rises roughly in proportion to diameter. In mammalian peripheral nerves the range is wide, with exact values depending on species and temperature:

| Fibre | Conduction velocity |
| --- | --- |
| Unmyelinated C fibres | about 0.6 to 2 m/s |
| Largest myelinated fibres | about 70 to 120 m/s |

That is roughly a hundredfold range: walking pace against a fast train.

### Demyelination

**Demyelination** strips the sheath from an internode. The bare axon has more membrane to charge and leaks more current. In rabbit nerve, loosening the myelin at the nodes also uncovers potassium channels in the paranodal axon, which the sheath normally covers (Chiu and Ritchie, 1980). So current reaches the next node late or too weak: conduction slows or blocks.

It does not always block. Acutely demyelinated rabbit internodes developed sodium and potassium currents of their own (Chiu and Schwarz, 1987). Hugh Bostock and Tom Sears (1978) produced segmental demyelination in rat ventral roots and recorded the current flowing along single fibres at many points. In some fibres the inward current moved smoothly along the bare internode, as along an unmyelinated axon, at 1.1 to 2.3 m/s: continuous conduction.

Multiple sclerosis damages central myelin and Guillain-Barré syndrome peripheral nerve; [neurodegeneration](/lessons/neurodegeneration/) covers disease, and [synaptic transmission](/lessons/synaptic-transmission/) picks up the spike at the terminal.

### Design problem

A nerve holds many fibres, so stimulating it gives a **compound action potential**, the summed spikes of all the fibres excited, and its earliest part comes from the fastest fibres because their spikes arrive first. In an invented experiment, stimulate a nerve at two sites 10 cm apart and record the compound action potential at a fixed electrode. The latency difference is 2.0 ms. After a 1 cm demyelinating lesion between the sites it is 7.0 ms, and the response is half as large. Find the velocity before and within the lesion, interpret the smaller response, and design a test of continuous conduction against slowed saltation.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Before injury, velocity = 0.10 m / 0.0020 s = 50 m/s for the fastest fibres.

Assume the 9 cm of intact nerve is unchanged: it takes 0.09 m / 50 m/s = 1.8 ms. The lesion takes 7.0 − 1.8 = 5.2 ms, so its velocity is 0.01 m / 0.0052 s, about 1.9 m/s.

A halved response means fewer fibres arrive together. Some may block, while others conduct slowly; unequal slowing also spreads arrival times and lowers the peak.

To separate the two modes, record longitudinal action currents at closely spaced points along the lesion, as Bostock and Sears did. Continuous conduction shows inward current moving smoothly along the bare internode; slowed saltation shows it only at the old nodes, with long delays between. Then apply TTX to one bare internode only, keeping its flanking nodes in normal solution: continuous conduction that depends on sodium channels in the bare membrane should fail, while slowed saltation, which relies on the nodes, should survive.

This describes the experimental lesion in this nerve, not how far conduction recovers in multiple sclerosis.

## Going deeper

### Where the voltage clamp came from

Kenneth Cole, working with George Marmont, developed the method in the late 1940s. The squid clamp of Hodgkin, Huxley and Katz was their own version.

### The exact shift in Step 3

A tenfold fall in external sodium shifts E_Na by 2.303RT/F, which at 279.45 K (6.3 °C) is 55.4 mV, so E_Na moves to −5.4 mV. With g_Na assumed unchanged,

> I_Na = 20 mS/cm² × (0 − (−5.4) mV) = +108 µA/cm²

### The 1952 equations

Hodgkin and Huxley drew the membrane as a capacitor in parallel with sodium, potassium and leak conductances:

> **g_Na = g_Na,max × m³ × h and g_K = g_K,max × n⁴**

Each of m, h and n relaxes toward a voltage-dependent value with first-order kinetics. The 1952 model used g_Na,max = 120 mS/cm², g_K,max = 36 mS/cm², leak 0.3 mS/cm² and Cm = 1 µF/cm², with rates for 6.3 °C scaled by 3 per 10 °C.

Why a fourth power? After a depolarizing step, current through the potassium channels already open changes at once, but the extra potassium conductance rises slowly, then faster. Raising n to the fourth power reproduces that S-shaped onset.

### How fast sodium channels recover

In one squid axon at 4.5 °C, Hodgkin and Huxley found sodium-current recovery roughly exponential, with a time constant near 12 ms: one cold axon, not a general value.

### Not every axon needs potassium channels to repolarize

In rabbit nodes recorded at 14 °C, potassium current is almost entirely absent and leak accounts for repolarization after the brief sodium current (Chiu, Ritchie and colleagues, 1979). The balance-point rule still holds: once sodium channels inactivate, leak is the largest conductance left, so the balance point falls back toward leak's equilibrium potential and Vm follows.

### How potent the blockers are

At frog nodes, TEA blocks the delayed potassium current with a dissociation constant near 0.4 mM (Hille, 1967), and tetrodotoxin blocks sodium current with an apparent dissociation constant of about 1 nM (Hille, 1968).

### The internode is not sealed

In rat cortical axons, recordings, electron microscopy and cable models support a conductive pathway beneath the myelin (Cohen and colleagues, 2020).
