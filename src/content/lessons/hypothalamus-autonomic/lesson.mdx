scientists:
  - name: "Walter B. Cannon"
    contribution: >-
      Studied how the sympathetic division and the adrenal medulla respond to emergencies, and
      gave the name homeostasis to the coordinated processes that keep the internal state of the
      body steady.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Cannon, Organization for physiological homeostasis, Physiol. Rev. 9:399–431 (1929); Cannon, The Wisdom of the Body (1932)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Cannon worked at Harvard Medical School, and much of his experimental work was on animals,
      cats among them, in which he followed what the sympathetic nerves and the adrenal medulla
      did during pain, cold, blood loss and strong emotional excitement. He saw the sympathetic
      division and the adrenal medulla act together, which is where the phrase about readiness for
      fight or flight comes from. In 1929 he set out his general argument in Physiological Reviews,
      in a paper titled Organization for physiological homeostasis. He took the steady internal
      environment that Claude Bernard had described and asked how it is kept steady. His answer
      was that many coordinated processes, often under nervous control, push back whenever a
      variable such as blood sugar, temperature or water content drifts, and he named that
      condition homeostasis. He expanded the argument for a wide readership in The Wisdom of the
      Body in 1932. The word gave physiologists a frame in which a regulated variable and the
      machinery that defends it could be studied separately, which is the frame this lesson uses.
  - name: "Geoffrey W. Harris"
    contribution: >-
      Argued from animal experiments that the hypothalamus controls the anterior pituitary by
      chemical messengers carried in the hypophysial portal vessels, rather than by nerve fibres.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Harris, Neural control of the pituitary gland, Physiol. Rev. 28:139–179 (1948); Harris & Jacobsohn, Proc. R. Soc. Lond. B 139:263–276 (1952)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the 1940s it was clear that the anterior pituitary drives the thyroid, the adrenal cortex
      and the gonads, and that the brain somehow influences it. The puzzle was the wiring. The
      posterior lobe is full of nerve fibres from the hypothalamus, but the anterior lobe has very
      few. Anatomists had described small veins running down the pituitary stalk from the base of
      the hypothalamus to the anterior lobe. Harris, working in laboratory mammals, argued that these
      portal vessels were the link: nerve endings at the base of the hypothalamus release chemical
      factors into the blood, and the blood carries them a short distance to the gland. He set out
      the case in a 1948 review in Physiological Reviews. With Dora Jacobsohn he reported in 1952
      that pituitary tissue grafted under the hypothalamus, where portal vessels could grow into
      it, regained function better than grafts placed elsewhere in the brain. The hypothesis set the
      task of isolating the factors, and TRH and GnRH were later purified and sequenced in the
      laboratories of Roger Guillemin and Andrew Schally.
  - name: "E. B. Verney"
    contribution: >-
      Showed in conscious dogs that small rises in the osmotic pressure of blood reaching the head
      stop a water diuresis through release of antidiuretic hormone, and proposed osmoreceptors
      that respond to cell shrinkage.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Verney, The antidiuretic hormone and the factors which determine its release (Croonian Lecture), Proc. R. Soc. Lond. B 135:25–106 (1947)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Drink a large volume of water and your kidneys soon produce a large volume of dilute urine,
      a water diuresis. Verney, a pharmacologist at Cambridge, used that response as an assay in
      trained, conscious dogs. Once a dog was in diuresis, he infused small volumes of
      concentrated solutions into the carotid artery, which supplies the head, and watched the
      urine flow. Hypertonic sodium chloride or sucrose stopped the diuresis, and the effect
      behaved like release of antidiuretic hormone from the posterior pituitary. Urea at a similar
      concentration was much less effective. Urea crosses cell membranes fairly readily, so it
      raises osmolality on both sides of a membrane and draws little water out of cells; sodium
      chloride and sucrose stay outside and shrink cells. Verney concluded that the sensor
      responds to the shrinking of some cell in the head, and he called it an osmoreceptor. He
      presented the work in his 1947 Croonian Lecture. Later work placed osmosensitive neurons in
      the lamina terminalis, the region this lesson's mouse experiments record from.
  - name: "Yuki Oka"
    contribution: >-
      With Mingyu Ye and Charles Zuker, used optogenetics in mice to show that activating excitatory
      neurons of the subfornical organ drives drinking, and activating its inhibitory neurons
      suppresses it.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Oka, Ye & Zuker, Thirst driving and suppressing signals encoded by distinct neural populations in the brain, Nature 520:349–352 (2015)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The subfornical organ, or SFO, is a small structure in the lamina terminalis that lacks a
      full blood-brain barrier and so samples the blood directly. In Charles Zuker's laboratory,
      Oka designed and carried out experiments that put channelrhodopsin, a light-gated cation
      channel, into defined SFO populations in mice. Mingyu Ye performed the slice patch-clamp
      recordings, which confirmed that light drove the channelrhodopsin-expressing neurons. When
      Oka stimulated excitatory, CaMKII-positive SFO neurons in fully water-satiated mice, the mice
      drank, and could drink nearly 8% of their body weight within 15 minutes. Stimulating the
      inhibitory, VGAT-positive SFO neurons in thirsty mice cut water licking by more than 80%,
      without suppressing the tested food or salt appetites. Light-driven mice still refused bitter
      or very salty water, so the stimulation did not override taste. The 2015 paper in Nature
      showed that defined cell types in one small organ are enough to push drinking up or down,
      which made those cells a target for recording during natural thirst.

techniques:
  - name: "Fibre photometry"
    blurb: >-
      A genetically encoded calcium indicator such as GCaMP is expressed in a chosen cell type,
      and an optical fibre over the region collects its fluorescence in an awake animal. The signal
      tracks the summed calcium activity of the population over seconds; it does not resolve single
      cells or spikes, and it does not measure a feeling such as thirst.
  - name: "Optogenetic activation and silencing"
    blurb: >-
      A light-gated channel or pump is expressed in a defined cell type, and light delivered
      through a fibre excites or inhibits those cells on command. Driving cells tests whether their
      activity is enough to produce a behaviour; silencing them tests whether it is needed, in that
      preparation and task.
  - name: "Infusion of hypertonic solutions"
    blurb: >-
      Small volumes of concentrated solutes are infused into an artery or the stomach while urine
      flow, a hormone or neural activity is recorded. Comparing solutes that do and do not cross
      cell membranes tells you whether a sensor responds to osmotic shrinkage or to a particular
      substance.
  - name: "Pituitary stalk section and grafting"
    blurb: >-
      Cutting the stalk, or moving pituitary tissue to a new site, separates the gland from its
      neural and vascular links to the hypothalamus. Comparing the hormone output of grafts at
      different sites tests which link carries the control.

  - stem: "Person P has an AVP threshold of 280 mOsm/kg and a gain of 0.43 pmol/L per mOsm/kg. Person Q has a threshold of 284 mOsm/kg and a gain of 0.86 pmol/L per mOsm/kg. Above both thresholds, at what plasma osmolality do the two have the same predicted AVP?"
    choices:
      - "286 mOsm/kg"
      - "288 mOsm/kg"
      - "284 mOsm/kg"
      - "290 mOsm/kg"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Set 0.43 × (Posm − 280) equal to 0.86 × (Posm − 284). Dividing by 0.43 gives Posm − 280 = 2 × Posm − 568,

**A thirsty mouse that starts to drink quiets a population of its thirst neurons within a minute, yet five minutes later its blood osmolality is still raised.**

These neurons drive thirst, so their quieting means the brain has judged that water is coming before any change in the blood is detected. If the brain only corrected errors it had measured in the blood, that would be hard to explain. To see what the neurons are reading, you need the idea this lesson is built on and the ways the hypothalamus sends its orders out.

The idea needs four terms. A **regulated variable** is a quantity the body holds within a range. For water it is **plasma osmolality**, the concentration of osmotically active particles per kilogram of water in plasma, the liquid part of the blood. A **threshold** is the value at which a corrective response starts. The **gain** is how much the response grows for each unit the variable moves past threshold. That distance past threshold is the **error**.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** The osmotic vasopressin response needs two numbers, not one set point: a threshold, where its osmotic component starts, and a gain, its slope above threshold. Above threshold, AVP = gain × (osmolality − threshold); below it, no osmotically driven release. Water-balance circuits also integrate more than the blood, and it helps to think of them as acting on an *estimate* of fluid balance: the blood, corrected for what is already on its way. **Feedback** builds the estimate from the blood. **Feedforward** updates it from what has just been swallowed, or from a learned cue, before the blood changes.

The hypothalamus acts through three kinds of output: pituitary hormones in the blood, autonomic nerves, and behaviour such as drinking.

## The loop, built on water

Water is the running example. The loop has three parts you can point to.

**What is held steady.** Plasma osmolality. When you lose water, it rises.

**What senses it.** Osmosensitive neurons, mainly in the **lamina terminalis**, the front wall of the third ventricle. Two of its parts, the **subfornical organ (SFO)** and the **organum vasculosum (OVLT)**, are **circumventricular organs**: their blood-brain barrier is incomplete, so their neurons sample the blood directly. The third part, the **median preoptic nucleus (MnPO)**, sits behind the barrier and integrates their signals. The magnocellular vasopressin neurons, described next, are also osmosensitive themselves.

**What pushes back.** Two effectors: vasopressin saves water at the kidney, and thirst takes water in. Both lower osmolality, so the response opposes the error. That makes the loop **negative feedback**.

The hypothalamus is a small region at the base of the forebrain, below the thalamus, built of **nuclei**, clusters of neuron cell bodies inside the brain. Vasopressin is the first of its outputs you need. Large, **magnocellular** neurons in the **supraoptic nucleus (SON)** and the **paraventricular nucleus (PVN)** make vasopressin (AVP) and oxytocin in their cell bodies, carry them down their axons, and release them from terminals in the **posterior pituitary** into the systemic blood. The posterior pituitary stores and releases vasopressin; it does not make it.

In the kidney, vasopressin acts on V2 receptors of collecting-duct cells and, through cAMP ([cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/)), increases the aquaporin-2 water channels in their membranes, so more water returns to the blood ([membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/) covers osmosis).

*An aside on the comparator.* Textbook loops often add a **comparator**, a part that decides how far the variable is from where it should be. Here the comparison is a function, not a separate part: it is spread across the sensing and integrating neurons, the MnPO among them. The threshold you measure summarizes the whole response and need not equal any one neuron's firing threshold, so measure both threshold and gain.

## Threshold and gain

Picture the experiment. Raise plasma osmolality in a controlled way in healthy adults and measure vasopressin as you go. Plot vasopressin up the side and osmolality along the bottom. The plot is flat at first, then rises in a straight line. The knee is the threshold, *when* release starts; the slope above it is the gain, *how much* it grows.

Endotext's review summarizes such data from healthy adults under controlled hypertonic stimulation with a regression line for the straight part:

> **AVP (pmol/L) = 0.43 × (Posm − 284), for Posm above 284 mOsm/kg**

Here Posm is plasma osmolality, 284 mOsm/kg is the threshold and 0.43 pmol/L per mOsm/kg is the gain. Treat both as illustrative averages: thresholds and slopes vary between individuals. In this line the estimate is plasma osmolality and the output is the plasma vasopressin concentration. It predicts a hormone level, not how fast osmolality changes.

<OsmoticThresholdGain />

**Predict.** Plasma osmolality rises from 282 to 286 mOsm/kg. Does vasopressin change?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only for the last part of the rise. From 282 to 284 the person is below threshold. Plugging 282 into the formula would give a negative concentration, which is impossible: the line describes release only above threshold, so it predicts no osmotically driven release there. With little vasopressin the kidney passes dilute urine, so dilute blood is corrected by the hormone's absence. Once osmolality passes 284, vasopressin rises by 0.43 pmol/L for each mOsm/kg beyond it, so at 286 it is 0.43 × 2 = 0.86 pmol/L.

</details>

**Worked problem.** A hypothetical adult follows this line. Plasma osmolality is 294 mOsm/kg, and after drinking it is 290 mOsm/kg. Assume the same relation holds at both times and that AVP has reached steady state for each value.

*Step 1: the first error* (distance above threshold). 294 − 284 = 10 mOsm/kg above threshold. AVP = 0.43 × 10 = 4.30 pmol/L.

*Step 2: the second error.* 290 − 284 = 6 mOsm/kg. AVP = 0.43 × 6 = 2.58 pmol/L.

*Step 3: the change.* 2.58 − 4.30 = −1.72 pmol/L. Check it another way: 0.43 × (290 − 294) = 0.43 × (−4) = −1.72 pmol/L. Less error, less hormone, less water saved.

**Predict.** Compare two made-up people. Person P has a threshold of 280 mOsm/kg and a gain of 0.43. Person Q has a threshold of 284 and a gain of 0.86. Who releases more vasopressin at 286 mOsm/kg? Who releases more at 290?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

At 286, P does; at 290, Q does.

| Posm (mOsm/kg) | P: 0.43 × (Posm − 280) | Q: 0.86 × (Posm − 284) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 282 | 0.86 | 0 (below threshold) |
| 284 | 1.72 | 0 (at threshold) |
| 286 | 2.58 | 1.72 |
| 288 | 3.44 | 3.44 |
| 290 | 4.30 | 5.16 |

Solve 0.43 × (Posm − 280) = 0.86 × (Posm − 284) and the lines cross at 288 mOsm/kg. Between 280 and 288 the lower threshold wins; above 288 the steeper gain wins. At or below 280 both release none. Overlay P and Q in the simulation to see the crossing.

</details>

Two people can give the same output by different routes, so one number cannot describe the loop.

## Two ways out: blood and wires

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The hypothalamus receives visceral sensory information (so do brainstem centres), and it reaches the body's organs in two ways. One is by blood: the pituitary gland below it, whose posterior lobe you have just met. The other is by wires: descending pathways to the autonomic nervous system. These are distinct outputs with their own targets, not one signal sent two ways. Nerves often act within seconds; hormone effects usually build over minutes (vasopressin at the kidney) to hours (the cortisol and thyroid axes).

### The blood channel: the anterior pituitary

The two pituitary lobes are controlled in completely different ways. The posterior lobe is reached by axons. **The anterior pituitary is reached by blood.** Neurons in hypothalamic nuclei send axons to the **median eminence** at the base of the hypothalamus and release peptides into capillaries there. Those capillaries drain into the long hypophysial portal veins, which run down the stalk and open into a second capillary bed in the anterior pituitary. A **portal system** is two capillary beds joined in series, so a releasing hormone reaches the gland at high concentration before it is diluted in the general circulation.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/hypothalamus-autonomic/two-pituitary-lobes.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: Side view of the hypothalamus and pituitary: long axons from large neurons end in the posterior lobe, while a releasing-hormone neuron ends at the median eminence, whose capillaries drain through portal veins down the stalk into a second capillary bed in the anterior lobe." caption="The posterior pituitary is reached by axons, the anterior by blood: releasing hormones enter capillaries at the median eminence and travel down portal veins to a second capillary bed in the gland." />

Two axes show the pattern, and both are negative-feedback loops with a hormone as the regulated variable.

- In the **HPA axis**, corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), made mainly in the PVN, stimulates corticotroph cells of the anterior pituitary to release ACTH. ACTH stimulates the adrenal cortex to make and release cortisol. Cortisol then inhibits the axis at both the hypothalamus and the pituitary.
- In the **HPT axis**, thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) stimulates the pituitary to release thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). TSH is the main regulator of the thyroid. Thyroid hormones feed back at both levels.

Picture each axis as two arrows down (hypothalamus to pituitary, pituitary to gland) and one inhibitory arrow back up from the gland's hormone, branching to both the hypothalamus and the pituitary. With other inputs held constant, a rise in cortisol or thyroid hormone turns down the drive from above. The shared shape does not mean these axes follow the vasopressin line. Cortisol and thyroid hormone act on intracellular receptors ([nuclear receptors](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/)). The HPA axis in stress belongs to [emotion and stress](/lessons/emotion-and-stress/).

**Predict.** A patient takes a high dose of a cortisol-like drug for months. What happens to CRH, to ACTH and to the adrenal cortex?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The drug acts like cortisol at the hypothalamus and pituitary, so the feedback arrow is pressed hard: CRH and ACTH are both suppressed. With little ACTH, the ACTH-dependent zones of the adrenal cortex shrink. That is why such drugs are withdrawn slowly: the axis needs time to recover.

</details>

### The wired channel: two synapses, two rules, two exceptions

The **autonomic nervous system** controls cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands. Descending pathways, many from the PVN, reach its **preganglionic** neurons, whose cell bodies lie in the brainstem or spinal cord. Each preganglionic axon runs out to a **ganglion**, a cluster of neuron cell bodies outside the central nervous system, and synapses there on a **postganglionic** neuron, whose axon reaches the target. So the usual chain has two neurons and two synapses: one in the ganglion, one on the target. The adrenal medulla, below, is the exception.

The two divisions differ in where the chain starts and where the ganglion sits.

- The **sympathetic** division leaves the spinal cord from the thoracic and upper lumbar segments, so it is called thoracolumbar. Its preganglionic axons are relatively short. They end in the paravertebral chain of ganglia beside the vertebral column or in prevertebral ganglia in the abdomen, and the postganglionic axons are relatively long.
- The **parasympathetic** division is conventionally called craniosacral; whether the sacral outflow belongs here is disputed (see the anecdote "Is the sacral outflow parasympathetic?"). Its preganglionic neurons lie in brainstem nuclei, whose axons leave in cranial nerves, and in the sacral cord. Its preganglionic axons are long and end in ganglia near or inside the target, so its postganglionic axons are short.

You can work out the transmitter at the main autonomic synapses from two rules and two exceptions; the transmitters themselves are in [transmitter systems](/lessons/transmitter-systems/).

**Rule 1: the first synapse is the same everywhere.** Every preganglionic neuron, sympathetic or parasympathetic, releases **acetylcholine** (ACh). In the ganglion it acts mainly on **nicotinic** receptors, ligand-gated cation channels like those at the neuromuscular junction, though of a neuronal subtype, so the postganglionic neuron is excited fast. Ganglia also use slower muscarinic and peptide transmission, hence *mainly*.

**Rule 2: the second synapse usually names the division.** Parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto **muscarinic** receptors on the target. Most sympathetic postganglionic neurons release **noradrenaline** onto **alpha** and **beta** adrenergic receptors. Muscarinic and adrenergic receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors ([G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/)), so the target responds more slowly than the ganglion does.

**Exception 1: sweat glands.** The sympathetic fibres to eccrine sweat glands release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors. They are sympathetic by anatomy, leaving the thoracolumbar cord and relaying in the chain ganglia, and cholinergic by chemistry.

**Exception 2: the adrenal medulla.** The adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion in which the second neuron has become a gland. Preganglionic fibres end directly on its **chromaffin cells**, modified postganglionic neurons with no axons, which release adrenaline and noradrenaline into the blood. This one sympathetic wire ends in a hormone.

| Synapse | Parasympathetic | Sympathetic, most targets | Sympathetic, sweat glands | Adrenal medulla |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| In the ganglion | ACh → nicotinic | ACh → nicotinic | ACh → nicotinic | ACh → nicotinic, on chromaffin cells |
| On the target | ACh → muscarinic | noradrenaline → alpha, beta | ACh → muscarinic | none: adrenaline and noradrenaline go into the blood |

So "sympathetic" does not mean "noradrenaline at every synapse".

<LessonFigure src="/figures/hypothalamus-autonomic/autonomic-two-neuron-chains.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: Two autonomic chains: the sympathetic one with a short preganglionic axon, a chain ganglion and noradrenaline at the target, the parasympathetic one with a long preganglionic axon, a ganglion near the target and acetylcholine on muscarinic receptors; insets show cholinergic sympathetic fibres to a sweat gland and preganglionic fibres ending on chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla, which release adrenaline into the blood." caption="Every autonomic ganglion uses acetylcholine on nicotinic receptors, and the target synapse usually names the division, noradrenaline for sympathetic and acetylcholine on muscarinic receptors for parasympathetic; sweat glands and the adrenal medulla are the exceptions." />

**Predict.** Atropine blocks muscarinic receptors. The **vagus nerve** carries the parasympathetic fibres to the heart. Someone is given a large dose of atropine. What happens to their heart rate, and to their sweating?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The heart rate rises and sweating stops. The vagus slows the heart's own pacemaker through muscarinic receptors, and it does so all the time, even at rest; remove that brake and the rate climbs. Sweating stops because the sympathetic sweat-gland fibres act on muscarinic receptors, which is Exception 1 at work. The skin becomes hot and dry.

</details>

**Both divisions are active at rest**, at a **tonic** level, a steady background rate of firing. Atropine revealed the vagal brake. Separate tests, such as blocking beta receptors, show steady sympathetic drive to the heart as well. So the heart sits at a balance between a steady parasympathetic brake and steady sympathetic drive, like a sink filled from a cold tap and a hot tap. Turning either tap changes the temperature, and in a resting person the cold tap is the stronger. "Fight or flight" and "rest and digest" describe which tap is turned up, not two positions of a switch, and many organs are set by the balance of the two at once.

**Some targets have only one tap.** Not every organ has **dual innervation**, a supply from both divisions. Most arteries, the sweat glands and the adrenal medulla receive sympathetic fibres and no parasympathetic ones. The nervous system controls them by turning that one input up or down. In many arteries, more sympathetic firing narrows the vessel and less lets it widen; local signals in the vessel wall also set its width.

### One nucleus, three channels

The PVN uses every route in this section. Its parvocellular (small-celled) neurons release CRH into portal blood, its magnocellular neurons release vasopressin from the posterior lobe, and its pre-autonomic neurons descend to the brainstem and spinal cord. Of the three, the autonomic outflow is usually the fastest.

## Thirst: acting before the blood changes

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Now back to the mouse. A pure feedback loop reacts to an error it has measured. **Feedforward** control acts on a disturbance that has been sensed or predicted before it changes the regulated variable. Mouse experiments show that thirst uses both.

Start with what the SFO can do. In 2015 Oka, Ye and Zuker drove excitatory SFO neurons with light in water-satiated mice, and the mice drank; driving inhibitory SFO neurons in thirsty mice cut licking sharply (numbers in Oka's profile). That shows the cells are enough to push drinking up or down. It does not show they are the only route, or that the SFO is a "thirst centre".

The next question was what these neurons do during ordinary drinking. Christopher Zimmerman, Zachary Knight and colleagues used **fibre photometry**, an optical fibre that collects the fluorescence of a calcium indicator from a whole population, to record SFO neurons marked by the gene *Nos1*, in awake mice. *Nos1* gives a genetic handle for targeting these neurons; the enzyme it encodes is not the point here.

### The picture

Picture their result (Nature, 2016) from water-restricted mice on one time axis, as a schematic. A vertical line marks the moment water becomes available. The top trace is SFO Nos1 population activity, recorded continuously: it starts to fall at the first lick and is down within one minute. The blood was not traced. Plasma osmolality was measured in separate samples by freezing-point osmometry, so picture it as points: it is still raised five minutes after water access.

So a fall in the blood is unlikely to be what turns the neurons off; something that comes with drinking does. The controls narrow down what.

**Predict.** Concentrated (hypertonic) saline is salt water more concentrated than plasma. It is a fluid, but drinking it would raise plasma osmolality, not lower it. If a mouse drinks it, does SFO activity fall?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes, at first, just as with water. But that fall reverses after about a minute. The fast signal counts fluid going in; a slower signal reports what the fluid contains.

</details>

| Condition | What it isolates | SFO Nos1 activity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water-restricted mice drink water | the whole act of drinking | falls at the first lick, down within a minute |
| Salt-challenged mice see water but cannot drink | the sight or expectation of water | does not fall |
| Salt-challenged mice lick an empty bottle | the licking movement | does not fall |
| Mice drink concentrated saline | fluid going in, but salty enough to raise osmolality | falls at first, then reverses after about a minute |
| Fasted mice eat | a meal, which can later draw water out of the blood | rises |

Read the rows in turn. Seeing water fails, so it is not the sight or expectation of water alone in this paradigm. Air-licking fails, so it is not the licking movement. Eating raises activity, as if the neurons anticipate the water that a meal will draw out of the blood (into the gut, and as absorbed food raises osmolality). An oral clue appears in the anecdote "Cold metal on the tongue".

### Design problem

The fast signal comes with fluid going in. But the blood was sampled at set times, not continuously, so a small early change could have been missed. What experiment would show where the signal comes from, whether it travels by a nerve, and whether the fall in SFO activity matters for ending thirst? (Hint: where else could fluid enter the body?)

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Bypass the mouth. Infuse water or hypertonic saline straight into the stomach while you record, and sample blood at the same times. If gut water lowers activity and gut saline raises it before blood osmolality moves, the gut is reporting what came in. Then cut the abdominal vagus nerve in one group and sham-operate another, to ask whether the signal travels that way. Finally, test whether the fall in SFO activity matters: hold the neurons active with light during the infusion and see whether the water still ends thirst.

</details>

### What the gut experiment found

Zimmerman and colleagues did these experiments in 2019. In dehydrated mice, water infused into the stomach inhibited the neurons after 107 ± 15 s (mean ± SEM), about two minutes; in hydrated mice the latency was 105 ± 13 s. Hypertonic saline activated them. Blood osmolarity, measured separately in hydrated mice from samples begun three minutes into the infusion, showed no detectable change, so the response comes ahead of any detectable systemic correction. Subdiaphragmatic vagotomy attenuated the satiating effect of gut water, and holding the neurons active prevented that satiation. So the gut reports what has come in, ahead of the blood.

Why does the brain need that? Swallowed water is absorbed gradually, so the blood lags behind fast drinking. An animal that read only its blood could keep drinking while water was still on its way, and drink well past its deficit. A toy model (Going deeper) shows that this overshoot appears when drinking is fast compared with absorption, and vanishes when the estimate also counts swallowed water not yet absorbed. That is the estimate in the one idea: **the blood, corrected for what is on its way.**

### A cue the brain learns

The gut and mouth signals *sense* fluid entering. Feedforward can also *predict*. Kim and colleagues (2021) found that in trained, water-restricted mice the arrival of a water bowl lowered vasopressin-neuron activity before the mice drank; the anecdote "A cue the brain learns" gives the details and the circuit. The rise with eating in the table is best read as a prediction too. Feedback reads the blood; feedforward reads what is coming.

Keep the limits. Photometry gives population calcium, not spikes and not the feeling of thirst. The work is in mice. Other sites, including the MnPO, integrate oral, gut and blood signals, so the result is a circuit, not a single centre.

Prediction of this kind returns for feeding in [motivation and reward](/lessons/motivation-and-reward/) and for daily timing in [sleep and rhythms](/lessons/sleep-and-rhythms/).

## Going deeper

### What the threshold-gain line does not cover

The line fits only the roughly straight part of the osmotic vasopressin response above threshold. The mouse recordings show that thirst circuits integrate anticipatory signals as well as the blood; they are not fitted to this equation, and no equation of this kind is given here for thirst, the endocrine axes or autonomic outflow. Nor do the experiments establish a common two-parameter law, or an optimal estimator, for hypothalamic responses in general; the estimate is a useful way to think, not a measured quantity. The line does not cover vasopressin released by falls in blood volume or pressure, which follows a steeper, non-linear relation, or vasopressin released by nausea; these can release vasopressin below the osmotic threshold. It does not describe the circadian and pulsatile rhythms of the HPA axis. The wiring that carries the outputs is anatomy; the idea says how that wiring is used, not why it is built that way.

### The enteric nervous system and the origin of ganglion neurons

Many textbooks also count the enteric nervous system in the gut wall as a third autonomic division; this lesson covers the other two. Autonomic ganglion neurons come from the neural crest, as [neural crest](/lessons/neural-crest/) explains; in the adult they are mature relay stations.

### Vasopressin also reaches the anterior pituitary

Vasopressin is not only a posterior-pituitary hormone. Parvocellular PVN neurons also release vasopressin into portal blood, where it amplifies the effect of CRH on ACTH release.

### Fever in threshold language

Fever offers a contrast to the water loop. In the standard model, fever raises the thresholds at which heat-producing and heat-saving responses switch on, so a person shivers and feels cold while body temperature is still normal. Textbooks often call this a raised thermoregulatory set point, but whether there is a single thermoregulatory set point is itself contested. Most work on the prostaglandin E2 mechanism behind that shift comes from rodents.

### A model of drinking with and without feedforward

A simple model makes the case for feedforward. Let D be the body-water deficit and G the swallowed water not yet absorbed, both in litres, with time in minutes. Start with D = 1 and G = 0, and ignore ongoing losses. Water leaves the gut at G/τ per minute, where τ is the absorption time constant, so D falls at G/τ and G changes at u − G/τ, where u is the drinking rate.

- *Blood-only estimate:* u = k × D, and drinking stops when D first reaches zero. With k = 1 per minute and τ = 5 minutes, drinking stops at about 4.1 minutes, after 2.48 L. With τ = 30 minutes it stops at about 9.1 minutes, after 5.70 L. The extra is still in the gut when drinking stops.
- *Estimate that counts swallowed water:* u = k × (D − G), the predicted deficit. The predicted deficit shrinks steadily toward zero, and total intake approaches 1.00 L for any k and τ.

Blood-only control also approaches 1.00 L when k × τ is 1/4 or less, that is, when drinking is slow compared with absorption. These are illustrative outputs of a toy model of the logic, not a fit to any animal's data; other gains and absorption rates give other overshoots.
