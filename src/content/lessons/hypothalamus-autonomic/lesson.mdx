    story: >-
      Cannon worked at Harvard Medical School, and much of his experimental work was on animals,
      cats among them, in which he followed what the sympathetic nerves and the adrenal medulla
      did during pain, cold, blood loss and strong emotional excitement. He saw the sympathetic
      division and the adrenal medulla act together, which is where the phrase about readiness for
      fight or flight comes from. In 1929 he set out his general argument in Physiological Reviews,
      in a paper titled Organization for physiological homeostasis. He took the steady internal
      environment that Claude Bernard had described and asked how it is kept steady. His answer
      was that many coordinated processes, often under nervous control, push back whenever a
      variable such as blood sugar, temperature or water content drifts, and he named that
      condition homeostasis. He expanded the argument for a wide readership in The Wisdom of the
      Body in 1932. The word gave physiologists a frame in which a regulated variable and the
      machinery that defends it could be studied separately, which is the frame this lesson uses.

techniques:
  - name: "Fibre photometry"
    blurb: >-
      A genetically encoded calcium indicator such as GCaMP is expressed in a chosen cell type,
      and an optical fibre over the region collects its fluorescence in an awake animal. The signal
      tracks the summed calcium activity of the population over seconds; it does not resolve single
      cells or spikes, and it does not measure a feeling such as thirst.
  - name: "Optogenetic activation and silencing"
    blurb: >-
      A light-gated channel or pump is expressed in a defined cell type, and light delivered
      through a fibre excites or inhibits those cells on command. Driving cells tests whether their
      activity is enough to produce a behaviour; silencing them tests whether it is needed, in that
      preparation and task.
  - name: "Infusion of hypertonic solutions"
    blurb: >-
      Small volumes of concentrated solutes are infused into an artery or the stomach while urine
      flow, a hormone or neural activity is recorded. Comparing solutes that do and do not cross
      cell membranes tells you whether a sensor responds to osmotic shrinkage or to a particular
      substance.
  - name: "Pituitary stalk section and grafting"
    blurb: >-
      Cutting the stalk, or moving pituitary tissue to a new site, separates the gland from its
      neural and vascular links to the hypothalamus. Comparing the hormone output of grafts at
      different sites tests which link carries the control.

**A thirsty mouse that starts to drink quiets a population of its thirst neurons within a minute, yet five minutes later the salt in its blood is still raised.**

If the brain only corrected errors it had measured in the blood, that could not happen. This lesson builds the machinery the hypothalamus uses to run the body's interior, two autonomic divisions and two pituitary lobes, then treats homeostasis as a feedback loop you can calculate, and ends with the mouse experiments that show the brain acting ahead of the error.

## Two divisions, two neurons each

The autonomic nervous system controls cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands. Many textbooks also count the enteric nervous system in the gut wall as a third division; this lesson covers the other two. Their basic unit is a chain of two neurons. The first, the **preganglionic** neuron, has its cell body in the brainstem or spinal cord and sends its axon out to a **ganglion**, a cluster of neuron cell bodies outside the central nervous system. The second, the **postganglionic** neuron, sits in that ganglion and sends its axon to the target. Autonomic ganglion neurons come from the neural crest, as [neural crest](/lessons/neural-crest/) explains; here they are mature relay stations.

The **sympathetic** division leaves the spinal cord from the thoracic and upper lumbar segments, so it is called thoracolumbar. Its preganglionic axons are relatively short. They end in the paravertebral chain of ganglia beside the vertebral column or in prevertebral ganglia in the abdomen, and the postganglionic axons are relatively long. The **parasympathetic** division is conventionally called craniosacral: its preganglionic neurons lie in brainstem nuclei, whose axons leave in cranial nerves, and in the sacral cord. Its preganglionic axons are long and end in ganglia near or inside the target, so its postganglionic axons are short. The word "conventionally" matters, because a 2016 study in mice argued that the sacral outflow is sympathetic, and others dispute it.

Now the transmitters, which you met in [transmitter systems](/lessons/transmitter-systems/). Learn them as a wiring diagram:

- Every preganglionic neuron, in both divisions, releases **acetylcholine**, which acts on **nicotinic** receptors, ligand-gated cation channels that excite the postganglionic neuron fast.
- Parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto **muscarinic** receptors on the target.
- Most sympathetic postganglionic neurons release **noradrenaline**, which acts on **alpha** and **beta** adrenergic receptors.
- The sympathetic fibres to eccrine sweat glands are cholinergic and act on muscarinic receptors.
- The **adrenal medulla** is a modified sympathetic ganglion. Preganglionic fibres end on its chromaffin cells, which have no axons and release adrenaline and noradrenaline into the blood.

Muscarinic and adrenergic receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors; how they signal belongs to [G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/). So "sympathetic" does not mean "noradrenaline at every synapse": the first synapse is always cholinergic.

Two further corrections. Not every organ has dual innervation: most arteries receive sympathetic fibres and no parasympathetic ones, as do sweat glands and the adrenal medulla. Its nervous control works by turning one input up or down, like a single tap. And the divisions are not a switch between "fight or flight" and "rest and digest". Both are active at rest, at a tonic level, and many organs are set by the balance of the two at once.

## The hypothalamus and the two pituitary lobes

The hypothalamus is a small region at the base of the forebrain, below the thalamus, built of **nuclei**, clusters of neuron cell bodies inside the brain. It receives visceral sensory information, as do brainstem centres, and it reaches the body in two ways: through descending pathways to the autonomic preganglionic neurons, and through the pituitary gland below it. The pituitary has two lobes, controlled in completely different ways.

**The anterior pituitary is reached by blood.** Neurons in hypothalamic nuclei send axons to the **median eminence** at the base of the hypothalamus and release peptides into capillaries there. Those capillaries drain into the long hypophysial portal veins, which run down the stalk and open into a second capillary bed in the anterior pituitary. A **portal system** is two capillary beds joined in series, so a releasing hormone reaches the gland at high concentration before it is diluted in the general circulation.

Two axes show the pattern. In the **HPA axis**, corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), made mainly in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN), stimulates corticotroph cells of the anterior pituitary to release ACTH, and ACTH stimulates the adrenal cortex to make and release cortisol. Cortisol then inhibits the axis at both the hypothalamus and the pituitary. In the **HPT axis**, thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) stimulates the pituitary to release thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and TSH is the main regulator of the thyroid, and thyroid hormones feed back at both levels. Cortisol and thyroid hormone act on intracellular receptors ([nuclear receptors](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/)). The HPA axis in stress belongs to [emotion and stress](/lessons/emotion-and-stress/).

**The posterior pituitary is reached by axons.** Large, **magnocellular** neurons in the supraoptic nucleus (SON) and the PVN make vasopressin (AVP) and oxytocin in their cell bodies, carry them down their axons, and release them from terminals in the posterior pituitary into the systemic blood. The posterior pituitary stores and releases vasopressin; it does not make it. In the kidney, vasopressin acts on V2 receptors of collecting-duct cells and, through cAMP ([cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/)), increases the aquaporin-2 water channels in their membranes, so more water returns to the blood ([membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/) covers osmosis).

## Feedback: set points, thresholds and gains

A simple model of a homeostatic loop has four parts. The **regulated variable** is what is held steady, here plasma osmolality, the concentration of osmotically active particles per kilogram of water. A **sensor** measures it: osmosensitive neurons in the lamina terminalis, the front wall of the third ventricle, which includes the SFO and the organum vasculosum (OVLT). These are **circumventricular organs**, sites with an incomplete blood-brain barrier. The magnocellular vasopressin neurons are also osmosensitive themselves, and the gut sends early signals too. A **comparator** decides how far the variable is from where it should be, and an **effector** pushes back: vasopressin to save water at the kidney, thirst to take water in. The loop is negative feedback, because the response opposes the error.

"Set point" sounds like one number, but describing a hormone response takes at least two. The **threshold** is the value at which the response starts. The **gain** is how much the response grows per unit of error above threshold. Endotext's review summarizes healthy adults under controlled hypertonic stimulation with a regression line:

> **AVP (pmol/L) = 0.43 × (Posm − 284), for Posm above 284 mOsm/kg**

Here 284 mOsm/kg is the threshold and 0.43 pmol/L per mOsm/kg is the gain. Treat both as illustrative averages: thresholds and slopes vary between individuals.

**Worked problem.** A hypothetical adult follows this line. Plasma osmolality is 294 mOsm/kg, and after drinking it is 290 mOsm/kg. Assume the same relation holds at both times and that AVP has reached steady state for each value.

*Step 1: the first error.* 294 − 284 = 10 mOsm/kg above threshold. AVP = 0.43 × 10 = 4.30 pmol/L.

*Step 2: the second error.* 290 − 284 = 6 mOsm/kg. AVP = 0.43 × 6 = 2.58 pmol/L.

*Step 3: the change.* 2.58 − 4.30 = −1.72 pmol/L. Check it another way: 0.43 × (290 − 294) = 0.43 × (−4) = −1.72 pmol/L. Less error, less hormone, less water saved.

*Step 4: below threshold.* At 280 mOsm/kg the formula would give 0.43 × (−4) = −1.72 pmol/L, which is impossible. The line describes release only above threshold, so it predicts no osmotically driven release there, not a negative one.

*Step 5: threshold against gain.* Now compare two made-up people at 290 mOsm/kg. Person P has a threshold of 280 and a gain of 0.43: 0.43 × 10 = 4.30 pmol/L. Person Q has a threshold of 284 and a gain of 0.86: 0.86 × 6 = 5.16 pmol/L. Solve 0.43 × (Posm − 280) = 0.86 × (Posm − 284) and the lines cross at 288 mOsm/kg, where both give 3.44 pmol/L. Between 280 and 288, P releases more; above 288, Q does; at or below 280 both release none. Two people can give the same output by different routes, so one number cannot describe the loop.

This is a static calculation, not a prediction of how fast anyone rehydrates. Fever offers a contrast: in the standard model, the thermoregulatory set point is raised, so a person shivers and feels cold while body temperature is still normal. Most work on the prostaglandin E2 mechanism behind that shift comes from rodents.

## Thirst: acting before the blood changes

A pure feedback loop reacts to an error it has measured. **Feedforward** control responds to a disturbance that is expected. Mouse experiments show that thirst uses both.

Start with what the SFO can do. In 2015 Oka, Ye and Zuker drove excitatory SFO neurons with light in water-satiated mice, and the mice drank; driving inhibitory SFO neurons in thirsty mice cut licking by more than 80%. That shows the cells are enough to push drinking up or down. It does not show they are the only route, or that the SFO is a "thirst centre".

The next question was what these neurons do during ordinary drinking. Christopher Zimmerman, Zachary Knight and colleagues used **fibre photometry**, an optical fibre that collects the fluorescence of a calcium indicator from a whole population, to record SFO neurons that express the enzyme Nos1, in awake mice.

### Design problem

Zimmerman and colleagues (Nature, 2016) report, in water-restricted mice: SFO Nos1 population activity starts to fall at the first lick and is down within one minute, while plasma osmolality, measured by freezing-point osmometry, is still raised five minutes after water access. In salt-challenged mice, seeing water without being allowed to drink does not lower the activity, and licking an empty bottle does not lower it. Drinking concentrated saline lowers it at first, as water does, but that fall reverses after about a minute. In fasted mice, eating raises it. What do these data show, and what experiment would you do next to find where the signal comes from?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Read the timing first. The population signal falls while the blood is still concentrated, so a fall in blood osmolality cannot be what turns it off. Something that comes with drinking does, ahead of any change in the regulated variable: feedforward. The controls narrow it. Seeing water fails, so it is not the sight or expectation of water alone in this paradigm. Air-licking fails, so it is not the licking movement. Saline gives the same first drop, so the fast signal tracks fluid going in, not water as such; a slower signal reports what the fluid contains. Eating raises activity, as if the neurons anticipate the water that a meal will draw out of the blood.

Next, bypass the mouth. Infuse water or hypertonic saline straight into the stomach while you record, and sample blood at the same times. If gut water lowers activity and gut saline raises it before blood osmolality moves, the gut is reporting what came in. Then cut the abdominal vagus nerve in one group and sham-operate another, to ask whether the signal travels that way. Finally, test whether the fall in SFO activity matters: hold the neurons active with light during the infusion and see whether the water still ends thirst.

Zimmerman and colleagues did these experiments in 2019. In hydrated mice, intragastric water inhibited the neurons after 105 ± 13 s (mean ± SEM; 107 ± 15 s in dehydrated mice), hypertonic saline activated them, the response came before any detectable change in blood osmolarity, vagotomy attenuated the satiating effect of gut water, and holding the neurons active prevented that satiation.

Keep the limits. Photometry gives population calcium, not spikes and not the feeling of thirst. The work is in mice. Other sites, including the median preoptic nucleus (MnPO), integrate oral, gut and blood signals, so the result is a circuit, not a single centre.
