## Explain: a thin, choosy boundary

<WordBank terms={['selectively permeable', 'concentration gradient', 'diffusion', 'osmosis', 'facilitated diffusion', 'passive transport', 'active transport', 'hypertonic', 'hypotonic', 'isotonic', 'endocytosis', 'exocytosis']} />

Every cell has a **cell membrane**, the thin boundary that separates the inside of the cell from its surroundings.

The membrane is built from phospholipids. Each phospholipid has a head that is attracted to water and two fatty tails that are not. In water, phospholipids line up in two layers. The heads face the watery outside and the watery inside of the cell. The tails point inward, toward each other, away from water.

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/crossing-the-membrane/membrane-section.webp" width={1200} height={800} keyItems={["phospholipid heads", "fatty tails", "channel protein", "carrier protein", "receptor protein", "sugar chains"]} given={[0, 1]} alt="AI-generated illustration: a side view of a cell membrane as two rows of phospholipids with heads facing out and tails meeting in the middle, crossed by a tunnel-shaped protein, a protein holding a small cargo, and a cup-topped protein holding a signal, with branched bead chains on the outer surface. Six numbered circles mark parts for the student to name." caption="The membrane is a double layer of phospholipids with proteins in it, and each kind of part has a job. Name the six numbered parts." />

Proteins sit in this double layer, and many cross it from side to side. The parts can drift sideways, so the membrane is flexible, not a wall.

Because of this build, the membrane is **selectively permeable**: it lets some substances cross easily and others hardly at all. Small molecules such as oxygen slip through the fatty middle. Charged ions such as sodium and chloride are almost entirely blocked and cross mainly through proteins.

The membrane does three jobs.

- **Transport.** It controls which materials enter and leave the cell, and how.
- **Communication.** Receptor proteins on the surface bind signal molecules from outside, such as hormones, and set off a response inside the cell.
- **Recognition.** Chains of sugar attached to surface proteins and lipids act like name tags. They help cells tell their own kind from others, which your immune system relies on.

A screen door is one analogy: it lets air in and keeps flies out. The analogy breaks down because a screen has fixed holes, while a membrane chooses by size, charge and its proteins.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** The cell membrane is a moving double layer of lipids with proteins, and that build lets it choose what crosses, receive signals and recognize other cells.</Support>

## Explain: moving downhill with no energy

A **concentration gradient** is a difference in how much of a substance is dissolved in one place compared with another. Molecules move at random all the time, so more cross from the crowded side than come back.

**Passive transport** is movement across a membrane from higher to lower concentration that needs no energy from the cell. It has three forms.

- **Diffusion** is the spreading of a substance from where it is more concentrated to where it is less concentrated.
- **Facilitated diffusion** is diffusion through a membrane protein. Channel proteins form water-friendly tunnels, and carrier proteins grab a molecule and change shape to pass it across. Glucose enters many of your cells by carriers, still moving from high to low.
- **Osmosis** is the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane. Much of it goes through protein water channels.

Here is the rule that trips people up: water moves on balance toward the side with more dissolved solute (a substance dissolved in the water, such as salt or sugar), because that side has less free water. Three words compare a solution with the inside of a cell.

| Solution outside the cell | Plain meaning | Net water movement | Plant cell | Red blood cell |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hypertonic**: more solute than inside | Saltier outside | Out of the cell | Loses turgor; plant wilts | Shrinks |
| **Hypotonic**: less solute than inside | Less salty outside | Into the cell | Firm, pressing on its wall | Swells, may burst |
| **Isotonic**: same solute as inside | Balanced | None; water crosses both ways equally | Less firm than in hypotonic | Keeps its shape |

A plant cell has a stiff wall outside its membrane, and water flowing in makes the cell press against it. That push is **turgor pressure**, and it holds up the leaves and stems of soft, nonwoody plants. In salty water, the cells lose water and turgor, so lettuce wilts and the cucumber slice goes floppy. A red blood cell has no wall, so in pure water it can swell until it bursts.

## Explain: paying to move uphill

**Active transport** is movement across a membrane that uses energy from the cell, often from ATP, and it can move a substance against its concentration gradient. ATP is the molecule that carries usable energy around the cell, as you saw in [the respiration lesson](/lessons/respiration-and-atp/).

The best-known pump is the sodium-potassium pump in animal cells. Each cycle, it uses ATP to move three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in. Both trips go uphill: sodium is already higher outside, and potassium is already higher inside. Without ATP, the pump stops.

Loads too big for any channel or pump travel in vesicles, small bubbles of membrane. This also needs energy.

- **Endocytosis** brings material into the cell. The membrane folds inward around it, and the pocket pinches off as a vesicle inside the cell. Some white blood cells engulf bacteria this way.
- **Exocytosis** releases material from the cell. A vesicle joins the cell membrane and spills its contents outside. [The organelles lesson](/lessons/organelles-as-a-system/) follows these vesicles.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/crossing-the-membrane/endocytosis-and-exocytosis.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: two rows of three frames. Bottom: a vesicle inside the cell, the vesicle joining the membrane, and its contents spilling outside." caption="Endocytosis folds the membrane around a load and pinches off a vesicle inside the cell; exocytosis runs the other way. Seawater holds about 35 grams of dissolved salts in each kilogram, according to NOAA. That is far saltier than your body fluids, which have about the same pull on water as a 0.9% salt solution. NOAA explains that getting rid of the extra salt takes more water than the seawater supplied, so the body loses water overall.

### Design problem

Plan an investigation to find the sugar concentration at which potato pieces neither gain nor lose mass. Name your variables, constants, control and sample size, and say how you will stay safe.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Independent variable:** sucrose concentration, for example 0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 mol/L.
- **Dependent variable:** the percent change in mass of each piece.
- **Constants:** one potato, same-size pieces, the same volume of solution, temperature, soaking time and blotting method.
- **Control:** the 0.0 mol/L dish of distilled water, with no added sugar.
- **Sample size and trials:** at least three pieces in each solution, assigned at random. Find each piece's percent change, then the mean and spread.
- **Analysis:** plot mean percent change against concentration and find where the line crosses zero, between a gain and a loss. If every piece gained or every piece lost, the balance point lies outside your range.
- **Safety:** care with the knife, labelled dishes, no tasting.

The zero point estimates where the solution is isotonic to the potato cells under your conditions. It does not measure exactly what is dissolved inside the cells.

</details>
