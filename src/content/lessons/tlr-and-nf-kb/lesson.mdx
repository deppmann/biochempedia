scientists:
  - name: "Charles Janeway Jr. and Ruslan Medzhitov"
    dates: "Janeway 1943–2003; Medzhitov born 1966"
    contribution: >-
      Janeway predicted in 1989 that animals carry receptors for molecular patterns found on
      infectious agents and absent from the host. In 1997 Medzhitov, working in Janeway's
      laboratory, showed that a human relative of the fly protein Toll switches on NF-κB and
      inflammatory genes.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Janeway, Cold Spring Harbor Symp. Quant. Biol. 54:1 (1989); Medzhitov, Preston-Hurlburt & Janeway, Nature 388:394 (1997)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Janeway wrote his 1989 essay, 'Approaching the asymptote? Evolution and revolution in
      immunology', for the Cold Spring Harbor symposium. In it he asked why a purified antigen
      provokes little immunity unless it is mixed with an adjuvant, and he argued that the immune
      system evolved to recognize infection through receptors for patterns that infectious agents
      share and the host lacks. He proposed pattern-recognition receptors before the receptors he
      had in mind had been found, and the essay offered no gene to clone. Medzhitov read it as a
      biochemistry student in Moscow and later joined Janeway at Yale as a postdoctoral fellow.
      Around the time of the 1996 report that Toll protects flies against fungal infection, the two cloned a
      human relative of Toll, built a permanently active version and expressed it in human cells.
      NF-κB switched on, along with genes for inflammatory cytokines. Their 1997 paper with
      Preston-Hurlburt did not show what the receptor detects. It tied the predicted class of
      receptor to a real human protein, and other laboratories then matched most family members to
      a microbial molecule.
  - name: "Bruce Beutler"
    dates: "1957–"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory showed by positional cloning that mice unable to respond to bacterial
      lipopolysaccharide carry mutations in Tlr4, which linked a mammalian Toll-like receptor to
      a specific microbial molecule.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Poltorak et al., Science 282:2085 (1998); Beutler, Nobel Lecture, December 2011."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Mouse strains such as C3H/HeJ had been known since the 1960s to tolerate doses of bacterial
      lipopolysaccharide that send other mice into shock, and the trait mapped to one locus,
      called Lps. In 1993 Beutler's laboratory turned to positional cloning to find the gene:
      breed mice, find the chromosome interval that always travels with the trait, and read every
      gene inside it. The search took five years. In 1998 the only intact gene found in the interval was Tlr4, a mouse relative of the fly gene Toll. In C3H/HeJ mice a single base
      change replaced a conserved proline with histidine at position 712, in the part of the
      receptor inside the cell. A second unresponsive strain, C57BL/10ScCr, had lost the gene
      entirely. Alexander Poltorak was first author of the report. Two independent mutations in
      one gene made the case that TLR4 is required to sense lipopolysaccharide. That gave a
      mammalian Toll-like receptor a defined microbial target, and it set the pattern for
      assigning the others.
  - name: "Ranjan Sen and David Baltimore"
    dates: "Baltimore 1938–2025"
    contribution: >-
      In 1986 they identified NF-κB as a protein that binds an enhancer of the antibody κ
      light-chain gene and showed that it can be switched on without new protein synthesis.
      Patrick Baeuerle, in the same laboratory, then found its cytoplasmic inhibitor, IκB.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Sen & Baltimore, Cell 46:705 (1986) and Cell 47:921 (1986); Baeuerle & Baltimore, Science 242:540 (1988)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The Baltimore laboratory wanted to know how antibody genes are switched on only in the
      cells that make antibodies. In 1986 Sen, a postdoctoral fellow, found a nuclear protein that
      bound the sequence GGGACTTTCC in the enhancer of the κ light-chain gene, and the two named
      it NF-κB. A mouse cell line, 70Z/3, carried a rearranged κ gene that it did not express and
      had no detectable NF-κB DNA-binding activity. Lipopolysaccharide was known to switch that κ gene on. Sen and
      Baltimore found that lipopolysaccharide also made the DNA-binding activity of NF-κB appear,
      and that it did so when protein synthesis was blocked. The factor therefore existed
      beforehand in an inhibited form. Patrick Baeuerle located that form in the cytoplasm,
      released it in cell extracts with a detergent, and in a 1988 paper with Baltimore named the
      inhibitor IκB. The work established a way to control a gene that needs no new protein: keep
      the activator finished, hold it outside the nucleus, and remove the inhibitor. That is how
      Toll-like receptors and cytokines such as TNF reach genes within minutes.
  - name: "Zhijian Chen"
    dates: "1966–"
    contribution: >-
      Showed that a ubiquitin chain joined through lysine 63 switches on a kinase and does not
      mark a protein for destruction. His laboratory later identified cGAS, the enzyme that
      detects DNA in the cytosol.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Deng et al., Cell 103:351 (2000); Sun et al., Science 339:786 (2013)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Chen trained in the enzymology of ubiquitin with Cecile Pickart at SUNY Buffalo and later worked at a Massachusetts biotechnology company that was developing the proteasome
      inhibitor later approved as bortezomib. In work with Tom Maniatis published in 1996, he
      found that the kinase activity that phosphorylates IκBα required a ubiquitination step
      first, the reverse of the expected order. After he moved to UT Southwestern, his laboratory
      purified a missing factor from cell extracts and rebuilt the reaction in a test tube. Their 2000 paper showed that TRAF6, with the
      enzyme pair Ubc13/Uev1A, links ubiquitin through lysine 63, and that this chain activates
      the IκB kinase complex without sending anything to the proteasome. Later, Lijun Sun in his
      laboratory built a cell-free system that responded to DNA and purified from it one enzyme,
      cGAS, reported in 2013, which makes cyclic GMP-AMP when it binds DNA. The first result
      showed a lysine-63 ubiquitin chain acting as a signal that switches on a kinase. The second gave the DNA alarm of the cytosol a
      defined enzyme and a defined messenger, which made the pathway something that can be
      measured and tested.

techniques:
  - name: "Positional cloning"
    blurb: >-
      Cross a mutant strain to a normal one, follow which stretch of chromosome is always
      inherited with the trait, narrow that interval, and sequence the genes in it. It finds a
      gene from its effect alone, with no guess about what the protein is. This is how Tlr4 was
      identified as the gene behind lipopolysaccharide resistance in mice.
  - name: "Gene knockout in mice"
    blurb: >-
      Delete one gene in a mouse and ask what the animal can no longer do. A mouse lacking a
      single Toll-like receptor stops responding to one class of microbial molecule, which shows
      that the response needs that receptor. Binding assays and structures then show how the
      ligand is recognized.
  - name: "Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (gel shift)"
    blurb: >-
      Mix a short labeled piece of DNA with a cell extract and run it on a gel. DNA with a protein
      bound to it moves more slowly. The assay reports whether an active DNA-binding protein is
      present, and it is how NF-κB was detected and its switching-on followed.
  - name: "Reconstitution from purified components"
    blurb: >-
      Rebuild a reaction in a test tube from purified proteins, then leave out one at a time. If
      the reaction runs, those parts are sufficient under the conditions tested. The need for TRAF6, Ubc13/Uev1A and
      lysine-63 chains in activating IKK was shown this way, once the factors had been purified from cell extracts.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "Which property makes a molecule a good target for a pattern-recognition receptor?"
    choices:
      - "It changes rapidly as a microbe evolves inside its host"
      - "It is shared by many microbes and absent from the host"
      - "It is unique to one strain of a single bacterial species"
      - "It is made by host cells only while they are infected"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A molecule shared by many microbes and absent from the host is what Janeway predicted: one
      receptor then covers a whole class of microbes without reacting to self, and the microbe
      cannot easily discard a molecule it depends on. A molecule unique to one strain would need
      a separate receptor for every strain, which is the antibody solution. A molecule made by
      infected host cells is a host signal such as a cytokine, and it reports infection
      second-hand. A rapidly changing molecule is the worst target, because the microbe would
      escape detection by mutation.
  - stem: "In a resting macrophage, what keeps NF-κB from switching on its target genes?"
    choices:
      - "Its gene is silent until a signal arrives at the nucleus"
      - "The inhibitor IκB holds it in the cytoplasm"
      - "A lysine-63 ubiquitin chain ties it to the TRAF6 ligase"
      - "Its mRNA is stored untranslated until a signal arrives"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The inhibitor IκB is right: NF-κB is fully made and folded in a resting cell, and IκB bound
      to it covers its nuclear localization signal and keeps it in the cytoplasm; a detergent that pulls IκB off releases active NF-κB from cytoplasmic extracts. A silent gene is wrong because lipopolysaccharide switches
      NF-κB on even when no new protein can be made. Stored, untranslated mRNA is wrong for the
      same reason: blocking translation does not block activation. A lysine-63 chain tying NF-κB
      to TRAF6 misplaces the chain, which TRAF6 builds after a signal in order to gather TAK1 and
      IKK; it does not hold NF-κB.
  - stem: "Which sequence of events follows the binding of lipopolysaccharide to TLR4 and MD-2?"
    choices:
      - "MyD88 and IRAKs assemble; TRAF6 builds chains; IKK is activated; IκB is degraded"
      - "TRAF6 builds chains; IκB is degraded; MyD88 and IRAKs assemble; IKK is activated"
      - "IKK is activated; MyD88 and IRAKs assemble; IκB is degraded; TRAF6 builds chains"
      - "MyD88 and IRAKs assemble; IκB is degraded; IKK is activated; TRAF6 builds chains"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Assembly of MyD88 and the IRAKs, then chain building by TRAF6, then activation of IKK, then
      degradation of IκB is correct because each step makes what the next one needs: the tower
      activates the IRAKs, an IRAK engages TRAF6, the lysine-63 chain brings TAK1 to IKK, and IKK
      marks IκB. The order that begins with IKK activation leaves nothing upstream to activate
      IKK. The order that begins with TRAF6 building chains degrades IκB before IKK is active,
      which skips the phosphorylation that marks IκB for destruction. The order that degrades IκB
      straight after the tower assembles makes the same mistake and builds the platform after
      the event it is needed for.
  - stem: "A macrophage is treated with a drug that blocks the proteasome and then with lipopolysaccharide. What do you expect to find?"
    choices:
      - "IκB is never phosphorylated and the IKK complex stays switched off"
      - "NF-κB enters the nucleus sooner than it does in untreated cells"
      - "Lysine-63 chains are never made and TAK1 stays switched off"
      - "Phosphorylated IκB builds up and NF-κB stays in the cytoplasm"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Phosphorylated IκB builds up and NF-κB stays in the cytoplasm, because every step before
      the proteasome still works: IKK is activated and phosphorylates IκB, and IκB receives its
      lysine-48 chain, but it cannot be destroyed and so it keeps hold of NF-κB. IκB never being
      phosphorylated is wrong because IKK is activated through lysine-63 chains, which are not the
      usual proteasome signal. NF-κB entering the nucleus sooner confuses marking IκB with
      removing it; phosphorylated IκB still binds NF-κB until it is degraded. Lysine-63 chains
      never being made is wrong because TRAF6 and Ubc13/Uev1A build them with no help from the
      proteasome.
  - stem: "A child has repeated severe infections with Streptococcus pneumoniae and little fever. In the laboratory, the child's blood cells make no cytokines in response to ligands for TLR2, TLR5, TLR7 or TLR9, but they respond to a ligand for TLR3. Which defect fits best?"
    choices:
      - "Loss of the TIR domain of TLR9"
      - "Loss of the DNA sensor cGAS"
      - "Loss of the inhibitor IκBα"
      - "Loss of the adaptor MyD88"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Loss of MyD88 fits, because TLR2, TLR5, TLR7 and TLR9 all pass their signal through MyD88,
      while TLR3 uses the adaptor TRIF and is spared. Severe infections with pus-forming bacteria
      and little fever are what MyD88 and IRAK4 deficiency look like in children. Loss of the TIR
      domain of TLR9 would abolish only the response to DNA with unmethylated CG. Loss of IκBα
      would leave NF-κB unrestrained and give more cytokine, when the child makes less. Loss of
      cGAS would affect the response to DNA in the cytosol and would leave every Toll-like
      receptor working.

When the cells are first treated with cycloheximide, which blocks translation, LPS still induces the activity at full strength. Which conclusion do the data support?"
    choices:
      - "NF-κB protein is newly translated in response to LPS"
      - "NF-κB mRNA is newly transcribed in response to LPS"
      - "NF-κB protein is present before LPS but inactive"
      - "NF-κB protein is degraded more slowly after LPS"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      NF-κB protein present before LPS but inactive is the only reading that survives the
      cycloheximide result: if activity appears at full strength with translation blocked, no new
      NF-κB protein was needed, so the protein was already made and something was holding it
      inactive. New transcription of NF-κB mRNA would be useless without translation, so it
      cannot explain activity under cycloheximide. New translation is ruled out directly by the
      drug. Slower degradation cannot raise the amount of a protein while none is being made, and
      it would not turn undetectable activity into strong activity in thirty minutes.
  - stem: "Cultured cells express either normal IκBα or a mutant IκBα in which serines 32 and 36 are replaced by alanine.     choices:

      - "About 18-fold; NF-κB turns on death genes constantly"
      - "About 18-fold; NF-κB cannot turn on survival genes"
      - "About 5-fold; NF-κB turns on death genes constantly"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      TNF kills 92 − 88 = 4% of the cells with normal IκBα and 90 − 18 = 72% of the cells with
      the mutant, and 72 / 4 = 18, so the increase is about 18-fold. In the mutant IκBα the two serines IKK

      proteasome and NF-κB stays in the cytoplasm; the survival genes that NF-κB normally switches on stay off, and the death
      signal from the TNF receptor goes unopposed. The 5-fold answers come from dividing the
      surviving fractions (88 / 18) when the question asks about the fractions killed. The
      answers in which NF-κB turns on death genes constantly have the mutation backwards: it
      blocks NF-κB, and the NF-κB targets at work here protect the cell.

      - "Lysine-48 chains activate IKK by recruiting it to TRAF6"
      - "Lysine-48 chains activate IKK by degrading an inhibitor"

    rationale: >-
      Lysine-63 chains activating IKK without any proteolysis fits every result: removing lysine
      63 abolishes activity, removing lysine 48 changes nothing, and with no proteasomes in the

    text: >-
      Cells of the innate immune system carry about ten kinds of Toll-like receptor. Each one
      detects a molecule that many microbes share and cannot easily change, such as the outer
      coat of some bacteria or the RNA of a virus. When a receptor binds its target, a chain of
      proteins inside the cell passes the message along until an inhibitor protein called IκB is
      destroyed. IκB had been holding a gene-switching protein, NF-κB, in the cytoplasm. Once it

  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are pattern-recognition receptors. Their leucine-rich-repeat
      ectodomains bind conserved microbial molecules, for example lipopolysaccharide for TLR4
      with MD-2 at the cell surface and DNA with unmethylated CG for TLR9 in endosomes, and
      ligand binding pairs the cytoplasmic TIR domains. The adaptor MyD88 assembles through death
      domains with IRAK4 and IRAK1 or IRAK2. An activated IRAK engages the E3 ligase TRAF6, which
      with Ubc13/Uev1A builds ubiquitin chains linked through lysine 63. These chains gather TAK1
      and the IKK complex, and TAK1 activates IKK. IKK phosphorylates IκBα, which then receives a
      lysine-48 chain and is degraded by the proteasome, freeing NF-κB, most often the p50/RelA dimer, to enter the
      nucleus. NF-κB induces inflammatory genes such as TNF, genes that keep the cell alive, and
      the gene for its own inhibitor IκBα, which brings the response down.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      The crystallized Myddosome contains death domains in a 6:4:4 ratio of MyD88, IRAK4 and
      IRAK2, assembled in order as a helix, so receptor pairing is converted into a threshold
      event: IRAK4 kinase domains reach the local concentration needed for phosphorylation in
      trans most readily inside the assembled tower. TRAF6 with Ubc13/Uev1A makes lysine-63 chains that

      IκBα at serines 32 and 36, the mark that an SCF-type E3 ligase recognizes. Resynthesis of

**Antibodies take days to appear, and a fruit fly makes none at all—yet a fly survives on rotting fruit, and your macrophages, white blood cells that swallow microbes, alter gene expression within minutes of touching a bacterium.** The first film animated how Toll-like receptors free **NF-κB** (a DNA-binding transcription factor that activates inflammatory genes such as tumor necrosis factor, or TNF). Here are the experiments behind pattern recognition, the molecular modules inside the relay, and what happens when this alarm signals too little or too much.

## The idea: detect what a microbe cannot give up

In 1989 the Yale immunologist Charles Janeway Jr. highlighted a puzzle: a purified protein injected alone provokes little immunity without an **adjuvant**, an additive that provokes the immune system. He predicted that animals carry receptors for "certain characteristics or patterns common on infectious agents but absent from the host"—**pathogen-associated molecular patterns**, detected by **pattern-recognition receptors** not yet identified as a class.

An adrenaline receptor fits adrenaline and noradrenaline. A pattern receptor fits a whole microbial class that the microbe cannot discard—such as a bacterial lipid coat or viral double-stranded RNA—so a short list of receptors covers threats for which antibodies need billions of variants.

TLR3 and endosomal TLR4 also signal through TRIF to IRF3 and interferon, cytosolic nucleic-acid sensors such as cGAS–STING use separate relays to IRF3, and TNF receptor 1 also protects cells through an immediate transcription-independent checkpoint on RIPK1.</Support>

### From fly Toll to ten human receptors

Proof began in an animal without antibodies. In 1996 Jules Hoffmann's laboratory showed that *Toll*, a fly gene setting the embryonic back-to-belly axis, is also required for adult antifungal gene expression via fly relatives of NF-κB. In 1997 Ruslan Medzhitov and Janeway cloned a human Toll relative that, locked "on", activated NF-κB and inflammatory genes, though its trigger was unknown. In 1998 Bruce Beutler's laboratory matched that trigger by positional cloning in mice barely reacting to **lipopolysaccharide** (**LPS**, or endotoxin), the lipid-and-sugar coat of Gram-negative bacteria (those with an outer membrane): the gene was *Tlr4*. Shizuo Akira's laboratory and others matched most of the ten human TLRs to their ligands using gene deletions, binding assays and structures. Fly Toll does not bind the microbe itself but a host protein cleaved after other proteins sense the microbe; binding the microbial molecule itself, for TLR4 with its partner MD-2, is the mammalian innovation (see Going deeper for the full fly and *Tlr4* stories).

| Receptor | What it detects | Where it sits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| TLR4, with its partner MD-2 | lipopolysaccharide | cell surface |
| TLR2, paired with TLR1 or TLR6 | bacterial lipoproteins | cell surface |
| TLR5 | flagellin, the protein of bacterial flagella | cell surface |
| TLR3 | double-stranded RNA | endosome |
| TLR7 and TLR8 | single-stranded RNA | endosome |
| TLR9 | DNA with unmethylated CG sequences | endosome |

Location tracks exposure: outer microbial molecules are read at the plasma membrane, whereas nucleic acids are exposed once swallowed microbes or dying host debris are broken open in **endosomes**, the compartments holding ingested cargo.

## From receptor to gene: three proximity tricks and one destruction

The first film animated the path from receptor to DNA; here are the domains and experiments behind each handoff.

Each Toll-like receptor has a horseshoe of **leucine-rich repeats** facing the microbial ligand and a cytoplasmic **TIR domain** (named for Toll and the interleukin-1 receptor). Ligand binding pairs two receptors side by side, pairing their TIR domains into a docking site for the adaptor **MyD88**.

**Predict.** In LPS-resistant C3H/HeJ mice, TLR4 reaches the cell surface and binds LPS with MD-2 normally, yet carries one amino-acid change inside its cytoplasmic TIR domain. Where would you expect signaling to fail?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

At the first intracellular handoff: LPS still pairs the ectodomains, but the damaged TIR domain cannot properly gather the adaptors that bring MyD88, so the kinase tower never assembles and NF-κB stays in the cytoplasm.

</details>

**1. A helical tower concentrates the first kinases.** MyD88 links a TIR domain to a **death domain**, a small module that binds others of its kind. MyD88's death domains stack with those of the kinases **IRAK4** and **IRAK1** or **IRAK2** into a helical tower, packing IRAK4 domains close enough to phosphorylate one another and their IRAK partners. An activated IRAK engages the E3 ubiquitin ligase **TRAF6**.

**2. A lysine-63 ubiquitin chain acts as a scaffold.** From [ubiquitin and proteolysis](/lessons/ubiquitin-and-proteolysis), a **lysine-48** chain sends a protein to the proteasome, whereas a **lysine-63** chain is a scaffold. In 2000 Zhijian Chen's laboratory purified a missing factor from cell extracts and reconstituted this step in a test tube: TRAF6, with the two-part enzyme **Ubc13/Uev1A**, builds lysine-63 chains that activate the **IκB kinase complex (IKK)**. The **TAB2** and **TAB3** partners of the kinase **TAK1** read the lysine-63 chain, bringing TAK1 beside IKK to phosphorylate IKK's activation loop.

**Predict.** A mutation blocks TAB2 and TAB3 from binding lysine-63 chains while leaving TRAF6 and the proteasome intact. What happens to IKK activation and to IκB after LPS arrives?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

TRAF6 still builds lysine-63 chains, but TAK1 cannot dock beside IKK. IKK stays off, IκB is not phosphorylated or degraded, and NF-κB remains trapped in the cytoplasm.

</details>

**3. Inhibitor destruction frees pre-made NF-κB.** **NF-κB**, a pair of Rel-family proteins, waits folded in the cytoplasm under **IκB**, whose **ankyrin repeats** (a curved protein-binding fold) cover NF-κB's nuclear localization signal. Active IKK phosphorylates IκBα at **serines 32 and 36**. That mark recruits a second E3 ligase that adds a **lysine-48** chain, sending IκB to the proteasome. Freed NF-κB enters the nucleus within minutes—no transcription or translation needed.

> **TLR pair → MyD88 → IRAK4 and IRAK1/2 → TRAF6 → lysine-63 chain → TAK1 → IKK → IκB destroyed → NF-κB enters the nucleus**

The pre-made pool was proved before TLRs were known. In 1986 Ranjan Sen and David Baltimore identified NF-κB binding a ten-base sequence in the immunoglobulin κ light-chain enhancer; in a cell line lacking the activity, LPS induced DNA binding even with protein synthesis blocked. The protein must already have been there, inhibited. In 1988 Patrick Baeuerle located the inactive pool in the cytoplasm, freed it with detergent, and named IκB.

**Refining the wiring.** Two refinements sit atop this core route. First, every TLR uses MyD88 except **TLR3**, which uses **TRIF** to drive interferon genes; TLR4 and TLR2 use the sorting adaptor **TIRAP** to recruit MyD88, and once internalized into an endosome TLR4 switches to TRIF via a second sorting adaptor, **TRAM**. Second, in cells related **Pellino** ligases can also make lysine-63 chains, and the IKK subunit **NEMO** also binds head-to-tail (linear) ubiquitin chains alongside lysine-63 chains, so in cells the two kinds of chain work together.

Once NF-κB reaches DNA, the cell faces a second problem: an alarm strong enough to clear bacteria can also kill cells or injure the host.

## Ending the response

Recall the second half of the guiding rule: NF-κB drives defense, survival and brakes, balancing protection against host injury. One gene that NF-κB switches on encodes **IκBα**, its own inhibitor; new IκBα enters the nucleus, takes NF-κB off the DNA and carries it back out. NF-κB also switches on **A20**, an enzyme that strips the lysine-63 chains and so shuts the pathway off further upstream. These two brakes implement the delayed negative feedback of [signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits), driving nuclear NF-κB in waves under a lasting stimulus.

**Predict.** Under continuous LPS, a cell degrades its starting IκBα normally, but the NF-κB binding site in the *IκBα* gene's promoter is deleted. How does nuclear NF-κB behave over time?

Initial nuclear entry is normal because freeing pre-made NF-κB needs no transcription. Afterward, without fresh IκBα to pull NF-κB off DNA, nuclear NF-κB stays high instead of dropping in waves (restrained only partially by upstream brakes such as A20).

</details>

## "The anti-death, death pathway": one receptor says die and stay alive

NF-κB also induces **tumor necrosis factor (TNF)**, a **cytokine** (a secreted immune signaling protein). TNF binds **TNF receptor 1** on the same cell and its neighbors, activating IKK and NF-κB to spread the alarm.

The TNF receptor 1 pathway earns the name "the anti-death, death pathway": the receptor is a death receptor, one that can instruct a cell to kill itself, yet it normally spares the cell. Two molecular checkpoints hold the death machinery back:

1. **Immediate receptor-level brake (no transcription needed):** at TNF receptor 1 itself, ubiquitination and phosphorylation of the kinase **RIPK1** keep it from acting.
2. **Delayed NF-κB survival shield:** NF-κB induces genes whose products block the death machinery.

Survival normally wins; in cultured cells with NF-κB blocked—for instance by an IκBα mutant that cannot be phosphorylated at serines 32 and 36—the same TNF kills. The death arm belongs to a later lesson.

## A second alarm: nucleic acid in the wrong place

Both films focused on TLRs and NF-κB, yet viruses lack cell walls and carry nucleic acid made of the host's own four nucleotides. Cells therefore monitor both endosomes and the cytosol, where **RIG-I** and **MDA5** catch viral RNA and signal through **MAVS** to interferon genes, alongside a cytosolic DNA sensor.

Purified in Zhijian Chen's laboratory and reported in 2013, **cGAS** binds double-stranded DNA of any sequence and joins GTP and ATP into **cyclic GMP-AMP**, a ring second messenger like [cAMP](/lessons/camp-and-pka). It binds **STING** in the endoplasmic reticulum membrane; activated STING moves toward the Golgi, recruits the kinase **TBK1**, and activates **IRF3** to switch on interferon genes, warning neighbors via the [JAK-STAT pathway](/lessons/jak-stat).

## Too little and too much

Clinical cases sharpen the balance the second film sketched.

**Too little signal.** Children lacking working MyD88 or IRAK4 suffer repeated, severe infections with pus-forming bacteria such as *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Staphylococcus aureus*, often with little fever, yet resist most other microbes and improve as they grow up.

**Too much signal.** In some Gram-negative infections, circulating LPS triggers widespread TNF and other mediators that widen and leak blood vessels, dropping blood pressure and injuring organs in **septic shock**. Human sepsis is broader than endotoxin shock: it often lacks a Gram-negative cause, combines inflammatory injury with immune suppression, and drugs blocking TLR4 or TNF have not improved patient survival. Much injury comes from the host's own alarm—and LPS-blind mice show both sides, resisting purified endotoxin yet dying far more easily from live Gram-negative infection.

**Controlled signal.** **Monophosphoryl lipid A**, a modified LPS fragment activating TLR4 with far less toxicity, serves as part of the adjuvant in licensed vaccines against human papillomavirus and shingles—one answer to Janeway's 1989 question.

### Design problem

A virus is built from the same four nucleotides as the cell that it infects. Design a way for a cell to detect the nucleic acid of a virus and ignore its own, using only where you place the receptor and one or two chemical differences. Then say what you would expect to go wrong in a person whose own DNA leaks into the wrong compartment.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with location. Put receptors inside endosomes, facing inward. A cell keeps its own DNA in the nucleus and mitochondria, while a swallowed virus is taken apart in the endosome and its nucleic acid is exposed there. TLR3, TLR7, TLR8 and TLR9 sit here.

Then add chemistry. TLR9 binds DNA in which the sequence CG is unmethylated. That is common in bacterial DNA and in many viral DNAs and rare in yours, where CG is scarce and usually methylated. TLR3 binds long double-stranded RNA, which a healthy cell makes little of and many viruses make as they copy their genomes.

In the cytosol, exposure does most of the work: long, free double-stranded DNA does not belong there. It is not location alone, since much of the sensor sits in the nucleus held on chromatin, and DNA packaging, clearance and control of the sensor keep it quiet. The sensor described above, cGAS, binds DNA of any sequence.

The weak point is the assumption that your own DNA stays put. If it leaks into the cytosol, the sensor fires with no infection and the cell makes interferon without stopping. People who lack TREX1, a nuclease that clears DNA from the cytosol, develop an inflammatory disease, Aicardi-Goutières syndrome, that resembles a viral infection present from birth.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Toll in the fly, in full

In 1996, in Jules Hoffmann's Strasbourg laboratory, Bruno Lemaitre showed that adult flies with a disabled Toll pathway failed to express the antifungal peptide drosomycin and died after *Aspergillus fumigatus* infection. Fly Toll does not contact the fungus directly: it binds Spatzle, a host protein cleaved into active form by a protease cascade triggered when other fly proteins detect fungal and bacterial cell walls.

### Cloning Tlr4, in full

Since the 1960s, inbred mouse strains had been known to tolerate lethal LPS doses via a single locus. Bruce Beutler's laboratory used **positional cloning**—reading every gene in the chromosome stretch inherited with the trait—and in 1998 (Alexander Poltorak, first author) identified *Tlr4*: C3H/HeJ mice carried one cytoplasmic amino-acid substitution, whereas C57BL/10ScCr mice had lost *Tlr4* entirely. Danielle Malo's group reported the same gene early in 1999, and Shizuo Akira's laboratory confirmed that *Tlr4*-knockout mice barely respond to LPS.

### The Rel family

Dorsal, Dif and Relish in the fly; RelA, RelB and c-Rel, along with p50 and p52, in mammals.
