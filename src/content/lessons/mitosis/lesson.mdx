techniques:
  - name: "Live-cell time-lapse microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Label chromosomes, kinetochores or microtubules with fluorescent proteins and image the
      same cell every few seconds or minutes through division. It gives positions over time, so
      you can measure rates and follow the fate of a single chromosome; Orr and colleagues
      sampled human cells every 30 seconds.
  - name: "Laser microsurgery"
    blurb: >-
      Focus a laser to destroy a chosen structure, such as one kinetochore, in a living cell and
      watch what happens next. It tests whether a structure is needed for an event, provided a
      lesion placed nearby but off the target is used as a control.
  - name: "Laser-trap force clamp"
    blurb: >-
      Hold a bead attached to a purified kinetochore particle in a focused laser beam and keep
      the force on it constant while it tracks a dynamic microtubule end. It measures how long
      an attachment survives at each force, with no ATP present, so the particles cannot be phosphorylated.
  - name: "Correlative light and electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Film a cell live, fix it at a chosen moment, then section the same cell for electron
      microscopy. The light movie gives the stage and the history; the electron micrographs show
      which microtubules end at which kinetochore.

**In the dividing human HeLa and RPE1 cells measured in 2019, most spindle microtubules were not made at the centrosomes.** This lesson builds the spindle, attaches and tests every chromosome, cuts the glue that holds sisters together, and then measures what happens from a movie.

You arrive at mitosis with each chromosome duplicated into two sister chromatids held together by cohesin, a protein ring that encircles DNA. CDK1-cyclin B has driven the cell into mitosis (see [cell-cycle control](/lessons/cell-cycle-control/)) and the nuclear envelope has broken down. The task now is to give each daughter one sister of every chromosome. How chromosomes pair and separate in meiosis, and what happens to an organism when the count goes wrong, belong to [chromosomes and meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/) and [chromosome changes](/lessons/chromosome-changes/).

## Building a bipolar spindle from several sources

The spindle is a bipolar array of microtubules, the dynamic polymers described in [cytoskeletal polymers and dynamics](/lessons/cytoskeleton-dynamics/). Their minus ends gather at the two poles and many plus ends point toward the chromosomes. A kinetochore is the protein machine built on each sister's centromere that couples the chromosome to growing and shrinking microtubule tips. A k-fibre is the bundle of microtubules that connects one kinetochore to a pole.

The classic model, search-and-capture, has centrosomes fire microtubules in random directions until a plus end happens to hit a kinetochore and is stabilized. Like casting fishing lines at random, it works, but alone it would be slow. Other sources add microtubules:

- **Near chromatin and kinetochores.** In human RPE1 cells, Sikirzhytski and colleagues used correlative light and electron microscopy and found short non-centrosomal microtubules near kinetochores from the start of spindle assembly. End-on attachments formed within the first three minutes of prometaphase. Inhibiting CENP-E, a kinetochore kinesin (see [molecular motors](/lessons/molecular-motors/)), impeded the conversion of side-on contacts into end-on attachments.
- **Branching on existing microtubules.** Augmin is a protein complex that recruits the gamma-tubulin ring complex (gamma-TuRC), the template that starts new microtubules, onto the side of an existing one, so a new microtubule branches off in the same direction. In HeLa and RPE1 cells, David and colleagues tracked growing ends and found that most spindle microtubules nucleated away from centrosomes; augmin-dependent amplification almost entirely explained where the growing ends were. In vitro, purified human augmin and gamma-TuRC were sufficient for branching, with NEDD1 bridging the two and phosphorylation by CDK1 and PLK1 needed for NEDD1 to bind augmin.

Keep the scope: these numbers come from two human cell lines and a test tube. Some cells, such as many oocytes, build spindles without centrosomes at all, and the share from each route differs between cell types.

## Attaching and correcting: kinetochores, Aurora B and tension

The right outcome is biorientation: sister kinetochores attached to opposite poles. Three wrong outcomes are common in early mitosis. In a monotelic attachment one sister is attached and the other is free. In a syntelic attachment both sisters face the same pole. In a merotelic attachment a single kinetochore is bound by microtubules from both poles.

Biorientation, unlike syntelic or monotelic attachment, puts the sisters under tension, because the two poles pull against the cohesin that holds the sisters together. Two mechanisms use that difference.

**Aurora B destabilizes.** Aurora B is a kinase that sits at the inner centromere, between the sister kinetochores, as part of the chromosomal passenger complex. It phosphorylates outer kinetochore proteins, including the microtubule-binding Ndc80 complex, which lowers their grip on microtubules. Lampson and colleagues blocked Aurora kinase activity in living vertebrate cells with a reversible inhibitor, let wrong attachments pile up, then washed the inhibitor out. Reactivated Aurora kinase corrected the errors by selectively disassembling kinetochore-microtubule fibres. How tension turns Aurora B's effect off is still argued. One popular model says stretching pulls the outer kinetochore out of reach of the kinase at the inner centromere; other evidence points to changes in kinetochore structure under force.

**Tension stabilizes.** Akiyoshi and colleagues removed the cell entirely. Purified budding-yeast kinetochore particles, held on microtubules in a laser trap, stayed attached longer at higher force: a mean of 21 plus or minus 5 minutes at 1 piconewton and 50 plus or minus 17 minutes at 5 piconewtons, with no detectable Aurora B and no ATP.

Keep the evidence separate: the in vitro result shows that force can stabilize attachment directly. It does not show that Aurora B is unnecessary in cells, where both mechanisms act together.

Correct attachment also satisfies the spindle-assembly checkpoint. An unattached kinetochore produces a signal that restrains APC/C-Cdc20 until every kinetochore is attached; the wiring of that signal belongs to [checkpoints](/lessons/checkpoints/).

## The one-way switch: securin, separase and cohesin

The APC/C, or anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome, is an E3 ubiquitin ligase: it tags target proteins with chains of ubiquitin that send them to the proteasome (the chemistry is in [ubiquitin and proteolysis](/lessons/ubiquitin-and-proteolysis/)). When the checkpoint releases it, APC/C bound to its coactivator Cdc20 tags two key targets.

1. **Securin.** Securin binds separase and keeps it inactive. Once securin is destroyed, separase is free.
2. **Cyclin B.** Its destruction inactivates CDK1, which lets the cell leave mitosis.

Separase is a cysteine protease. It cuts the kleisin subunit of cohesin, Scc1 (called Rad21 in vertebrates), and the ring opens. Note the division of labour: the APC/C and the proteasome destroy securin; separase cuts cohesin. Separase does not degrade securin.

Uhlmann and colleagues tested whether cutting Scc1 is enough. In budding yeast arrested at metaphase, where separase is held inactive, causing Scc1 to be cleaved was sufficient to separate and segregate the sisters. A cut protein cannot be rejoined, so anaphase cannot be reversed.

Vertebrates do not wait for separase to remove all their cohesin. During prophase and prometaphase the bulk of cohesin on chromosome arms leaves without Scc1 cleavage. Hauf and colleagues showed in human cultured cells, including HeLa, that phosphorylation of the cohesin subunit SA2 was essential for this prophase pathway. Centromeric cohesin is protected by shugoshin. When McGuinness and colleagues depleted shugoshin from HeLa cells, centromeric cohesin was lost early, and their results suggested that shugoshin works by preventing SA2 phosphorylation at centromeres. So in a vertebrate metaphase chromosome the sisters are held mainly at the centromere, and that is the cohesin separase cuts. In budding yeast, by contrast, most cohesin is cleaved at anaphase.

## Anaphase A and B, read from a movie

Anaphase has two movements. **Anaphase A** is the movement of chromosomes toward their poles. **Anaphase B** is the separation of the poles themselves. In anaphase A, the k-fibres shorten. Gorbsky, Sammak and Borisy bleached a mark on kinetochore microtubules in cultured mammalian cells and found that the chromosomes moved toward the mark: the fibres lost subunits mainly at the kinetochore, though a small loss elsewhere was not excluded. In anaphase B, in many animal cells, motors slide apart the overlapping microtubules in the spindle midzone and dynein at the cell cortex pulls on astral microtubules.

### Worked problem: two rates from one movie

The numbers here are illustrative. You film a cultured mammalian cell with labelled poles and kinetochores. At anaphase onset (t = 0) the poles are 12.0 um apart and each sister kinetochore is 5.5 um from its pole. At t = 4.0 min each kinetochore is 1.5 um from its pole and the poles are 16.0 um apart. Assume the spindle is straight and movement is steady.

**Step 1, anaphase A.** Kinetochore-to-pole distance fell from 5.5 to 1.5 um in 4.0 min.

> anaphase A rate = (5.5 - 1.5) / 4.0 = 1.0 um/min

**Step 2, anaphase B.** Pole-to-pole distance rose from 12.0 to 16.0 um.

> anaphase B rate = (16.0 - 12.0) / 4.0 = 1.0 um/min of pole separation

Each pole moved 0.5 um/min relative to the spindle centre.

**Step 3, sister separation.** The distance between sisters is the pole distance minus both kinetochore-to-pole distances. At t = 0 it is 12.0 - 2(5.5) = 1.0 um. At t = 4.0 min it is 16.0 - 2(1.5) = 13.0 um. The rate is 12.0 / 4.0 = 3.0 um/min.

**Check.** Each sister moves toward its pole and the poles move apart, so sister separation should equal twice anaphase A plus anaphase B: 2(1.0) + 1.0 = 3.0 um/min. It does.

A movie that tracks only chromosomes cannot tell you how much of their separation came from the poles.

## When segregation fails: merotely, laggards and micronuclei

A merotelic kinetochore is attached, so it produces no checkpoint signal. Cimini and colleagues showed in PtK1 cells, a rat kangaroo kidney line, that merotelic attachment escaped the checkpoint. Lagging single chromatids appeared in 1.16% of untreated anaphases and 17.55% after recovery from a nocodazole block, and electron microscopy confirmed microtubules from both poles on the lagging kinetochores.

A lagging chromosome is a trajectory, not an outcome. It can reach the main nucleus late or end up in a micronucleus, a small separate nucleus. In human RPE1 and U2OS cells, Orr and colleagues found that most transient laggards rejoined the main nucleus; a midzone Aurora B mechanism helped correct errors in anaphase and delayed nuclear-envelope assembly on laggards. How persistent segregation errors feed chromosomal instability in tumours belongs to [cancer genetics](/lessons/cancer-genetics/); how spindle orientation sets cell fate is in [asymmetric division](/lessons/asymmetric-division/), and how mitotic defects in neural progenitors shrink the brain is in [microcephaly and malformations](/lessons/microcephaly-and-malformations/). The final split of the cell is [cytokinesis](/lessons/cytokinesis/).

**Worked problem, continued: watch the denominators.** Orr and colleagues saw at least one transient laggard in 9% of RPE1 cells. That number is per cell. They found that 6% of lagging chromosomes formed micronuclei. That number is per chromosome. If every affected cell had exactly one laggard, the fraction of all cells with a laggard-derived micronucleus would be 0.09 x 0.06 = 0.0054, about 0.5%. If some cells had two or more laggards, the true figure is higher. Say the assumption out loud whenever you multiply rates with different denominators.

### Design problem

Is anaphase held back by a signal from unattached kinetochores, or by lack of tension? Rieder and colleagues destroyed the last unattached kinetochore in PtK1 cells, and anaphase followed in a reported 20 plus or minus 2.5 minutes, without the damaged chromosome moving to the spindle middle. Li and Nicklas pulled on a mono-oriented chromosome with a microneedle in praying-mantis spermatocytes, and the delay ended. Do these results contradict each other, and what experiment would separate the two ideas?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

They need not contradict each other. Tension stabilizes attachment, so in a cell, pulling on a kinetochore also tends to make it fully occupied, and a fully occupied kinetochore stops signalling. Occupancy and tension are coupled.

To separate them, change one while holding the other fixed. In vitro, reconstituted yeast kinetochores in a laser trap (Akiyoshi 2010) let you set force while the number of attached microtubules is known. In cells, you could ablate the last unattached kinetochore and compare it with a cell in which that kinetochore is held attached but under low tension, for example after a drug that dampens spindle forces.

Readout: time from last attachment, or from the lesion, to anaphase onset in single cells. Control: irradiate a spot of the same size near the kinetochore but off it, which should not release the delay. Limit: mantis spermatocytes in meiosis and PtK1 cells in mitosis are different systems, so a difference between the two papers may reflect the cell, not the mechanism.
