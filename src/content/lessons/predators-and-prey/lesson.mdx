      Wolves in a pack hunt together, share the prey they catch, and help raise the pups. Making a

      When prey become scarce, predators run short of food, so fewer predators survive and raise

## Explain: populations, communities and food webs

<WordBank terms={['population', 'community', 'predator', 'prey', 'food web', 'first-order consumer', 'second-order consumer', 'herd', 'pack']} />

A **population** is all the members of one species living in one place at the same time. All the moose on Isle Royale make up one population. All the wolves make up another.

A **community** is all the populations that live together in one place. On Isle Royale, the community includes the wolves, the moose, the fir trees, beavers, birds and many more. The populations in a community depend on one another.

A **predator** is an animal that catches and eats other animals. The animal it eats is its **prey**. Moose are the main prey of the island's wolves.

You met food chains in [Food Webs and Energy](/lessons/food-webs-and-energy/). A **first-order consumer** eats producers, and a **second-order consumer** eats first-order consumers. A moose that eats fir trees is a first-order consumer. A wolf that eats the moose is a second-order consumer.

> balsam fir → moose → wolf

A **food web** links many food chains to show who eats whom in a community. Each arrow points from the food to the eater. It shows which way energy moves. When a wolf eats a moose, some of the energy stored in the moose passes to the wolf. Every animal gives off some of its energy as heat.

Virginia has predators and prey too. In the Chesapeake Bay, ospreys dive for menhaden, a small fish.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A population is one species in one place, a community is all the populations living together, and a food web shows who eats whom.</Support>

## Explain: how predators and prey change each other's numbers

Predators and prey affect each other's numbers. Here is the pattern scientists often see:

- When prey are plentiful, predators find food easily. More predators survive and raise young, so the predator population grows.
- More predators catch more prey, so the prey population falls.
- With less prey to eat, predators run short of food. Fewer survive, and the predator population falls.
- With fewer predators hunting, more prey survive. The prey population grows again.

So the predator numbers often rise and fall a little after the prey numbers. The predators usually run short of food before the last prey animal is caught.

{/* DIAGRAM: a loop of four arrows showing the predator and prey cycle, with numbered parts: 1 many moose, 2 wolves increase, 3 fewer moose, 4 wolves decrease, and back to 1 */}

Real populations are messier than this pattern. On Isle Royale, harsh winters, ticks, disease and the moose's plant food also change the counts. Scientists report that the island's wolf and moose numbers do not follow one simple pattern. Predators are one cause of change, not the only one.

Other limits on populations, such as competition for food and space, come in [Competition and Cooperation](/lessons/competition-and-cooperation/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Predators and prey help control each other's numbers, so the predator population often rises and falls a little after the prey population.</Support>

## Explain: herds and packs

Many animals live in groups. These social behaviors help them get what they need.

A **herd** is a group of plant-eating animals that live and move together. More eyes can spot danger, and adults can guard young. Muskoxen, large shaggy plant eaters of the far north, give one example. When several predators come near, adults can form a circle with their young inside. Moose mostly live alone, so they do not do this.

A **pack** is a group of predators, such as wolves, that live and hunt together. On Isle Royale, wolves often hunt moose as a pack. Pack members share the prey they catch. They also help care for the pups, and, once the pups stop drinking their mother's milk, they feed them meat that they bring up from their own stomachs.

| Group | Who | How the group helps |
|---|---|---|
| Herd | Muskoxen and other plant eaters | Spot danger and guard the young |
| Pack | Wolves | Hunt large prey, share it, raise pups |

### Design problem

Does living in a group protect prey? You can test this with a model. Spread 30 paper squares, each 2 centimeters on a side, on a square of floor 1 meter by 1 meter. They are the prey. Drop a 10 centimeter cardboard square from 1 meter high. It is the predator. Any prey it touches is caught. Plan a fair test that compares prey in one tight group with prey spread out.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Are prey in a tight group caught on fewer drops than prey spread out?
- **Independent variable:** how the prey are placed, in one tight group or spread out evenly.
- **Dependent variables:** the number of drops that catch any prey, and the number of prey caught on each drop.
- **Constants:** 30 prey squares, the same field, the same predator card, the same drop height of 1 meter, the same person dropping with eyes closed.
- **Control:** the spread-out field, which you compare the group against.
- **Trials:** 20 drops for each setup. Put caught prey back after each drop. Repeat the whole test three times and find the mean.
- **Safety:** drop only paper and cardboard, keep the area clear, and pick up all pieces.

A tight group is likely to be caught on fewer drops, but some drops may catch many prey at once. This model has limits. Real predators aim, and real herds watch and move.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The wolves on Isle Royale have not eaten every last moose. When moose become scarce, wolves can run short of food, so fewer may survive and the moose can recover. When moose are plentiful, wolves can increase. Weather, food, disease, and people moving wolves also changed the numbers.
