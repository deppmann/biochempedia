> **The one idea: wolves usually run short of food before the moose run out.** When there are few moose for each wolf, wolves tend to catch moose less often. If they get too little food, fewer wolves survive and raise pups, so the moose can recover.
>
> *What the film left out:* moose for each wolf is a rough clue, not a measure of what wolves eat. It is one number for the whole island, not each wolf's share. By itself it cannot show that wolves are hungry, so keep that in mind each time you read "few moose for each wolf" below. The rule fits a predator that eats mostly one kind of prey, in a place like Isle Royale where neither animal can easily leave or arrive. A predator with other food may not go hungry, so it could eat one prey down to nothing. Weather, ticks, disease, inbreeding (close relatives having young together) and people moving wolves also change the counts.

## Explain: populations, communities and food webs

<WordBank terms={['population', 'community', 'predator', 'prey', 'food web', 'first-order consumer', 'second-order consumer', 'herd', 'pack']} />

The first film defined **population**, **predator** and **prey**.

A **community** is all the populations that live together in one place. On Isle Royale, the community includes the wolves, the moose, the fir trees, beavers, birds and many more. The populations in a community depend on one another.

A **food web** links many food chains. A moose eats producers, the plants that make their own food, so it is a **first-order consumer** ([Food Webs and Energy](/lessons/food-webs-and-energy/)). A wolf that eats the moose is a **second-order consumer**.

> balsam fir → moose → wolf

When a wolf eats a moose, only part of the moose's energy passes to the wolf. Every animal gives off some energy as heat, lost to the chain. So each wolf needs many moose over its life, and the island can feed far fewer wolves than moose.

## Explain: herds and packs

Many animals live in groups, and these social behaviors help them get what they need. A **herd** is a group of plant-eating animals that live and move together. More eyes can spot danger, and adults can guard young. When predators come near, adult muskoxen, large shaggy plant eaters of the far north, can form a circle with their young inside. Moose mostly live alone, so they do not.

A **pack** is a group of predators, such as wolves, that live and hunt together. Pack members share the prey they catch and help care for the pups.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/predators-and-prey/muskox-herd-and-wolf-pack.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: on the left, adult muskoxen stand in a ring facing outward with two calves inside while wolves approach. On the right, a wolf pack feeds together on a moose, and an adult wolf at a den lowers its head to feed two pups." caption="A herd can guard its young, and a pack hunts together, shares the prey and raises pups." />

| Herd | Muskoxen and other plant eaters | Spot danger and guard the young |
| Pack | Wolves | Hunt large prey, share it, raise pups |

### Design problem

Does living in a group protect prey? Test it with a model. The prey are 30 paper squares, each 2 centimeters on a side, on a square of floor 1 meter by 1 meter. The predator is a 10 centimeter cardboard square dropped from 1 meter high. Any prey the card touches is found, but the predator eats only one prey per drop. That is a rule of the model, not true of every real predator. Plan a fair test that compares prey in one tight group with prey spread out.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do prey in a tight group lose fewer members than prey spread out?
- **Independent variable:** how the prey are placed, in one tight group or spread out evenly.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of prey eaten (one for each drop that touches any prey).
- **Constants:** 30 prey, the same field, card and drop height, the same person dropping with eyes closed from random spots all over the field.
- **Control:** the spread-out field, which you compare the group against.
- **Trials:** 50 drops for each setup, or pool the class's drops, since a tight group is rarely hit. Put caught prey back after each drop.
- **Safety:** drop only paper and cardboard, and pick up all pieces.

If drops land at random all over the field, a tight group is hit on far fewer drops. With the one-prey rule, it then loses fewer prey. Without that rule, one drop can catch many grouped prey, and the group can lose about as many in all. Real predators aim, though, and real herds watch and move.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Weather and food versus wolves

One long study found that weather and food explained more of the year-to-year change in moose numbers than wolves did. A study of records like this can show which factors go with the changes, but it cannot by itself prove a cause. Predators are one cause of change, not the only one.

### Virginia connection: the same rule?

In the Chesapeake Bay, ospreys dive for menhaden, a small fish. Would the rule fit them? Ask first whether the predator eats mostly one kind of prey.

### How pack wolves feed their pups

Adults feed the pups meat that they bring up from their own stomachs, starting while the pups are still drinking their mother's milk.
