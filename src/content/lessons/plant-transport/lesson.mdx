scientists:
  - name: "Henry H. Dixon and John Joly"
    contribution: >-
      Proposed the cohesion theory of sap ascent: evaporation from leaves pulls water up the
      xylem, and the cohesion of water lets the column hold together under tension.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Dixon and Joly, On the ascent of sap, Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London B 186:563-576 (1895); an earlier note in Annals of Botany (1894)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the 1890s botanists knew that water rose through the wood of trees, but not what lifted
      it. Henry Dixon, a botanist, and John Joly, a physicist, both working in Dublin, put forward
      an answer built on the cohesion of water itself (the hydrogen-bond explanation below is the modern one). Water molecules cling to one another through
      hydrogen bonds, so a thin, unbroken column of water inside a narrow tube can be pulled
      without snapping, the way a rope transmits a pull from one end to the other. On their idea,
      water evaporating from the wet walls of leaf cells creates a pull, and that pull is passed
      down continuous water columns through the stem to the roots. The living cells of the stem
      do not push the water; the column is under tension, below atmospheric pressure. They set
      out the argument in a short note in 1894 and a longer paper in 1895. The cohesion-tension
      theory, tested and refined for more than a century since, is the framework most modern
      measurement of xylem tension is read against.
  - name: "Ernst Münch"
    contribution: >-
      Proposed the pressure-flow (mass-flow) hypothesis of phloem transport: sugar loaded at a
      source raises the pressure there, removal at a sink lowers it, and sap flows between them.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Münch, Die Stoffbewegungen in der Pflanze (Gustav Fischer, Jena, 1930)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Ernst Münch was a German forest botanist. In a book published in Jena in 1930 he set out in full an
      explanation for how sugar travels through the phloem that is still the standard one. He
      pictured two compartments joined by a tube, each wrapped in a membrane that lets water
      through but holds sugar back, and both sitting in water. Put a strong sugar solution in one
      compartment and water rushes in by osmosis, raising its pressure. That pressure pushes the
      solution along the tube toward the other compartment. In the plant, he argued, a leaf making
      sugar is the first compartment, a growing root or fruit using sugar is the second, and the
      sieve tubes of the phloem are the connecting tube, with the xylem supplying the water. The
      idea needs no pump organ and predicts that sap flows from wherever sugar is added to wherever
      it is taken out, which can be up the stem or down it. Testing whether the pressures in real
      plants are large enough took most of the next century.
  - name: "Michael Knoblauch and N. Michele Holbrook"
    contribution: >-
      With colleagues, measured sieve-tube pressure, sap viscosity, flow speed and tube anatomy in
      the same morning glory plants and found strong support for pressure flow as the mechanism of
      phloem transport there.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Knoblauch, Knoblauch, Mullendore, Savage, Babst and colleagues (including Holbrook), Testing the Münch hypothesis of long distance phloem transport in plants, eLife 5:e15341 (2016)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      For decades the main objection to Münch's idea was arithmetic. Sieve tubes are narrow and
      blocked at intervals by perforated end walls, so pushing sap along many metres of them
      seemed to demand more pressure than living cells could hold. Michael Knoblauch, then listing
      Washington State University as his address, and colleagues including N. Michele Holbrook
      tested the objection in one plant, the morning glory Ipomoea nil. They measured the
      pressure inside working sieve tubes with tiny pressure probes, estimated sap viscosity from
      fluorescence lifetimes, timed sugar movement with a carbon-11 tracer, and measured the tubes
      under the microscope. Then they lengthened the path by removing leaves from long vines. If
      the tubes stayed the same, the pressure needed would have soared. Instead both changed: source turgor rose from about 0.75 MPa in young plants to more
      than 2.2 MPa in the longest vine, and its sieve tubes conducted five to six times better, so
      the estimated reach of pressure flow matched the length of the vine. The study turned an old argument into a set of measured
      numbers for one species.
  - name: "Tobias D. Wheeler and Abraham D. Stroock"
    contribution: >-
      Built a synthetic tree from a hydrogel with microchannels in which evaporation drove water
      flow at pressures of -1.0 MPa or lower, showing that cohesion-tension transport works without
      any living tissue.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Wheeler and Stroock, The transpiration of water at negative pressures in a synthetic tree, Nature 455:208-212 (2008)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The cohesion-tension theory says something odd: water in a tree is pulled, not pushed, and it
      stays liquid even though its pressure is below zero. Critics had long asked whether water
      under that much tension would simply boil into vapour or break into bubbles. Tobias Wheeler
      and Abraham Stroock, engineers, answered by building the mechanism out of something that had
      never been alive. They made a slab of hydrogel, a water-filled polymer, with fine channels
      running through it. One end, the "leaf", lost water by evaporation into air that was not
      saturated with vapour. The other end, the "root", sat in liquid water. Water flowed through
      the channels from root to leaf, and the liquid inside held at pressures of -1.0 MPa or lower.
      No cell, pump or energy supply beyond evaporation was involved. The device showed that the
      physics Dixon and Joly proposed can move water by itself, and it opened a line of work on
      synthetic plant-like plumbing.

techniques:
  - name: "Scholander pressure chamber"
    blurb: >-
      A cut leaf or twig is sealed in a steel chamber with its cut end poking out, and gas pressure
      is raised until sap just appears at the cut surface. The pressure needed balances the tension
      the xylem water was under, so it gives an estimate of the shoot's water potential.
  - name: "Pressure probes in sieve tubes"
    blurb: >-
      A fine glass tip is pushed into a single living sieve tube. The tip holds a tiny trapped volume of oil,
      and how far that oil is squeezed shows the pressure (the pico-gauge Knoblauch and colleagues used). The probe reads the turgor pressure
      inside a working cell rather than inferring it from the tissue.
  - name: "Radiotracers and aphid stylets"
    blurb: >-
      A leaf fed carbon dioxide labelled with carbon-11 makes labelled sugar, and detectors along the
      stem time its arrival, giving a flow speed. Separately, an aphid feeding from a sieve tube can
      be cut away from its mouthparts; sap keeps oozing from the severed stylet, which shows the
      sieve tube is under positive pressure and yields nearly pure phloem sap.
  - name: "X-ray microtomography"
    blurb: >-
      X-ray images taken from many angles are combined into a three-dimensional picture of a stem.
      Air-filled and water-filled xylem conduits look different, so the same intact stem can be
      scanned before and after a treatment to count embolisms.

**The tallest measured coast redwoods (Sequoia sempervirens) stand more than 100 m high, and every leaf at the top is supplied mainly with water from roots in the soil.** The first film showed the force comes from the top. This page adds where that pull fails, the valve that controls it, and the other half: sugar, which is pushed.

You met the two tissues in [plant body and growth](/lessons/plant-body-and-growth/): **xylem**, whose dead, hollow cells carry water and minerals up from the roots, and **phloem**, whose living cells carry sugar from where it is made to where it is used. Both rely on **bulk flow**, movement of a whole fluid driven by a pressure difference, far faster than diffusion over long distances ([size and scaling](/lessons/size-and-scaling/)).

> **The one idea.** A plant has no heart or other pump organ. Two local processes set up pressure differences, and sap flows in bulk down the resulting differences in pressure plus height. In the xylem, evaporation from wet leaf cell walls lowers the pressure at the top, so the water column is **pulled** up under **tension**, a pressure below zero. In the phloem, sugar loaded at a **source** (a part that exports sugar, usually a mature leaf) draws water in by osmosis and raises the pressure, so sap is **pushed** toward a **sink** (a part that takes sugar up, such as a root, a fruit or a growing shoot tip).
>
> *Scope:* water crossing a membrane moves toward lower total water potential. Inside an open conduit (xylem tracheids and vessels, phloem sieve tubes), sap moves as one fluid, solutes and all, toward lower pressure plus height; dissolved sugar still matters there by making sap thicker (more viscous). Along a short phloem path height is small next to source-to-sink pressure differences, so later sections say "pressure" for short; uphill, the pressure difference must also pay for height. This is a steady-state picture; the tallest trees and the refilling of emptied conduits are open questions (Going deeper).

## Water potential: which way water crosses

The film's one number, **water potential** (Ψ, the Greek letter psi), is the potential energy of water relative to pure water at atmospheric pressure and the same temperature and height. It is measured in megapascals (MPa; 1 MPa is about ten atmospheres), and that reference is defined as 0 MPa. Three parts add up:

**Turgor** is a walled cell's contents pushing out against the wall: a positive Ψ_p, the opposite of tension. How water crosses membranes, through channels called aquaporins, is in [membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/).

**Predict.** Assume equal heights (so ignore Ψ_g) and a membrane that passes water but not solutes. (Add the cell's parts; water leaves whichever side has the higher total.)

Out of the cell. Its total is -0.80 + 0.50 = -0.30 MPa, higher than the bath's -0.40 MPa, so water leaves despite the extra solute: turgor counts as much as solute. Where it stops is in Going deeper.

</details>

## The pull: what the first film left out

The evaporation the first film showed is **transpiration**, the loss of water vapour from a plant to the air, mostly through the leaves. Of the two conducting cell types, conifers such as Douglas fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii) move water mainly through tracheids, while most flowering plants also have wider vessels. The hydrogen bonds behind cohesion, a network that constantly breaks and re-forms, are in [water and weak bonds](/lessons/water-weak-bonds/).

**Could the roots push instead?** **Root pressure** builds when roots move minerals into the xylem and water follows by osmosis; it causes the drops seen on some leaf edges at dawn. In woody plants and vines it is small: the highest value in a survey of 109 tropical vines and woody species was about 0.15 MPa.

**Predict.** Gravity costs about 0.01 MPa per metre. Could the strongest root pressure in that survey hold water up to a leaf 30 m high?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. A leaf 30 m up needs 0.30 MPa of extra tension just to hold a still column, before any flow; 0.15 MPa could in principle raise water about 15 m, half the height, and root pressure is weak or absent when transpiration is fastest.

</details>

**A pulled column can fail.** An **embolism**, a gas bubble that fills a conduit and breaks its column, grows more likely as drought raises tension; **pits**, thin patches in the walls between neighbouring conduits, limit its spread.

## Stomata: the valve on the pull

A waxy cuticle greatly slows both water and carbon dioxide, so gas moves mainly through **stomata**, pores each bordered by two **guard cells**. Guard cells that take up potassium ions and accompanying anions (negative ions) lower their water potential, so water flows in and turgor bows them apart, opening the pore; losing those solutes closes it. Light favours opening; drought, through the hormone abscisic acid ([plant signals](/lessons/plant-signals/)), favours closing.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/plant-transport/stoma-open-closed.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: top row, two surface views of a stoma labelled Open and Closed. In the open view, swollen guard cells bow apart around a yellow pore (solutes in, water in, turgor up). In the closed view, slack guard cells lie pressed together (solutes lost, turgor down). Bottom, a leaf cross-section with the pore open between two guard cells: a grey arrow shows carbon dioxide passing in from outside air to the air space in the leaf, and a blue arrow shows water vapour passing out." caption="When guard cells take up potassium ions and accompanying anions, water flows in, turgor rises, and the paired cells bow apart, opening the pore. An open pore lets carbon dioxide in and water vapour out, so every opening is a trade." />

Far more water leaves an open stoma than carbon enters: the leaf's air spaces are nearly saturated with vapour and outside air is much drier, so the vapour gradient out is far steeper than the small carbon dioxide gradient in. You computed water-use efficiency in [organism problems](/lessons/organism-problems/). Many cacti use crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM), opening their stomata at night when air is cooler and evaporation slower; the biochemistry is in [the Calvin cycle](/lessons/calvin-cycle/).

Some of the carbon an open stoma lets in is fixed into sugar, and the sugar a leaf exports must reach roots, fruits and shoot tips. In flowering plants it travels in **sieve tubes**, chains of living **sieve-tube elements** joined end to end through perforated end walls called **sieve plates**; each element has lost its nucleus and depends on a neighbouring **companion cell**. Conifers move sugar in a related cell type, sieve cells. Nothing evaporates at a root to pull sap there, so what moves it? The second film follows Ernst Münch's answer and its test.

## The push: what the second film left out

**A sink is defined by what it does, not where it sits.** A storage organ can switch roles: a potato tuber is a sink while filling and a source when sprouting.

**Predict.** Recall the scope rule: water crossing a membrane follows total water potential, but sap inside an open tube follows pressure plus height. Take the first film's illustrative values: leaf xylem about -1.5 MPa, root xylem about -0.6 MPa. Assume the sieve tubes at each end are close to balance with the xylem beside them. Phloem sap runs from the leaf to the root. Does it run toward lower total water potential?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. It runs from about -1.5 MPa to about -0.6 MPa: up the total water-potential gradient. What falls along the path is pressure plus height. In the morning glory study, for example, source sieve tubes of moderate-sized plants held about 1.08 MPa of turgor, against about 0.59 MPa estimated at the sink.

</details>

**Why would slightly wider pores matter so much?** A sieve plate is a wall of narrow holes, and a hole's resistance to flow climbs steeply as it narrows, faster than its area shrinks, so a modest widening multiplies how easily sap passes.

**Loading can be active or passive.** In Arabidopsis thaliana, SWEET proteins export sucrose from cells beside the phloem into the cell walls. Proton-coupled carriers, powered by a proton gradient across the membrane, then take it into the sieve-tube and companion-cell complex, spending energy; Chen and colleagues (2012) found that sweet11 sweet12 double mutants loaded the phloem poorly. Many trees studied so far load passively instead, with sugar moving down its concentration gradient into the phloem (Fu and colleagues surveyed 45 dicot species in 2011).

## Two plumbing systems compared: xylem, phloem and a vertebrate circulation

A vertebrate heart pushes blood round a closed loop at positive pressure ([circulation and the heart](/lessons/circulation-and-heart/)); a resting adult human's peak (systolic) arterial pressure, 120 mmHg above atmospheric, is about 0.016 MPa. Xylem water moves mainly one way, under negative pressure powered by evaporation, and most is lost to the air, not returned. Source phloem runs at about 1 MPa in moderate-sized morning glory plants (roughly 70 times that arterial pressure) and over 2 MPa in the longest vines, set by osmosis at each end, not a pump organ. Root uptake of water and minerals is in [plant nutrition](/lessons/plant-nutrition/).

### Design problem

Physiologists count embolized conduits to judge drought damage, and some studies reported overnight refilling; stems are usually cut under water to keep air out. Torres-Ruiz and colleagues (2015) found that cutting under water while the xylem was under tension itself created embolisms in Juglans microcarpa, Eucalyptus pauciflora, Helianthus annuus and Acer campestre. Design a test of whether air-filled vessels seen in a cut stem were already there in the living plant.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Scan an intact stem with X-ray microtomography, which tells air-filled from water-filled conduits, then cut it under water and scan the same region again. Conduits full before and empty after were emptied by the cut.

Control for the suspected cause: relax the tension before cutting (let the plant rehydrate until its water potential is only slightly negative; Torres-Ruiz and colleagues suggest about -0.2 to -0.4 MPa), scan again to check that rehydration has not itself refilled any conduits, then cut and scan. If cutting at low tension adds few embolisms and cutting at high tension adds many, with handling and timing matched, tension at the moment of cutting is the likely cause.

Repeat across species and tensions, with several plants per tension (the original study had one scan per tension in two of its species), since Torres-Ruiz and colleagues found the effect depended on species, tension and existing embolism.

A clean result shows that cutting can create false embolisms; it does not prove that refilling never happens in intact plants, which needs repeated scans of uncut stems over time.

</details>

## Going deeper

### More on water potential

*Equilibrium.* In the Predict cell above, if the cell's solute potential stayed at -0.80 MPa, water would stop moving when turgor fell to Ψ_p = -0.40 - (-0.80) = +0.40 MPa. Check: -0.80 + 0.40 = -0.40 MPa. A real cell losing water concentrates its solutes, so Ψ_s would shift too.

*Gravity.* The precise figure is about 0.0098 MPa per metre; the rounded 0.01 MPa per metre is the one Woodruff and colleagues used in their 2004 study of Douglas fir.

*Matric potential,* a fourth term, matters in dry soil and cell walls; this lesson sets it aside.

### Faster stomata

Papanatsiou and colleagues (2019) expressed a light-gated potassium channel, BLINK1, in Arabidopsis thaliana guard cells. The stomata opened and closed faster, and under fluctuating light the plants made about 2.2 times the biomass of controls without using more water: one species, one light regime.

### The morning glory measurements

In moderate-sized plants they measured a sap speed of about 123 micrometres per second by carbon-11 tracing (three plants), a sap viscosity of about 1.7 mPa s from fluorescence lifetime imaging, and a source sieve-tube turgor of about 1.08 MPa with a pico-gauge pressure probe. Sink pressure was constrained from root cortex cells (about 0.59 MPa), not measured in sieve tubes, so the real source-to-sink pressure difference may be somewhat smaller than calculated. From the tube anatomy they calculated that flow needed about 0.21 MPa per metre. The authors estimated that, at the faster sap speed measured in the long vines (about 256 micrometres per second) and with the tube geometry of the smaller plants, the higher pressure alone would have carried sap only about 3.7 m.

The root cortex value from 7.5 m plants was also assumed for the longer vines, which adds to that sink-pressure caveat.

### Open questions

Whether pressure flow alone serves the tallest trees has not been tested: sieve-tube anatomy and sink pressures have not been measured along a whole path. How often embolized conduits refill in intact plants is also unsettled (see the Design problem).
