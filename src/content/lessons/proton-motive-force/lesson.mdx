**Give isolated mitochondria ADP and they start using oxygen faster, though no part of the respiratory chain ever touches ADP.**

The record comes from an oxygen electrode, a probe that tracks the oxygen left in a chamber of mitochondria. With fuel but no ADP, the oxygen line slopes down slowly. Add a little ADP and the slope steepens at once: State 3, respiration with ADP. When the ADP has all become ATP, the slope flattens again: State 4, respiration with the ADP used up. The chain burns oxygen; ATP synthase, a separate protein, uses ADP. How does the chain know there is ADP?

In the [last chapter](/lessons/oxidative-phosphorylation/) Complexes I, III and IV pumped protons out of the matrix, like water raised behind a dam. The water is the protons, its height is the **proton-motive force**, Δp, and the turbine is ATP synthase, a real rotary motor a few nanometers wide.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Every proton that crosses the inner membrane is priced by one shared number, the proton-motive force Δp, worth about 19 kJ per mole of protons. The pumps earn Δp and must push against it, so they slow as it rises. ATP synthase and any leak spend it, and they speed up as it rises. Δp settles where earning equals spending. To predict any drug, disease or change in demand, ask which side it changed (earning, spending through the synthase, or spending through a leak), then follow Δp to the other side.

This holds for an intact, sealed inner membrane near steady state, where Δp is the only link between the chain and the synthase. It tells you which way each rate moves, not by how much. One exception: if the chain stops completely, the synthase can run backward (IF1, near the end).

## How we know: a gradient alone makes ATP

In 1961 Peter Mitchell proposed that the energy linking electron flow to ATP synthesis is stored not in a high-energy chemical intermediate but in a proton gradient across a sealed membrane: the chemiosmotic hypothesis. Two kinds of experiment settled it.

Jagendorf and Uribe soaked chloroplast membranes in acid, then jumped them into base: a hand-built proton gradient, with no light and no electron transport. They made ATP in the dark. Racker and Stoeckenius rebuilt vesicles from only purified lipids, bacteriorhodopsin (a light-driven proton pump borrowed from a salt-loving microbe) and ATP synthase. Shine light, and the pump builds a gradient, the gradient drives the synthase, and ATP appears, with no respiratory chain anywhere. With many other results, these experiments won the argument, and in 1978 Mitchell the Nobel Prize.

## What the dam stores: Δp, in one number

Two pulls draw a proton back into the matrix. The first is **electrical**: the pumps carry positive charge out, leaving the matrix about 150 mV negative, which attracts the positive protons back. This is the **membrane potential**, ΔΨ ("delta psi"). The second is **chemical**: the matrix is about 0.5 to 1 pH unit more alkaline, so protons are scarcer inside and drift in. The matrix is both *negative* and *alkaline*, so both pulls point inward.

But one is in millivolts and the other in pH units, and you cannot add 150 to 0.75. The bridge: at body temperature, a tenfold concentration difference, exactly one pH unit, pushes as hard as 61.5 mV ([log scales](/math-toolkit/#log-scales)).

### One price for every proton

That 19 kJ per mole is a price both machines face at once.

**For the synthase it is income.** Making ATP and delivering it to the cytosol costs about 50 to 60 kJ/mol in a working cell, far more than the 30.5 kJ/mol standard value, because the cell keeps ATP high and ADP low. 55 / 19 is about 3, so **at least about three protons** must fall per ATP delivered. The machine actually spends about 3.7 (counted below). That margin keeps it running forward; a machine spending exactly the minimum would sit at equilibrium and make nothing.

**For the pumps it is a cost.** Electrons passing from one NADH to oxygen release about 220 kJ per mole at standard potentials (about 210 in the cell), and the chain pumps about ten protons per NADH. At Δp = 200 mV those ten protons cost 10 × 19.3 = 193 kJ, about 88% of the standard total. What is left over, only a few tens of kJ (about 27 at standard potentials, about 17 in the cell), is the pumps' **margin**. The chain runs close to its limit: if Δp climbs to about 220 mV, the margin is gone and electron flow slows to a trickle. That is **back-pressure**.

## A motor, not a metaphor

ATP synthase has two parts named, unhelpfully, F<sub>o</sub> and F<sub>1</sub>.

F<sub>o</sub> sits in the membrane: a ring of c-subunits (the rotor) pressed against a stationary a-subunit that holds two offset half-channels. A proton enters one half-channel from the intermembrane space and lands on a conserved acidic residue on the c-ring. A charged group cannot sit in the oily core of the membrane; only a c-subunit whose acid carries a proton can turn away from the a-subunit into the lipid. The proton-motive force biases the ring's random jostling. Protons board easily from the acidic, positive side and leave easily into the alkaline, negative matrix, so the ring steps mostly one way. The voltage and the pH difference both supply that bias, which is why the acid-bath chloroplasts could turn theirs with a pH jump alone. Nearly a full turn later, that c-subunit returns to the a-subunit and releases its proton into the matrix through the second half-channel.

F<sub>1</sub> is the catalytic head in the matrix: three α and three β subunits arranged like orange segments around a bent, asymmetric γ shaft. The c-ring and γ are bolted together, so the proton flow that spins the rotor spins the shaft. γ's off-center bend presses differently on each β subunit at any instant, holding each in a different shape.

## Boyer's inside-out insight: where the energy goes

Paul Boyer asked: what if it takes no energy to *make* ATP at the catalytic site, but it takes energy to get the ATP *off*?

That is the binding-change mechanism. Each β subunit cycles through three states as γ turns: **Open** (releases finished ATP, takes in ADP and Pi), **Loose** (ADP and Pi held loosely) and **Tight** (ATP forms spontaneously and is held so firmly it won't leave). Each 120° turn of γ moves all three forward one state and releases one ATP, so a full turn releases three. John Walker later caught the three β subunits in a single crystal, one holding an ATP analogue, one ADP, one empty: a frozen snapshot of Boyer's cycle.

So where does Δp's energy go? On the enzyme, making the bond costs almost nothing either way. The gradient pays for the rest: above all, prying the finished ATP loose, and, as later work showed, driving the binding of ADP and Pi.

## Counting protons and ATP

The vertebrate c-ring has 8 subunits, the smallest ring and lowest proton cost per ATP known. Eight protons and three ATP per turn: 8 / 3 ≈ 2.7 H⁺ per ATP. But the ATP must leave the matrix. The ATP–ADP translocase, one of the inner membrane's most abundant proteins, swaps ATP⁴⁻ out for ADP³⁻ in, an electrogenic 1:1 exchange in which ΔΨ pulls one net negative charge outward: a proton tax on every ATP shipped. The phosphate carrier imports Pi with a proton. Together, the two carriers cost about one more proton-equivalent, for about 3.7 H⁺ per exported ATP, just above the floor of about three.

Cytoplasmic NADH can't cross the inner membrane, so a shuttle hands its electrons in. The **glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle** delivers them to FAD and CoQ, bypassing Complex I, so that NADH is worth only about 1.5 ATP. The **malate-aspartate shuttle** regenerates matrix NADH, worth about 2.5 ATP. Many tissues run both, and the mix depends on enzyme levels and metabolic state.

On exams, use NADH ≈ 2.5 and FADH₂ ≈ 1.5, which gives **about 30–32 ATP per glucose**. Those rounded values assume about 4 H⁺ per ATP. The c8 machine's 3.7 would allow about 2.7 and 1.6, or about 32–34 per glucose (depending on the shuttle) before any leak, but measured P/O ratios in isolated mitochondria come out near the exam values (Going deeper, "Shuttles and measured yields"). The word "approximately" is doing real work.

**Predict.** Resting mitochondria have fuel but almost no ADP. You add ADP. What happens first to Δp, then to the pumps, then to oxygen use?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

ADP lets the synthase spend, so Δp dips. A lower Δp lowers the price of each proton the pumps push out, their margin grows, electrons flow faster, and oxygen use rises: State 3. When the ADP is used up, spending drops, Δp climbs back toward the pumps' ceiling, and the chain idles: State 4. The chain feels ADP only through Δp.

</details>

Δp itself moves only a little. A 10 mV dip in Δp saves about 10 kJ on the pumps' ten protons, widening their few-tens-of-kJ margin by a third or more. So Δp barely rises when ADP runs out; small changes in Δp drive large changes in flow.

This is **acceptor (respiratory) control**: ADP availability is a main control on the pace, alongside substrate supply and other signals. Energy charge, a gauge of the cell's balance of ATP, ADP and AMP, works upstream instead, telling enzymes such as PFK-1 how much fuel to send.

Poisons dissect the coupling, and the table they make is heavily tested. Read each on three gauges, O₂ consumption, ATP output and gradient size, and ask which side it changed.

**Predict.** Respiring mitochondria get **oligomycin**, which plugs F<sub>o</sub>. Minutes later they also get an uncoupler (DNP or FCCP). Predict all three gauges after each.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

**Oligomycin** removes spending through the synthase. Δp *rises* as back-pressure, and the pumps slow until earning matches the small remaining leak. O₂ down to the leak rate, ATP down, gradient **up**.

**The uncoupler** adds a large leak. Δp drains without making ATP, the pumps' margin opens wide, and the chain races to refill the gradient. O₂ **up**, usually past the starting rate; gradient down; ATP still zero. Given alone, an uncoupler gives O₂ up, ATP down, gradient down.

</details>

**ETC inhibitors** change the earning side: rotenone at Complex I, antimycin A at Complex III, cyanide or CO at Complex IV. Pumping stops: O₂ down, ATP down, gradient down. (That holds in isolated mitochondria whose fuel enters the chain upstream of the block, such as pyruvate plus malate with rotenone, and with no ATP to run the synthase backward; see Going deeper, "When blocking the chain does not empty the gradient.")

Biology runs its own uncoupler. **UCP1 (thermogenin)** in brown fat deliberately leaks protons to make heat instead of ATP, the basis of nonshivering thermogenesis: O₂ rises while ATP output stays low.

The earning side can also stop. In ischemia, when oxygen runs short, the chain stalls and the gradient collapses, so the synthase can run backward, burning ATP to hold Δp up. The **IF1 inhibitor protein** locks the rotor at low pH, stopping that reverse spin and saving the cell's ATP.

## Going deeper

### Mitchell, Glynn House and the Nobel banquet

The high-energy intermediate was the molecule everyone was hunting and no one could find, and Mitchell's alternative met years of resistance. Forced out of Edinburgh by illness, he restored a half-ruined Cornish mansion, founded a small private research institute there, and kept measuring protons with his collaborator Jennifer Moyle. At the 1978 Nobel banquet he said he was "not a little astonished."

### Signs in the Δp equation

The lesson's equation uses sizes, so both terms come out positive. Measured as inside minus outside, ΔΨ ≈ −150 mV and the equation reads Δp = ΔΨ − 61.5 mV × ΔpH ≈ −196 mV. Same size; only the sign bookkeeping differs.

### Where energy charge fits

Energy charge, ([ATP] + ½[ADP]) / ([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP]), is held near 0.85–0.90. This fuel gauge tunes PFK-1, isocitrate dehydrogenase and pyruvate dehydrogenase, while the chain itself feels ADP only through the synthase and Δp.

### Shuttles and measured yields

The glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle is prominent in brown fat and some skeletal muscle and brain cells; the malate-aspartate shuttle is major in heart and liver, and also active in muscle and brain. Measured P/O ratios fall below the c8 ceiling mainly because of proton leak and slip: some protons return without making ATP.

### Rotor and speed details

The c-ring's acidic residue is a glutamate in mitochondria and an aspartate in E. coli. Swap the long, draggy actin filament for a tiny bead and the motor spins roughly 130 revolutions per second, about 400 ATP per second.

### When blocking the chain does not empty the gradient

Rotenone blocks Complex I, but electrons from succinate still enter at Complex II. And in intact cells making ATP by glycolysis, the reversed synthase can spend that ATP to keep ΔΨ partly up after the chain is blocked.

### More on UCP1, IF1 and old gradients

Brown-fat thermogenesis can be lit up on a PET-CT in a cold adult. Pigs carry a broken UCP1 gene, which is why piglets rely on shivering. IF1 is overexpressed in some cancers, where its role is still debated. The proton gradient is older than mitochondria: chloroplasts and most bacterial flagella run on it too.

### Leber hereditary optic neuropathy as a respiratory-chain disease

Mitochondrial DNA mutations show the same principle. mtDNA is maternally inherited and present in many copies, so disease often depends on **heteroplasmy**, the mix of mutant and normal genomes in a cell, and on the mutant fraction, and strikes high-demand tissues first. LHON, most often caused by the m.11778G>A mutation in ND4 (a Complex I subunit), can blind otherwise healthy young adults, mostly men, over weeks, though many carriers never lose vision.
