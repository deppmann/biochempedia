techniques:
  - name: "Liposome reconstitution"
    blurb: "Strip the question down to its minimum parts. Racker and Stoeckenius rebuilt artificial vesicles from purified lipids plus just two proteins — bacteriorhodopsin (a light-driven proton pump) and ATP synthase. Shine light, build a gradient with no electron transport chain anywhere, and ATP still appears. Putting a function back together from defined components is the strongest way to prove what is sufficient to cause it."
  - name: "Single-molecule rotation assay"
    blurb: "Noji, Yoshida and colleagues (1997, 1998) fixed isolated α3β3 heads to a glass slide, glued a fluorescent actin filament to the γ shaft, added ATP, and watched the filament spin under a microscope — in discrete 120° steps, one per ATP hydrolyzed. Watching one molecule work, rather than averaging billions, turned 'rotary motor' from a model into a thing you can see turn."
  - name: "Polarographic respirometry (the oxygen electrode)"
    blurb: "A Clark-type oxygen electrode measures O2 consumption by isolated mitochondria in real time. Add ADP and respiration jumps (State 3); let ADP run out and it slows (State 4). This is how acceptor/respiratory control was discovered and how modern Seahorse assays still separate coupled respiration from proton leak using oligomycin and FCCP."

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "A suspension of actively respiring mitochondria is treated with oligomycin. Compared with the untreated control, what happens to oxygen consumption and the size of the proton gradient?"
    choices:
      - "O2 consumption rises; gradient collapses"
      - "O2 consumption falls; gradient increases"
      - "O2 consumption rises; gradient increases"
      - "O2 consumption unchanged; gradient collapses"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "Oligomycin plugs the Fo proton channel, so protons cannot flow back through ATP synthase. The gradient builds up as back-pressure (it increases), and because the gradient now strongly opposes further pumping, the electron transport chain stalls and oxygen consumption falls. The 'rises; collapses' option describes an uncoupler, not oligomycin. The 'rises; increases' and 'unchanged; collapses' combinations are internally inconsistent with a blocked channel."
  - stem: "Why does omitting the membrane potential (ΔΨ) when calculating the proton-motive force lead to a large underestimate of the available energy?"
    choices:
      - "ΔΨ is negligible because the membrane is a perfect insulator"
      - "ΔΨ and the ΔpH term oppose each other, so ignoring ΔΨ overcounts energy"
      - "ΔΨ contributes a large share of Δp — typically the larger of the two terms"
      - "ΔΨ only matters in chloroplasts, not mitochondria"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "Δp combines a chemical term from the ~0.5–1-unit pH difference and an electrical term from ΔΨ (~0.15 V, matrix negative). Both add to pull protons inward, and in actively respiring mitochondria ΔΨ is typically the larger term, so dropping it discards most of the driving force. ΔΨ is not negligible, the two terms add rather than oppose, and ΔΨ is central in mitochondria."
  - stem: "A cell moves cytosolic NADH electrons into the mitochondrion by the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle rather than the malate-aspartate shuttle. What is the consequence for ATP yield from that NADH, and why?"
    choices:
      - "It yields ~2.5 ATP because electrons enter at Complex I"
      - "It yields ~1.5 ATP because electrons enter via FAD/CoQ, bypassing Complex I"
      - "It yields ~3.5 ATP because the shuttle also pumps extra protons"
      - "It yields 0 ATP because the electrons are lost"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "The glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle delivers electrons to FAD and then CoQ, bypassing Complex I and its proton pumping, so that NADH is worth only about 1.5 ATP. The malate-aspartate shuttle, by contrast, regenerates matrix NADH for ~2.5 ATP; many tissues use both. The shuttle does not pump extra protons, and the electrons are not lost."
  - stem: "Isolated F1 (the α3β3γ catalytic head, detached from Fo) is placed in solution with ATP and no membrane or proton gradient. What does it do?"
    choices:
      - "It hydrolyzes ATP and spins in the reverse direction"
      - "It synthesizes ATP using the energy of the detergent"
      - "It does nothing because it requires a proton gradient to function"
      - "It pumps protons across the surrounding solution"
    answer: 0
    rationale: "With no gradient to supply directionality, isolated F1 runs the reaction the thermodynamically favorable way: it hydrolyzes ATP, and the γ shaft rotates in the opposite sense from synthesis. This is precisely why the proton-motive force is described as setting the direction rather than enabling the chemistry. F1 cannot synthesize ATP without an energy input, it is not gradient-dependent for hydrolysis, and without an intact Fo and membrane it cannot pump protons."
  - stem: "A patient with brown adipose tissue activated by cold exposure shows increased mitochondrial oxygen consumption but little extra ATP production in that tissue. Which protein best explains this pattern?"
    choices:
      - "Adenine nucleotide translocase (ANT)"
      - "Cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV)"
      - "IF1 inhibitor protein"
      - "UCP1 (thermogenin)"
    answer: 3
    rationale: "UCP1 is a regulated proton-leak carrier in brown fat; it lets protons re-enter the matrix without passing through ATP synthase, so the energy of the gradient is released as heat (nonshivering thermogenesis). The electron transport chain races to refill the dissipating gradient, raising O2 consumption while ATP output stays low. ANT exchanges ATP for ADP, Complex IV is a pump (not a leak), and IF1 would suppress reverse ATP hydrolysis, not uncouple respiration."

  - stem: "A point mutation in a c-subunit increases the number of c-subunits in the rotor ring from 8 to 12 in an engineered mitochondrion, with everything else unchanged. Compared with wild type, this mutant requires:"
    choices:
      - "fewer protons per ATP, so it is more efficient"
      - "the same protons per ATP, since 3 ATP form per turn regardless"
      - "more protons per ATP, so it is less efficient"
      - "more ATP per proton, so its P/O ratio rises"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "H+/ATP equals the number of c-subunits divided by 3 (three ATP per full turn). Going from 8 to 12 c-subunits raises H+/ATP from ~2.7 to 4, meaning more protons are spent per ATP and the machine is less efficient (lower P/O ratio). This is why the vertebrate c8 ring has the lowest proton cost per ATP known. The yield per turn is fixed at 3 ATP, but more protons are needed to complete each turn, so 'same protons per ATP' is wrong, and P/O falls rather than rises."

  - stem: "At rest, a cell's mitochondria are in a low-ADP, State 4-like condition with a high proton gradient. The cell suddenly begins intense work, sharply raising cytosolic ADP. Which sequence correctly describes acceptor (respiratory) control taking effect?"
    choices:
      - "ADP rises → ATP synthase spins faster → gradient is drawn down → electron transport and O2 consumption increase"
      - "ADP rises → energy charge increases → PFK-1 is inhibited → respiration slows"
      - "ADP rises → gradient increases → ATP synthase stalls → O2 consumption falls"
      - "ADP rises → uncoupling proteins open → gradient collapses → ATP output rises"
    answer: 0
    rationale: "Acceptor control means ADP availability sets the rate.   - level: "highschool"

    text: "The electron transport chain uses the energy from food to pump protons (H⁺) across the inner mitochondrial membrane, building a gradient like water behind a dam. ATP synthase is a real spinning motor: protons flow back through it, turning a shaft that forces ADP and phosphate together into ATP. Block the proton flow and ATP stops; poke a leak (an uncoupler) and the energy comes out as heat. This whole setup, chemiosmosis, makes most of the ~30 ATP you get from one glucose."
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: "The proton-motive force Δp = ΔΨ + a ΔpH term (~0.15 V plus ~0.5–1 pH unit, ~17–20 kJ/mol H⁺) couples the redox chemistry of Complexes I–IV to phosphorylation. ATP synthase converts that potential mechanically: Fo's c-ring rotor steps as protons protonate and deprotonate a conserved acidic residue through the a-subunit's two half-channels, spinning the γ shaft inside the α3β3 head. Boyer's binding-change mechanism (O/L/T) shows energy is spent releasing tight-bound ATP, three per 360°. With a c8 ring (~2.7 H⁺/ATP) plus translocase and Pi-carrier costs (~3.7 total), and shuttle-dependent NADH valuation (conventionally 2.5 vs 1.5, which assumes ~4 H⁺/ATP), the yield lands near 30–32 ATP/glucose under acceptor control; exact values depend on c-ring size, transport costs and leak."
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: "Mitchell's chemiosmotic coupling reframed oxidative phosphorylation as a transmembrane electrochemical proton potential doing work, with ΔΨ typically dominating Δp in actively respiring mitochondria. F1Fo is a two-stroke rotary engine. Proton transfer through the staggered a-subunit half-channels onto the c-ring carboxylate ratchets the rotor, driving γ against the α3β3 stator; the bent γ's asymmetry then enforces sequential O→L→T occupancy. The major energy-requiring step is release of tightly bound ATP, not bond formation. The asymmetric 1BMF crystal (Walker) supports the three-state cycle, single-molecule assays show rotation directly (Noji/Yoshida), and forcing γ to turn mechanically makes ATP (Itoh 2004). Stoichiometry is set by c-ring number (species-variable 8–17), the electrogenic ANT exchange, and the symporting Pi carrier; integrated flux is governed by Δp-dependent respiratory control, IF1 gating, and regulated uncoupling (UCP1). Pathology tracks heteroplasmic mtDNA load with tissue thresholds (LHON ND4, usually homoplasmic, is an exception)."

In the last chapter we built a dam. Complexes I, III and IV spent the energy of electrons falling toward oxygen to pump protons out of the mitochondrial matrix, stacking them in the intermembrane space like water rising behind a wall. That dam stored real energy. Today we open the floodgates.

The Itaipu Dam holds back a reservoir for one reason: height stores energy, water or not — and stacked protons store energy the same way, though as electrochemical potential rather than *mgh*. Let it fall through a turbine and the height becomes electricity. A mitochondrion does the same trick with protons. The reservoir is the proton gradient; the turbine is ATP synthase, an actual rotary motor a few nanometers wide; and the electricity is ATP. Everything in this chapter is one idea wearing different clothes: **electron transport and ATP synthesis are a single coupled machine, joined by protons.**

## The seventeen-year argument

Here is the part most textbooks rush past. The person who figured this out spent years arguing against most of his field.

In 1961 Peter Mitchell proposed that the energy linking electron flow to ATP synthesis is not stored in some high-energy chemical intermediate — the molecule everyone was hunting and no one could find — but in a gradient of protons across a sealed membrane. He called it the chemiosmotic hypothesis. Many leading biochemists resisted the idea, and the argument ran for years. Forced out of Edinburgh by illness, he restored a half-ruined Cornish mansion, founded a small private research institute there, and kept measuring protons with his collaborator Jennifer Moyle.

The vindication came from experiments you can hold in your head. Jagendorf and Uribe soaked chloroplast membranes in acid, then jumped them into base — a hand-built proton gradient, no light, no electron transport — and watched them make ATP in the dark. Racker and Stoeckenius went further: they rebuilt artificial vesicles containing only purified lipids, bacteriorhodopsin (a light-driven proton pump borrowed from a salt-loving microbe), and ATP synthase. Shine light, and the pump builds a gradient; the gradient drives the synthase; ATP appears. No respiratory chain anywhere in the vesicle. Together with many other results, these experiments won the argument. In 1978 Mitchell won the Nobel Prize; at the banquet he said he was "not a little astonished."

## What the dam actually stores: Δp

The proton-motive force, written Δp, has two parts, and forgetting one is the single most common mistake here.

The first is **chemical**: protons are simply more concentrated outside the matrix, a difference of roughly 0.5 to 1 pH unit. The second is **electrical**: pumping positive charge outward leaves the matrix negative, a membrane potential ΔΨ of about 0.15 volts. You need both. Together they come to roughly 17 to 20 kJ per mole of protons returning — and a student who tracks only the pH difference throws away most of the energy, because the electrical term is much the larger of the two. Note the asymmetry the cell maintains: the matrix is both *alkaline* and *negative*, which is exactly what pulls a positive proton back in.

## A motor, not a metaphor

ATP synthase has two parts named, unhelpfully, F<sub>o</sub> and F<sub>1</sub>.

F<sub>o</sub> sits in the membrane and is the proton channel: a ring of c-subunits (the rotor) pressed against a stationary a-subunit that holds two offset half-channels. A proton enters the half-channel from the intermembrane space and protonates a conserved acidic residue on the c-ring — a glutamate in mitochondria, an aspartate in *E. coli*. Neutralizing that charge lets the ring rotate one notch into the oily membrane interior; nearly a full turn later, that c-subunit comes back around to the a-subunit and releases its proton into the matrix through the second half-channel. Protons walk the ring around, one step at a time.

F<sub>1</sub> is the catalytic head poking into the matrix: three α and three β subunits arranged like orange segments around a single bent, asymmetric γ shaft. The c-ring and γ are bolted together. So the proton flow that spins the rotor spins the shaft, and the shaft's own rotation is what drives chemistry: γ's off-center bend presses differently on each of the three β subunits at any instant, holding each in a different shape — the mechanical seed of the three-state cycle below.

## Boyer's inside-out insight

Here is the question Paul Boyer posed that reorganized the whole field. What would you say if I told you it takes no energy to *make* ATP at the catalytic site — but it takes energy to get the ATP *off*?

That is the binding-change mechanism. Each β subunit cycles through three states as γ turns: **Open** (empty; it releases finished ATP and takes in fresh ADP and Pi), **Loose** (ADP and Pi trapped, but held loosely), and **Tight** (ATP forms spontaneously, held so firmly it won't leave). The proton gradient's main job is to wrench the tight site open and let the finished ATP go; later work showed that rotation also drives the binding of ADP and Pi. At any instant the three β subunits sit in three different states. Each 120° turn of γ moves all three forward one state and releases one ATP, so a full 360° rotation releases three. John Walker later caught all three β subunits in different nucleotide states in a single crystal — a frozen snapshot of Boyer's cycle, drawn one structure at a time.

You can watch it turn. Noji and Yoshida glued isolated α<sub>3</sub>β<sub>3</sub> heads from a heat-loving bacterium to glass, attached a fluorescent actin filament to the γ shaft, added ATP, and saw the filament rotate in discrete 120° steps, one step per ATP. The world's smallest motor, running under a microscope. Swap the long, draggy filament for a tiny bead and the same motor spins roughly 130 revolutions per second, about 400 ATP per second. That assay was the motor running backward: isolated F<sub>1</sub> with no gradient *hydrolyzes* ATP and spins the opposite way from synthesis. The gradient does not make the chemistry possible; it sets the direction. The reverse was later shown directly: when Itoh and colleagues turned γ with magnets in the synthesis direction, ATP appeared.

## Counting protons and ATP

The vertebrate c-ring has 8 subunits, the smallest ring and lowest proton cost per ATP known. Eight protons per turn, three ATP per turn, gives about 2.7 H⁺/ATP. But the ATP must still leave the matrix: the ATP–ADP translocase (the most abundant protein in the inner membrane) swaps ATP⁴⁻ out for ADP³⁻ in, an electrogenic 1:1 exchange in which ΔΨ pulls one net negative charge outward, spending part of the gradient — a proton tax on every ATP shipped. Add the phosphate carrier importing Pi with a proton, and the real cost is closer to ~3.7 H⁺ per exported ATP.

Two more wrinkles set the final glucose tally. Cytoplasmic NADH from glycolysis can't cross the inner membrane, so it hands its electrons across by a shuttle. The **glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle** (prominent in brown fat and some skeletal muscle and brain cells) delivers to FAD and CoQ, bypassing Complex I, so it is worth only ~1.5 ATP. The **malate-aspartate shuttle** (major in heart and liver, and also active in muscle and brain) regenerates matrix NADH, worth ~2.5 ATP. Many tissues run both, and the mix depends on enzyme levels and metabolic state. This is why the honest answer is **~30–32 ATP per glucose**, using the rounded textbook values NADH ≈ 2.5 and FADH₂ ≈ 1.5. Those values assume about 4 H⁺ per ATP; with the ~3.7 above, the numbers come out a little higher (about 2.7 and 1.6), and some estimates reach 33. The word "approximately" is doing real work, and it is fair game on an exam.

## Setting the rate, and breaking it on purpose

The cell throttles all of this with **acceptor (respiratory) control**: ADP availability is a main control on the pace, alongside substrate supply and other signals. Plenty of ADP and respiration races (State 3). When ADP runs low, ATP synthase stops draining the gradient, the gradient backs up against the pumps, and the chain idles (State 4). The master gauge is energy charge, ([ATP] + ½[ADP]) / ([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP]), held near 0.85–0.90 — a fuel gauge that simultaneously tunes PFK-1, isocitrate dehydrogenase, and pyruvate dehydrogenase.

Poisons let you dissect the coupling, and the three-way table they make is heavily tested. Read each one on three gauges: O₂ consumption, ATP output, and gradient size.

- **ETC inhibitors** (rotenone at CI, antimycin A at CIII, cyanide/CO at CIV) stop pumping: O₂ down, ATP down, gradient down.
- **Oligomycin** plugs F<sub>o</sub>: protons can't fall, the gradient *rises* as back-pressure, and the stalled pumps slow O₂ consumption to the leak rate — so O₂ down, ATP down, gradient **up**.
- **Uncouplers** (DNP, FCCP) punch a leak in the membrane: the gradient drains without making ATP, and the chain races to refill it — so O₂ **up**, ATP down, gradient down.

The biology runs its own uncoupler: **UCP1 (thermogenin)** in brown fat deliberately leaks protons to make heat instead of ATP, the basis of nonshivering thermogenesis you can light up on a PET-CT in a cold adult. (Pigs carry a broken UCP1 gene, which is why piglets rely on shivering.) And the IF1 inhibitor protein locks the rotor at low pH during ischemia, stopping the synthase from running backward and burning ATP when the gradient collapses. IF1 is also overexpressed in some cancers, where its role is still debated.

Mutations bring it home. mtDNA is maternally inherited and present in many copies, so disease often depends on **heteroplasmy**, the mix of mutant and normal genomes in a cell, and on the mutant fraction, and strikes high-demand tissues first. LHON, most often caused by the m.11778G>A mutation in ND4 (a Complex I subunit), can blind otherwise healthy young adults, mostly men, over weeks, though many carriers never lose vision. The proton gradient is older than mitochondria: chloroplasts and most bacterial flagella run on it, and bacteriorhodopsin builds one from light.
