techniques:
  - name: "Lesion-symptom mapping"
    blurb: >-
      Structural MRI outlines each patient's damage, and the damaged voxels or connections are
      related statistically to scores on language tests across a cohort. It shows which damage
      goes with which deficit, but strokes damage neighbouring regions together, so it cannot by
      itself isolate the one region a function needs.
  - name: "Tachistoscopic divided-visual-field testing"
    blurb: >-
      A person fixates a central point while a word or picture is flashed briefly to one side,
      too briefly for an eye movement to bring it to the centre. Because each visual hemifield
      projects to the opposite hemisphere, the method sends input to one hemisphere at a time,
      which is what made the split-brain experiments readable.
  - name: "Functional transcranial Doppler ultrasonography"
    blurb: >-
      Ultrasound probes measure blood-flow velocity in the left and right middle cerebral arteries
      while a person performs a language task such as word generation. The side with the larger
      rise is taken as dominant, which lets lateralization be measured quickly and without
      injection in healthy people.
  - name: "Individual-subject fMRI localizers"
    blurb: >-
      A short contrast (sentences against nonwords, or native speech against degraded speech)
      defines language-responsive regions in each person's own brain, and those regions are then
      tested on new tasks. This avoids blurring across people, but an activation shows involvement,
      not that the region is needed.

**In 1861 Paul Broca examined a man who could say almost nothing beyond one syllable, yet appeared to follow much of what was said to him.**

When he died, Broca found damage in the left frontal lobe and argued that speech production lives there. That pair, a symptom and a lesion, started a century and a half of mapping language onto the brain. This lesson asks how good that map is. You will sort the aphasias, see what MRI found in Broca's own specimens, treat "language is on the left" as a number with a base rate, work out the split-brain experiments from the wiring, and see how imaging of healthy brains has redrawn the picture.

## Two patients and a model: four tests sort the aphasias

An **aphasia** is an impairment of producing or understanding language caused by acquired brain damage, most often a stroke. It is a disorder of language, and it does not mean a loss of intelligence. It must be kept apart from **dysarthria**, a motor speech disorder in which weak or uncoordinated speech muscles slur articulation while language itself (grammar, word choice, comprehension, writing) is intact.

Clinicians sort aphasias with four questions. Is speech **fluent**, with normal phrase length and rhythm? Is **comprehension** of spoken words and sentences preserved? Can the person **repeat** a phrase? Can they **name** a pictured object? Naming is impaired in almost every aphasia, so it rarely discriminates on its own.

- **Broca aphasia**: nonfluent. Speech is slow and effortful, short phrases with disrupted grammar. Comprehension is relatively preserved, though complex sentences often trip it. Repetition is poor.
- **Wernicke aphasia**: fluent, often rapid speech that conveys little meaning, with word substitutions. Comprehension of words and sentences is severely impaired, and so is repetition.
- **Conduction aphasia**: fluent speech, comprehension relatively good, but repetition is disproportionately poor, with sound errors the person often tries to correct.
- **Dysarthria**: articulation is impaired; all four language tests are normal once speech motor demands are removed, for instance in writing.

Carl Wernicke described the fluent syndrome in 1874 after damage in the posterior superior temporal gyrus, and predicted that cutting the connection between posterior and frontal language cortex would spare comprehension but wreck repetition. In the 1960s Norman Geschwind built these cases into a model: a posterior area for word sounds and meaning, a frontal area for speech planning, and a **tract** (a bundle of axons in the central nervous system), the arcuate fasciculus, joining them. The model is a good teaching scaffold. Its anatomy is where it strains: conduction aphasia, for example, is now tied mainly to damage in left posterior temporal and parietal cortex rather than to a cut tract alone (Buchsbaum and colleagues, 2011).

## What the lesions really were: deeper than Broca could see

**Broca's area** is conventionally the pars opercularis and pars triangularis of the left inferior frontal gyrus. Broca kept his two patients' brains whole, and in 2007 Nina Dronkers and colleagues imaged them with high-resolution MRI. In both, the damage extended further medially than Broca could measure. He had noted that Leborgne's lesion reached deep structures, but without slicing the brains he could not trace how far. His founding cases never isolated a small patch of surface cortex.

Modern cohorts also complicate a one-region story. In 70 people with chronic aphasia, Fridriksson and colleagues (2015) used lesion-symptom mapping and found that damage to the pars opercularis alone was not sufficient to produce Broca aphasia. Patients with Broca aphasia also had more damage in the left superior temporal gyrus, roughly Wernicke's area, and damage to the two regions together sorted over 95% of patients correctly. **Wernicke's area** is worse defined: where its boundaries lie is still disputed (Mesulam and colleagues, 2015), so no single outline should be learned as settled anatomy.

One influential replacement is a network of two streams, an idea developed by Gregory Hickok and David Poeppel among others; other dual-pathway models divide the work differently. A **dorsal stream** runs from posterior temporal and inferior parietal cortex to frontal cortex and maps speech sounds onto articulation. A **ventral stream** runs through the temporal lobe and maps sounds onto meaning. Fridriksson and colleagues (2018) tested this in 159 native English speakers at least six months after a single left-hemisphere stroke, relating damaged regions and estimated connections to 16 speech and language measures. Motor speech impairment went mostly with dorsal-stream damage, comprehension impairment more strongly with ventral-stream damage, and naming, repetition and grammar depended on both. Keep the hedges: strokes follow arterial territories and damage neighbouring regions together, so these are associations.

## Lateralization by the numbers: a probability, not a rule

**Lateralization** is a functional difference between the two cerebral hemispheres. Language is left-dominant in most people, but how most? Knecht and colleagues (2000) measured it in 326 healthy adults with functional transcranial Doppler during word generation. Right-hemisphere dominance occurred in 4% of strong right-handers, 15% of ambidextrous people and 27% of strong left-handers. Left-handedness raises the odds of right-sided language; it does not make it typical. The exact figure depends on the method: an fMRI study of 50 healthy left-handers found right-sided activation in 10%, a bilateral pattern in 14%, and complete right-sided lateralization in only one person (Pujol and colleagues, 1999).

**Worked problem.** Use Knecht's rates as fixed probabilities. The cohort sizes below are assumptions for the exercise, not measured prevalences, and they ignore everyone between the handedness extremes.

*Part (a).* Take 100 strong right-handers and 100 strong left-handers.

- Expected right-dominant right-handers: 100 × 0.04 = 4.
- Expected right-dominant left-handers: 100 × 0.27 = 27.
- Right-dominant overall: (4 + 27) / 200 = 31 / 200 = 15.5%.

Now pick one right-dominant person. The chance that they are left-handed is

> **P(left-hander | right dominance) = 27 / (27 + 4) = 27 / 31 = 0.871, or 87.1%**

Compare the reverse conditional: P(right dominance | left-hander) = 27%. A right-dominant person in this sample is very likely left-handed, yet most left-handers are left-dominant. The two statements answer different questions.

*Part (b).* Now assume a population that is 90% strong right-handers and 10% strong left-handers.

- Right-dominant right-handers: 0.90 × 0.04 = 0.036.
- Right-dominant left-handers: 0.10 × 0.27 = 0.027.
- Right-dominant total: 0.063, or 6.3%.

> **P(left-hander | right dominance) = 0.027 / 0.063 = 0.429, or 42.9%**

Check: 0.036 / 0.063 = 0.571, and 0.429 + 0.571 = 1. The rates did not change; the base rate did, and the answer fell from 87% to 43%. Rightward language is now more often found in a right-hander, simply because right-handers are so many.

Lateralization is function by function. That is different from the popular claim that people are "left-brained" (logical) or "right-brained" (creative) in personality, which the evidence does not support: a resting-state fMRI analysis of more than 1,000 people (Nielsen and colleagues, 2013) found no sign that individuals are globally left- or right-dominant. How age at exposure shapes language is a developmental question, treated in [sensitive periods and behaviour](/lessons/sensitive-periods-behavior/).

## Cutting the commissures: what split-brain patients show

A **commissure** is a tract that crosses the midline to link the two hemispheres; the corpus callosum is the largest ([the map of the nervous system](/lessons/nervous-system-map/)). How commissural axons find the midline during development belongs to [crossing the midline](/lessons/crossing-the-midline/). Cutting the forebrain commissures was used to treat epilepsy that drugs could not control.

To read the results, you need the crossings. **Contralateral** means on the opposite side; **ipsilateral**, on the same side.

- **Vision.** At the optic chiasm only the fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross. So everything in the left visual **hemifield** (the half of space left of the fixation point) reaches the right hemisphere, from both eyes ([visual cortex](/lessons/visual-cortex/)). The variable is the hemifield, not the eye.
- **Touch.** Fine touch from the hand ascends in the dorsal columns and crosses in the medulla, so the left hand reports to the right hemisphere ([touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/)).
- **Movement.** Most corticospinal fibres cross at the pyramidal decussation, so each hand is controlled mainly by the opposite hemisphere ([the basal ganglia](/lessons/basal-ganglia/)).
- **Hearing** reaches both hemispheres ([hearing and balance](/lessons/hearing-and-balance/)), which makes sound a leak in any lateralized test.

Gazzaniga, Bogen and Sperry (1962) reported patient W.J., whose corpus callosum and anterior and hippocampal commissures had been cut. Blindfolded, he could use familiar objects with his left hand but could not name them. With brief tachistoscopic flashes, his left hand could select some simple patterns shown in the left field that he could not identify verbally; without that control the deficit was not shown satisfactorily. The authors warned that possible earlier brain injury limited how far one patient generalizes.

Later testing showed that the disconnected right hemisphere understands some spoken language, so it is mute more often than it is deaf to language (Sperry, Nobel lecture, 1981). The tidy hand-by-field rule also has exceptions: Pinto and colleagues (2017) found that two patients with complete callosal section recognized stimuli in either field above chance with any response, while still failing to compare across fields. Whether the surgery creates two minds is unsettled; a 2020 collective review judged the evidence insufficient to decide.

### Design problem

Design a test showing that a split-brain patient's right hemisphere understood a written word it cannot say. List your controls and what the result would and would not show.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Track the eyes and start each trial only when gaze holds on a central dot. Flash a noun, such as "spoon", in the left visual field, placed well away from the dot so it does not fall near the midline, for 150 ms or less, shorter than most saccade latencies. Keep tracking gaze and discard any trial with an eye movement. The word's visual input then goes to the right hemisphere. Behind a screen, the left hand searches a tray of objects by touch; the patient also says what he saw. The prediction is a verbal report of "nothing" or a guess, with the left hand choosing the spoon above chance.

Controls: right-field trials, where he should name the word; right-hand responses to left-field words, which should fail if the hand-by-field rule holds; silent objects and a screen, so neither sound nor sight of the hand cues the speaking hemisphere; randomized fields and objects. A positive result across many such words shows right-hemisphere comprehension of the tested words in this patient. It does not show two persons, and it may not generalize: patients are few, all had epilepsy, some had earlier damage, and Pinto and colleagues found the hand-by-field rule is not universal.

## The language network in living brains

Lesions show what a brain cannot do without some tissue. Imaging shows what healthy tissue does. Fedorenko, Behr and Kanwisher (2011) located language regions in each person's own brain, by contrasting sentences with nonword lists, and then found that most of those regions responded little or not at all to arithmetic, working memory, cognitive control and music; one left middle frontal region also responded to verbal working memory. How cleanly language separates from other kinds of thinking is still debated. Malik-Moraleda and colleagues (2022) ran a similar localizer in 86 native speakers of 45 languages from 12 families, contrasting native-language passages with acoustically degraded ones, and found a broadly shared fronto-temporo-parietal network whose regions' activity was strongly correlated. In left language regions, native speech produced a 2.13% blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal change from fixation against 0.84% for degraded speech, about 2.5 times larger. All participants were also fluent in English, and each language was represented by two native speakers (four languages by one), so fine differences between languages stay open.

Neither result shows necessity: an activation means involvement, and only damage or disruption tests need. The strongest account combines the two kinds of evidence, and the next lesson, [attention and decisions](/lessons/attention-and-decisions/), applies the same discipline to choosing what to process.
