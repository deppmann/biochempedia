      Most of what was known about which hemisphere holds language came from people with

    story: >-
      Fedorenko, working with Michael Behr and Nancy Kanwisher at MIT, asked whether the brain
      regions that respond to language also do other demanding mental work. Group-averaged fMRI
      studies had reported overlap between language and arithmetic, music or working memory. But
      averaging across people can merge neighbouring regions whose exact positions differ from
      one brain to the next. So in each participant they first located language regions with a
      localizer scan, reading sentences contrasted with lists of nonwords, and then measured how
      those same regions responded to arithmetic, working-memory, cognitive-control and music
      tasks. The language regions showed little or no response to the nonlinguistic tasks they
      tested (PNAS, 2011). In 2022 Saima Malik-Moraleda, Fedorenko and colleagues applied the same
      logic to 86 native speakers of 45 languages and found a broadly shared
      fronto-temporo-parietal network. The approach gave the field a way to define language
      regions person by person before asking what else they do.

techniques:
  - name: "Lesion-symptom mapping"
    blurb: >-
      Structural MRI outlines each patient's damage, and the damaged voxels or connections are
      related statistically to scores on language tests across a cohort. It shows which damage
      goes with which deficit, but strokes damage neighbouring regions together, so it cannot by
      itself isolate the one region a function needs.
  - name: "Tachistoscopic divided-visual-field testing"
    blurb: >-
      A person fixates a central point while a word or picture is flashed briefly to one side,
      briefly enough that an eye movement is unlikely to bring it to the centre, ideally with gaze tracked. Because each visual hemifield
      projects to the opposite hemisphere, the method sends input to one hemisphere at a time,
      which is what made the split-brain experiments readable.
  - name: "Functional transcranial Doppler ultrasonography"
    blurb: >-
      Ultrasound probes measure blood-flow velocity in the left and right middle cerebral arteries
      while a person performs a language task such as word generation. The side with the larger
      rise is taken as dominant, which lets lateralization be measured quickly and without
      injection in healthy people.
  - name: "Individual-subject fMRI localizers"
    blurb: >-
      A short contrast (sentences against nonwords, or native speech against degraded speech)
      defines language-responsive regions in each person's own brain, and those regions are then
      tested on new tasks. This avoids blurring across people, but an activation shows involvement,
      not that the region is needed.

**In 1861 Paul Broca examined a man who could say almost nothing beyond one syllable, yet appeared to follow much of what was said to him.**

The man was Leborgne. When he died, Broca found damage on the surface of the left frontal lobe and argued that articulate speech depends on that part of the frontal lobe. Within a few years he was arguing that the left side was special. That pair, a symptom and a lesion, started a century and a half of mapping language onto the brain.

## Three puzzles about speaking

1. **The man who understood.** Leborgne could hardly speak, yet much of his understanding survived. What had broken?
2. **The hand that knew.** A man whose corpus callosum has been cut stares at a dot on a screen. A picture of a key flashes to the left of the dot. He says he saw nothing, yet his left hand, reaching behind a screen, picks out the key. How can he fail to name aloud what his left hand can identify?
3. **The left-hander.** In one large study of healthy adults, more than one strong left-hander in four had language on the right side of the brain. Yet most strong left-handers still had it on the left. What does handedness tell you about one person?

One idea answers the first two and frames the third.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** You can say only what reaches the language network (the brain regions that handle words and sentences) of the hemisphere that speaks, by an unbroken route. Each bedside test travels part of that route, so the pattern of what fails points to the stretch that most likely broke. This is a simplified sketch, not a diagnosis: real strokes often break several stretches, one pattern can come from different lesions, and the pattern does not say which small patch of cortex was lost.

The sketch covers the classic syndromes after a lasting left-hemisphere stroke in people whose language is on the left, and split-brain tests. It leaves out the melody and flow of speech across sentences, where the right hemisphere contributes, as well as reading and writing and the aphasias that worsen over time.

## One route to speech: four tests find the break

Picture a map with a starting point and two roads. When you hear a word, auditory cortex in both hemispheres analyses its sounds. The route we follow starts on the left, at the back of the superior temporal lobe, roughly what textbooks call Wernicke's area. There the word's **sound form**, its pattern of speech sounds, is recognized. Two roads leave from there, and in this sketch traffic runs both ways along each.

- The **ventral road** (ventral means toward the bottom) runs forward and down through the temporal lobe to **meaning**. It is how you understand "spoon". Run backwards, it takes a meaning you want to say to that word's sound form.
- The **dorsal road** (dorsal means toward the top) runs up through parietal cortex to the frontal lobe, roughly what textbooks call Broca's area. There a **frontal planner** assembles the phrase, with its grammar, and turns each sound form into an **articulation plan**, the sequence of lip, tongue and larynx movements that will say it. The dorsal road links how a word sounds with how to say it and holds a sound sequence while it is planned. It is how you can repeat a word you have never heard, such as "blick".

Spontaneous speech starts from meaning, finds each known word's sound form and passes it to the same frontal planner. That passage does not depend wholly on the dorsal road: speech-generation connections carry known words to the planner, and the dorsal road adds sound-to-movement support, checking the plan against the word's sound. The planner then drives the motor system that moves the muscles: motor cortex and its pathways to the brainstem motor nuclei, helped by the basal ganglia and cerebellum. In the dual-stream model of Gregory Hickok and David Poeppel (2007), the dorsal road is strongly left-sided in most people, while understanding single words gets help from both hemispheres.

An **aphasia** is an impairment of producing or understanding language caused by acquired brain damage, most often a stroke. It is a loss of language, not of intelligence: many people with aphasia, especially nonfluent aphasia, often know what they want to say and cannot get it out.

At the bedside, a clinician runs four tests, each travelling a different part of the map.

| Test | What the clinician does | Stretch it travels |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fluency** | Listens to spontaneous speech: phrase length, rhythm, grammar | meaning → word sound forms → frontal planner; distributed speech generation and motor planning |
| **Comprehension** | "Point to the door, then the ceiling." | sound form → ventral road → meaning |
| **Repetition** | "Say after me: no ifs, ands or buts." | sound form → dorsal road → planner; for a real word, meaning can also help |
| **Naming** | Holds up a pen: "What is this?" | seen object → meaning → word's sound form → planner |

Fluency describes the speech itself: phrase length, rate, effort, melody and grammar. Frontal planning is a major contributor among interacting speech-generation and motor-planning processes, and fluent speech can still carry severe word and sound errors. Naming asks for one exact word on demand, so damage at many stages (meaning, finding the sound form, the planner) can spoil it. That is why naming is impaired in almost every aphasia and rarely tells the syndromes apart.

Two people describe the same picture of a busy kitchen (quotations composed for teaching, in the style of recorded descriptions).

**Broca aphasia.** *"Boy ... cookie ... uh ... fall. Mother ... water. Water."* Speech is nonfluent: slow and effortful, in short phrases with disrupted grammar and the small grammatical words missing. She follows most of a conversation, although a complex sentence such as "the dog that the cat chased was brown" may defeat her. She cannot repeat a phrase well. Where did the route break? Toward the frontal end, where the phrase is assembled and planned. Spontaneous speech and repetition both pass through it. Understanding simple sentences does not; the dog sentence can fail because its meaning hangs on grammar. Leborgne inspired the name Broca aphasia, but his near-mutism and limited historical testing do not establish this typical modern syndrome, and his extensive lesion, described in the last section, prevents assigning his deficit to one stage. Real Broca lesions usually reach beyond the frontal end.

**Wernicke aphasia.** *"Well, the boy is over there doing the tooken, you know, and she's got it going all over and it's fine, it's all fine."* Speech flows, often rapidly, with a normal melody, but it conveys little meaning, and words are swapped or invented. He cannot follow "point to the door", and he cannot repeat. Often he does not seem to notice his errors. Where did the route break? Toward the start, where both roads begin, and the damage usually touches several steps there, from recognizing word sounds to linking them with meaning. So comprehension and repetition fail together. Speech generation and frontal planning are relatively spared, which lets speech flow, but the damaged start feeds them the wrong words and sounds.

**Predict.** A stroke impairs only the dorsal road's link from sound to movement. Sound-form cortex, the ventral road and speech generation, including the planner, are spared. What will the four tests show?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Speech is relatively fluent, because speech generation still reaches the planner, though sound errors creep in without the dorsal road's check. Comprehension is relatively good, because it travels the other road. Repetition is disproportionately poor, because it leans on the dorsal road. Naming is often impaired too, with the same kind of sound errors. This is **conduction aphasia**. Asked to repeat "the pipe leaks", she says "the kipe ... the pipe ... leaps": sound errors that she tries again and again to correct. Real words can sometimes be repeated by way of meaning, which still reaches the planner through speech generation, so repetition is the most direct test of the dorsal road when it uses nonwords or unfamiliar phrases.

</details>

**Predict.** Now a stroke breaks only the ventral road, from sound form to meaning. Sound-form cortex, the dorsal road and speech generation are spared. What will the four tests show?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Speech stays relatively fluent, because speech generation is spared. Comprehension is poor, because the road to meaning is cut. Repetition is relatively preserved: she can echo a sentence she does not understand, because the dorsal road is intact. If the break blocks traffic both ways, naming is poor too, because a meaning can no longer reach its word's sound form, and for the same reason her own speech tends to be empty or to use wrong words. The sketch predicts this pattern, and it exists: **transcortical sensory aphasia**, in which naming is usually poor.

</details>

**Dysarthria**, a motor speech disorder in which weak or uncoordinated speech muscles slur articulation, lies beyond the end of the map. The language route is intact; the break is in the motor system that carries out the plan: motor cortex and its descending pathways, the basal ganglia, the cerebellum, the brainstem nuclei, the nerves or the muscles. In isolated dysarthria, grammar, word choice, comprehension and writing are all intact.

This map is not new. Carl Wernicke described the fluent syndrome in 1874, after damage in the posterior superior temporal gyrus. From his own diagram he predicted that cutting the connection between posterior and frontal language cortex would spare comprehension but wreck repetition, and patients with that pattern, conduction aphasia, were later described. In the 1960s Norman Geschwind built these cases into a coarser version of the same map: a posterior area for word sounds and meaning (Wernicke's area), a frontal area for speech planning (Broca's area), and a **tract**, a bundle of axons in the central nervous system, joining them: the arcuate fasciculus. The model is a good teaching scaffold. Today the two roads are usually called the **dorsal stream** and the **ventral stream**, though other dual-pathway models divide the work differently.

## Cutting the bridge: split-brain patients

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

A **commissure** is a tract that crosses the midline to link the two hemispheres; the corpus callosum is the largest ([the map of the nervous system](/lessons/nervous-system-map/)). How commissural axons find the midline during development belongs to [crossing the midline](/lessons/crossing-the-midline/). Cutting the forebrain commissures was used to treat epilepsy that drugs could not control.

A split-brain patient is an unusually clean test of the rule, because the cut removes the main bridges to the speaking hemisphere while leaving the sensory and motor pathways in place, within limits noted below. To read the results, you need the crossings. **Contralateral** means on the opposite side; **ipsilateral**, on the same side.

- **Vision.** At the optic chiasm only the fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross. So everything in the left visual **hemifield** (the half of space left of the fixation point) reaches the right hemisphere, from both eyes ([visual cortex](/lessons/visual-cortex/)). The variable is the hemifield, not the eye.
- **Touch.** Fine touch from the hand ascends in the dorsal columns and crosses in the medulla, so the left hand reports to the right hemisphere ([touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/)).
- **Movement.** Most corticospinal fibres cross at the pyramidal decussation, so each hand is controlled mainly by the opposite hemisphere ([descending control and the basal ganglia](/lessons/basal-ganglia/)).
- **Hearing** reaches both hemispheres ([hearing and balance](/lessons/hearing-and-balance/)), which makes sound a leak in any lateralized test.

Gazzaniga, Bogen and Sperry (1962) reported patient W.J., whose corpus callosum and anterior and hippocampal commissures had been cut. Blindfolded, he could use familiar objects with his left hand but could not name them, because touch from that hand reached only his right hemisphere, which could not speak. Pictures needed brief flashes, so that he was unlikely to look toward them, since an eye movement could expose a picture to both hemispheres. With flashes from a tachistoscope, his left hand could select some simple patterns shown in the left field that he could not identify verbally. The authors warned that possible earlier brain injury limited how far one patient generalizes.

Now the second puzzle comes apart. You can say only what reaches the speaking hemisphere, and in most people that is the left. A key flashed in the left field reaches only the right hemisphere. With the callosum cut, it has no route to speech, so the man says he saw nothing. His right hemisphere did take in the key: it feels with and controls the left hand, so that hand finds the key.

**Predict.** Flash the same key in the right visual field instead. What does he say, and can his left hand find the key?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

He names the key, because the right field reaches the left, speaking hemisphere. Classically his left hand cannot pick it out, because the right hemisphere, which runs that hand, never saw the key. The answer mirrors the key puzzle.

</details>

**Predict.** Now his eyes are closed, and an unseen spoon is placed in his right hand behind the screen. Can he name it?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes. Touch from the right hand crosses in the medulla and reaches the left hemisphere, which usually holds speech, so the route to speech is unbroken. If speech were on the right in some patient, the naming answers would flip: he could name the left-field key but not the spoon in his right hand. The hand results would stay the same, because the crossings do not move.

</details>

The right hemisphere is not empty of language: later testing showed that the disconnected right hemisphere understands some spoken language, so it is mute more often than it is deaf to language (Sperry, Nobel lecture, 1981). That fits the route: single-word comprehension draws on both hemispheres, while the road to speech is usually on the left.

The tidy hand-by-field rule also has exceptions. Two patients reported in 2017 did not follow it, and whether the surgery creates two minds is still unsettled (see Going deeper).

## Which hemisphere speaks: a probability

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

**Lateralization** is a functional difference between the two cerebral hemispheres. Language is left-dominant in most people, but how most? Knecht and colleagues (2000) measured it in 326 healthy adults with functional transcranial Doppler during word generation: probes over each temple track blood-flow velocity in the middle cerebral artery on each side, and the side whose flow rises more is scored as dominant. Right-hemisphere dominance occurred in 4% of strong right-handers, 15% of ambidextrous people and 27% of strong left-handers. Left-handedness raises the odds of right-sided language; it does not make it typical. The exact figures depend on the method (see Going deeper).

That answers the third puzzle in one direction. Now ask it the other way round.

**Worked problem.** Use Knecht's rates as fixed probabilities. The cohort sizes below are assumptions for the exercise, not measured prevalences, and they ignore everyone between the handedness extremes.

**Predict.** Take 100 strong right-handers and 100 strong left-handers. Pick one person whose language is on the right. Is that person more likely to be left-handed or right-handed?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

*Part (a).*

- Expected right-dominant right-handers: 100 × 0.04 = 4.
- Expected right-dominant left-handers: 100 × 0.27 = 27.
- Right-dominant overall: (4 + 27) / 200 = 31 / 200 = 15.5%.

The chance that the right-dominant person is left-handed is written P(left-hander | right dominance), read as "the chance of being a left-hander among people with right dominance":

> **P(left-hander | right dominance) = 27 / (27 + 4) = 27 / 31 = 0.871, or 87.1%**

Compare the reverse conditional: P(right dominance | left-hander) = 27%. A right-dominant person in this sample is very likely left-handed, yet most left-handers are left-dominant. The two statements answer different questions.

</details>

<LateralizationBaseRate />

*Part (b).* Now change the **base rate**, the share of each group in the population: assume 90% strong right-handers and 10% strong left-handers. Take 1,000 people, 900 and 100.

- Right-dominant right-handers: 900 × 0.04 = 36 (as a share of everyone, 0.90 × 0.04 = 0.036).
- Right-dominant left-handers: 100 × 0.27 = 27 (0.10 × 0.27 = 0.027).
- Right-dominant total: 63, or 6.3%.

> **P(left-hander | right dominance) = 27 / 63 = 0.429, or 42.9%**

Check: 36 / 63 = 0.571, and 0.429 + 0.571 = 1. The rates did not change; the base rate did, and the answer fell from 87% to 43%. Rightward language is now more often found in a right-hander, simply because right-handers are so many.

For one person, then, handedness gives odds, not an answer. That is one reason why, before epilepsy surgery, the side of language has been tested directly, for instance with an anaesthetic injection into one carotid artery, rather than inferred from handedness.

Lateralization is function by function. That is different from the popular claim that people are "left-brained" (logical) or "right-brained" (creative) in personality, which the evidence does not support: a resting-state fMRI analysis of more than 1,000 people (Nielsen and colleagues, 2013) found no sign that individuals are globally left- or right-dominant. How age at exposure shapes language is a developmental question, treated in [sensitive periods and behaviour](/lessons/sensitive-periods-behavior/).

## How precise is the map? Lesions and living brains

Return to Leborgne's brain. Was the damage one small patch of cortex?

**Broca's area** is conventionally the pars opercularis and pars triangularis of the left inferior frontal gyrus. Broca kept his two patients' brains whole, and in 2007 Nina Dronkers and colleagues imaged them with high-resolution MRI. In both, the damage extended further **medially**, toward the midline, than Broca could measure: it ran deep beneath the surface, into white matter that carries connections between language regions. He had noted that Leborgne's lesion reached deep structures, but without slicing the brains he could not trace how far. His founding cases never isolated a small patch of surface cortex.

Modern cohorts point the same way. In 70 people with chronic aphasia, Fridriksson and colleagues (2015) used lesion-symptom mapping. Damage to the pars opercularis alone was not sufficient to produce lasting Broca aphasia. Patients with Broca aphasia also had more damage in the left superior temporal gyrus, roughly Wernicke's area, so their lesions were not confined to the frontal end of the route. A syndrome reflects damage along the route, not one patch. The study reported this association; it did not test why comprehension stayed relatively good. **Wernicke's area** is even less settled: where its boundaries lie is still disputed (Mesulam and colleagues, 2015), so no single outline should be learned as settled anatomy.

Fridriksson and colleagues (2018) then tested the two streams directly in 159 native English speakers at least six months after a single left-hemisphere stroke, relating damaged regions and estimated connections to 16 speech and language measures. Motor speech impairment went mostly with dorsal-stream damage and comprehension impairment more strongly with ventral-stream damage, while naming, repetition and grammar depended on both. That fits the sketch. Conduction aphasia, too, is now tied mainly to damage in left posterior temporal and parietal cortex rather than to a cut tract alone (Buchsbaum and colleagues, 2011).

One caution covers all of these studies: a stroke often damages neighbouring regions together, because they share an arterial supply, so lesion maps show associations, which damage goes with a deficit, not which patch is needed alone.

Lesions show what a brain cannot do without some tissue; imaging shows what healthy tissue does. Fedorenko, Behr and Kanwisher (2011) located language regions in each person's own brain, by contrasting sentences with nonword lists, and then found that most of those regions responded little or not at all to arithmetic, working memory, cognitive control and music; one left middle frontal region also responded to verbal working memory. How cleanly language separates from other kinds of thinking is still debated. Malik-Moraleda and colleagues (2022) ran a similar localizer in 86 native speakers of 45 languages from 12 families, contrasting native-language passages with acoustically degraded ones, and found a broadly shared fronto-temporo-parietal network whose regions' activity was strongly correlated. The sampling leaves fine differences between languages open (see Going deeper).

Neither result shows necessity: an activation means involvement, and only damage or disruption tests need. The strongest account combines the two kinds of evidence, and the next lesson, [attention and decisions](/lessons/attention-and-decisions/), applies the same discipline to choosing what to process.

## Design problem

Design a test showing that a split-brain patient's right hemisphere understood a written word it cannot say. List your controls and what the result would and would not show.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

First establish which hemisphere speaks in this patient, from presurgical testing where it exists, and check that he names words flashed in the right field. That check shows the right field has access to speech; it does not by itself prove speech is only on the left. Lateralization is a probability, and everything below assumes speech on the left.

Track the eyes and start each trial only when gaze holds on a central dot. Flash a noun, such as "spoon", in the left visual field, placed well away from the dot so it does not fall near the midline, for 150 ms or less, shorter than most saccade latencies. Keep tracking gaze and discard any trial with an eye movement. The word's visual input then goes to the right hemisphere. Behind a screen, the left hand searches a tray of objects by touch; the patient also says what he saw. The prediction is a verbal report of "nothing" or a guess, with the left hand choosing the spoon above chance.

Controls: right-field trials, where he should name the word; right-hand responses to left-field words, which should fail if the hand-by-field rule holds; silent objects and a screen, so neither sound nor sight of the hand cues the speaking hemisphere; randomized fields and objects. A positive result across many such words shows right-hemisphere comprehension of the tested words in this patient. It does not show two persons, and it may not generalize: patients are few, all had epilepsy, some had earlier damage, and Pinto and colleagues found the hand-by-field rule is not universal.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Lateralization depends on the method

An fMRI study of 50 healthy left-handers (Pujol and colleagues, 1999) found right-sided activation in 10%, a bilateral pattern in 14%, and complete right-sided lateralization in only one person. The figures do not conflict: 10% of 50 is five people, and only one person in the sample was completely right-lateralized, so right-sided activation was usually partial.

### How well two regions sorted the patients

In Fridriksson and colleagues' 2015 cohort of 70, damage to the pars opercularis and the left superior temporal gyrus together sorted over 95% of patients correctly as having Broca aphasia or not.

### The 45-language study in numbers

In left language regions, native speech produced a 2.13% blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal change from fixation against 0.84% for degraded speech, about 2.5 times larger. All participants were also fluent in English, and each language was represented by two native speakers (four languages by one) (Malik-Moraleda and colleagues, 2022).

### Two patients who broke the rule

Pinto and colleagues (2017) found that two patients with radiologically complete callosal section recognized stimuli in either field above chance, whether they answered by pointing with either hand or by speaking, while still failing to compare stimuli across fields. The authors read this as evidence against divided consciousness. Whether callosotomy divides consciousness is unsettled: a 2020 collective review (de Haan and colleagues), written by researchers of differing views, judged the evidence insufficient to decide.
