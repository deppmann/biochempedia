## The big idea

To a child, soil is brown stuff that gets on shoes. Rub a pinch between two fingers, though, and it feels gritty, smooth or sticky. That is a clue that soil is a mix.

Soil is made of bits of rock in three sizes. **Sand** is the biggest and feels gritty. **Silt** is smaller and feels smooth, like flour. **Clay** is the smallest and feels sticky when wet. Soil also holds **humus**, the dark, broken-down remains of plants and animals, plus air and water.

When you shake soil in water and let it sit, the bits settle by size. Big sand grains sink in about a minute. Silt takes an hour or more. Clay can take a day or longer. The jar shows the parts of soil as layers your child can see and measure.

Virginia’s grade 3 standard asks children to name these parts and explain why soil matters to living things. It also asks them to see soil as a resource worth protecting, since new soil takes a very long time to form.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child while the soil and water are out. You tighten the lid and check it before anyone shakes the jar.
- Use a plastic jar with a lid that screws on tight. Do not use glass. Children shake it with both hands, sitting down or standing over a towel. For ages 5 to 7 the adult does the long shaking. The jar then rests on a low, steady surface.
- Use soil from a garden bed or a bag of topsoil. Do not use soil from within 10 feet of roads, driveways or old painted buildings, from lawns treated with weed or bug killers, or from where cats use the soil as a toilet.
- Keep small stones and soil away from babies and toddlers. Nobody puts soil or muddy fingers in their mouth.
- Wash hands with soap and water after touching soil. Wipe up spills right away so the floor is not slippery.
- If you find worms or bugs in the soil, put them back outside where you dug.
- If you do the planting test for ages 8 to 10, use dry beans from the kitchen, not seeds from a packet, which may be treated with chemicals. Count the beans out and back into a lidded jar, keep them away from children under 4, and never taste or eat them. Raw kidney beans are poisonous.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Look and feel.** Spread a spoonful of soil on white paper. Let your child poke through it with a spoon or a magnifying glass. Ask them to find bits of rock, roots and old leaves. Have them rub a pinch between their fingers. Is it gritty, smooth or sticky?
2. **Predict.** Ask, "If we shake soil in water and wait, what will it look like?" Have your child draw their guess.
3. **Fill the jar.** Pick out sticks and stones larger than a pea. Fill the jar about one-third full of soil. Slowly add water until it is about 1 inch (2 to 3 cm) from the top. Watch for bubbles rising as the water goes in. That is air that was in the spaces in the soil.
4. **Shake.** You screw the lid on tight. Shake the jar hard for 2 to 3 minutes over a towel, taking turns. Then set it on a flat spot where nobody will bump it.
5. **Check at 1 minute.** The bottom layer is sand. Your child puts a strip of tape on the side to mark its top and draws it. Bits floating on the water are usually bits of old plants on their way to becoming humus.
6. **Check after 1 hour.** A layer of silt has usually formed on top of the sand. The water is still cloudy. Mark the new layer with tape. In a sandy soil the water may already be fairly clear.
7. **Check after 1 to 2 days.** A clay layer usually forms on top of the silt as the water slowly clears. Clay-rich soils can stay cloudy for several days. Measure each layer in centimeters and compare. Do not move the jar while you wait.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder what soil is made of. What did you find on the paper?"
- "What do you think the bubbles are?"
- "Which bits do you think will sink first, the big ones or the tiny ones?"
- "What is floating on top? Where do you think it came from?"
- "Do you think soil from another spot would make the same layers?"
- "What do you think a plant gets from soil?"

## Why it works

Sand grains are big enough to see one by one. Silt bits are much smaller, about as fine as flour. Clay bits are too small to see even with a magnifying glass. Small particles sink much more slowly, because for their tiny weight, the water holds them back a lot. Clay bits also carry tiny electric charges that push them apart, so they can stay floating for days.

Bits of old plants float because they are light and hold air. Some sink later and lie on top of the clay.

The layers are a rough guide, not a lab test. Some clay clumps and settles with the silt, so the clay layer can look thinner than its real share. Lab soil tests use special methods to get exact amounts. Your jar still shows what soil is made of, and why sandy, silty and clay soils feel and behave differently.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Skip the measuring. Your child marks each layer with a strip of tape and draws the jar at 1 minute, 1 hour and 1 day. Ask one question each time: "What sank? Is the water still cloudy?" Talk about "big bits" and "tiny bits" instead of sand, silt and clay. Have your child crouch to look at the jar from the side, at eye level, without touching it.

### Ages 8 to 10
Fill two jars the same way with soil from two different places, such as a garden bed and a spot under a tree. Measure each layer in centimeters and make a bar graph. Then plant three dry beans from the kitchen in cups of sand, garden soil and clay soil. Give each cup the same water and light, and measure growth for two weeks.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If you see only one thick layer, your soil may be mostly sand or mostly clay. Try soil from another spot. If the water is still muddy after three days, the soil is probably high in clay. Do not add detergent, salt or any other powder or cleaner to the jar. Powdered dishwasher detergent can burn eyes, skin and mouths, and salt makes clay clump and sink with the silt. Clay settles slowly on its own, so just wait longer.
