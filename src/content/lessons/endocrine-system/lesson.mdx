  - name: "Parabiosis and decapitation in insects"
    blurb: >-
      Join two animals so that their blood mixes, or cut off the head at chosen times after a
      trigger such as a meal. Timing shows when a signal from the head is needed, and a shared
      blood supply shows whether that signal travels in the blood.
  - name: "Radioimmunoassay"
    blurb: >-
      Let the hormone in a sample compete with a radioactively labelled copy for a limited amount
      of antibody. The bound label falls as the sample's hormone rises, and a standard curve gives
      the concentration, even at the low levels at which hormones circulate in blood.
  - name: "Hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp"
    blurb: >-
      Infuse insulin at a fixed rate and infuse glucose at whatever rate holds blood glucose
      steady. The glucose infusion needed measures how strongly the body responds to insulin, and
      tracers can split that into uptake by tissues and output by the liver.

**In experiments published in 1849, a Göttingen physician put a testis back into the belly of castrated young cockerels, cut off from its original nerves. The birds still grew combs and crowed. How can an organ change the whole body without its own nerves?**

The first film, above, answers with a tap pouring into a tank of blood and a drain emptying it; the drain stands for clearance, every way the body removes or breaks down the hormone.

> **The one idea.** A hormone's level in the blood is a balance between secretion in and clearance out. Negative feedback adjusts the secretion; the half-life sets how fast the level can follow.
>
> *Scope:* this is a simple model for reasoning about loops, not a description of any one patient. Its assumptions (one well-mixed compartment, first-order clearance, brief pulses) are stated where the numbers start, in the half-life section, and every rule about half-life in this lesson holds inside them.

## What makes a hormone

The first film defined a **hormone**; the cells it reaches are **target cells**, and their **receptors** are proteins that bind the hormone and change what the cell does. Like a radio broadcast, it reaches every house but only tuned sets hear it. Glands that release hormones into the blood are **endocrine** glands, and together they make up the endocrine system. Unlike the point-to-point signals of [nervous control](/lessons/nervous-control/), hormones usually act more slowly and reach the whole body.

The first film left out how thin the evidence was. Berthold used very few birds and did not identify the substance, which we now know includes testosterone, and the graft's new blood supply and lost nerve connection come from later accounts of his examination. The result rules in a blood-borne product of the testis, in this bird, and no more. What outlived it is the design: remove a gland, then restore the gland or its product and see the loss reversed.

Next steps: how a receptor signals inside the cell is in [nuclear receptors](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/) and [G-protein-coupled receptors](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/); secretin, a gut hormone found by Bayliss and Starling in a dog, is in [signals and distance](/lessons/signals-and-distance/).

## Peptides and steroids: two chemistries, two speeds

Most hormones fall into two chemical groups. **Peptide hormones**, such as insulin, glucagon and ACTH, are chains of amino acids. They dissolve in plasma (the liquid part of blood), so most circulate free, and because they cannot cross the fatty cell membrane, they mostly bind receptors on the cell surface. Their effects usually start within seconds to minutes. **Steroid hormones**, such as cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone and estrogens, are made from cholesterol. They dissolve poorly in water, so most of the circulating hormone, about 90 percent or more for cortisol and testosterone in humans, rides on carrier proteins. Because they are fat-soluble, they cross membranes, and their classical receptors sit inside the cell, where the bound receptor changes which genes are transcribed. Transcription can begin to change within minutes, but effects that depend on new proteins usually take much longer, often hours.

Carrier binding also slows the drain: it is one reason steroids such as cortisol have long half-lives. On the usual free-hormone view, the small free fraction is what reaches cells and feeds back.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/endocrine-system/peptide-vs-steroid.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: Two target cells side by side. On the left, small peptide hormone blocks float free in plasma and bind a receptor on the cell surface. On the right, steroid hormones ride on carrier proteins, one free steroid crosses the membrane and binds a receptor inside the cell that acts on genes in the nucleus." caption="A typical peptide hormone circulates free and binds a surface receptor, acting within seconds to minutes; a typical steroid rides on carrier proteins, crosses the membrane, and its receptor inside the cell changes transcription, with effects that usually build over hours." />

Not every peptide works the same way: glucagon's receptor is a G-protein-coupled receptor, but insulin's receptor is itself an enzyme, a tyrosine kinase (see [receptor tyrosine kinases](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)). And not every steroid action is slow: some begin within minutes and appear to start at the cell membrane (see Going deeper). A third group, the **amine hormones** made from single amino acids, splits between the two. Epinephrine dissolves in plasma and binds surface receptors, like a peptide. Thyroid hormone travels mostly on carrier proteins, enters cells with the help of transporters and binds a receptor inside, like a steroid.

## Hypothalamus and pituitary: a chain with feedback

In mammals the **hypothalamus**, at the base of the brain, controls the **pituitary gland** beneath it, and the pituitary controls several other glands. The first film drew one chain; here are the others.

The **anterior pituitary** is a gland in its own right. Hypothalamic neurons release **releasing hormones** into capillaries, and **portal vessels**, short veins that carry blood from one capillary bed straight to a second one instead of back to the heart, take that blood directly to the anterior pituitary. The hormones arrive concentrated, without passing through the rest of the body. Each releasing hormone has a pituitary partner:

- CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone) triggers ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone), which drives the adrenal cortex.
- TRH triggers TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), which drives the thyroid.
- GnRH triggers LH and FSH (luteinizing and follicle-stimulating hormones), which drive the gonads.

The anterior pituitary also makes growth hormone and prolactin. Prolactin is the exception: dopamine from the hypothalamus, carried in the same portal vessels, mainly holds its release down. How the portal link was shown is in [hypothalamus and autonomic control](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/).

The **posterior pituitary** does not make the hormones it releases. ADH (antidiuretic hormone) and oxytocin are made by neurons whose cell bodies sit in the hypothalamus, and those axons end in the posterior pituitary, which stores and releases the hormones into the blood. ADH increases water reabsorption in the [kidney](/lessons/kidney/); oxytocin contracts the uterus during birth.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/endocrine-system/pituitary-two-routes.webp" width={1200} height={811} alt="AI-generated illustration: A block diagram of the hypothalamus above the pituitary gland. On the left, releasing hormones enter a capillary tuft and a portal vessel carries them to a second tuft in the anterior pituitary. On the right, axons from hypothalamic cells run down into the posterior pituitary, which stores and releases ADH and oxytocin into the blood." caption="Two routes from hypothalamus to pituitary: releasing hormones travel in portal vessels to the anterior pituitary, while ADH and oxytocin are made in the hypothalamus and released from the axon endings in the posterior pituitary." />

Each chain is held in check the way the first film's stress axis was, by **negative feedback**: the final hormone turns down the glands above it. In the human thyroid chain, thyroid hormone (T4, and the more active T3) lowers TRH and TSH release, acting at both the hypothalamus and the pituitary.

The tablets add a second tap. As the level rises, feedback lowers TRH and TSH, and with less TSH the person's own thyroid secretes less. Feedback can turn down only the secretion it reaches; it cannot turn off the tablets.

</details>

Not every loop is negative: before ovulation, rising estradiol triggers a surge of LH, a positive feedback that amplifies a change.

## Glucose and cortisol as loops

**Blood glucose.** In a healthy adult human after a meal, glucose rises. Beta cells in the pancreatic islets respond by releasing insulin. Insulin increases glucose uptake by skeletal muscle and fat, and it reduces the liver's glucose output by slowing glycogen breakdown and new glucose synthesis. Glucose falls, and so does the stimulus for insulin. Between meals, alpha cells release glucagon, which makes the liver break down glycogen and release glucose. The chemistry of the liver's response belongs to [integration of metabolism](/lessons/integration-of-metabolism/) and [gluconeogenesis](/lessons/gluconeogenesis/).

**The stress axis.** What the first film left out is where cortisol meets the glucose loop. Cortisol raises the liver's glucose production, though the evidence is from high-dose infusions in human volunteers, not an ordinary stressful day (see Going deeper). For the brain's side of stress, see [emotion and stress](/lessons/emotion-and-stress/).

## How fast a level follows: half-life

**Worked problem.** These are the numbers behind the second film's falling curve. This is a model, not an intact axis. Take an adult human with plasma cortisol of 400 nmol per L at time zero, and suppose secretion stops completely. Assume constant blood volume and first-order elimination (a constant fraction lost per minute), with a **half-life** (t-half, the time for the plasma concentration to fall by half once secretion stops) of 75 minutes, chosen within the textbook range of about 60 to 90 minutes.

*What the numbers stand for.* The level is total plasma cortisol (free plus carrier-bound) in one well-mixed compartment, and the half-life is an effective one that folds carrier binding and tissue uptake into a single drain; real free and bound cortisol behave in more complicated ways. The concentration C(t) at t minutes, starting from C0, is

> **C(t) = C0 x 2^(-t / t-half)**

1. *After 150 minutes.* t / t-half = 150 / 75 = 2 half-lives, so C = 400 x 1/4 = 100 nmol per L.
2. *When does it reach 50 nmol per L?* 400 to 200 to 100 to 50 is three halvings, so 3 x 75 = 225 minutes.
3. *Compare a peptide.* Suppose, for illustration, a peptide hormone with a half-life of 5 minutes also starts at 400 nmol per L. In 30 minutes, which is 6 half-lives, it falls to 400 x 2^-6 = 400 / 64 = 6.25 nmol per L. Cortisol in the same 30 minutes falls only to 400 x 2^-0.4 (30 / 75 = 0.4 half-lives), about 303 nmol per L.

These numbers are invented, as the second film warned of its curves.

Stopping secretion is the special case. With the tap on, the higher the level, the more drains out:

> **change in level per minute = secretion per minute ÷ V − k × level**
>
> V is the volume the hormone spreads through. k = ln 2 / t-half (ln 2 is about 0.69); for cortisol that is close to 1 percent cleared each minute. The level holds steady when the two terms match: steady level = secretion ÷ (k × V).

Change the drain itself, and feedback enters.

**Predict.** In the model, suppose something halves the rate at which cortisol is cleared (k halves), while the loop stays intact. Does the cortisol level double?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

With secretion held fixed it would: by the steady-level equation, halving k doubles the level. But the rising cortisol feeds back and lowers CRH and ACTH, so the adrenal secretes less, and the new level is less than double. How much less depends on how strong the feedback is. Feedback works on the tap, so it can partly cancel a change at the drain.

</details>

## Hormones across animals: moulting insects, metamorphosing frogs

Other animals run the same logic on different molecules, so name the animal each time.

The bug Rhodnius prolixus takes one large blood meal per larval stage and moults some days later. Vincent Wigglesworth found that larvae decapitated soon after the meal did not moult, while those decapitated later did, so a signal from the head was needed for a limited time. Joined by a glass tube so that their blood mixed (**parabiosis**), an early- and a later-decapitated larva both moulted: a moult-promoting signal travelled in the blood. It is Berthold's remove-and-restore logic in another animal.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/endocrine-system/rhodnius-parabiosis.webp" width={1200} height={885} alt="AI-generated illustration: Four headless blood-fed Rhodnius prolixus larvae. Above, one decapitated soon after the meal stays intact and one decapitated later splits its old skin to moult. Below, an early-decapitated and a later-decapitated larva are joined by a glass tube so their blood mixes, and both moult." caption="A moult-promoting signal travels in the blood: a Rhodnius prolixus larva decapitated too early does not moult alone, but moults when its blood mixes with that of a larva decapitated later." />

We now know the chain behind it:

- Neurosecretory cells in the brain release prothoracicotropic hormone (PTTH).
- PTTH makes the prothoracic glands secrete **ecdysteroids**, steroid hormones of arthropods that drive the moult.
- A second hormone, **juvenile hormone** from a small gland behind the brain called the corpus allatum, keeps each moult a larval one; only when it falls does the bug moult into an adult.

The shared blood could have carried either the brain hormone or the ecdysteroid it had already triggered in the partner. A brain hormone driving a gland that makes a steroid parallels CRH and ACTH driving cortisol in mammals, though the insect chain has one tier fewer.

In frogs, tadpoles become froglets by **metamorphosis**, a body remodelling in which limbs grow and the tail is resorbed, driven by thyroid hormone. In an early report usually credited to J. F. Gudernatsch (1912), tadpoles fed mammalian thyroid tissue metamorphosed early. The reverse test also holds: blocking a tadpole's own thyroid hormone synthesis prevents metamorphosis, and the animal grows into a giant tadpole. As in mammals, the brain and pituitary drive the thyroid, and corticosteroids, the frog's stress hormones, work together with thyroid hormone during metamorphosis. Mammals use thyroid hormone to set metabolic rate, among other roles; the response depends on the tissue, the life stage and the animal.

Plants have hormones too, such as auxin, but no blood; they move signals through tissues and vascular tissue, covered in [plant hormones and responses](/lessons/plant-signals/).

### Design problem

An adult human loses all the insulin-making beta cells of the pancreas. Predict what happens to blood glucose after a meal, and name the two actions of insulin that explain it. Then design a test that would show the change comes from missing insulin.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with the loop: insulin increases glucose uptake by skeletal muscle and fat and slows the liver's glycogen breakdown and new glucose synthesis. With neither action, glucose rises higher after a meal and falls more slowly.

Next, put the hormone back. If insulin is the missing step, giving insulin should bring the post-meal rise down: Berthold's design again.

Then rule out the other side of the loop: glucagon, released between meals to make the liver release glucose, opposes insulin and does not take over its job after a meal.

Conclusion: without insulin the loop cannot bring glucose back down. This is a prediction from the loop, not a measured result, and a real patient's course depends on many other factors.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Rapid steroid action in hypothalamic slices

In slices of hypothalamus from young male rats, the synthetic glucocorticoid dexamethasone reduced excitatory synaptic input to recorded neurons within 3 to 5 minutes (Di, Malcher-Lopes, Halmos and Tasker, 2003). Is this the classical genomic action, through an intracellular receptor that changes transcription? Design the tests, and say what each result would mean.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with timing. Effects that need new transcription and protein synthesis usually take longer than 3 to 5 minutes, so the onset alone makes a genomic action unlikely.

Next, keep the steroid outside the cell. Dexamethasone chemically linked to albumin, a large protein, cannot cross the membrane (a permanent conjugate, not loose carrier binding). In this study the conjugate still reduced synaptic input. Add albumin alone as a control.

Then put the steroid only inside. Delivered into the neuron through the recording pipette, dexamethasone did not reproduce the effect. Together these point to a site reached from outside the membrane.

A transcription blocker is a further test: if the rapid effect does not need new transcription, blocking transcription should leave it intact. This tests transcription, not where the receptor sits, and it is a prediction, not a result from this paper as given here.

Finally, look for what carries the signal. Blockers of the CB1 cannabinoid receptor (AM251 and AM281) abolished the effect, so it depended on endocannabinoid signalling in this preparation.

Conclusion: a rapid, membrane-initiated steroid action in rat hypothalamic slices. The membrane receptor was not identified, and the result does not show how cortisol feedback works in a living human.

</details>

### Experiments behind the glucose and stress loops

In mice, deleting the insulin receptor only in liver cells stopped insulin from suppressing the liver's glucose output normally. Cortisol raises glucose production: in normal human volunteers given high doses of hydrocortisone while their insulin, glucagon and growth hormone were held in fasting ranges, glucose production rose through new glucose synthesis.
