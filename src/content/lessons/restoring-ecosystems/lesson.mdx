## Explain: living and nonliving changes

<WordBank terms={['biotic factor', 'abiotic factor', 'fossil fuel', 'deforestation', 'ocean acidification', 'pesticide', 'conservation', 'restoration', 'sustainable practice']} />

A **biotic factor** is a living part of an ecosystem, such as eelgrass, oysters or eagles. An **abiotic factor** is a nonliving part, such as water, air, sunlight or temperature. A change in either one can change the whole ecosystem.

Natural changes count too. By 1933, a disease and a hurricane had wiped out the eelgrass in Virginia’s coastal bays. The disease was a biotic change. The storm was an abiotic one.

You met habitat loss and invasive species in [People Change Habitats](/lessons/people-change-habitats/). Here are six more ways people change ecosystems.

| Area | What people do | One effect |
|---|---|---|
| Climate | Burn coal, oil and gas | Carbon dioxide builds up in the air and warms Earth |
| Water supply | Pump water from streams and wells | Streams can carry less water |
| Air quality | Release exhaust from cars and factories | The gas ozone near the ground can slow photosynthesis in sensitive trees, such as black cherry and tulip poplar |
| Energy production | Run power plants | Some release carbon dioxide and gases that pollute the air; some return warmer water to rivers |
| Ocean acidification | Add carbon dioxide to the air | The ocean absorbs it and becomes more acidic |
| Waste management | Throw away, reuse or treat waste | Wastewater with extra nutrients can feed algae; reusing things saves energy |

A **fossil fuel** is a fuel formed from the remains of ancient living things, such as coal, oil or natural gas. **Deforestation** is clearing forests for farms, roads or towns. Burning fossil fuels and clearing forests both add carbon dioxide to the air.

Air moves around the whole planet, so a smokestack in one place adds to climate change everywhere. Factories can also pollute air and water far away.

**Ocean acidification** is ocean water becoming more acidic as it absorbs carbon dioxide from the air. Seawater is still not an acid like vinegar. But the change leaves less of the material oysters use to build shells and corals use to build skeletons.

These changes touch the cycles of matter. Burning fossil fuels moves carbon from underground into the air ([The Carbon and Water Cycles](/lessons/carbon-and-water-cycles/)). Extra fertilizer adds nitrogen to rivers. Changes can help too: new meadows and forests take in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis.

## Explain: why Virginia’s eagles came back

A **pesticide** is a chemical used to kill pests, such as insects. DDT was once a common one in the United States.

DDT breaks down into a related chemical called DDE. DDE can make bird eggshells thin. Very thin bald eagle eggshells often broke while the parents sat on them. Other eggs failed to hatch. So few young eagles were raised.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/restoring-ecosystems/thin-eggshells.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a bald eagle sits on a large stick nest in a tree by a river. Two close views compare eggs: one with a thick shell and a chick inside, and one with a thin, cracked shell and no chick." caption="DDE, which forms when DDT breaks down, made bald eagle eggshells thin. Thin shells often broke while the parents sat on them, so few young eagles were raised." />

DDT was not the only problem. People also destroyed eagle habitat, and some shot eagles illegally.

Then people acted:

- In 1972, the United States banned most uses of DDT.
- Laws protected eagles and their nests.
- People protected the places where eagles nest.

**Conservation** is protecting living things and their habitats so they last into the future. Saving eagles was conservation.

Recovery took decades. One reason is that young eagles take several years to grow old enough to breed. The count also did not rise every single year.

Recovery is a bit like savings that earn interest: new pairs raise young that become pairs too. The comparison breaks because eagles also die and face new dangers.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Removing a harmful pesticide and protecting eagles and their nests let Virginia’s eagles recover over decades.</Support>

## Explain: helping damaged places heal

**Restoration** is helping a damaged ecosystem recover. The water was clean enough for eelgrass, but almost no plants were left to make seeds. So Virginia scientists spread eelgrass seeds by hand in the coastal bays. The new plants made seeds that spread past the planted areas, and by 2020 a study reported thousands of hectares of eelgrass.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/restoring-ecosystems/eelgrass-from-seeds.webp" width={1200} height={429} alt="AI-generated illustration: three side views of a shallow bay. First, a person in a boat scatters eelgrass seeds over two marked plots. Second, short eelgrass grows in the plots. Third, eelgrass fills the plots and spreads beyond them into a wide meadow." caption="Restoration: scientists spread eelgrass seeds by hand in plots, and the new plants made seeds that spread past the planted areas." />

Other Virginia projects build oyster reefs in rivers such as the Lafayette, keep strips of trees along streams and upgrade wastewater plants ([Big Changes](/lessons/eutrophication-and-big-changes/)).

A **sustainable practice** is a way of using resources that meets people’s needs now and leaves enough for the future. Examples are using only the fertilizer crops need and reusing things.

Recovery has limits:

- **It is often partial.** A review of 400 studies found that damaged places often improve. Full recovery was less common while scientists watched. On average, planting did not beat simply ending the damage; see Going deeper.
- **It can look different.** Within a few years, small animals in the restored eelgrass looked much like those in a natural meadow nearby. Later the restored meadow also held some kinds that the natural meadow lacked.
- **Protecting comes first.** A 2017 study found that restoring cannot always replace protecting a healthy ecosystem.
- **It depends on the damage.** Recovery needs the cause to be removed. Damage that lasts a long time is harder to undo.

### Design problem

Does a strip of plants along a stream keep soil from washing into the water? Plan a fair test with trays of soil.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does a strip of grass at the low end of a soil tray reduce the soil that washes off?
- **Independent variable:** a strip of grass sod at the low end, or bare soil.
- **Dependent variable:** the mass of soil caught from the runoff, in grams. Weigh a dry coffee filter first. Pour the runoff through it, let the filter dry, weigh it again and subtract the filter's mass. Very fine clay may pass through the filter.
- **Control:** trays of bare soil with no grass strip.
- **Constants:** the same soil, tray size, slope, amount of water (500 mL) and pouring speed.
- **Repeated trials:** three trays of each kind. Find the mean for each.
- **Safety:** wear goggles and gloves, wipe up spills so no one slips, and wash your hands.
- **Meaning:** less soil from grass trays supports the idea that the strip holds soil back. Trays are a model, not a stream.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Eelgrass: the exact figure

A 2020 study reported 3,612 hectares of eelgrass in Virginia's coastal bays.

### Does planting beat simply ending the damage?

In a review of 400 studies, on average, planting and other active work did not bring faster or fuller recovery than simply ending the damage. The authors point out that this must be read with care, because few studies directly compared different restoration actions in the same place after the same disturbance.
