      J. W. Grier studied wild bald eagles in northwestern Ontario, Canada. He counted the young each
      nesting area produced. In 1974, the average was 0.46 young per nesting area. By 1981, it was
      1.12. He also tested eggs that never hatched for DDE, a chemical that forms when the pesticide DDT breaks down. Eggs with more DDE came from areas that raised
      fewer young. A study like this shows a link over time. It cannot test every other cause.

## Explain: changes start chains of effects

<WordBank terms={['biotic factor', 'abiotic factor', 'fossil fuel', 'deforestation', 'ocean acidification', 'pesticide', 'conservation', 'restoration', 'sustainable practice']} />

A **biotic factor** is a living part of an ecosystem, such as eelgrass, oysters or eagles. An **abiotic factor** is a nonliving part, such as water, air, sunlight or temperature. A change in either kind, natural or caused by people, can start a chain of effects.

**Predict 2.** A disease and a hurricane had wiped out eelgrass in Virginia's coastal bays by 1933. Which change was biotic, and which was abiotic?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

</details>

## Explain: from nearby changes to global effects

People also change living and nonliving factors. You met habitat loss and invasive species in [People Change Habitats](/lessons/people-change-habitats/). These six areas show other pathways.

|---|---|---|

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Air moves around the planet, so carbon dioxide from one place adds to climate change elsewhere. Factories can also pollute air and water far away.

A **fossil fuel** is a fuel formed from ancient living things, such as coal, oil or natural gas. **Deforestation** is clearing forests for farms, roads or towns. Burning fossil fuels and clearing forests both add carbon dioxide to the air.

The ocean absorbs some of that gas. **Ocean acidification** means seawater becomes more acidic as it absorbs carbon dioxide. Seawater is still not an acid like vinegar. But the change leaves less of the material oysters use for shells and corals use for skeletons.

Burning fossil fuels also moves carbon from underground into the air ([The Carbon and Water Cycles](/lessons/carbon-and-water-cycles/)). Extra fertilizer adds nitrogen to rivers. Changes can help too: new meadows and forests take in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis.

## Explain: helping a damaged place heal

In the second film, **restoration**, helping a damaged ecosystem recover, meant supplying a missing starter. The film did not show other kinds of repair work, or the evidence that recovery has limits.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/restoring-ecosystems/eelgrass-from-seeds.webp" width={1200} height={429} alt="AI-generated illustration: three side views of a shallow bay. First, a person in a boat scatters eelgrass seeds over two marked plots. Second, short eelgrass grows in the plots. Third, eelgrass fills the plots and spreads beyond them into a wide meadow." caption="Eelgrass restoration supplied missing seeds; new plants then made seeds that spread beyond the planted plots." />

Other Virginia projects build oyster reefs in rivers such as the Lafayette, keep strips of trees along streams and upgrade wastewater plants ([Big Changes](/lessons/eutrophication-and-big-changes/)). A **sustainable practice** meets people's needs now and leaves enough resources for the future. Using only the fertilizer crops need and reusing things are examples.

The evidence for those limits:

- **It is often partial.** A review of 400 studies found that damaged places often improved, but full recovery was less common while scientists watched.
- **It can look different.** Within a few years, small animals in the restored eelgrass looked much like those in a nearby natural meadow. Later, the restored meadow held some kinds the natural meadow lacked.
- **Protection is safer than repair.** A 2017 study found that restoration cannot always replace protecting a healthy ecosystem.
- **The kind and length of damage matter.** Recovery needs the cause reduced or removed, and long-lasting damage is harder to undo.

## Elaborate: test a sustainable practice

Does a strip of plants along a stream keep soil from washing into the water? Plan a fair test with trays of soil.

**Predict 5.** After the same amount of water, which tray will lose less soil: bare soil or soil with grass at its lower end?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The grass tray should lose less soil if the strip slows runoff and holds soil. The test still has to measure the result.

</details>

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does a strip of grass sod at the low end of a soil tray reduce the soil that washes off?
- **Independent variable:** a strip of grass sod at the low end, or bare soil.
- **Dependent variable:** the mass of soil caught from runoff, in grams. Weigh a dry coffee filter first. Pour the runoff through it, let the filter dry, weigh it again and subtract the filter's mass. Very fine clay may pass through the filter.
- **Control:** trays of bare soil with no grass strip.
- **Constants:** the same soil, tray size, slope, amount of water (500 mL) and pouring speed.
- **Repeated trials:** three trays of each kind. Find the mean for each.
- **Safety:** wear goggles and gloves, wipe up spills so no one slips, and wash your hands.
- **Meaning:** less soil from grass trays supports the idea that the strip holds soil back. Trays are a model, not a stream.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Does planting beat simply ending the damage?

In a review of 400 studies, active work such as planting did not, on average, bring faster or fuller recovery than simply ending the damage. Read that result with care: few studies directly compared different restoration actions in the same place after the same disturbance. Ending a disturbance can also take substantial work.
