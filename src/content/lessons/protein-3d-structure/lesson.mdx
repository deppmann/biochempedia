summary: 'A protein''s amino acid sequence, in a given environment, sets its three-dimensional shape, and that shape sets its job. Change one residue and both can break, as in sickle cell anemia. Anfinsen proved the fold is written in the sequence by unfolding an enzyme and watching it refold on its own. One rule then carries the lesson: geometry allows, water chooses. The flat peptide bond leaves two rotatable bonds per residue, and the Ramachandran plot shows the few local shapes they allow: helices, strands and the turns that link them. Water picks the arrangement of those pieces by pushing nonpolar side chains into a core, and the same burial between chains builds quaternary structure. Folded proteins are only marginally stable, so one buried residue can tip them. A chain finds its fold in microseconds to seconds because native-like contacts tilt its energy landscape toward the fold, not because it searches at random. Disordered proteins, prions and serpins mark where the rule bends.'
hook: 'One sequence, one working shape. Change a single letter and the shape, and the job, can break.'

      colleagues, he asked a question no one had put precisely before: for every amino
      acid in a chain, which pairs of backbone angles — phi and psi — are possible
      without atoms colliding? They calculated the allowed distances, shaded the
      forbidden combinations, and plotted the result axis by axis. The survivors
      clustered into a few islands — a broad one for the beta sheet and other extended
      chains, one for the right-handed alpha helix, and a small left-handed region —
      with the rest of the plane nearly empty. Sixty years on, his plot is a
      standard sanity check run on new protein structures. The Nobel
      Committee never called; the prizes that touched his field passed him by. He died
      in Chennai in 2001, after Parkinson's disease and the loss of his wife of
      fifty-three years.

techniques:
  - name: "X-ray crystallography"
    blurb: >-
      Fire X-rays at a protein crystal and the diffraction pattern, once its missing
      phases are recovered, yields an electron-density map; at high resolution it shows
      where individual atoms sit. Kendrew solved myoglobin this way in 1958 — the first
      three-dimensional protein fold ever solved, at low resolution — by stacking hand-drawn
      electron-density contours on sheets of plastic until the fold rose into three
      dimensions.
  - name: "Reading a Ramachandran plot"
    blurb: >-
      Plot a residue's two rotatable backbone angles, phi against psi, and the allowed
      combinations cluster into a few islands — mainly the alpha-helix and the broad
      beta/extended region, plus a small left-handed region — with the rest of the map
      disfavored by atoms colliding. It is a standard sanity
      check on new structures.
  - name: "Denaturation and refolding (Anfinsen's assay)"
    blurb: >-
      Strip a protein to a random coil with urea and a reducing agent, watch activity
      vanish, then wash the chemicals away. If the protein refolds to full activity on
      its own, the folding instructions had to be in the sequence — nowhere else.

    choices:

      - 'Buried in the core, because removing them from water releases ordered water (an entropy gain) and lets them pack tightly together.'
      - 'Buried in the core, because nonpolar side chains form covalent bonds with one another there.'
      - 'Spread evenly, because the hydrophobic effect plays no part in tertiary structure.'
    answer: 1
    rationale: 'The hydrophobic effect is the main driving force of tertiary folding: nonpolar side chains are buried in the core and polar and charged side chains stay on the surface. Burying nonpolar groups releases ordered water, which is entropically favorable, and packs them tightly. Nonpolar side chains do not hydrogen-bond well with water, they do not form covalent bonds with each other (disulfides need cysteine), and the hydrophobic effect is central, not absent.'

  - stem: "Why is glycine required at every third position of the collagen Gly-X-Y repeat, and why does substituting a larger residue (as in osteogenesis imperfecta) destabilize the structure?"

      - "Glycine is the only residue small enough to fit at the crowded center of the triple helix where the three chains pack together; a bulkier side chain causes steric clash."

    rationale: >-
      In the collagen triple helix the three chains pack so tightly that only glycine's

  - stem: 'A student argues that Levinthal''s paradox is solved because the Ramachandran plot leaves each residue only a few allowed backbone shapes. Why is that argument not enough?'
    choices:
      - 'Even with only two shapes per residue, a 100-residue chain has about 10³⁰ shapes, so a blind search would still take billions of years; folding is fast because native-like contacts bias the search toward the fold.'
      - 'The Ramachandran plot allows almost every φ/ψ pair, so it removes very few shapes.'
      - 'Real proteins take about as long as the age of the universe to fold, so there is no paradox to solve.'

    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The filter works one residue at a time. It shrinks each factor, from three
      shapes to two, but a 100-residue chain still has a hundred factors: 2¹⁰⁰ is
      about 10³⁰ shapes, billions of years at one shape every 10⁻¹³ seconds. The
      search is fast because it is biased, not because it is small. The plot in
      fact forbids most φ/ψ pairs; small proteins fold in microseconds to seconds,
      so the paradox is real; and chaperones prevent clumping but do not choose
      the fold.

**Swap one amino acid in hemoglobin, glutamate for valine at position 6 of beta-globin, and the protein sticks together into long fibers once it releases its oxygen.**

That is sickle cell anemia. One wrong letter in a chain of amino acids breaks a machine that carries oxygen. So the shape of a protein must be stored somewhere, and stored precisely. Where is it stored, and how does a floppy chain find that one shape?

## One sequence, one shape

Start with the chain itself. The **primary structure** of a protein is its amino acid sequence: amino acids linked by peptide bonds into a chain, from its free amino end (the N-terminus) to its free carboxyl end (the C-terminus). Each amino acid in the chain is called a **residue**. The order is exact, not random. Insulin, for example, is 51 residues in one defined order. That single fact carries the whole chapter:

> Sequence dictates structure. Structure dictates function. One changed residue can
> break both.

The fold decides what a protein can do: carry oxygen, cut another protein, hold a tendon together. Sickle cell shows the whole chain of cause in one residue.

The sequence raises four questions, and this lesson answers them in order.

1. **Where is the shape stored?** In the sequence, in helper proteins, or in the cell?
2. **Which shapes are even possible?** A chain of a hundred residues looks as if it could bend any way at all.
3. **What picks one shape out of the possible ones?**
4. **How does the chain find it fast?** There are far too many shapes to try one at a time.

Two terms first. The **backbone** is the repeating chain of atoms that every residue shares: N–Cα–C, over and over, where Cα is the central carbon of each amino acid. The **side chains** (R groups) hang off each Cα. Some are **nonpolar** (oily, like leucine), and some are polar or charged (like lysine and aspartate).

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson. Geometry allows; water chooses.** The backbone can bend only a few ways, so only a few local shapes are possible: helices, strands and the turns that link them. Among the ways to pack those pieces, the winner tucks the oily side chains inside, away from water, and packs them well. Contacts like these tilt the chain's landscape toward that fold, so it does not have to search for it at random. The rule is for water-soluble proteins in water, with small single-domain proteins as the clean case.

## The proof: Anfinsen's refolding experiment

So where does the folding information actually live? Christian Anfinsen settled it with ribonuclease, an enzyme held together in part by four disulfide bonds: covalent –S–S– bridges between the side chains of two cysteines. He unfolded it completely with urea and a reducing agent: zero activity, a random coil. The reducing agent broke the disulfide bonds, and urea pulled the chain apart. Then he washed the chemicals away and waited.

The conclusion, in his words: "The native conformation is determined by the totality of interatomic interactions and hence by the amino acid sequence, in a given environment." For most small proteins, the fold is the lowest **free-energy** state the sequence can reach. Free energy here is a balance. Folding costs the chain freedom: an unfolded chain can wiggle through a huge number of shapes, and a folded one keeps very few. In return it gains favorable contacts and, as you will see, freedom for the water around it.

The words "in a given environment" matter, and the disulfides show why. A disulfide forms only when two cysteine thiols (–SH groups) are oxidized; the bridged pair is called cystine, and it can link sites within a chain or between chains. The cytosol is reducing and keeps cysteines as free thiols. The inside of the endoplasmic reticulum (the ER lumen) and the outside of the cell are oxidizing. Secreted proteins make their disulfides in the ER, which is why disulfides are found mostly in extracellular, secreted proteins. Anfinsen's tube, open to air, was oxidizing too.

## The flat peptide bond: two hinges per residue

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The shape is in the sequence. Next question: which shapes can a chain take at all? The answer starts with the bond that links one residue to the next.

Look at the peptide bond. It joins the carbon of one residue's C=O group to the nitrogen of the next residue's N–H group. It can be drawn two ways. In one drawing, C=O is a double bond and C–N is single. In the other, the oxygen carries a negative charge (C–O⁻) and the C–N bond is double, with a positive charge on the nitrogen (C=N⁺). The real bond is a blend of the two. So the C–N bond has **partial double-bond character**, and it is short, about 1.32 Å.

A double bond cannot twist without breaking its overlap, so the peptide bond does not rotate. Six atoms are locked in one flat plane: the Cα of the first residue, its C and O, the N and H of the next residue, and that residue's Cα. Almost always the two Cα atoms sit on opposite sides of the bond, the **trans** form. The **cis** form, with both on the same side, is rare, though markedly more common when the next residue is proline (an X-Pro bond).

So picture the backbone as a string of flat, rigid cards, one card per peptide bond. The cards are joined at each Cα. Of the three backbone bonds per residue, one (the peptide bond) is frozen. Only **two can turn**, one on each side of the Cα. Read along the backbone, N–Cα–C, and phi comes first, psi second:

- **phi (φ)**, the rotation about the N–Cα bond;
- **psi (ψ)**, the rotation about the Cα–C bond.

The path of a protein backbone is set by these two angles, repeated down the chain.

## The map of allowed shapes: the Ramachandran plot

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Two free angles sounds like freedom. It is not.

Take two linked cards and turn one hinge while holding the other still. At some settings, nothing happens. But at many settings an atom on one card runs into an atom on the next: a C=O comes too close to an N–H, or a side chain runs into the backbone. Those settings are **sterically disfavored**. The atoms would have to sit in the same space.

Now do this for every pair of angles. Put φ on the horizontal axis and ψ on the vertical, each running from −180° to +180°, and shade every pair that does not clash. When G. N. Ramachandran calculated which pairs were sterically allowed, the survivors clustered into three islands. **Only a small fraction of φ/ψ space is accessible.** That map is the **Ramachandran plot**:

- a broad region for **extended strands**, the beta sheet and other stretched chains (upper left);
- a compact region for the **right-handed alpha helix**, around φ ≈ −57°, ψ ≈ −47° (lower left);
- a small **left-handed** region (upper right), used mostly by glycine.

Glycine roams more widely than any other residue. Its side chain is a single hydrogen. Every other residue has a Cβ, the first carbon of its side chain, which can bump into the backbone; glycine has none.

The plot is also the cheapest first check on any new structure, measured or predicted. If every residue's φ/ψ falls in the allowed islands, the structure is plausible; if many stray into the forbidden zone, something is probably wrong. "Forbidden" means strongly disfavored, not impossible. A few residues, held by their surroundings, do sit in strained spots, and these outliers are often the ones that matter for catalysis.

## Helices and sheets: the backbone bonds to itself

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

The islands are not random. Each one is the geometry of a regular shape that lets the backbone hydrogen-bond to itself. Shapes built this way, from **hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H groups**, are **secondary structure**. It is local: it describes a stretch of chain, not the whole protein.

- **The alpha helix** is a right-handed coil with 3.6 residues per turn. In the interior of the helix, every backbone C=O is hydrogen-bonded to the N–H four residues ahead (residue i to residue i+4). At the ends, some groups have no partner: in the first turn, the N–H groups have no C=O four residues back, and in the last turn, the C=O groups have no N–H four ahead. The side chains bristle outward. Hydrogen-bond partners in a helix are *near each other* in sequence.
- **The beta sheet** is built from strands stretched nearly flat, with side chains alternating above and below. Each strand is hydrogen-bonded to its neighbouring strands, running parallel or antiparallel. Those strands can lie *far apart* in sequence.

Proline shows the rule has teeth. Its side chain loops back and bonds to its own backbone nitrogen, forming a ring.

**Predict.** Proline has no N–H to donate, and its ring is bulky. Where in a helix could a proline sit without breaking it?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In the first turn. There, the N–H groups have no partners four residues back anyway, so losing one costs nothing. In the middle of a helix, proline breaks it for two reasons. It has no N–H to donate to the C=O four residues back, and its ring bumps into the backbone of the turn before it. (The ring also fixes proline's φ near −65°. That is a helical angle, so the fixed φ is not what breaks the helix.)

</details>

**Collagen: the same ring, the opposite effect.** In **collagen**, the rigid ring is an asset. It pre-shapes each strand into an extended helix. A repeating Gly-X-Y sequence, where X and Y are often proline and hydroxyproline, lets three chains wind together into a triple helix. Glycine, the only residue small enough, sits at the crowded center every third position. What kills one structure builds another. Substitute a larger residue for glycine at that center of type I collagen and you get one of the most common causes of osteogenesis imperfecta, brittle-bone disease.

Geometry has now done its job. It allows a few local shapes. It does not say which helices and strands a protein makes, or how they pack together. Something else chooses.

## The hydrophobic core: water pushes oil inward

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

What holds a fold together? Mostly water's push.

Picture one oily side chain, such as leucine's, sitting in water. Water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other all the time, in a shifting network. Next to the oily surface they cannot hydrogen-bond to it. So the water molecules in contact with it arrange themselves into a tighter, more ordered network around it, like a cage. Ordered water has lost freedom, which is lost entropy.

Now bring two oily side chains together. Less oily surface faces the water, so less water has to be caged. The freed water molecules tumble away and gain entropy. That gain is what drives the oily groups together. It is called the **hydrophobic effect**.

A common mistake is to think oil attracts oil and pulls itself together. Oily groups do attract each other, but they attract water about as well, so that attraction gives them no net preference. They are **pushed together by water**, not pulled together by each other. Once they are together, tight packing adds to the gain.

So in a water-soluble protein, **nonpolar side chains are buried in a core** and polar and charged side chains sit on the surface, where they hydrogen-bond with water. The hydrophobic effect is the main driving force of **tertiary structure**, the whole chain packed into a compact 3D shape by interactions between side chains *far apart* in sequence. Myoglobin is the classic example: eight helices cradle a heme group around an oily core.

Burial adds a strong push toward secondary structure. The backbone's C=O and N–H groups want hydrogen-bond partners, and in an unfolded chain, water supplies them. When part of the backbone is buried in the core, those partners are gone. A buried polar group with no partner costs free energy. The cheapest fix is for the backbone to hydrogen-bond to itself, and helices and sheets are the regular ways to do that. So geometry offers helices and sheets, and burial makes them more worth forming.

Water drives the collapse; tight packing and hydrogen bonding, from the backbone and from side chains, make the result specific. Disulfide bonds and salt bridges add more.

## Between chains: quaternary structure

Burial does not stop at the edge of one chain. Several folded chains can bury nonpolar surface against each other and hold together as one machine. That assembly is **quaternary structure**. It uses the same non-covalent forces as tertiary structure, now acting between chains. Hemoglobin is two alpha and two beta subunits, each myoglobin-like, working as one.

Now return to sickle cell. Swapping glutamate for valine at position 6 puts a nonpolar residue on the surface of beta-globin, where a charged one used to be. That makes a new sticky patch. Releasing oxygen changes hemoglobin's shape and exposes an oily pocket. The patch on one hemoglobin fits into that pocket on a neighbouring one, and the molecules stack into long fibers. Sickle cell is an interface between whole hemoglobin molecules that should not exist, built by the same rule that builds the core and holds the subunits together.

The four levels, each with the force that builds it:

| Level | What it is | Main force | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | The sequence of residues | Covalent peptide bonds | Insulin's 51 residues in one exact order |
| Secondary | Local helices and sheets | Hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H | The alpha helix; the beta sheet |
| Tertiary | The whole chain's fold | The hydrophobic effect, plus hydrogen bonds, salt bridges and disulfides | Myoglobin: eight helices around a heme |
| Quaternary | Several chains as one unit | The same non-covalent forces, between chains | Hemoglobin: two alpha and two beta subunits |

## Only just stable: the melt

Here is the surprise. The favorable contacts and freed water only just pay for the chain's lost freedom, so a folded protein is only **marginally** more stable than the coil. That is why heat can undo it, and why one bad residue can tip it.

Two terms describe a melt. The **melting temperature (Tm)** is the temperature at which half the molecules are unfolded. **Cooperativity** means the structure unfolds as one unit: the contacts support each other like the cards in a house of cards, so when enough of them fail, the rest let go together. A cooperative protein gives a steep curve. When a small, single-domain protein melts, it often goes from mostly folded to mostly unfolded over roughly ten degrees.

Lysine to arginine on the surface swaps one positive charge for another, still in water, so stability barely changes.

## Finding the fold fast: Levinthal's paradox and the funnel

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

One puzzle is left. How does the chain *find* its fold?

Count the options. Give each residue of a 100-residue chain just three backbone shapes, one from each island of the Ramachandran map: helix-like, strand-like or left-handed. That is a generous count, since the left-handed island is rarely used outside glycine. The chain then has 3 × 3 × 3 … one hundred times over, which is 3¹⁰⁰, about 5 × 10⁴⁷ shapes. Suppose it could try a new shape every 10⁻¹³ seconds, about as fast as a bond can turn. Trying them all would take about 10²⁷ years. The universe is about 10¹⁰ years old. Yet small proteins fold in microseconds to seconds. That gap is **Levinthal's paradox**, after Cyrus Levinthal, who pointed it out in the late 1960s.

**Predict.** You have just learned that the Ramachandran plot forbids most backbone angles. Is that what solves the paradox?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The count already used the Ramachandran filter: three shapes per residue is what survives it, generously counted. Push the filter further and give each residue only **two** shapes. You still have 2¹⁰⁰, about 10³⁰ shapes, and billions of years of searching. A filter that works one residue at a time makes each factor smaller, but there are still a hundred factors.

</details>

So the answer is not fewer shapes. It is that the residues do not choose blindly.

Picture two landscapes. On a flat golf course with a single hole, every wrong shape is as good as every other. The ball rolls at random and almost never drops in. That is the random search Levinthal described. Now let the ground slope toward the hole from every side. Wherever the ball starts, each roll tends to carry it downhill. This sloping landscape is the **folding funnel**. Its width at the top stands for the huge number of unfolded shapes. Its depth stands for how favorable the chain's contacts are, with the water they free counted in. The native fold sits at the bottom.

What makes the slope? The same forces that make the fold stable. When two oily side chains that belong together in the core meet, they push out some ordered water and the energy drops a little. When a stretch of backbone makes its first helical hydrogen bonds, it drops a little more. But for small proteins, the first few contacts cost more freedom than they pay back, so folding starts with a small uphill step, and partly folded shapes are less stable than either end: the same cooperativity you saw in the melt. Once a core is in place, each further correct contact makes that shape more stable than its neighbours, so it tends to last. Wrong contacts tend to break apart again. The chain does not need to know the answer in advance. It needs only to keep more of what is right than of what is wrong, and a small preference at each step is enough to make the search fast once the first contacts are in place. The interactions that make the fold stable also guide the chain to it.

Real funnels are not smooth. They have dips where a partly wrong shape gets stuck for a while: **kinetic traps**. That is one reason cells make chaperones, proteins that stop half-folded chains from clumping together while they search. Chaperones prevent accidents and raise the yield of folded protein. They do not choose the destination. The sequence, in its environment, still does.

## Where the rule bends

The rule was stated for water-soluble proteins in water. Each case below either breaks one of those conditions or shows where the idea of one stable, lowest-energy working fold fails. The rule itself still helps explain each one.

- **Intrinsically disordered proteins** have no stable fold on their own. Their sequences have too few nonpolar residues and too much net charge to pay for a core. Many fold only when they meet a partner, and disordered regions are common in many proteins.
- **Prions** take the same sequence to a *second* stable fold, rich in beta sheet, that aggregates and kills. The normal prion protein is helix-rich. So a sequence sets a shape, or a set of shapes, for a given environment.
- **Serpins**, a family of protease inhibitors that includes alpha-1-antitrypsin, work in a fold that is not their most stable one. The working fold is a kinetic trap; the lowest free-energy state is a more stable, inactive form.
- **Membrane proteins** sit in lipid, not water, so the solvent is flipped and so is their surface: greasy residues face the lipid. Porins even run a polar channel through the middle.

For folded domains, the rule holds very widely. Today we see folds by X-ray crystallography, NMR and cryo-electron microscopy, and predict them from sequence.

## Going deeper

None of these details changes the main line above.

### How small a preference is enough?

In a simple model of a 100-residue chain (after Zwanzig, Szabo and Bagchi, 1992), each residue makes a local move about once a nanosecond, and each residue in its correct state is favored by a small energy U. With no preference, folding takes about 10²⁹ years, longer than the 10²⁷ years above because of the slower move rate. A preference of about 2 kT per correct residue brings it to about a second, and about 3 kT brings it to microseconds. (kT is the thermal energy per molecule.) The model gives each residue three states, one right and two wrong. Its per-residue preference, summed over the chain, is far larger than any real protein's net stability. In a real protein most of the contact energy is cancelled by the chain's lost freedom, a balance this simple model leaves out.

### How marginal is marginal?

A typical net free energy of unfolding of 20 to 60 kJ/mol at 37 °C means folded molecules outnumber unfolded ones by roughly a thousand to ten billion to one. That is stable. But a single buried polar or charged substitution can cost 10 to 25 kJ/mol or more, enough to wipe out much of that margin.

### The hydrophobic effect and temperature

The picture of freed, ordered water is right near room temperature, where the hydrophobic effect is mostly entropic. At higher temperatures it becomes increasingly enthalpic, because burying nonpolar surface comes with a large change in heat capacity.

### Seeing a fold: crystallography and cryo-EM

**X-ray crystallography** turns a protein crystal's diffraction pattern into an electron-density map, atom by atom at high resolution. It gave Kendrew myoglobin's fold in 1958 and Perutz hemoglobin in 1960, after more than twenty years of work, and together the two structures founded structural biology. **Cryo-electron microscopy** now does the same for large assemblies without crystals, by averaging many thousands, often hundreds of thousands, of images of frozen-hydrated particles.

### Predicting a fold: AlphaFold

Fifty years after Anfinsen, AlphaFold gave striking computational support for the idea that the sequence holds the fold. Given a sequence and alignments of its evolutionary relatives, the neural network predicts many structures to near-experimental accuracy. Its creators, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper, shared the 2024 Chemistry Nobel with David Baker, who was honored for protein design.

### History: how the shapes were found

Sanger read insulin's sequence in 1955. Pauling worked out the alpha helix from a sickbed in 1948 — paper, a slide rule, and the chemistry — and, with Robert Corey and Herman Branson (on the helix), published both helix and sheet in 1951. They predicted both structures from chemical first principles before anyone had seen either. The helix even seemed at first to clash with keratin's X-ray pattern. Perutz soon found the helix's predicted 1.5 Å repeat in hair and hemoglobin, and X-ray work confirmed the helix. In Madras in 1963, Ramachandran's plot showed why the predictions worked: both structures sit where the chemistry allows the backbone to go.

### Prions: a debated energy picture

Why can a prion's sequence reach a second fold at all? One influential view is that the native helix-rich fold is kinetically trapped, and the deadly aggregate is a lower-energy state the sequence could always have reached. This is still debated. Other researchers argue that Anfinsen's idea still fits once you count the environment and all the states a sequence can reach. A prion also spreads by templating its shape onto normal copies of the same protein, with no DNA or RNA required.

### Alpha-1-antitrypsin: one mutation, two organs

Alpha-1-antitrypsin is another one-letter disease. A single substitution, Glu342 to Lys, changes how the protein folds: it folds slowly, and its molecules can link into long chains (polymers) instead of staying single. In the liver, the misfolded protein is held inside the cells that make it instead of being secreted, and damages them. In the lungs, too little of the protein circulates, so the enzyme elastase is poorly controlled and lung tissue breaks down, causing emphysema.

### A rule of thumb: protein mass

An average residue adds about 110 Da to a protein's mass, after the loss of the elements of water when each peptide bond forms. Multiply residues by 110 for daltons and divide by 1,000 for kilodaltons; most proteins are 5 to 200 kDa.
