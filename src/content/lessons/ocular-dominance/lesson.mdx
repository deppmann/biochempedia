      spikes from outside the cell. Stimuli are shown to each eye in turn, with the other eye

  - name: "Monocular deprivation by lid suture"
    blurb: >-
      The eyelids of one eye are sewn shut for a chosen period at a chosen age, so that eye gets
      only diffuse light while the other sees normally. Reopening the eye and recording from the
      cortex shows how the balance between the eyes has changed.
  - name: "Transneuronal autoradiography"
    blurb: >-
      A radioactive amino acid such as tritiated proline is injected into one eye. It is built into
      proteins, carried to the thalamus, partly passed to the thalamic neurons, and carried on to
      their endings in layer 4 of the cortex, where film exposed to brain sections shows that eye's
      stripes.
  - name: "Ocular dominance in the mouse"
    blurb: >-
      Mouse visual cortex has a small binocular zone and no ocular dominance columns. Plasticity is
      read from single units scored for eye preference and summarized as a contralateral bias index,
      or from visually evoked potentials and imaging, before and after closing one eye.

**Sew one eyelid of a kitten shut for about a week when it is a month old, and most cells in its visual cortex stop answering to that eye. Close an adult cat's eye for months, and the cortex looks normal.**

The loss is not explained by damage to the eye. When it is reopened, its retina still responds to light. What changed is in the brain, and it changed only because of what the animal saw, at an age when the wiring could still be rewritten. The lectures for this course put it in one line: "Sensory Environment influences synaptic connections." The questions are how you measure that influence, what the closed eye lost, and why the window shuts.

## The idea: experience decides which eye drives a cortical cell

Each eye sends its axons to the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN), a relay station in the thalamus. There the two eyes' axons end in separate layers, so most LGN cells are driven by one eye. LGN axons then carry the signal to the primary visual cortex, at the back of the brain, where they end mainly in layer 4. Above and below layer 4, many cortical cells receive input traced back to both eyes. In cats and monkeys, the cortex is the first place where many single cells are driven by both eyes.

David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel measured that combination one cell at a time. In **extracellular single-unit recording**, a fine metal electrode sits next to one neuron and records its spikes from outside. With the animal anaesthetized, they showed a bar of light to one eye while the other was covered, then switched.

Each cell was placed on a scale of seven **ocular dominance groups**. Group 1 cells answer only to the contralateral eye (the eye on the opposite side from the hemisphere being recorded). Group 7 cells answer only to the ipsilateral eye (same side). Group 4 cells answer about equally to both, and groups 2, 3, 5 and 6 favor one eye to a lesser or greater degree. Some teaching versions, including the one in this course's lectures, collapse this into fewer bins; this lesson uses the original seven.

Count the cells in each group and you get an **ocular-dominance histogram**. In a normal adult cat it is a hump: most cells sit in the middle groups, and the hump leans toward the contralateral side.

The anatomy has a matching pattern. Inject a radioactive amino acid such as tritiated proline into one eye. It is built into proteins, carried to the LGN, partly passed across to the LGN cells, and carried on to their endings in layer 4. Expose film to slices of cortex and the injected eye's endings appear as stripes, alternating with unlabeled stripes that belong to the other eye. In macaque monkeys these **ocular dominance columns** are sharp in layer 4C; in cats the pattern is less regular.

## Closing one eye: a large shift, mainly in the young

In 1963 Wiesel and Hubel reported what happens when a kitten grows up with one eye closed. They sutured the lids of one eye at or near birth and recorded after about three months of closure. Very few cortical cells could be driven by the closed eye, though its retina and its LGN cells still responded. The LGN cells serving the closed eye did look smaller. An adult cat with one eye closed for months showed no such change.

In 1970 they asked when. They closed one eye in kittens of different ages for different lengths of time. In this kitten series, susceptibility began around the start of the fourth week, stayed high until somewhere between six and eight weeks, and declined through the third month. In kittens whose right eye was closed for only six days during the fourth or fifth week, the number of cells the closed eye could drive fell to a fraction of normal. Six days of unequal vision had moved most of the histogram toward the open eye.

The anatomy follows the physiology. Hubel, Wiesel and Simon LeVay closed one eye in infant macaque monkeys and later traced the stripes in layer 4C. The open eye's stripes had widened and the closed eye's had narrowed. Their 1980 study proposed that in newborn monkeys the two eyes' territories in layer 4C still overlap and separate over the first postnatal weeks, so a closure tilts the separation toward the open eye. Later tracing in newborn monkeys with no visual experience found the stripes already well separated at birth, so an early closure may instead move borders that already exist. In kittens, single thalamic axons serving a deprived eye were later seen to lose branches within about a week of closure.

## Competition: the amount and timing of activity set the strength of a projection

There are two ways to read the closed eye's loss. It could be **disuse**: connections that carry no patterned signal wither. Or it could be **competition**: the two eyes share the cortex, and the one that is more active takes territory from the other.

### Design problem

You have kittens of the age at which one-eye closure causes a near-complete shift. You close both eyes for the same weeks. What should the cortex look like under the disuse hypothesis, what under the competition hypothesis, and how would you tell them apart?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Under disuse, each eye's connections wither in proportion to how little they were used. With both eyes closed, each is as unused as the single closed eye was, so you would expect a large loss of responses to both eyes, perhaps a cortex with few responsive cells.

Under competition, what hurts a closed eye is the advantage of the open one. With both eyes closed, neither has an advantage, so you would expect little imbalance and many cells still responding to each eye.

Record from many cells, score each on the seven-group scale, and also count the cells that respond abnormally or not at all. Wiesel and Hubel did this in 1965. Many cells still responded to each eye, though a share were abnormal or unresponsive. The large loss that disuse predicts was absent. Closing both eyes caused far less harm to each eye's share of cells than closing one, so the shift after one-eye closure comes mainly from competition between the eyes.

</details>

The lecture's phrase for this result is the "Competition Hypothesis: differences in the amount of activity determine the strength of the projection." Later experiments made "activity" literal. Stryker's laboratory silenced one eye of kittens with **tetrodotoxin**, a toxin that blocks sodium channels and silences the retinal ganglion cells, the eye's output neurons. Cortical responses shifted toward the eye that kept firing, so an imbalance of activity between the eyes is enough to cause a shift. Silencing both eyes during development kept the two eyes' axons from separating into stripes. Later work showed that "the quieter eye loses" is not the whole story. In kittens, lid suture weakens the closed eye's responses more than tetrodotoxin does, and in mice silencing the deprived eye prevents the early loss of its responses. A closed eye's retina keeps firing under the lid, and that leftover, unpatterned activity, out of step with the cells it drives, seems to weaken its own synapses.

Amount is not the whole story. Timing matters too. In **strabismus**, the eyes are misaligned, so each points at a different part of the scene. Hubel and Wiesel produced it in young kittens by cutting one eye muscle. Each eye still received a normal amount of patterned light. But the two eyes now sent unrelated signals to any cell they shared. The histogram changed shape: most cells answered to one eye or the other, and few answered to both. With similar amounts of activity, neither eye took over, but the cells the two eyes had shared came to answer to one eye.

A rule that fits both results is the one Donald Hebb proposed: an input that repeatedly takes part in firing a cell is strengthened. Inputs that fire together onto one cell support each other; inputs that fire out of step compete for it. One molecular candidate for detecting "together" is the NMDA receptor, which passes current only when glutamate arrives while the cell is already depolarized (see [that lesson](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/)). Activity also raises the cortex's production of BDNF, a neurotrophin that acts through the receptor kinase TrkB ([that lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)), and it was proposed as one of the signals that rewards the winning input. In mice, though, blocking TrkB did not prevent the shift; it impaired recovery after the closed eye was reopened. Before the eyes open, structured activity of a different kind comes from the retina itself ([that lesson](/lessons/spontaneous-activity/)).

## Critical period versus sensitive period

A critical period is a time when a developing system is especially susceptible to, and requires, specific experience to develop normally. The lectures call such windows "windows of opportunity and vulnerability," and draw a distinction: critical periods begin and end abruptly; sensitive periods rise and fall gradually. The more common definition, from Eric Knudsen, is different. A sensitive period is any window in which experience has an unusually strong effect. A critical period is a sensitive period in which the experience is essential for normal development and its effects last. Real windows often fall between the two, and how sharp one looks depends on the species, the manipulation and the measure. A closure that does nothing detectable to an adult cat's ocular dominance histogram does not mean the adult cortex cannot change in any way.

Name the species with every number. In the 1970 kitten series, sensitivity began near the start of week four and stayed high until some time between weeks six and eight. In macaques the effect of early closure is strongest in the first weeks after birth. In mice, Joshua Gordon and Michael Stryker found in 1996 that a few days of closure shifted responses most strongly near postnatal day 28, with little effect from the same closure in adults. Mice have no ocular dominance columns. Their binocular cortex is a small zone dominated by the contralateral eye, so plasticity is read from single cells, summarized as a contralateral bias index, or from evoked potentials and imaging. The mouse's value is its genetics, which later work used to ask what opens and closes the window ([that lesson](/lessons/critical-period-control/)).

The clinical version of this story is **amblyopia**: reduced vision, usually in one eye, that glasses do not immediately restore, with no structural disease of the eye to explain it. It usually arises when the two eyes give the young brain unequal input, though poor input to both eyes, such as a large focusing error in both, can cause it too. Common causes are strabismus, a large difference in focus between the eyes, and anything that blocks one eye's image, such as a congenital cataract or a drooping lid. Strabismus affects a few percent of children.

The standard treatment follows from competition. After correcting the optics, the doctor covers the good eye with a patch for part of the day, or blurs it with atropine drops, so the weak eye carries the useful visual input. Treatment generally works best in young children, consistent with a sensitive period, although trials have found that some older children still improve. A cataract that blocks one eye from birth is usually removed early in infancy for the same reason.
