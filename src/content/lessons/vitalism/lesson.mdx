**Before the first chapter of any biochemistry course, there is a belief that had to die.** Into the early nineteenth century, many chemists thought the molecules of living things required living things to make them. Sugar, fat, urea, the acids of blood and muscle — all of it was supposed to carry a spark, a *vital force*, that ordinary matter did not have and chemistry could not supply. This was the organizing principle of chemistry itself — the line Berzelius built his entire system on — and the whole field of biochemistry is what happened when it fell.

## The wall

By the early nineteenth century the most respected chemist in Europe was Jöns Jacob Berzelius, who gave the science the letter symbols it still uses — H, O, C — replacing older pictorial signs with a compact shared notation. He also drew the era's sharpest line. On one side was inorganic matter, the chemistry of rocks and salts and gases, which chemists could make at will. On the other was organic matter, the chemistry of living bodies. Berzelius analysed both with the same methods, but the wall between them rested on a single conviction: that the molecules of life required life to make them.

The student who would walk across it had trained in Berzelius's own kitchen laboratory in Stockholm, and had once been told, after rushing an analysis, that the work was "quickly and badly done." His name was Friedrich Wöhler.

## Wöhler's flask, 1828

In February 1828, Wöhler reported that he had tried to make an inorganic salt called ammonium cyanate, from a cyanate salt and ammonia, and what crystallized when he evaporated the solution was urea — the very molecule the body makes to dispose of nitrogen. There was no kidney in the room and no vital spark anywhere in the apparatus. He wrote to Berzelius with a confession he could not keep to himself: *"I can no longer, as it were, hold back my chemical urine; and I have to let out that I can make urea without needing a kidney."*

Look closely at what had happened, because it is subtler than "he made life from non-life." Urea and ammonium cyanate have the *same molecular formula* — CH₄N₂O, one carbon, four hydrogens, two nitrogens, one oxygen. They are the same atoms, connected differently. Warming the solution let the atoms rearrange (by way of ammonia and cyanic acid, chemists later found), and the rearrangement alone turned a lifeless salt into a signature of metabolism. Wöhler could not yet say why; there was no theory of structure in 1828. His result became one of the founding cases for what Berzelius soon named isomerism, two substances with one composition, and chemists later explained it as structure. That idea is the one this whole course depends on: structure, not just composition, is what a molecule *is*.

Wöhler's flask did **not** kill vitalism that afternoon, whatever the textbooks imply. Most chemists, Berzelius included, read it as a striking case of two substances with one composition rather than a refutation, and a skeptic could add that the cyanate had been prepared from materials of animal origin, so perhaps he had only turned organic matter into organic matter. The vital force pulled back, but it had somewhere to stand. Wöhler himself found the field daunting. In 1835 he wrote to Berzelius that organic chemistry seemed to him "a primeval forest, a monstrous and boundless thicket," one a chemist might well dread to enter.

## The seventy-year retreat

What followed was a fighting retreat spanning seventy years, each synthesis driving the vital force into a smaller corner.

In 1845 Hermann Kolbe, trained under Wöhler, built acetic acid by a route that left the loophole very little room. He started from carbon disulfide — made by heating charcoal with sulfur to red heat, with no living thing taking part anywhere in the chain — and ran the final step not with a cell or a seed but with an ordinary battery. Kolbe's own aim was to prove a theory of how molecules are assembled, but the effect outran his intent. It did not end vitalism on its own, but it made the provenance objection hard to sustain. (That two-carbon acetyl group he assembled from lifeless carbon turns out to be one of the central fragments of metabolism, carried as acetyl-CoA into the citric acid cycle in every cell of yours that has mitochondria.)

A decade later Marcellin Berthelot took a different step, rejoining glycerol and fatty acids to remake natural fats, and making new ones that nature does not. His pieces still came from natural fats, but each bond that joined them formed by the same reversible, equilibrium chemistry that governs reactions in the mineral world.

Molecule by molecule, the ground was disappearing. But in chemistry one redoubt remained, and it was defended by the most formidable scientist of the age.

## The last redoubt: fermentation

Louis Pasteur had proved that fermentation depended on living yeast: sterile grape juice kept free of yeast stayed inert, while juice that yeast could reach foamed into alcohol, with or without air. It was a true and important result. But from it Pasteur drew a line stronger than his data required — that fermentation was inseparable from the *intact living cell*. Pasteur wrote of fermentation as a chemical act tied to a vital, living process, but he argued from experiments, not from a mystical force, and his view carried the authority of the man who had just proven fermentation was alive. It was not unopposed: chemists such as Moritz Traube held that yeast worked through soluble ferments, and soluble ferments like invertase had already been pulled out of yeast. But no one could make alcoholic fermentation run without cells. The "needs life" intuition had retreated from individual molecules to whole processes, and for decades the argument there stayed open.

The crack began with a word. In 1877 Wilhelm Kühne, who believed that vision and digestion and fermentation were all chemistry waiting to be lifted out of the cell, proposed a name for the soluble ferments, such as pepsin, trypsin and the invertase of yeast: *enzyme*, from the Greek for "in leaven." It named not the cell and not the tissue but the chemical worker within. Soluble ferments like these had already been pulled out of cells. Whether alcoholic fermentation could be pulled out too was still open, and in 1897 Eduard Buchner showed it could.

Buchner ground brewer's yeast with sand, pressed out a clear, slightly yellow juice with almost no intact cells in it, and added sugar to preserve it. The sugar began to ferment on its own, carbon dioxide rising through a flask of dead yeast, little more than a year after the death of the man who had ruled it impossible. Could scraps of living yeast have survived? Buchner's controls said no. Juice saturated with chloroform, an antiseptic, still fermented, and so did juice passed through a sterile filter fine enough to hold back every yeast cell. Gentle warming destroyed the activity, but heat kills cells as well as chemicals, so that result alone could not settle the question; the filter and the antiseptic did. The carrier of the fermenting activity, he wrote, *"must be regarded to be a dissolved substance, undoubtedly a protein."* He named it zymase and took the 1907 Nobel Prize — often called the first ever given for a fundamentally biochemical discovery. In the chemistry of the cell, the vital force was out of places to hide (it lived on for a while in embryology and philosophy).

## Why this is Chapter Zero

Seventy years of successive syntheses — Wöhler's urea in 1828, Kolbe's acetic acid, Berthelot's fats, and finally Buchner's cell-free fermentation in 1897 — made the vital force unnecessary, one molecule and one process at a time, until almost no ground was left for it to stand on. What replaced it is the single premise the rest of this book assumes without restating: a living cell obeys **the same chemistry as everything else** — weighable and accountable, with no separate law of its own.

Everything downstream is the working-out of that premise. The catalysts Kühne named and Buchner freed are, nearly all of them, proteins. James Sumner took the first big step in 1926 when he crystallized urease, the enzyme that takes urea back apart, and held a catalyst of life in his hand as a pile of crystals. Others doubted the crystals were the enzyme until more enzymes were crystallized in the early 1930s. The acetyl group Kolbe built from charcoal is the hub of metabolism. And urea itself, the molecule Wöhler conjured from a salt, is built in your liver right now by five enzymes working in a loop called the urea cycle — the subject of one of the last chapters in this course. The thread that runs from his flask to that cycle is the whole discipline: life is chemistry, and chemistry can be measured, mapped, and understood.
