**In 1896 a flask of pressed yeast juice, with almost no intact cells in it, turned sugar into alcohol. That experiment, rather than Wöhler's urea nearly seventy years earlier, is often credited with launching modern biochemistry.**

That sounds backwards if you learned that Friedrich Wöhler killed the idea of a vital force in 1828 by making urea, a molecule of the body, from a lifeless salt. Into the early nineteenth century many chemists held that the molecules of living things needed living things to make them. Sugar, fat, urea, the acids of blood and muscle were all supposed to carry a spark, a *vital force*, that ordinary matter lacked. That belief took seventy years to fall, and why it took so long is the logic of this lesson.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A claim that something "needs life" is tested by doing that thing with the life taken out. A test counts only as far as you can show the life is really gone. Each step of the seventy-year retreat took life out of one more place: out of the making of a molecule (Wöhler), out of its starting materials (Kolbe), and finally out of a whole working process (Buchner, whose filter and antiseptic showed that no living cell remained).

It is a rule for testing a claim, not a law of chemistry, and "really gone" means gone by evidence the opponents of the day accepted. Wöhler is the first case to test it on.

## The wall

By the early nineteenth century the most respected chemist in Europe was Jöns Jacob Berzelius, who gave the science the letter symbols it still uses (H, O, C), replacing older pictorial signs with a compact shared notation. He also drew the era's sharpest line: inorganic matter, the chemistry of rocks and salts and gases, which chemists could make at will, against organic matter, the chemistry of living bodies.

In Berzelius's day, *organic* meant obtained from a living thing: sugar from cane, urea from urine, fat from tallow. The category was defined chiefly by where a substance came from, not by its formula. (Today *organic chemistry* is the chemistry of carbon compounds whatever their source, though simple ones such as CO₂, carbonates and CS₂ are conventionally filed under inorganic.) Keep that in mind; every argument in this lesson turns on it. Berzelius analysed both kinds with the same methods, but the wall between them rested on the conviction that only life could make them.

The student who would walk across it had trained in Berzelius's own kitchen laboratory in Stockholm, and had once been told, after rushing an analysis, that the work was "quickly and badly done." His name was Friedrich Wöhler.

## Wöhler's flask, 1828

In February 1828 Wöhler, then twenty-seven, reported that he had tried to make an inorganic salt, ammonium cyanate, from a cyanate salt and ammonia. When he evaporated the solution, the crystals were not the salt but urea, the very molecule your body makes to get rid of nitrogen. There was no kidney in the room. He wrote to Berzelius: *"I can no longer, as it were, hold back my chemical urine; and I have to let out that I can make urea without needing a kidney."*

Look closely at what happened, because it is subtler than "he made life from non-life."

**Same atoms, different connections.**

- **Ammonium cyanate** is a salt, two ions held together by charge: NH₄⁺ (ammonium) and OCN⁻ (cyanate).
- **Urea** is one neutral molecule held together by covalent bonds: a carbon double-bonded to an oxygen, with an NH₂ group on each side, H₂N–C(=O)–NH₂.

Both come to one carbon, four hydrogens, two nitrogens and one oxygen: CH₄N₂O. Compounds with the same formula and different connections are **isomers**. Berzelius soon named the idea isomerism, and Wöhler's urea was one of its founding cases.

<WoehlerIsomerization />

**How the atoms move** (chemistry worked out later; optional on a first read). Chemists found two steps, by way of ammonia and cyanic acid:

1. The ammonium ion hands a proton (H⁺) to the cyanate. That leaves ammonia (NH₃) and cyanic acid (HNCO).
2. The nitrogen of ammonia uses its lone pair to attack the carbon of cyanic acid, and a proton shifts. The result is urea.

**Which side wins?** Urea, the more stable form by a wide margin: at equilibrium a solution like Wöhler's is almost all urea. Warming did not force the change; it sped up one already downhill, and evaporation pushed it further: crowding the ions together favors the side with fewer particles, and two ions become one molecule (Going deeper, "Why urea wins"). So when the opening film says "heat rearranged the connections," it means heat let them rearrange.

Wöhler could not have said any of this; there was no theory of molecular structure in 1828. But his result carries the idea this whole course depends on: **what a molecule is depends on how its atoms are connected, not just on which atoms it contains.**

So Wöhler's flask did **not** kill vitalism that afternoon, whatever the textbooks imply. It took life out of the *making* of one molecule but left it standing in the *starting materials*.

## Kolbe narrows the loophole, 1845

**Predict.** What would a synthesis have to start from to answer the skeptic's objection?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

From the elements, or from something no one could call organic.

In 1845 Hermann Kolbe, trained under Wöhler, built acetic acid by a route that left the loophole very little room. He started from carbon disulfide, made by heating charcoal with sulfur to red heat:

> carbon disulfide → carbon tetrachloride → tetrachloroethylene → trichloroacetic acid → acetic acid

You need not remember the chain; what matters is where it starts. He ran the final step not with a cell or a seed but with an ordinary battery, a reduction: its current supplied the electrons that swapped the last three chlorines for hydrogens. Charcoal is usually made from wood, but once charred to carbon it counted, for chemists of the day, as an element: mineral matter with only a distant tie to life. Nothing in the chain still carried life.

Kolbe's own aim was to prove a theory of how molecules are assembled, but the effect outran his intent. It did not end vitalism on its own, but it made the provenance objection (where the starting materials came from) hard to sustain. His two-carbon acetyl group, carried as acetyl-CoA into the citric acid cycle in every cell of yours that has mitochondria, is the hub of metabolism.

## Berthelot's fats, 1854

A decade later Marcellin Berthelot took a different step, rejoining glycerol and fatty acids to remake natural fats:

> glycerol + 3 fatty acids ⇌ fat (a triglyceride) + 3 H₂O

Each fatty acid joins the glycerol by an ester bond and releases one water. Add water and the reaction runs back: that is hydrolysis. This is the same reversible, equilibrium chemistry that governs reactions in the mineral world, with no special law for the body's molecules. Berthelot also made new fats that nature does not.

**Predict.** By the rule, where was life removed in Berthelot's work, and where was it still standing?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Life was out of the joining step, but his glycerol and fatty acids came from natural fats, so life was still in the parts. A partial test, then.

</details>

Molecule by molecule, the ground was disappearing. One redoubt remained, defended by the most formidable scientist of the age.

## The last redoubt: fermentation

Louis Pasteur had proved that fermentation, yeast turning sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, depended on living yeast: sterile grape juice kept free of yeast stayed inert, while juice that yeast could reach foamed into alcohol, with or without air. But from that true result Pasteur drew a line stronger than his data required: that fermentation was inseparable from the *intact living cell*. Pasteur wrote of fermentation as a chemical act tied to a vital, living process, but he argued from experiments, not from a mystical force.

The "needs life" intuition had retreated from single molecules to whole processes. Some ferments, such as pepsin and the invertase of yeast, already worked outside cells, and in 1877 Wilhelm Kühne named them *enzymes*, from the Greek for "in leaven". The open question was narrower and harder: could sugar-to-alcohol fermentation, the one Pasteur had tied to life, be pulled out of the cell too? For decades no one could make it run without cells.

## Buchner's flask, 1896–97

In late 1896 (published in January 1897), Eduard Buchner ground brewer's yeast with sand, pressed out a clear, slightly yellow juice with almost no intact cells in it, and added sugar to preserve it. The sugar began to ferment on its own, carbon dioxide rising through a flask of dead yeast, little more than a year after the death of the man who had tied it to the living cell.

Could scraps of living yeast have survived?

The carrier of the fermenting activity, Buchner wrote, *"must be regarded to be a dissolved substance, undoubtedly a protein."* He named it zymase and took the 1907 Nobel Prize, often called the first ever given for a fundamentally biochemical discovery. Zymase turned out to be not one substance but a team: Harden and Young later split it into enzymes plus small heat-stable cofactors, chief among them cozymase, now known as NAD. That team is glycolysis plus the two steps that make ethanol, a later chapter of this course.

In the chemistry of the cell, the vital force was out of places to hide. (Outside chemistry, in theories of how embryos develop and in philosophy, it lived on for a while.)

## Why this is Chapter Zero

Seventy years of experiments made the vital force unnecessary. What replaced it is the single premise the rest of this course assumes without restating: a living cell obeys **the same chemistry as everything else**. Its reactions can be pulled out, measured, and explained by the same rules of structure, equilibrium and energy, with no separate law of its own.

The catalysts Kühne named and Buchner freed are, nearly all of them, proteins. And urea itself, the molecule Wöhler conjured from a salt, is built in your liver right now by five enzymes working in a loop called the urea cycle, the subject of one of the last chapters in this course. Life is chemistry, and chemistry can be measured, mapped, and understood.

## Going deeper

### Why urea wins

Standard free energies of formation put the conversion well downhill: at room temperature a 0.1 M solution comes to roughly 98–99% urea at equilibrium. The conversion releases heat, so warming *lowers* the equilibrium constant, about twenty- to thirtyfold by 100 °C, yet a boiling 0.1 M solution is still over 90% urea. These are approximate, ideal-solution estimates.

### The primeval forest, and a later myth

Wöhler himself found the field daunting. In 1835 he wrote to Berzelius that organic chemistry seemed to him "a primeval forest, a monstrous and boundless thicket," one a chemist might well dread to enter. Historians argue that the tidy story in which his flask ended vitalism was assembled decades later.

### Pasteur's critics, and Kühne's word

Pasteur's view was not unopposed. Chemists such as Moritz Traube held that yeast worked through soluble ferments. Wilhelm Kühne studied the chemistry of digestion and of vision, and believed that both, like fermentation, were chemistry waiting to be lifted out of the cell. His word *enzyme*, which also covered trypsin, named not the cell and not the tissue but the chemical worker within. It classified a kind of ferment; it did not settle whether yeast fermentation could leave the cell.

### Sumner's crystals

In 1926 James Sumner crystallized urease, the enzyme that takes urea back apart, and held a catalyst of life in his hand as a pile of crystals. He argued it was a protein. Others doubted the crystals were the enzyme until more enzymes, Northrop's among them, were crystallized in the early 1930s.

### The rule's last test

In 1969 Gutte and Merrifield assembled active ribonuclease A from amino acids in the laboratory; at the same time a group at Merck synthesized the large S-protein fragment of the enzyme, which became active when joined to its S-peptide partner.
