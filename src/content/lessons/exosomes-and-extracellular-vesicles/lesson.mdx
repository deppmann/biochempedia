techniques:
  - name: "Differential ultracentrifugation"
    blurb: >-
      Spin a fluid at increasing speeds and keep the last pellet: low speed removes cells,
      medium speed removes debris and large vesicles, and about 100,000 g brings down small
      vesicles. It tells you what sediments under those conditions, which includes anything of
      similar size and density, so the pellet is a starting material and not a pure preparation.
  - name: "Density-gradient flotation"
    blurb: >-
      Place the pellet in a gradient of sucrose or iodixanol and spin until each particle
      stops at its own density. Small vesicles usually float at about 1.13 to 1.19 g/mL in sucrose, though vesicle densities vary,
      while free protein stays in denser fractions. Testing every fraction shows whether an
      activity travels with the vesicles.
  - name: "Gold labelling for electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Couple a ligand or an antibody to particles of gold, which appear as black dots in the
      electron microscope, and use it to mark one protein. It showed the transferrin receptor
      on the internal vesicles of multivesicular bodies and on the vesicles released when those
      bodies fused with the cell surface.
  - name: "Protease and nuclease protection"
    blurb: >-
      Treat vesicles with a protease or an RNase, with and without detergent. Cargo inside a
      sealed vesicle is spared until detergent opens the membrane, while cargo on the surface or
      loose in the preparation is destroyed, unless a bound protein shields it, as Argonaute proteins shield microRNA from RNase. It tells you where a molecule is, and that
      decides how it could act.

**A scrap of membrane shed by a B cell can switch on only the T cells that match its peptide.** The first film animated how those vesicles form right-side out; here we examine the proof behind that case, trace the membrane inversions and volume math, and build the controls that separate real signalling from centrifuge artefacts.

## Vesicles can carry a signal: the clearest case

**Extracellular vesicles** are small membrane sacs released into surrounding fluid. Wrapped around a drop of cell interior, each sac can carry membrane proteins on its surface and cytosolic protein and RNA inside.

The cleanest proof comes from the immune system. B lymphocytes load peptides onto **MHC class II** molecules—the membrane proteins that display peptides to T cells—inside compartments packed with small internal vesicles. In 1996 Graça Raposo and colleagues showed that B cells release those vesicles carrying peptide-loaded MHC class II. Added to cultured T cells, the vesicles stimulated only T cells whose receptor recognised that exact peptide on that MHC molecule.

**Predict.** If a stray soluble growth factor had pelleted alongside the B-cell vesicles, how would the matching and non-matching T-cell lines respond?

<Support>**The one idea.** Extracellular vesicles keep cell orientation: ligands face out, internal cargo stays sealed; proving vesicle signalling requires separating vesicles, locating cargo, and counting copies. **Scope:** This rule covers intact extracellular vesicles budding away from the cytosol (exosomes and microvesicles), not intracellular synaptic or secretory vesicles. Internal cargo can still signal if it escapes to the cytosol or is sensed in an endosome, and proving a claim also needs mock, dose and specificity controls.</Support>

## Where the vesicles come from: one compartment, two exits

You met the **multivesicular body** (multivesicular endosome, or MVE) earlier in this unit on the road to the lysosome. To see why its vesicles keep the cell's orientation, trace one receptor through the two membrane inversions behind the first film:

1. **Endocytosis (first inversion).** Plasma membrane pinches inward to form an endosome, turning the receptor's ligand-binding domain *inward* into the endosome fluid while its tail stays in the cytosol.
2. **Inward budding (second inversion).** The endosome membrane buds *inward*, away from the cytosol, pinching off internal vesicles carrying ubiquitin-tagged receptors. Pushing cytosol into each bud flips the membrane back: the ligand-binding domain again faces *outward*, and cytosol is sealed inside.

The compartment is a sorting room with two doors: one to the incinerator and one to the street. Fusing with a **lysosome** digests the internal vesicles; moving to the surface and fusing with the plasma membrane releases them intact as **exosomes**. The word exosome therefore names an origin: a vesicle that formed inside a multivesicular body. Exosomes are single-membrane sacs usually quoted as about 30 to 100 nanometres across (authors differ on the limits), too small to resolve in a light microscope.

**Microvesicles** (or **ectosomes**) bud straight outward from the plasma membrane, skipping the endosome. That bud also forms away from the cytosol, with zero inversions instead of two, so a microvesicle has the same orientation as an exosome.

**Predict.** Could a protease-protection test on a mixed preparation tell an exosome from a microvesicle?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Both kinds carry their ectodomains outside and cytosol inside, so a protease cuts the same surface proteins on both and spares the same lumenal ones. Telling them apart needs evidence of origin, such as images of a multivesicular body fusing with the surface.

</details>

Because the two kinds share orientation, size range and common markers—**tetraspanins** such as CD9, CD63 and CD81, membrane proteins with four membrane-spanning helices—no agreed test reliably separates them once mixed in medium. The International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) therefore urges "extracellular vesicle" or "small extracellular vesicle" unless origin is proved. **Synaptic vesicles** differ completely: they remain inside neurons, fuse to release soluble transmitter, and are recycled.

This route was first seen in maturing red blood cells (**reticulocytes**), which still make haemoglobin and import iron via transferrin receptors, whereas mature red cells make neither and carry essentially none. In 1983 two groups found those receptors leaving on roughly 50 nanometre vesicles; rat (1983) and sheep (1985) micrographs caught multivesicular bodies fusing with the surface, and in 1987 Rose Johnstone's group named the released vesicles exosomes, viewing them as protein disposal (see Going deeper). B-cell MHC class II compartments release internal vesicles the same way.

Inward budding uses **ESCRT** protein complexes with partners such as **ALIX**; alternative routes exist (one depending on the lipid **ceramide**), and **Rab** small GTPases help dock multivesicular bodies at the surface in some cells. Which machinery operates varies by cell type, and what chooses the exit door is only partly known (details in Going deeper).

## What is inside, and three ways it could act

By geometry, vesicles carry membrane proteins (including receptors), cytosolic proteins, membrane lipids and RNA. Messenger RNA and **microRNA** (short RNAs that lower output of matching messenger RNAs) drew wide attention after Jan Lötvall's group reported both in exosome preparations from mast cells (a kind of immune cell) in 2007 and detected mouse proteins after adding those vesicles to human mast cells. Yet carefully purified small vesicles hold mostly transfer RNA and other small non-coding RNAs, and a 2014 counting study found far less than one copy of any given microRNA per vesicle on average.

Sphere geometry sets that ceiling. A 100 nanometre sphere encloses at most about $5 \times 10^{-19}$ litres (less inside its bilayer). At 1 micromolar in cytosol, passive trapping captures only about 0.3 molecules per vesicle unless cargo is concentrated. Meanwhile, a vesicle's outer surface can display many copies of a membrane protein without anything crossing a bilayer.

**Predict.** If an exosome is 50 nanometres across (as in reticulocytes) instead of 100 nanometres, by roughly what factor does its total sphere volume drop, and what happens to the passive average at 1 micromolar?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Volume scales with diameter cubed, so halving diameter cuts volume eightfold ($2^3 = 8$), dropping the passive average from about 0.3 to roughly 0.04 molecules per vesicle (less inside the bilayer).

</details>

A vesicle could therefore act in three ways, each requiring different evidence:

1. **Surface ligand.** An outer membrane protein binds a target receptor directly—nothing enters, avoiding the copy-number limit (as in B-cell vesicles).
2. **Membrane fusion.** The vesicle merges with the plasma membrane or an endosome membrane, releasing cargo into the cytosol—the main proposed route for RNA or cytosolic enzymes (leaky-endosome escape is also proposed).
3. **Endocytic uptake.** The target swallows the vesicle. Uptake alone proves little, because swallowed material usually reaches the lysosome; it signals only if cargo is sensed on the way or escapes.

## How would you know? The pellet is defined by the centrifuge

The second film showed why spinning mixes vesicles with debris; here are the isolation protocol, the landmark contaminant test and the full control checklist.

Standard isolation uses **differential ultracentrifugation**: a slow spin clears cells and debris, a faster spin removes large vesicles, and about 100,000 times gravity for an hour or more pellets small vesicles. Everything of similar size and density sediments too: protein aggregates, lipoproteins, RNA–protein complexes, viruses, vesicles from calf serum in the medium, and stuck soluble growth factors.

The contaminants have changed conclusions. In 2019 Robert Coffey's laboratory separated such pellets on density gradients and independently captured vesicles on antibodies without high-speed spinning; **Argonaute** proteins (which carry microRNA) were not detected in exosomes, and small vesicles were not vehicles of active DNA release.

In practice, proving vesicle signalling comes back to separating vesicles, locating cargo and counting copies:

| Artefact to rule out | Control or assay | Clean-signal result (and key caveat) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dense protein / aggregate** | **Density-gradient flotation** (load pellet at *bottom* of sucrose gradient) | Small vesicles in sucrose usually rise to about 1.13–1.19 g/mL and free protein stays below; activity should float with the membrane markers (some lipoproteins can still co-isolate) |
| **Soluble factor / serum** | Post-spin **supernatant** and cell-free **mock pellet** | Neither reproduces the pellet's activity (even vesicle-depleted serum keeps much of its RNA) |
| **Surface or loose cargo** | **Protease / RNase protection** ± detergent | Lumenal cargo survives enzyme alone and vanishes only with detergent (bound proteins like Argonaute can shield loose RNA from RNase) |
| **Unrealistic stoichiometry** | Count cargo and particles; check donor release rate | Enough copies and realistic dose (though a rare subset could carry most copies) |

The central measurement is:

> **copies per vesicle = copies of the cargo measured ÷ particles counted**

If that ratio sits far below one, or if the effect needs far more vesicles than donor cells release, the signalling claim remains weak however clean the pellet looks.

**Predict.** In a density-purified preparation, RNase alone removes 90% of the copies of one RNA. What does that say about where most of that RNA sat, and about a copies-per-vesicle number calculated before the treatment?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Most of it was outside sealed vesicles, loose or stuck to their surface, because RNase without detergent cannot reach lumenal cargo. Dividing the untreated count by particles therefore overstated the vesicle cargo about tenfold; only the protected copies belong in the numerator.

</details>

### Design problem

You spin the medium from cell type A, add the pellet to cell type B, and B changes its behaviour. Design the controls you need before you may say that exosomes from A carried a signal to B. Name at least three other explanations, and the experiment that removes each one.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**The serum did it.** Calf serum brings vesicles and growth factors. Grow A without serum, or in vesicle-depleted serum (a long spin still leaves much serum RNA behind), and test a mock pellet from cell-free medium. If the mock matches the real pellet, the medium—not A—is the source.

**A soluble factor pelleted too.** A cytokine from A may have aggregated or stuck to vesicles. Float the pellet on a density gradient and test every fraction on B; activity should peak with vesicle markers and particle counts.

**A contaminant did it.** If B is an immune cell, trace bacterial lipopolysaccharide switches on Toll-like receptor 4. Assay for lipopolysaccharide and repeat with B cells lacking that receptor.

**Unrealistic dose or non-specific cargo.** Count added particles, compare with A's release rate and titrate down. A pellet from A cells lacking the candidate cargo should fail—checking also whether deleting the gene altered A or its other vesicles.

With all four done you have a strong case for vesicle signalling from A. To call them exosomes, you must also prove multivesicular-body origin.

</details>

## Where the evidence is good, and where it is argued

- **Antigen presentation.** The strongest case, set out at the top of this lesson: T-cell activation followed peptide and MHC class II and acted at the surface with nothing entering. Two years after 1996, a group including Raposo reported that dendritic-cell vesicles loaded with tumour peptides suppressed or eliminated established tumours in mice; how much such vesicles add in vivo remains less clear.
- **Disposal.** Reticulocyte transferrin-receptor shedding stands cleanly without needing any message.
- **Axon repair.** **Schwann cells** (the glia wrapping peripheral axons) release vesicles that sensory axons take up, speeding regrowth in culture and after nerve injury. Because this rests on adding prepared vesicles, count it as supported rather than closed.
- **"Cancer uses exosomes to evade therapy."** Tumour vesicles can display **PD-L1**, the ligand for the checkpoint receptor **PD-1**. In a mouse prostate-cancer model, deleting **Rab27a** sharply cut exosome release and stopped tumour growth in mice with a working immune system, while tumours still grew in immunodeficient mice. The same study also deleted PD-L1 itself, and exosomal PD-L1 injected into the mice restored growth of tumours that could not secrete their own. Tumour vesicles have also been reported as antibody-drug decoys, and circulating vesicle PD-L1 has been tracked in melanoma patients, though patient effect size remains uncertain.
- **As tools.** Blood and urine vesicles carry proteins and RNA from source tissues, motivating biomarker tests and drug- or RNA-loading efforts; both depend on knowing exactly what is in the tube.

## Going deeper

### How the route was found

The reticulocyte has lost its nucleus but still needs iron, which arrives on the blood protein transferrin.

In 1983 two laboratories reported where its receptors go. In St. Louis, Clifford Harding, John Heuser and Philip Stahl coupled transferrin to particles of gold, which show as black dots in the electron microscope, and followed it into rat reticulocytes. The gold reached endosomes and then appeared in a compartment packed with small vesicles (about 50 nanometres across, by the authors' later account), with the gold on them. Some of these compartments appeared to have fused with the plasma membrane, and their small vesicles were outside the cell. In Montreal, Bin-Tao Pan and Rose Johnstone cultured sheep reticulocytes and found that the receptor lost from the cells turned up in the medium, on vesicles they could collect in a centrifuge. Their electron micrographs of 1985 showed the same route in the sheep cells. In 1987 Johnstone's group used the name **exosome** for these vesicles.

### What sets the door

In a mouse oligodendrocyte cell line, loading one myelin protein into the internal vesicles needed the lipid ceramide and not ESCRT. In the human cell line HeLa, the small GTPases Rab27a and Rab27b were needed for multivesicular bodies to dock at the surface. Each result holds for the cells it was measured in.

### Early reports of RNA in vesicles

Messenger RNA in vesicles from embryonic stem cells was reported in 2006, a year before the mast cell study from Jan Lötvall's laboratory in Gothenburg.

### Axon repair in detail

The laboratory of Felipe Court proposed in 2012, and reported with data in 2013, that vesicles from cultured Schwann cells are taken up by sensory axons, speed their regrowth in culture and after injury to the sciatic nerve, and lower the activity of the GTPase RhoA, which is involved in growth cone collapse.

### Cancer: decoys, a marker in blood, and the Rab27a question

Tumour vesicles have been reported to carry the target of an antibody drug, such as HER2 or CD20, and to bind the antibody before it reaches the cell. The decoy experiments were done mostly in culture, where the experimenter chooses the dose. In one study of people with melanoma, the size of the early rise in PD-L1 on circulating vesicles during anti-PD-1 treatment separated responders from non-responders. A correlation in blood cannot show whether vesicle PD-L1 alters the response or reflects the amount of tumour; the authors themselves read the early rise as the tumour responding to reactivated T cells. In the prostate cancer study, Rab27a has other jobs in secretion, which is why the PD-L1 deletion and exosomal PD-L1 rescue carry the argument.

### Loading vesicles with RNA

A 2011 report from Matthew Wood's laboratory described engineered vesicles delivering a gene-silencing RNA to the mouse brain, although a 2013 study with Wood among its authors showed that the method used to load such RNA can form aggregates that make loading look more efficient than it is.
