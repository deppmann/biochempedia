techniques:
  - name: "Selective plating on minimal medium"
    blurb: >-
      Cells are spread on a medium lacking the nutrients that the parents need, sometimes with
      an antibiotic that kills the donor. Only cells that have gained every missing
      ability grow, so one recombinant can be found among many millions of cells.
  - name: "Filter (U-tube) test"
    blurb: >-
      Two strains are placed on opposite sides of a filter that lets medium, DNA and phage pass
      but stops cells. Recombinants on the far side rule out a need for contact; their absence
      points to conjugation.
  - name: "Interrupted mating"
    blurb: >-
      Hfr donors and F− recipients are mixed, and samples are agitated at set times to break the
      pairs apart before plating. The time at which each donor marker first appears among
      recombinants orders the genes and spaces them in minutes.
  - name: "Cotransduction with P1"
    blurb: >-
      Phage grown on a donor carries random host fragments into a recipient. Transductants are
      selected for one donor marker and scored for others; the fraction that also carries a
      second marker rises the closer the two genes lie.

    rationale: >-
      Conjugation needs a donor carrying a conjugative plasmid such as F to make contact with a recipient, which is why a

      a filter, though DNase stops it. Transduction uses phage particles, which are small enough
      to pass a filter. Lysogeny is a temperate phage's own genome settling in its host as a prophage, not a
      route that requires two bacteria to touch.

    rationale: >-
      Unless F has integrated into the chromosome, as in an Hfr cell, transfer starts at the F

  - stem: "Phage lambda transduces gal and bio genes of E. coli but almost never genes elsewhere on the chromosome. What explains this restriction?"

      - "Lambda packs host DNA at random but loses all non-gal pieces"
      - "The gal and bio genes are the only host genes on a plasmid"

    rationale: >-
      Lambda normally integrates at one preferred attachment site, between gal and bio. When the prophage is

  - stem: "Ordered arrival maps gene order; time gaps map distance. In an interrupted-mating experiment, markers a, b and c first appear at 6, 17 and 25 min. Assuming steady transfer, how far apart are b and c?"
    choices:
      - "6 minutes, the entry time of the first marker"
      - "8 minutes, the gap between their entry times"
      - "19 minutes, the gap between a and c"
      - "42 minutes, the sum of the two entry times"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Distance on the time map is the difference in times of entry: 25 minus 17 is 8 minutes.
      Six minutes is when the first marker enters, which mostly reflects where the origin lies.
      Nineteen minutes is the distance from a to c, a different pair. Adding entry times has
      no meaning on the map, because each time is measured from the same start of mating.
  - stem: "P1 grown on a wild-type donor is used to transduce a recipient mutant at genes m and n. Among 1,000 m+ transductants, none is n+. Which conclusion is best supported?"
    choices:
      - "m and n are adjacent genes that are always inherited together"
      - "n cannot be transduced by P1 under any conditions"
      - "m and n probably lie farther apart than one P1 fragment"
      - "m and n must lie on two different bacterial plasmids"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      A P1 particle holds roughly 100 kb, about 2 minutes of the E. coli map, so two genes are
      carried together only if they lie within that span; zero cotransduction suggests they are
      farther apart, though a finite sample gives only a bound. Adjacent genes would show high
      cotransduction, not none. P1 packages any part of the chromosome, so n can be transduced
      if selected directly. Nothing in the data points to plasmids; distant genes on one
      chromosome give the same result.

**In 1946, two *Escherichia coli* K-12 strains, each unable to make several nutrients, were mixed and then spread on a plate that supplied none of them. A few colonies grew. Neither strain alone gave any. Where did the working genes come from?**

An **auxotroph** is a mutant that needs a nutrient added because it cannot make it; the wild type is a **prototroph**. In Joshua Lederberg and Edward Tatum's cross at Yale, one strain needed threonine, leucine and thiamine. The other needed biotin, phenylalanine and cystine. A colony on bare minimal medium could make all six, so it carried working genes from both parents. They read this as recombination: one bacterium had inherited genes from another.

**Predict 1.** A revertant mutates back to a working allele and can imitate a recombinant. What two controls make reversion a weaker explanation here?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Each parent lacked several abilities, so one lineage would need several reversions. Each parent plated alone also gave no colonies.

</details>

> **The one idea.** How donor DNA travels determines the map: ordered arrival maps gene order and time gaps map distance; shared carriage maps proximity because closer genes travel together more often.
>
> *Scope:* ordered arrival means an Hfr time-of-entry experiment; turning time gaps into distances assumes approximately steady transfer. Shared carriage means generalized cotransduction. A recovered colony also needs the incoming piece expressed and usually integrated.

## Three routes, two tests

The first film sorted the three routes with two tests: a filter that stops cells, and **DNase** (deoxyribonuclease), an enzyme that destroys free DNA.

| Route | What carries the DNA | Crosses a cell-blocking filter | Stopped by DNase while DNA is extracellular |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transformation | Free DNA, taken up by a competent recipient | Yes | Yes |
| Conjugation | A donor in direct contact with the recipient | No | No |
| Transduction | A bacteriophage (a virus that infects bacteria) holding bacterial DNA in its coat | Yes | No; the phage coat protects the DNA |

**Competence** is a physiological state that allows uptake of free DNA. *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Bacillus subtilis* can become naturally competent; Griffith's pneumococci and Avery, MacLeod and McCarty's purified DNA belong to [the DNA-as-genetic-material lesson](/lessons/dna-the-genetic-material/).

## Conjugation: a chromosome enters in order

The first film followed the fertility factor, **F**, from an F+ donor into an F− recipient, then into the chromosome of an **Hfr** (high frequency of recombination) donor. F, the classic conjugation system of *E. coli* K-12, is a **plasmid**, a small DNA circle that replicates separately from the chromosome. It carries genes for a pilus and for DNA transfer; other conjugative plasmids carry their own transfer genes. In an F+ by F− cross, transfer starts from a fixed point on F, the origin of transfer.

Because the rest of F would enter last and mating pairs usually break first, the recipient in an Hfr cross rarely becomes Hfr or F+.

Élie Wollman and François Jacob broke mating pairs apart in a blender at intervals, then plated the recipients on medium where only recombinants grew while streptomycin killed the donors.

### Worked problem: interrupted mating

Hfr strains 1 and 2 are each mated with a streptomycin-resistant F− recipient. Assume both transfer at the same steady speed, and that first appearance marks entry. Strain 1 gives a 8 min, b 14 min, c 20 min and d 31 min. Its order is a-b-c-d, with gaps of 6, 6 and 11 minutes.

**Predict 2.** Strain 2 gives d 4 min, c 15 min, b 21 min and a 27 min. Work out its gaps. Where does its transfer start, relative to d?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Its order is d-c-b-a, with gaps of 11, 6 and 6 minutes. Strain 2 reads the same map backwards: its origin lies on the far side of d, about 4 minutes before d, and it transfers in the opposite orientation.

> **a --6-- b --6-- c --11-- d**

Hfr strains with different origins and orientations produced overlapping orders that closed into a circle.

</details>

The *E. coli* map runs from 0 to 100 minutes, a scale that came from pooling many time-of-entry measurements, not from a chromosome that always crosses in exactly the same time.

**Predict 3.** Two Hfr strains give the same marker order but transfer at consistently different speeds. Can their raw time gaps be overlaid as distances without recalibration?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Time becomes distance only under the steady-transfer assumption, and different rates stretch the two time maps by different amounts, even when the marker order agrees.

</details>

### Arrival is not yet inheritance

Bacteria make no zygote. *E. coli* is haploid, with one circular chromosome, so a recipient holding its own segment plus the matching donor segment is only briefly a **merozygote**, or partial diploid.

In the simple linear-fragment model, stable replacement in the circular chromosome needs an even number of crossovers, at least two, one on each side. After transduction, a piece that is not integrated may still be expressed briefly as an **abortive transductant**, but it is not copied as cells divide. Integration uses the recipient's recombination machinery: recipients with a *recB* mutation yield fewer recombinants in Hfr crosses.

## Transduction: host DNA inside a phage

Norton Zinder, working with Lederberg, found that genes passed between *Salmonella typhimurium* strains across a cell-blocking filter, and the agent resisted DNase. They traced it to a temperate phage later called P22. A **temperate phage** can kill its host or settle as a **prophage**, usually inserted into host DNA; that settled state is **lysogeny**.

Lambda is temperate too, but its transduction is **specialized** because it integrates at a specific attachment site, between the *gal* and *bio* genes of *E. coli* K-12, and can take adjacent host DNA when the prophage excises inaccurately. P22 and P1 are **generalized** transducers, which occasionally pack a random host-chromosome fragment in place of phage DNA.

### Cotransduction as a local map

A **transductant** is a recipient that acquired a donor marker by transduction, and the **cotransduction frequency** is the fraction of those selected for one donor marker that also received another. A P1 head holds about 100 kb, roughly 2 minutes of the 4.6-million-base-pair *E. coli* chromosome, so only genes within that span can ride together.

P1 grown on a wild-type donor infects an *x y z* triple mutant. Only *x+* is selected. Of 500 *x+* transductants, 350 are also *y+* and 100 are also *z+*.

**Predict 4.** Calculate both cotransduction frequencies. Which marker is closer to *x*, and which gene orders do the numbers allow?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

For *y* with *x*, 350/500 = 0.70, or 70%. For *z* with *x*, 100/500 = 0.20, or 20%. Both fit within one P1 fragment of *x*, and *y* lies closer to *x*. Two-factor data cannot say which side of *x* each marker occupies, so *x-y-z* and *y-x-z* both fit.

</details>

## Why bacteria and phage made genetics fast

Both maps follow from how the DNA travelled: ordered arrival places genes around the whole chromosome, and shared carriage places them within one phage load.

A haploid cell has no second allele to mask a recessive mutant genotype, though a phenotype may still lag for a few generations because a growing cell can carry several copies of its replicating chromosome. Cultures hold billions of cells, and *E. coli* can divide about every 20 minutes in rich medium. Most important, **selection** does the searching: minimal medium or an antibiotic lets only cells with every selected ability grow, revealing one recombinant among many millions. Many resistance genes ride on plasmids and can encode an enzyme that destroys the drug; plasmids became tools in [recombinant DNA](/lessons/recombinant-dna-and-pcr/).

Each **plaque**, a clear spot on a bacterial lawn, begins from one infectious unit under the assay conditions, so phage are counted as plaques, and plaque appearance is a phenotype. Two mutant phages infecting one cell can recombine, and their progeny map like a fly cross. Seymour Benzer used a selective host to find rare T4 recombinants among very large numbers of progeny, and mapped mutations within the *rII* region in [the complementation lesson](/lessons/complementation-and-pathways/).

### Design problem: three-factor transduction

Grow P1 on the wild-type donor, infect the *x y z* mutant, select *x+*, and score *y* and *z*:

| Class | Count |
|---|---|
| *x+ y+ z+* | 95 |
| *x+ y+ z* | 255 |
| *x+ y z+* | 5 |
| *x+ y z* | 145 |

**Predict 5.** Which order makes the rarest class, *x+ y z+*, require four crossovers rather than two?

<details>
<summary>One way to reason it through</summary>

The donor fragment carries *x+ y+ z+*. For order *x-y-z*, keeping donor *x+* and *z+* but recipient *y* requires exchanges outside *x*, between *x* and *y*, between *y* and *z*, and outside *z*: four.

For *y-x-z*, the same class needs one exchange between *y* and *x* and one beyond *z*. For *x-z-y*, it needs one beyond *x* and one between *z* and *y*. Those are two-exchange products, so they should not be the rarest. The order is *x-y-z*.

Check the margins: *y+* is 95 + 255 = 350 of 500, or 70%; *z+* is 95 + 5 = 100 of 500, or 20%. The middle gene, *y*, cotransduces more often than the outer gene *z*, as the order predicts. Plate the recipient alone and phage alone to control reversion and contamination.

</details>

## Going deeper

In 1950 Bernard Davis separated *E. coli* strains in a U-shaped tube by a filter that stopped cells while medium moved back and forth; no recombinants appeared, supporting contact-dependent conjugation. Luca Cavalli-Sforza and William Hayes are usually credited with independently isolating Hfr strains in the early 1950s. A 1976 recalibration set the full map at 100 minutes.

Zinder and Lederberg reported P22 transduction in 1952. M. L. Morse, Esther Lederberg and Joshua Lederberg reported lambda's restricted transduction of galactose genes in 1956.

Later markers are recovered less often as mating pairs break. Cotransduction frequency also depends on fragment length and on recombination around each marker, so its relation to distance is nonlinear. Base-pair coordinates are now the main frame, but P1 remains useful for moving one region into a clean background to test which mutation causes a phenotype.
