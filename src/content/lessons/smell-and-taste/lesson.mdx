      regulatory model in which each neuron expresses one or a small number of receptors. That

      swapped for another, the axons moved, which suggested that the receptor contributes to its
      own axon's targeting without being the only cue. Axel and Buck shared the 2004 Nobel Prize
      in Physiology or Medicine. The map gave the field a way to see receptor identity laid out in
      the brain.

    story: >-
      Sour is the taste of acid, and acid means protons. For years the sour cell had a marker, the
      channel subunit PKD2L1, but no confirmed detector. Liman's laboratory reasoned that the
      detector might be a proton channel expressed in sour cells and not in others. Tu and
      colleagues compared the transcriptomes of mouse taste cells, picked candidate genes, and
      expressed each in HEK-293 cells or frog oocytes while measuring acid-evoked currents. One,
      otopetrin 1, gave a proton-selective current: its reversal potential shifted by 53 mV per pH
      unit. The 2018 paper appeared in Science. In 2019 Teng and colleagues tested it in
      the animal. In Otop1-null mice, acid-evoked currents in taste cells were lost, and responses
      of the gustatory nerves to acids were severely and selectively reduced. A parallel study from
      the Zuker laboratory reached the same conclusion. The work gave sour taste a molecule, and it
      also showed that acid avoidance alone cannot be used to measure sour taste.

techniques:
  - name: "Calcium imaging with single-cell RT-PCR"
    blurb: >-
      Dissociated olfactory neurons loaded with a calcium indicator are exposed to a panel of
      odorants, and each responding cell is then picked up and its receptor transcript identified by
      RT-PCR. The result is a matrix linking receptor identity to the odorants that activate it.
  - name: "Gene targeting to label or swap receptors"
    blurb: >-
      A receptor gene is modified so that the neurons expressing it also make a visible marker, or
      its coding sequence is replaced with another receptor's. Following the labelled axons shows
      where receptor-defined neurons project and whether the receptor helps decide it.
  - name: "Knockout with cell-restricted rescue"
    blurb: >-
      A gene is deleted from the whole animal, then restored only in one cell type with that
      cell's promoter. If the function returns, that cell type is enough to carry it in that
      assay; if a related function does not return while the rescued one does, it
      probably needs other cells.
  - name: "Gustatory nerve recording"
    blurb: >-
      Electrodes on the chorda tympani or glossopharyngeal nerve record the summed activity of taste
      afferent fibres while solutions flow over the tongue. It measures what the periphery sends to
      the brain, separately from what the animal chooses to drink.

**Sweet, umami and bitter taste cells in mice all pass their signal through one enzyme, PLCβ2. In 2003 Zhang and colleagues deleted it, and the taste nerves stopped responding to the tested sweet, amino-acid and bitter stimuli all at once.**

One enzyme served three tastes, yet a normal mouse does not mistake a sweetener for a bitter compound. The same mouse has dozens of bitter-receptor genes, yet bitter is one taste. Its nose, with about a thousand odorant-receptor genes, reads an odour from a pattern across its receptors. Why do the two chemical senses code so differently?

## One coding problem, two answers

Smell and taste both turn chemicals into spikes. Both start with receptor cells that carry receptor proteins, and both have to tell the brain what arrived. That raises four puzzles.

1. **Identity.** A receptor cell's output is its activity. How can the brain learn which molecule arrived?
2. **Wiring.** Most mature mouse olfactory neurons express one odorant-receptor gene at high abundance, and neurons sharing that receptor usually converge on about two glomeruli per bulb. What does that arrangement achieve?
3. **Shared machinery.** If sweet, umami and bitter cells use the same enzyme, what keeps the three tastes apart?
4. **Pooling.** Why is bitter one taste when there are dozens of bitter receptors?

One rule makes sense of all four, and it needs two terms. A **line** is a set of cells whose activity reaches the brain as one channel. In smell, a line is all the neurons that express one receptor, together with the glomeruli (knots of nerve endings in the olfactory bulb) they converge on. In taste, a line is one kind of receptor cell: the sweet cells, the bitter cells, and so on. A **pattern** is the relative strength of activity across lines, not just which lines are on.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A receptor cell reports only how strongly it is active, not which molecule activated it. So a chemical sense can tell two stimuli apart only if they produce different patterns of activity across its separate lines. Smell keeps about a thousand receptor types on separate lines, so an odour is read as a pattern across many lines. Taste sorts its receptors into a few kinds of cell, with bitter cells pooling many, so a taste is read mainly from which kind of cell is active.

A line whose activity always means one quality, whatever turned it on, is called a **labeled line**. The taste section tests whether taste cells are labeled lines.

The first sentence of the rule is not new. In vision it is the principle of univariance: a cone's response cannot say whether it met a dim light at the wavelength it is most sensitive to or a brighter light far from it ([the retina](/lessons/retina/)). Reading a stimulus from many lines at once is the population code of [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/).

Keep the rule's scope in view. It is about the periphery (the receptor cells and the nerves leaving them) and mostly about mice. It is a first approximation that ignores spike timing. It says what information survives to the first relay in the brain; it does not count how many odours an animal can tell apart, and circuits further in can recombine lines.

## Smell keeps its lines apart

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

**Olfactory sensory neurons** sit in the olfactory epithelium high in the nasal cavity. Each sends cilia into the mucus, where odorants bind, and an axon through the bone above to the **olfactory bulb**. There the axons end in **glomeruli**, round knots of neuropil (tangles of axons and dendrites) where receptor-neuron axons meet the dendrites of the bulb's output neurons, the mitral and tufted cells.

The receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors, with seven transmembrane segments. They come from a family of roughly a thousand genes in the mouse and a few hundred intact genes in humans. Linda Buck and Richard Axel found the first candidate members in 1991; how the family grew and shrank belongs to [molecular evolution](/lessons/molecular-evolution/).

**One receptor gene per neuron.** The evidence is single-cell sequencing (Hanchate and colleagues, 2015), which found one abundant receptor transcript in most mature mouse neurons.

**Predict.** A neuron expresses only one receptor gene. Can it respond to more than one odorant?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes, if its receptor binds more than one. One receptor per neuron is a statement about genes, not about odorants, and the next section shows that a single receptor does bind many. What the gene rule fixes is that each neuron speaks for one receptor type, so all the neurons with that receptor form one line.

</details>

Where do the axons of one line go? Mombaerts and colleagues (1996) engineered mice so that neurons expressing one chosen receptor also made a marker that filled their axons. Picture the result. The labelled cell bodies are scattered across the epithelium. Their axons gather as they travel, and they end in two fixed glomeruli out of the roughly 1,800 in each bulb. To test whether the receptor itself helps choose the target, they replaced the coding sequence of the receptor P2 with that of another receptor, M12. The axons moved to new glomeruli, distinct from both P2's and M12's usual targets: the receptor helps guide its own axon but is probably not the only cue, since the swapped-in receptor did not simply carry the axons to its own site.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/smell-and-taste/glomerulus-convergence.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a side view shows five gold olfactory neurons scattered across the epithelium sending axons up through the bone that gather together and end in a single gold glomerulus in the olfactory bulb, where a mitral cell dendrite reaches in; other neurons' axons end in other glomeruli." caption="Neurons that chose the same receptor are scattered across the epithelium, but their axons gather onto a fixed glomerulus (two per bulb in the mouse; one drawn here), so each glomerulus collects mainly from one receptor type." />

Each glomerulus therefore collects mainly from neurons expressing one receptor type. The glomerular layer is largely a map of lines: receptor identity laid out as position. How that map is built is covered in [partners, layers and self-avoidance](/lessons/partners-layers-self-avoidance/); olfactory neurons are also replaced throughout life ([adult neurogenesis](/lessons/adult-neurogenesis/)).

In the canonical olfactory neuron, every receptor uses the same steps. The bound receptor activates the G protein Golf, which drives adenylyl cyclase 3 to make cAMP. cAMP opens cyclic-nucleotide-gated channels that let in sodium and calcium, and a calcium-activated chloride current amplifies the depolarization. The molecular steps are in [senses and cGMP](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/), [G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/) and [cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/). Because the cascade is shared, it does not attach a chemical name to the response. Which odorant arrived is carried by the pattern of response strengths across lines (in real neurons, timing can contribute too).

From the bulb, mitral and tufted cells project to the piriform cortex and other olfactory areas without first relaying in the thalamus, unlike vision, hearing and touch, although a route through the thalamus to frontal cortex also exists.

## Reading the pattern

If each receptor type defines one line, what does that line detect? Malnic, Hirono, Sato and Buck (1999) loaded dissociated mouse olfactory neurons with a calcium indicator and applied a panel of odorants. Then they identified the receptor in each responding cell by single-cell RT-PCR. Picture the result as a grid, with receptors down the side, odorants across the top, and a filled square wherever a cell responded.

The grid showed three things.

- One receptor recognized several odorants.
- One odorant was recognized by several receptors.
- Changing an odorant's concentration could change which receptors it activated.

Odour identity, on this view, is a **combinatorial code**: a pattern across receptors rather than one receptor per odour.

A grid of filled and empty squares is a simplification. Real responses are graded, so two odorants that activate the same set of receptors can still be told apart if the relative strengths differ. The exercise below uses on-or-off squares only to keep the counting simple.

**Worked problem: a made-up receptor matrix.** Suppose you imaged mouse olfactory neurons and found four receptor types, A to D. These data are invented for illustration. At a low concentration the odorants activate these receptors:

| Odorant | A | B | C | D |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | on | on | off | off |
| 2 | off | on | on | off |
| 3 | on | on | on | off |
| 4 | off | off | off | on |

Assume the brain can tell two odorants apart only if their patterns differ.

*Step 1: remove receptor B.* Odorant 1 becomes A; odorant 2 becomes C; odorant 3 becomes A+C; odorant 4 stays D. All four patterns are still different, so the model predicts no loss of discrimination: B was never the only difference between two odorants.

**Predict.** Restore B and remove receptor A instead. Which odorants can no longer be told apart?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Odorants 2 and 3. Odorant 1 becomes B; odorant 2 is B+C; odorant 3 is also B+C; odorant 4 is D. Odorants 2 and 3 now give the same pattern, so the model predicts they can no longer be told apart. Losing a line costs only the distinctions that depended on it.

</details>

**Predict.** Restore all four receptors. Now raise odorant 1 to tenfold concentration. In this invented example, C has a lower affinity for odorant 1: it needs more odorant to reach its response threshold, so it is recruited only at the higher concentration. Which odorant's pattern does odorant 1 now match, and what does the model predict?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Its pattern becomes A+B+C, the low-concentration pattern of odorant 3. The model predicts that odorant 1 at high concentration could be mistaken for odorant 3: a change in quality with concentration. This is the third finding of the Malnic grid in miniature: real odorants recruited additional receptors at higher concentrations.

</details>

*Last step: capacity.* More separate lines allow more possible patterns. If you treat each of N receptor types as a switch that is either on or off:

> **number of distinct non-empty patterns = 2^N − 1**

Four switches give 2^4 = 16 patterns. Removing the all-off pattern leaves 15 that could report an odorant.

The 2^N figure is the capacity of a toy model, not a count of smells: responses are graded and noisy, and the bulb and cortex transform the pattern. What a mouse can tell apart needs a behavioural test; a matrix alone cannot settle it. Try the model below.

## Taste pools its receptors into cells

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Taste starts in **taste buds**, clusters of specialized epithelial cells, not neurons, on the tongue and palate. Afferent fibres of cranial nerves VII, IX and X carry their signals to the nucleus of the solitary tract in the medulla, which relays through the thalamus (in rodents by way of the pontine parabrachial nucleus) to gustatory cortex in the insula. The textbook map with sweet at the tip and bitter at the back is discredited: taste buds across the tongue detect every quality, with modest regional differences in sensitivity.

The five qualities taught for mammals are sweet, sour, bitter, salty and umami. A taste bud holds glial-like supporting cells (type I) and these receptor cells:

| Cell | Quality | Detector |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type II | sweet | T1R2 + T1R3 |
| Type II | umami | T1R1 + T1R3 |
| Type II | bitter | many T2Rs |
| Type III | sour | the proton channel OTOP1 |
| ENaC cells (mice; their place among the cell types is still debated) | low salt | the sodium channel ENaC |

Taste has far fewer lines than smell, partly because it has fewer receptor genes but mainly because it pools them. Sweet cells carry one main receptor, the pair **T1R2 and T1R3**. Umami cells carry **T1R1 with T1R3**; in mice this pair responds to many L-amino acids, which is why mouse experiments often test "amino-acid" responses. Bitter cells draw on the **T2R** family, about 25 in humans and roughly 35 in mice, and in rodents a single bitter cell expresses many T2Rs at once (Adler and colleagues, 2000). All three kinds of receptor are G-protein-coupled.

Now look at what happens after the receptor. In all three kinds of type II cell the same steps follow (the names matter less than the fact that the chain is identical):

- the enzyme **PLCβ2** makes IP3;
- IP3 opens **IP3R3** channels, which release calcium from the endoplasmic reticulum ([PLC, IP3, DAG and PKC](/lessons/plc-ip3-dag-pkc/));
- calcium opens **TRPM5**, a channel gated by calcium that passes monovalent cations, sodium and potassium, so the cell depolarizes (in mice TRPM4 contributes alongside it; Dutta Banik and colleagues, 2018);
- the cell has no conventional vesicular synapse, so instead depolarization opens **CALHM1/3**, a voltage-gated channel, and ATP flows out through it onto the afferent nerve fibres.

If the signal leaving a sweet cell is chemically the same as the signal leaving a bitter cell, what keeps sweet and bitter apart?

<LessonFigure src="/figures/smell-and-taste/taste-bud-cells.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a taste bud shows supporting cells, sweet and bitter type II cells, a sour type III cell, a taste pore and afferent nerve fibres; an inset shows the same chain in every type II cell, from receptor to PLCβ2, calcium release, TRPM5 and ATP release through CALHM1/3." caption="Sweet, umami and bitter cells each run the identical chain from receptor to ATP release, so the cascade alone cannot tell the brain which quality was tasted." />

The rule answers it. A cell reports how strongly it is active, not what activated it. So the cascade does not need to differ: what differs is *which cell* the nerve fibre listens to. If that is right, the identity of the cell, not the receptor or the cascade, should set the quality.

Zhang and colleagues tested this in mice in 2003. First they deleted the gene for PLCβ2 from the whole animal. Responses of the taste nerves to the tested sweet, amino-acid and bitter stimuli were lost. In brief-access lick tests, which count a mouse's licks at a spout over a few seconds, preference for sweet and amino-acid stimuli was strongly reduced and avoidance of bitter was lost. Sour and salty responses remained, because those cells use other routes. Then they put PLCβ2 back, but only in cells that express T2R bitter receptors.

**Predict.** PLCβ2 is now present in bitter cells and nowhere else in the taste bud. Which responses come back: bitter only, all three, or none?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Bitter responses returned, in the nerve and in behaviour. Sweet and amino-acid responses did not. The enzyme is shared, but it works only for the quality of the cell it sits in. Sweet and bitter therefore run in separate cells, each a separate line to the nerve.

</details>

That experiment shows the lines are separate. It does not yet show that the cell, rather than the receptor, decides what the line *means*. For that, put a receptor into the wrong cell. Zhao and colleagues (2003) expressed an engineered receptor, activated only by a synthetic drug the mouse would otherwise ignore, in sweet cells alone. Mueller and colleagues (2005) went further and put a human bitter receptor, T2R16, into mouse sweet cells. Its ligand, phenyl-β-D-glucopyranoside, is a bitter compound that normal mice ignore at the concentrations tested.

**Predict.** Offered that ligand, do these mice avoid the solution, drink it, or ignore it as normal mice do?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

They drink it. The mice became attracted to the bitter ligand, and the engineered-receptor mice to the synthetic drug, while normal mice stayed indifferent. The receptor detected the molecule, but the sweet cell decided what the detection meant. Mueller and colleagues also put the engineered receptor in bitter cells, and those mice avoided the same drug. In this sense each kind of taste cell acts as a labeled line in the mouse periphery: its activity means one quality, whatever turned it on.

</details>

One consequence is easy to miss. Because a rodent bitter cell pools many T2Rs, its rate alone cannot tell the brain *which* bitter compound it met, only how strongly it was activated. Two different bitter compounds, each at a concentration that activates the cell equally, send the same signal from that cell. Not every bitter cell need carry the same set of T2Rs, so the bitter pathway as a whole may keep some distinctions. Still, pooling fits a sense that mostly answers eat it or reject it, not which molecule it is.

## Sour and salt: the channel routes

**Type III cells** detect sour. They form conventional synapses, releasing amine transmitters such as serotonin by calcium-dependent exocytosis; serotonin is probably not their only signal, since mice lacking the nerve's ATP receptors, P2X2 and P2X3, lose taste nerve responses to every quality (Finger and colleagues, 2005). Their acid sensor is **OTOP1**, a proton-selective channel identified by Tu and colleagues in 2018. In Otop1-null mice, gustatory nerve responses to acids were severely and selectively reduced (Teng and colleagues, 2019). PKD2L1, long used to mark sour cells, is not the primary sour receptor.

Two results show why the cell, the nerve and the behaviour have to be kept apart. Zhang and colleagues (2019) put OTOP1 into sweet cells, and those cells then responded to sour stimuli: a result about the cells, not about what the mice tasted. Otop1-null mice show the opposite split. Despite losing OTOP1-mediated proton currents and most of their nerve response to acid, they still avoided acid in two-bottle tests, so avoidance is not a pure measure of sour taste. Some acid sensitivity remained, so this is not evidence that all sour-cell activity was absent.

**Salty** is the least settled quality. In mice, deleting the ENaCα subunit of the epithelial sodium channel from taste cells abolished the amiloride-sensitive response (the part blocked by the drug amiloride) to low sodium concentrations and the mice's attraction to low salt (Chandrashekar and colleagues, 2010). High NaCl, which mice avoid, also recruited sour and bitter cells (Oka and colleagues, 2013). In this simplified account, high NaCl in mice with ENaC intact drives three cell classes, ENaC sodium cells plus sour and bitter cells as two additional aversive pathways: a multi-line pattern, which the rule allows. Non-sodium salts such as KCl recruit the aversive pathways without the sodium-selective ENaC pathway. How human salt taste is transduced is unsettled.

Fat is a candidate quality, not an established one, and kokumi, a sense of richness that enhances other tastes, is usually not counted as a quality of its own. Much of what people call flavour is smell: odorants from food in the mouth reach the nose from behind (retronasal smell).

## Back to the puzzles, and where the rule stops

Now return to the four puzzles. Identity is carried by which lines are active and how strongly. Smell keeps each receptor type on its own line, because most mature neurons express one receptor gene and like axons converge on about two glomeruli per bulb; that is what the wiring achieves. Taste can share one cascade across sweet, umami and bitter cells because the nerve reads which cell fired, not what happened inside it. And bitter can be one taste because bitter cells signal one quality whatever receptor fired, and each pools many bitter receptors.

Smell meets an open-ended world of molecules with a large, overlapping receptor array: quality is read from a pattern. Taste mostly answers a few questions (eat it or reject it, and what it contains) with cells dedicated to each quality: quality is read mainly from which cell fires, a labeled line at the periphery.

That is the leading view from mouse genetics, and it has limits. Some taste cells and some taste ganglion neurons respond to several qualities, so whether taste is coded by labeled lines or by patterns across fibres, and how strictly the lines stay separate further into the brain, is still argued. These experiments do not show one exclusive wire for each human percept, and one stimulus, such as high NaCl, can drive several cell classes.

### Design problem

Is "bitter" a property of the receptor or of the cell carrying it? Design an experiment in mice that tells the two apart.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Choose a receptor whose ligand the mouse normally ignores, such as an engineered receptor activated only by a synthetic drug, and express it only in sweet cells by driving it from the T1R2 gene's promoter. Give the drug in a brief-access lick test. If the cell sets quality, the mice should lick the drug eagerly, as for a sweetener; if the receptor did, they should not. Then express the same receptor only in T2R bitter cells: the cell hypothesis predicts avoidance of the same drug. The Zuker and Ryba laboratories reported experiments of this design (Zhao and colleagues, 2003; Mueller and colleagues, 2005).

Controls: confirm by in situ hybridization or antibody staining that the receptor is only in the targeted cells; record the chorda tympani, a taste nerve, to show the drug now drives a response; test wild-type littermates, which should ignore the drug; and run a concentration series. A complementary test is the PLCβ2 rescue in T2R cells described above.

Limits: this is the mouse periphery. Licking is a behaviour, not a reported percept, and brain circuits could in principle re-sort the signal.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How the receptor family was found

In 1991 Linda Buck and Richard Axel described 18 members of a gene family from rat olfactory tissue that encoded proteins with seven transmembrane segments, the shape of G-protein-coupled receptors. Their title kept a hedge: the family *may encode* odorant receptors. The paper did not show what any receptor binds or where its neurons project.

### One copy of the gene, and neurons that have not chosen yet

In the mouse neurons Chess and colleagues examined in 1994, a given receptor gene was transcribed from only one of its two alleles. Hanchate and colleagues also found that many immature neurons express several receptor genes at low levels, so singular expression is something a neuron arrives at as it matures.

### Receptor pharmacology

In heterologous cell assays, human T1R2/T1R3 responded to diverse sweeteners and human T1R1/T1R3 to L-glutamate, with 0.2 mM IMP shifting the glutamate EC50 (the concentration giving a half-maximal response) about 30-fold lower (Li and colleagues, 2002). Meyerhof and colleagues (2010) screened 25 human bitter receptors against 104 bitter compounds and found that receptors varied greatly in how many compounds activated them. The rodent finding that one bitter cell expresses many T2Rs should not be read as every human bitter cell expressing all of them: human cells express subsets (Behrens and colleagues, 2007).

### Worked problem: one TRPM5 channel

Prawitt and colleagues measured a unitary conductance of 25 pS for human TRPM5 expressed in HEK-293 cells. Take outward current as positive, so inward current is negative, as in the [action potential](/lessons/action-potential/) lesson. Assume a linear current-voltage relation, a membrane potential of −60 mV and a reversal potential of 0 mV, as for a non-selective cation channel. Then I = g × (Vm − Erev) = 25 pS × (−60 mV − 0 mV) = −1,500 fA = −1.5 pA. The minus sign means inward current of positive charge. This is a stipulated example, not a measured taste-cell current.
