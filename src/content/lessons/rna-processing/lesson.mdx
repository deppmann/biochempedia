  - name: "RNA sequencing with splice-junction reads"
    blurb: >-
      Sequence short fragments of cDNA made from mRNA and map them to the genome. Reads that span
      two exons record which exons were joined, so the share of messages that include a given exon
      can be estimated across tissues.

      - "By a 2' to 5' phosphodiester bond, like the lariat branch"
      - "By a peptide bond to a cap-binding protein on the RNA"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The cap guanosine is joined through its 5' carbon to the 5' carbon of the first nucleotide
      by three phosphates, a 5' to 5' triphosphate bridge, so the RNA has no free 5' end for an
      exonuclease. A 3' to 5' bond is the ordinary backbone linkage, and the cap is defined by
      not being one. The 2' to 5' bond belongs to the lariat branch in splicing, not to the cap.
      Cap-binding proteins hold the cap by non-covalent contacts; no peptide bond is involved.
  - stem: "In the first chemical step of nuclear pre-mRNA splicing, which group attacks the phosphate at the 5' splice site?"

      - "The 3'-OH of a free guanosine nucleotide"
      - "The 2'-OH of the adenosine at the branch point"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The 2'-OH of the branch-point adenosine attacks the 5' splice-site phosphate, which frees the
      upstream exon and forms the lariat. The 3'-OH of the upstream exon is the attacking group in
      the second step, after it has been freed. The guanine of GU is the first base of the intron
      and sits beside the phosphate that is attacked; it is not the attacker. A free guanosine starts self-splicing of group I
      introns such as the Tetrahymena intron, not spliceosomal splicing.

    choices:
      - "The tail is added by a polymerase that uses no template after the RNA is cut"
      - "The tail is copied from a run of thymines in the DNA just past the gene's end"
      - "The tail is added to every eukaryotic mRNA, including replication-dependent histone mRNAs"
      - "The tail is added at the AAUAAA sequence itself, which becomes the first adenines"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      After the pre-mRNA is cut, poly(A) polymerase adds adenosines without copying any DNA, so the

  - stem: "Skipping an exon of 87 nucleotides from the coding region of an mRNA would most likely have what effect on the protein?"
    choices:
      - "A frameshift after the junction and a premature stop codon"
      - "No change, because exons do not carry coding sequence"
      - "Loss of 29 amino acids with the downstream frame kept"
      - "Loss of the cap and fast decay of the shortened mRNA"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Eighty-seven is 29 times 3, so removing the exon removes 29 codons and the downstream
      reading frame is unchanged; the protein lacks 29 amino acids (assuming the junction does not
      happen to make a stop codon). A frameshift needs a length that is not a multiple of three. The
      claim that exons carry no coding sequence confuses exons with introns. The cap is added at
      the 5' end early in transcription, and skipping an internal exon does not remove it.
  - stem: "A variant changes the G of the GU at the 5' end of an intron to A. Which outcome would you predict first, and why might the cell show more than one product?"
    choices:
      - "Every transcript keeps the whole intron, because the spliceosome cannot remove it at all"
      - "Exon skipping or use of a nearby cryptic 5' site, with the balance set by the sequences around it"
      - "The intron is removed normally, because U1 reads only the branch point and not the 5' site"
      - "The mRNA loses its poly(A) tail, because the 5' splice site also serves as the cleavage signal"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A weakened splice site most often causes exon skipping; less often a nearby cryptic 5' site
      takes over, and one allele can give both, as the cloned beta-thalassemia allele did in HeLa

## One transcript, three processing decisions

RNA polymerase II first makes a **primary transcript**, or pre-mRNA ([transcription](/lessons/transcription/)). Its **exons** are kept; the **introns** between them are removed in splicing.

> **The one idea.** Processing marks transcript ends and chooses which segments join into messenger RNA.
>
> *Scope:* eukaryotic protein-coding RNA polymerase II transcripts. Other eukaryotic RNAs and bacterial RNAs follow different routes.

The three decisions (a 5' **cap**, 3' cleavage with a **poly(A) tail**, and **splicing**) need not wait: capping begins when the RNA is only about 25 to 30 nucleotides long, and early introns often splice while later regions are still being copied.

## Marking the two ends

The cap, 7-methylguanosine (m7G), is joined to the first transcribed nucleotide 5' to 5' through three phosphates, where a normal backbone links 3' to 5'. That reversed link blocks 5'-to-3' exonucleases. The film's helpers are a nuclear cap-binding complex for export and the cytoplasmic translation factor eIF4E, which grips the cap to help recruit the translation machinery.

The film's 3' signal is AAUAAA, read in mammals by CPSF. Cross-linking with purified human CPSF by Chan and colleagues in 2014 showed its subunits CPSF30 and Wdr33 contacting it directly. CstF binds a downstream GU-rich stretch, and the nuclease CPSF73 cuts between the two, about 10 to 30 nucleotides beyond AAUAAA. **Poly(A) polymerase** then adds the tail.

## The evidence for split genes

Until 1977 a gene was expected to be a continuous stretch of DNA, as in *E. coli*. Two groups mapping the messages of adenovirus 2, a DNA virus that grows in human cells, found otherwise. At MIT, Susan Berget, Claire Moore and Phillip Sharp purified hexon mRNA, the message for the virus's main coat protein, and paired it with mapped viral DNA. At Cold Spring Harbor, Louise Chow, Richard Gelinas, Thomas Broker and Richard Roberts paired viral DNA with RNA from infected-cell polysomes. Both viewed the hybrids by electron microscopy and mapped the messages' 5' ends with single-stranded DNA fragments. Chow and colleagues also made **R loops**, where RNA displaces one DNA strand and pairs with the other.

Split genes were soon found in animal genes, including globin genes, and Roberts and Sharp received the 1993 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries of split genes. Introns are not simply junk: some contain sequences that control splicing or transcription.

## How the spliceosome joins exons

Most major-class (U2-type) human introns follow the film's **GU-AG rule**. The **branch point** usually lies a few dozen nucleotides before the 3' end, with a pyrimidine-rich run between it and the AG.

Chemically, the film's two attacks are **transesterifications**, reactions that exchange one phosphodiester bond for another.

1. The branch adenosine's 2'-OH attacks the phosphate at the 5' splice site. The intron's 5' G is now joined to branch A through a 2'-to-5' bond, which closes the **lariat**.
2. The freed upstream exon's 3'-OH attacks the phosphate at the 3' splice site, joining the exons.

**Predict.** Does this bond-exchange chemistry itself need ATP?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. No bond is lost overall, so the exchanges need no ATP; the machinery spends ATP to assemble and to rearrange between catalytic states.

</details>

The film's guides are small nuclear ribonucleoproteins, or **snRNPs** (U1, U2, U4, U5 and U6), each a small RNA with proteins; with many other proteins they build the **spliceosome** anew on each intron. Part of the guiding is base pairing: U1 snRNA pairs with the 5' splice site, and U2 snRNA with the branch-point sequence, leaving branch A bulged out. After the U4/U6.U5 tri-snRNP joins, U6 folds with U2 into the active site; cryo-EM structures from yeast and humans show U6 positioning catalytic metal ions. The active site is largely RNA, supported and rearranged by proteins.

This fits a 1982 result from Thomas Cech's laboratory: a *Tetrahymena* ribosomal RNA **group I intron** removed itself without protein, using external guanosine rather than branch A for the first attack. Self-splicing group II introns in organelles and bacteria form a lariat as the spliceosome does.

Because splice sites are short, loosely defined signals read by snRNAs and proteins together, a transcript can offer more than one possible site, and the joins can change.

## One gene, several messages

Wang and colleagues (2008) classed the patterns they found in human transcriptomes into skipped exons, alternative 5' or 3' splice sites, mutually exclusive exons and retained introns, the four patterns in the second film, and estimated that 92 to 94 percent of human genes undergo alternative splicing. Their regulation belongs to [eukaryotic gene regulation](/lessons/eukaryotic-regulation/).

## What a splice-site mutation can do

A weakened splice site most often causes exon skipping; less often a **cryptic splice site**, a sequence not normally used, takes over, or the intron is retained. Competing signals nearby set the outcome, so it cannot be promised for one variant ([mutation](/lessons/mutation/)).

The film's beta-thalassemia allele changed the GT at the start of intron 2. In HeLa cells its main RNA retained the intron's first 47 nucleotides, and a smaller amount joined exon 1 directly to exon 3. Because 47 = 3 x 15 + 2, that insertion shifts the frame.

That is the second film's rule for net changes within coding sequence: **coding-length changes keep the downstream frame only in multiples of three.**

> **frame kept if (change in coding length) / 3 leaves remainder 0**

Why does losing one site so often drop a whole exon? Vertebrate exons are often recognized as units by the sites at both ends.

**Worked problem.** An imagined human gene has four exons. Exon 1 has a 60-nucleotide 5' UTR followed by 90 coding nucleotides beginning with AUG, for 150 total. Exons 2, 3 and 4 have 87, 100 and 400 nucleotides; exon 4 contains the stop codon and 3' UTR. Introns 1, 2 and 3 have 900, 1,500 and 2,000. Ignore cap and tail.

1. **Mature mRNA:** 150 + 87 + 100 + 400 = 737 nucleotides. Adding 4,400 intron nucleotides gives a 5,137-nucleotide pre-mRNA.
2. **A 5' splice-site mutation of intron 2 causes exon 2 to be skipped:** 737 - 87 = 650. Because 87 = 3 x 29, the frame stays aligned and the protein loses 29 amino acids.
3. **Exon 3 skipped:** 737 - 100 = 637. Because 100 = 3 x 33 + 1, the frame shifts by one base after the new exon 2-to-exon 4 junction. A premature stop in exon 4 is likely, though sequence is required. In mammalian cells, a premature stop in the last exon usually escapes exon-junction-dependent nonsense-mediated decay, so a truncated protein could be made ([genetic code and translation](/lessons/genetic-code-and-translation/)).
4. **Intron 1 retained:** 737 + 900 = 1,637. Such RNAs are often held in the nucleus or degraded.

### Design problem

A patient variant changes intron 3 position +5 from G to A; the G of GU is +1. The gene is expressed in muscle. Design a minigene test, predict band sizes and state the limits.

**Predict.** If one band lies close to the normal-splicing size, is its size alone enough to call the junction normal?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. A nearby cryptic site can make a nearly normal-sized band, so sequence every band.

</details>

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Clone exon 3, intron 3 and exon 4, with enough flanking intron to keep the outer splice sites (assuming exon 4 is not last), between two vector exons of a splicing reporter ([hacking the cell](/lessons/hacking-the-cell/)). Transfect wild-type and +5 versions into a cell line, ideally muscle-derived, and run RT-PCR with primers in the vector exons.

With invented lengths of 100 vector-exon nucleotides between the primers, 120 for exon 3, 150 for exon 4 and 800 for intron 3, normal splicing gives 370 bp. Exon 3 skipping gives 250 bp; intron 3 retention gives 1,170 bp.

Controls are empty vector at 100 bp, the wild-type minigene, a change known to disrupt the same 5' splice site, such as +1 G to A, and a no-reverse-transcriptase sample to detect plasmid DNA. The minigene lacks the rest of the gene and the cell line may lack muscle splicing factors, so the result is evidence, not proof; confirm it in patient RNA if available. The assay tests splicing, not the variant's clinical meaning.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Variations at the ends

Three activities build the cap in order; in mammals, one protein carries the first two. Triphosphatase removes the outermost, gamma phosphate. Guanylyltransferase transfers GMP from GTP through a lysine-bound intermediate. Methyltransferase moves a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine to guanine N7, making cap 0; in many organisms, 2'-OH methylation of the first nucleotide makes cap 1 ([nucleotide metabolism](/lessons/nucleotide-metabolism/)).

**Predict.** If the triphosphatase fails, can guanylyltransferase still attach GMP normally?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Guanylyltransferase needs the end that triphosphatase prepares by removing the outermost phosphate.

</details>

New mammalian tails are commonly described as about 200 to 250 adenosines; yeast tails are shorter, and tails shrink with time, so any tail length needs an organism and whether new RNA or the whole population was measured. In L4-stage *C. elegans* larvae, sequencing individual tails found a median of 57. Animal replication-dependent histone mRNAs are a textbook exception: they are cut but normally form a 3' stem-loop instead of receiving a poly(A) tail.

Bacterial mRNAs lack an m7G cap, though some carry other caps such as NAD+, and lack spliceosomal introns, though some genes contain self-splicing introns. Bacterial ribosomes can translate an RNA while it is still being made; in eukaryotes the nuclear envelope separates the two, giving time to process the message first.

### The snRNP exchange and the minor spliceosome

After the tri-snRNP joins, U1 leaves and U6 takes over the 5' splice site. The helicase Brr2 unwinds U4 from U6, and U4 leaves before U6 and U2 form the active site.

A second, minor spliceosome removes well under 1 percent of introns in human genes. It uses U11, U12, U4atac and U6atac, plus the shared U5.
