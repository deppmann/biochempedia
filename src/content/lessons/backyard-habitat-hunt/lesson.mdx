## The big idea

Plan about 30 minutes outside. Your child will count animals to test a guess and sort living and nonliving things. Together, look for clues about what one animal needs.

> **Say it together:** "Every animal needs food, water, air, a safe place to hide, and room to move. The place that gives it all of those is its **habitat**. A habitat has living parts, like grass, and nonliving parts, like soil and rocks."

From standing height, a lawn looks like a green carpet. On hands and knees, it turns busy. You might see ants carrying crumbs, spiders on tiny webs or beetles running for cover.

Each animal depends on both living parts (plants, animals, fungi) and nonliving parts (soil, rocks, water, air, sunlight). Shelter can be living, like a tree, or nonliving, like a rock or a crack in the soil. If one need is missing, the animal may move away or may not survive.

The string square shrinks a whole yard to a size a child can study. Older children can count one kind of animal as a **population**—a group of one kind of living thing in one place. All the kinds living there together make a **community**.

<SafetyNote>
- **Stay close and pick a safe spot.** Stay within arm’s reach throughout. Choose dry ground away from roads, ponds, creeks and pools. Stay out of even shallow water. Never leave a child alone near water. Ask permission before studying a park or schoolyard. Skip lawns with warning flags or recent weed or bug spray.
- **Choking hazard (under 4).** Keep small items, including pebbles, seeds, berries, pegs and broken bits of sticks, away from children under 4. Keep long string untangled. **Call 911 if choking.**
- **Adult handles sharp items, glass and heat.** Before anyone kneels, the adult checks for glass, sharp litter and pet waste. Pick another spot if you find any. The adult handles tent pegs, sharp sticks and glass magnifying lenses. Never point a magnifying glass at the sun; it can hurt eyes and start a fire.
- **Weather, sun and ticks.** Skip storms, lightning or extreme heat or cold. Use hats, sunscreen and shade, bring drinking water on warm days, wear closed shoes and long pants, wash hands with soap and water afterward, and check for ticks.
- **Plants, animals and logs.** Never taste or eat any plant, berry or mushroom during the hunt. Do not touch unknown plants. Look at animals where they are—touch nothing, and never scoop or trap them. Only the adult rolls a light log or flat rock, wearing gloves (latex-free if needed) while children stand back. Never lift it; roll it toward you so it stays between you and anything underneath, then roll it back gently, watching fingers and feet. Never put hands or feet where you cannot see.

- **Snakes:** Never reach under logs, in rock piles or in brush. Virginia has three kinds of venomous snakes; the copperhead lives statewide and hides well in dead leaves. Step back and let any snake go. If a snake bites anyone, **call 911** right away. Keep the person still and calm, and remove rings or tight clothing near the bite; do not cut, suck, ice or tie off the bite.
- **Stinging insects and ant mounds:** Skip holes with yellowjackets flying in and out, and avoid ant mounds. Fire ants live in parts of Virginia (mostly the southeast, Southside, central areas and Lee County in the far southwest). Check the square for loose-soil mounds before kneeling, and pick another spot if you see one. If a child disturbs a mound, move them away and brush the ants off quickly, since fire ants sting many times.
- **Poison ivy:** Grows across Virginia on the ground and as a hairy vine; teach "leaves of three, let it be."
- **Bites, stings and allergies:** Ants, bees, wasps and some fuzzy caterpillars bite or sting; millipedes leave irritating fluid on skin; black widow spiders live in Virginia. Touch no salamanders, centipedes, millipedes, spiders or caterpillars. Ask beforehand if anyone has a bee, wasp or ant sting allergy; bring their prescribed epinephrine auto-injector and follow their plan. After a bite or sting, if anyone has trouble breathing, swelling of the lips, tongue or face, hives spreading over the body, vomiting, dizziness or fainting, use their epinephrine auto-injector if they have one and **call 911**. For other bites, stings or irritating fluid on skin, wash the skin with soap and water. If irritating fluid gets in an eye, rinse it with gently running water for at least 20 minutes. Then call Poison Help at **1-800-222-1222** or a doctor.

Afterward, check skin, hair, clothes and pets (especially behind knees, waist and hairline) and shower or bathe within two hours. Remove any attached tick with fine-tipped tweezers, and call a doctor if a rash or fever follows.

## What to do

Work one job at a time: count first, sort second, then trace one animal's needs.

1. **Pick a spot.** Choose a lawn patch, garden edge or schoolyard corner, and check it for hazards first. To study under a small log instead, skip the square. Guess how many animals are under it. Follow Step 5 and count the animals you see before the adult rolls the log back. Then compare your count with your guess.
2. **Mark the square.** Push the 4 sticks in about 1 metre apart and wrap the string around them to frame 1 square metre.
3. **Predict before you look.** Before parting the grass, ask: *"How many animals do you think live in our square? More or fewer than your fingers?"* Write the guess down.
4. **Look closely for 10 minutes.** Kneel and part the grass gently with a pencil. Check the soil, under fallen leaves and at plant bases, using a magnifying glass if you have one. Expect ants, spiders, small beetles, pill bugs or earthworms, depending on the season and weather. Ask, “What can you see moving near the soil?” Count each animal inside the square, list birds or flying insects separately, and compare with the guess.
5. **Roll a log (optional).** First ask: *"What do you think is hiding under this log? Why would it pick that spot?"* With children standing back, the adult wears gloves and rolls a light log or flat rock toward themselves. Look for pill bugs, worms, beetles, millipedes or, in damp woods, a small salamander. Watch for a minute or two, noticing the cool, damp soil. Ask: *"What do you think will happen to the pill bugs if we leave the log rolled over?"* Roll it back slowly so animals on the ground can crawl back beside it.
6. **Sort living and nonliving.** Model one of each first: *"This grass is living, and this rock is nonliving. What else can you sort?"* List living things (grass, clover, ants, a mushroom) and nonliving things (soil, a rock, a puddle, sunlight, air).
7. **Trace one animal's habitat.** Pick one animal your child spotted. Ask, *"Where could it find food and water? Where could it hide? How much space might it use?"* An ant might eat crumbs and seeds, drink dew, shelter in a soil nest, and use the square and nearby ground as space.
8. **Leave it as you found it.** Pull up the sticks and string, and put back any leaves you moved.

With a class, give each group of three its own square and one job (counting, sorting or the four needs), then share results.

### Two-question wrap-up

Bring your child back to the "Say it together" idea:
- *"What living and nonliving things did we find that our animal might use?"*
- *"If we took away its water or its hiding place, what would happen?"*

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "How many animals do you think we'll find? More or fewer than your fingers?"
- "I wonder what this ant eats. Where could it find water?"
- "What do you think is living under this log? Let's guess before we look."
- "Is the soil alive? Is anything in it alive?"
- "What do you think will happen to the pill bugs if we leave the log rolled over?"
- "If people built a patio on this square, which animals would have to move?"

## Why it works

A habitat is the place where an animal lives and finds what it needs. What counts depends on the animal, not on a label like "forest" or "yard." A very small animal can meet all of its needs in a small space. A soil mite may spend its life in a small patch of soil and leaf litter, while a robin uses many backyards. That is why 1 square metre can hold parts of many animals' habitats at once, though most animals you spot also use ground outside the square.

Under a log, the soil stays cooler and damper than in the open. Animals that dry out easily, such as pill bugs and salamanders, gather there. Rolling the log back matters because their shelter is part of their habitat; a log left upside down may dry out.

Habitats also change. Mowing, a dry spell, a new patio or a change of seasons can remove food, water or shelter. Unmowed or planted patches often hold more kinds of animals than short lawn, though results vary with the place and weather.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Skip the log and the counting. Hunt for three living things and three nonliving things inside the square, and draw them. Pick one animal and draw it in its home, with something it eats, something it drinks and a place it hides. Five minutes of looking is plenty.

### Ages 8 to 10
Compare two squares: one in mowed lawn and one in a garden bed or unmowed edge. Count animals for the same 10 minutes in each and make a bar graph. Count one kind, like one kind of ant, as a **population**. List all kinds together as the **community**. Then build a shoebox model of one animal's habitat showing its food, water, shelter and space.

## If it doesn't go as planned

On a cold, hot or very dry day, small animals may stay hidden. Try early morning, after rain or in a shady spot with leaf litter. If your child loses interest, switch to a "find five" challenge: five animals, five plants, five nonliving things. If you find nothing under a log, put it back and try a damper place another day.
