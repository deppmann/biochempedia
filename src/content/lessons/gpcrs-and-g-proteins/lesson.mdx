      membrane, so the chain was drawn passing through the bilayer seven times with short loops on
      each face. When genes for hormone receptors were cloned a few years later, that drawing was

techniques:
  - name: "Cyanogen bromide cleavage and Edman sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      Cyanogen bromide cuts a protein chain at methionine, and Edman chemistry then removes and
      identifies one amino acid at a time from the end of each fragment. Overlapping fragments are
      pieced into the full sequence. This is how rhodopsin was read before its gene was cloned.
  - name: "Hydropathy analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Score each amino acid in a sequence for how well it tolerates water, then average the scores
      along the chain. A run of about twenty water-avoiding residues is long enough to cross a
      membrane as a helix. Seven such runs in rhodopsin and in the β-adrenergic receptor gave the
      seven-pass model years before a crystal structure of either existed.
  - name: "Expression of a cloned receptor in cells that lack it"
    blurb: >-
      Put the gene for a candidate receptor into a cultured cell line that does not make it, add
      test ligands, and measure a second messenger such as cAMP. A response that appears only in
      cells carrying the gene, rises with dose, and is lost after pertussis toxin is strong evidence for
      both the ligand and the G protein family.
  - name: "Crystallography of stabilised receptors"
    blurb: >-
      GPCRs are scarce and unstable in detergent, so crystals were obtained by holding the receptor
      in one shape: an inverse agonist in the pocket with T4 lysozyme fused in for the inactive
      state, an agonist with the G protein and a llama antibody fragment for the active one.
      Comparing the two structures shows how the active receptor differs from the inactive one.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "How is the polypeptide chain of a G-protein-coupled receptor arranged in the plasma membrane?"
    choices:
      - "It crosses seven times, with the amino end outside the cell"
      - "It crosses seven times, with the amino end inside the cell"
      - "It crosses once, with the amino end outside the cell surface"
      - "It crosses twelve times, with both ends inside the cytosol"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      A GPCR is one chain with seven membrane-spanning helices, its amino end outside the cell and
      its carboxyl end in the cytosol. Seven crossings with the amino end inside reverses the
      orientation, which would put the ligand pocket and the G-protein-binding loops on the wrong
      sides. A single crossing with the amino end outside describes single-pass receptors such as
      receptor tyrosine kinases. Twelve crossings with both ends in the cytosol is the layout of
      many transporters, and no receptor of this family is built that way.
  - stem: "Which change on the cytoplasmic face of a GPCR follows agonist binding and lets a G protein dock?"
    choices:
      - "The agonist passes through the bundle and binds Gα in the cytosol"
      - "A pore opens through the helix bundle and lets calcium ions enter"
      - "Two receptor chains pair up and phosphorylate each other's tails"
      - "Helix 6 swings outward and opens a cavity for the tail of Gα"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      In the β2 receptor structures the inner end of helix 6 moves outward by about 14 Å and opens
      a cavity that the tail of Gα enters. Pairing of two chains followed by phosphorylation of
      each other's tails is how receptor tyrosine kinases start, and a GPCR has no kinase domain. A
      pore that admits calcium describes a ligand-gated ion channel; the helix bundle of a GPCR
      does not conduct ions. The agonist does not pass through the bundle: it stays in the outer
      pocket, and only the change in shape reaches the cytosol.
  - stem: "Which Gα family has phospholipase C-β as its main direct target?"
    choices:
      - "Gq/11"
      - "G12/13"
      - "Gs"
      - "Gi/o"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Gαq and Gα11 bind and activate phospholipase C-β, which cuts PIP2 into IP3 and DAG. Gs is
      wrong because its target is adenylyl cyclase, which it stimulates. Gi/o is wrong because its
      α subunit inhibits adenylyl cyclase. G12/13 is wrong because it acts on exchange factors for
      the small GTPase Rho.
  - stem: "Acetylcholine slows the heartbeat but makes airway smooth muscle contract. What differs between the two tissues?"
    choices:
      - "The concentration of acetylcholine that each nerve releases"
      - "The rate at which acetylcholine is destroyed after its release"
      - "The chemical form of acetylcholine that reaches each tissue"
      - "The receptor subtype and the G protein that it couples to"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Pacemaker cells carry M2 receptors coupled to Gi, whose βγ opens potassium channels and slows
      firing; airway muscle carries M3 receptors coupled to Gq, which raises calcium and causes
      contraction. The chemical form is wrong because the same acetylcholine molecule acts at both
      sites. The concentration released is wrong because at each site the amount of acetylcholine
      changes the size of the response, not its direction. The rate of destruction is wrong for
      the same reason: it sets how long the signal lasts and has no say in which pathway the cell
      uses.
  - stem: "An agonist lowers cAMP in a cultured cell line. After the cells are treated with pertussis toxin, basal cAMP is unchanged and the agonist still binds its receptor, but cAMP no longer falls. What has the toxin done?"
    choices:
      - "Blocked adenylyl cyclase from making any cAMP"
      - "Blocked the receptor from activating Gi"
      - "Blocked Gs from hydrolysing its bound GTP"
      - "Blocked the agonist from reaching its pocket"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Pertussis toxin attaches ADP-ribose to a cysteine near the tail of Gαi, so the receptor can
      no longer activate Gi and the inhibition of adenylyl cyclase is lost. Blocking GTP hydrolysis
      by Gs is what cholera toxin does; that raises cAMP by itself and does not explain the loss of
      an inhibitory response. Blocking the agonist from its pocket is ruled out by the stem, which
      says binding is unchanged. Blocking adenylyl cyclase is ruled out because basal cAMP is
      unchanged, which shows the enzyme still works.

  - stem: "Cells that lack a certain Gs-coupled receptor make cAMP at 10 pmol per minute. Cells engineered to express the receptor at a high level make 30 pmol per minute with no ligand added. Drug R lowers the rate in the receptor-expressing cells to 12 pmol per minute and has no effect on cells that lack the receptor. How is R best classified, and how much of the receptor's own basal signaling does it remove?"
    choices:
      - "A neutral antagonist that removes 60% of it"
      - "A neutral antagonist that removes 90% of it"
      - "An inverse agonist that removes 90% of it"
      - "An inverse agonist that removes 60% of it"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      With no ligand the receptor adds 30 − 10 = 20 pmol per minute. R cuts the rate to 12, which
      leaves 2 of those 20, so it removes 18 of 20, or 90%. A drug that lowers signaling below the
      basal level is an inverse agonist. The 60% answers come from dividing the drop of 18 by the
      total rate of 30, which wrongly counts the 10 pmol per minute the cell makes without the
      receptor. The neutral antagonist answers are wrong because a neutral antagonist given alone
      leaves basal signaling unchanged; it would only block other ligands.
  - stem: "A gene for an orphan seven-pass receptor is expressed in cultured cells that lack it. Forskolin, which activates adenylyl cyclase directly, raises cAMP to 100 units in these cells. Forskolin plus compound Z gives 40 units in cells with the receptor and 100 units in cells without it. After overnight treatment with pertussis toxin, forskolin plus Z gives 100 units in cells with the receptor. Which conclusion do the data support?"
    choices:
      - "The receptor couples to Gs, and Z is its antagonist"
      - "The receptor couples to Gi/o, and Z is its agonist"
      - "The receptor couples to Gi/o, and Z is its antagonist"
      - "The receptor couples to Gq/11, and Z is its agonist"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Z lowers cAMP by 60% only in cells that carry the receptor, so Z activates the receptor and
      the receptor restrains adenylyl cyclase. Pertussis toxin, which stops receptors from
      activating Gi/o, removes the effect, so the coupling is to Gi/o with Z as agonist. An
      antagonist at a Gs-coupled receptor would have nothing to block here, since no Gs agonist was
      added, and pertussis toxin does not act on Gs. Coupling to Gq/11 predicts a rise in calcium
      rather than a pertussis-sensitive fall in cAMP. An antagonist at a Gi/o-coupled receptor
      would not lower cAMP by itself; it would only stop an agonist from doing so.
  - stem: "A patch of membrane is pulled from a heart pacemaker cell so that its cytoplasmic face is exposed to the bath. Adding purified Gαi loaded with a GTP analogue that cannot be hydrolysed leaves a potassium channel in the patch closed. Adding purified βγ opens the channel. In intact cells treated with pertussis toxin, acetylcholine no longer opens this channel. Which model fits all three results?"
    choices:
      - "The receptor activates Gi, and freed Gαi then opens the channel"
      - "The receptor activates Gs, and a rise in cAMP opens the channel"
      - "The receptor activates Gi, and freed βγ then opens the channel"
      - "The receptor binds the channel directly and opens it by contact"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Pertussis toxin blocks activation of Gi/o, so losing the acetylcholine response places Gi
      between receptor and channel, and purified βγ opening the channel in an isolated patch shows
      which half of the G protein carries the message. Freed Gαi opening the channel is ruled out
      because active Gαi left it closed. Gs and cAMP are ruled out because pertussis toxin does not
      act on Gs and the patch needed no cAMP. Opening by direct contact is ruled
      out because the toxin, whose enzyme modifies the G protein and not the receptor or channel, abolished the response.
  - stem: "A researcher treats intestinal epithelial cells with cholera toxin, which ADP-ribosylates the Gαs subunit and prevents its normal GTPase activity. Following toxin exposure, intracellular cAMP remains elevated even after the activating hormone is removed. Which mechanism best explains this result?"
    choices:
      - "Cholera toxin prevents GDP from being exchanged for GTP on Gαs, trapping the G protein in its inactive state"
      - "Cholera toxin permanently activates the receptor's extracellular ligand-binding domain"
      - "Cholera toxin prevents Gαs from hydrolyzing GTP to GDP, allowing continued stimulation of adenylyl cyclase"
      - "Cholera toxin activates phosphodiesterase, increasing the conversion of cAMP into AMP"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Normally, agonist-bound GPCRs promote GDP-to-GTP exchange on
      Gαs, allowing Gαs-GTP to stimulate adenylyl cyclase and increase
      cAMP production. Gαs then hydrolyzes GTP to GDP, terminating
      its active signaling state. Cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates Gαs
      and inhibits this GTPase activity, trapping activated Gαs
      in its GTP-bound state and causing persistent adenylyl cyclase
      stimulation. It does not activate the receptor directly or
      prevent GTP binding. Phosphodiesterase degrades cAMP, so
      activating it would lower cAMP rather than increase it.
  - stem: "A newly discovered hormone binds a GPCR on smooth muscle cells and rapidly produces two responses: increased cytosolic Ca²⁺ and translocation of protein kinase C (PKC) to the plasma membrane. A selective inhibitor of phospholipase C-beta (PLC-β) abolishes both responses, but direct activation of PKC with a membrane-permeable DAG analogue remains possible. Which pathway best explains the hormone's normal signaling mechanism?"
    choices:
      - "Gαs activates adenylyl cyclase, producing cAMP that directly releases calcium from the endoplasmic reticulum"
      - "Gαi inhibits adenylyl cyclase, decreasing cAMP and directly generating DAG"
      - "Gαq directly phosphorylates PKC, while Gβγ independently releases calcium"
      - "Gαq activates PLC-β, which cleaves PIP₂ into IP₃ and DAG; IP₃ releases intracellular calcium while DAG helps activate PKC"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Gq-coupled receptors activate PLC-β through Gαq-GTP. PLC-β
      hydrolyzes membrane phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate
      (PIP₂) into inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP₃) and
      diacylglycerol (DAG). IP₃ binds receptors on the endoplasmic
      reticulum, causing Ca²⁺ release. DAG remains associated with
      the membrane and helps recruit and activate conventional PKC,
      which also requires Ca²⁺. Blocking PLC-β eliminates both
      second messengers, while directly supplying a DAG analogue
      can bypass the blocked enzyme for PKC activation. Gs and Gi
      primarily regulate adenylyl cyclase, and Gαq does not
      directly phosphorylate PKC.
  - stem: "Two cell lines express equal numbers of the same Gs-coupled receptor. One is normal, while the other lacks both β-arrestin proteins. Following continuous exposure to a saturating agonist, both cell lines initially show similar increases in cAMP. After 15 minutes, the normal cells show greatly reduced cAMP production despite continued agonist exposure, while β-arrestin-deficient cells maintain a much stronger response. What best explains this difference?"
    choices:
      - "β-arrestin normally binds activated, phosphorylated GPCRs and limits further G-protein coupling, producing receptor desensitization"
      - "β-arrestin normally converts Gαs-GTP into Gαi-GTP, switching the receptor from stimulatory to inhibitory signaling"
      - "β-arrestin is necessary for the initial activation of adenylyl cyclase, so its absence should prevent all cAMP production"
      - "β-arrestin irreversibly hydrolyzes the agonist, preventing it from interacting with receptors"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Sustained GPCR activation commonly triggers receptor
      phosphorylation by GPCR kinases (GRKs). β-arrestins bind
      phosphorylated, activated receptors and interfere with
      subsequent heterotrimeric G-protein coupling, causing
      desensitization. β-arrestins can also promote receptor
      internalization through clathrin-associated machinery.
      Cells lacking β-arrestins therefore often show reduced
      desensitization and more sustained G-protein signaling.
      The similar initial cAMP responses indicate that initial
      receptor–Gs coupling remains functional. β-arrestin
      does not convert Gs into Gi or directly destroy the
      agonist, and it is not required for the initial
      Gs-mediated activation of adenylyl cyclase.
  - stem: "A Gs-coupled receptor normally stimulates cAMP production when an agonist binds. A researcher introduces a mutation into Gαs that allows GDP binding but prevents GDP release and subsequent GTP loading. The receptor still binds agonist normally, and adenylyl cyclase remains functional when directly stimulated by forskolin. What is the most likely effect of this mutation?"
    choices:
      - "cAMP production becomes constitutively elevated because GDP-bound Gαs activates adenylyl cyclase"
      - "Agonist binding fails to stimulate cAMP production because Gαs cannot undergo the nucleotide exchange required for activation"
      - "The receptor switches to Gq signaling because GDP-bound Gαs directly activates PLC-β"
      - "Agonist binding causes excessive cAMP production because Gαs can no longer hydrolyze GTP"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Heterotrimeric G proteins are inactive when their Gα
      subunit is GDP-bound and associated with Gβγ. An activated
      GPCR functions as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor
      (GEF), promoting GDP release so abundant cellular GTP can
      bind. GTP binding activates Gαs, enabling stimulation
      of adenylyl cyclase. A mutation that prevents GDP release
      traps Gαs in its inactive state even when the receptor
      binds agonist. The forskolin response shows that adenylyl
      cyclase itself still functions. This mutation is
      mechanistically different from impaired GTP hydrolysis,
      which would prolong activation after GTP binding.
  - stem: "A Gi-coupled receptor inhibits adenylyl cyclase when activated by an agonist. Researchers compare normal cells with cells lacking a regulator of G-protein signaling (RGS) protein that normally accelerates Gαi-mediated GTP hydrolysis. After a brief agonist pulse followed by complete agonist removal, both cell types initially show similar decreases in cAMP production. Which change is most likely in the RGS-deficient cells?"
    choices:
      - "cAMP production recovers faster because Gαi spontaneously releases GTP without RGS"
      - "Gi activation cannot occur because RGS proteins are required for GDP-to-GTP exchange"
      - "cAMP production remains suppressed longer because Gαi-GTP persists before returning to its inactive GDP-bound state"
      - "The receptor begins stimulating adenylyl cyclase because RGS deficiency converts Gi into Gs"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      RGS proteins act as GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs),
      accelerating the intrinsic GTP hydrolysis activity of
      many Gα subunits. In the Gi pathway, Gαi-GTP inhibits
      adenylyl cyclase until GTP is hydrolyzed to GDP.
      Removing the RGS protein slows this inactivation,
      prolonging the inhibitory signal after agonist removal.
      Initial activation can remain normal because the GPCR,
      not RGS, promotes GDP-to-GTP exchange. RGS proteins do
      not convert Gi into Gs, and losing a GAP does not
      accelerate GTP hydrolysis. This illustrates how
      G-protein signaling duration depends on the GTPase
      cycle as well as receptor activation.
  - stem: "A GPCR is exposed separately to two ligands. At saturating concentrations, agonist A produces a maximal cAMP response of 100 units, while ligand B produces only 35 units. When a high concentration of B is added to cells already stimulated with A, the response falls from 100 to 50 units. Both ligands compete for the same binding site, and neither changes receptor abundance. How is B best classified?"
    choices:
      - "A full agonist, because it activates the receptor when administered alone"
      - "An inverse agonist, because it reduces the response produced by a full agonist"
      - "A neutral antagonist, because it competes with the full agonist for the same binding site"
      - "A partial agonist, because it activates the receptor with lower efficacy than A and competitively reduces A's response when both are present"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      A partial agonist binds and activates a receptor but has
      lower maximal efficacy than a full agonist under the
      same assay conditions. B produces 35 units alone,
      demonstrating positive intrinsic efficacy, but cannot
      produce the full 100-unit response of A. When both
      compete for the same binding site, B can displace A
      from receptors and reduce the overall response because
      B produces less activation per occupied receptor.
      A neutral antagonist would produce no receptor-mediated
      activation by itself, while an inverse agonist would
      reduce constitutive receptor activity below its basal
      level. The ability to reduce a full agonist's response
      does not automatically make a ligand an inverse agonist.
  - stem: "A researcher constructs concentration–response curves for a Gs-coupled receptor agonist in the absence and presence of drug X. Without X, the agonist has an EC50 of 2 nM and a maximal cAMP response of 100 units. With 10 nM X, the agonist EC50 increases to 20 nM, but sufficiently high agonist concentrations still produce 100 units. Drug X has no measurable effect on basal cAMP when administered alone. Which pharmacological mechanism best fits these observations?"
    choices:
      - "X is a reversible competitive antagonist whose inhibition can be overcome by increasing agonist concentration"
      - "X is an irreversible antagonist that permanently eliminates 90% of the receptor population"
      - "X is an inverse agonist that reduces constitutive receptor activity below basal levels"
      - "X is a positive allosteric modulator that increases the agonist's apparent affinity for the receptor"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      A reversible competitive antagonist competes with an
      agonist for the same receptor-binding site. It shifts
      the agonist concentration–response curve to the right,
      increasing the apparent EC50 without reducing the
      maximal response when enough agonist is supplied.
      Here the EC50 increases tenfold, from 2 to 20 nM,
      while Emax remains 100 units, which is characteristic
      of surmountable competitive antagonism. X also has no
      intrinsic effect on basal signaling, consistent with
      a neutral antagonist. An irreversible antagonist
      can reduce Emax when sufficient receptors are
      inactivated, although receptor reserve can initially
      mask this effect. An inverse agonist would decrease
      constitutive signaling, while a positive allosteric
      modulator generally enhances rather than inhibits
      agonist responses.

Adrenaline makes the heart beat harder, relaxes airway muscle and narrows skin blood vessels. The hormone matters, but it cannot specify those opposite outcomes by itself.

> **The one idea.** GPCR subtype, G protein, and downstream proteins decide the first response.
>
> *Scope:* the first response through the canonical GPCR–heterotrimeric-G-protein pathway in a particular cell. Ligand dose and efficacy still matter, one receptor may use several G proteins, and prolonged signaling recruits other partners.

## From an outside pocket to an inside switch

The first film drew this relay as cartoons; here are the real parts.

A **G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)** is one protein chain that crosses the plasma membrane seven times. Each crossing is an alpha helix of twenty or more mostly water-avoiding amino acids. The amino end lies outside the cell and the carboxyl end inside, the architecture from the receptor field guide.

**"Lots of inputs/ligands."** The fold accepts very different inputs: adrenaline, acetylcholine, dopamine, histamine, glucagon and other peptide hormones, odorants, bitter and sweet tastes, and light. Roughly a third of approved drugs act on a GPCR.

An **agonist** activates its receptor (the lesson on binding and dose-response). In the beta-2-adrenergic receptor, adrenaline binds among the helices toward the outer face. Comparing the receptor's inactive crystal structure with one caught holding Gs (with a high-affinity agonist bound) shows the largest change inside: the cytoplasmic end of helix 6 moves outward by about 14 angstroms, opening the cavity where the tail of G alpha docks.

The **heterotrimeric G protein** has three subunits. G alpha holds a guanine nucleotide; G beta and G gamma bind so tightly that they act as one beta-gamma unit. Lipid tails on G alpha and G gamma hold the trio at the membrane's inner face, as in the lesson on lipid anchors.

The active receptor acts as a **guanine-nucleotide exchange factor (GEF)**: it promotes GDP release from G alpha, and GTP, more plentiful than GDP in the cytosol, fills the empty site. In the textbook picture G-alpha–GTP then leaves beta-gamma; in living cells, activated Gi may instead rearrange without fully separating.

> **active receptor + Gα(GDP)·βγ + GTP → active receptor + Gα(GTP) + βγ + GDP**

The receptor appears on both sides because it is a catalyst, the source of the gain from the lesson on signaling circuits. One activation cycle ends when G alpha hydrolyses GTP and the GDP-bound trimer reforms.

**Predict.** A mutant G alpha can bind GDP but cannot release it. The receptor still binds agonist. What happens to the first downstream response?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It fails. The receptor activates, but G alpha cannot release GDP to load GTP, so the G protein stays inactive.

</details>

## "All the pathways": four G-alpha families, then many targets

The human genome has 16 G-alpha genes in four sequence families.

| G-alpha family | Main direct target | First consequence | Example receptors |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gs | adenylyl cyclase, activated | cAMP rises | beta-adrenergic, glucagon |
| Gi/o | adenylyl cyclase, inhibited | cAMP falls; beta-gamma acts | M2 muscarinic, alpha-2-adrenergic, CB1 cannabinoid |
| Gq/11 | phospholipase C-beta | PIP2 becomes IP3 and DAG; calcium rises | alpha-1-adrenergic, M3 muscarinic |
| G12/13 | exchange factors for Rho | actin rearranges; cells change shape and move | thrombin, lysophosphatidic acid |

Beta-gamma is a signal too: in heart pacemaker cells, beta-gamma released from Gi opens the GIRK potassium channel, short for G-protein-gated inwardly rectifying potassium channel. Potassium flows out and moves the membrane potential away from the firing threshold. G-alpha-i also lowers cAMP, reducing current through pacemaker channels that cAMP opens directly.

## One ligand, different cellular answers

The first film traced acetylcholine: through M2 and Gi it slows pacemaker cells, and through M3 and Gq it contracts airway muscle. One transmitter quiets one tissue and excites another. Acetylcholine also marks the rule's boundary: at skeletal muscle it meets an ion channel from the receptor field guide, not a GPCR.

Adrenaline has a larger cast: nine human adrenergic receptor genes. Three alpha-1 subtypes couple mainly to Gq, three alpha-2 subtypes to Gi, and three beta subtypes to Gs.

**Predict.** Assign one adrenergic subtype and G-alpha family to make the heart beat harder, relax airway muscle and narrow skin vessels. Then predict the effect of blocking only the heart subtype.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Skin vessels: alpha-1 on Gq, because raised cytosolic calcium contracts smooth muscle. Airway muscle: beta-2 on Gs, because raised cAMP favors relaxation there. Heart: beta-1 on Gs. Heart and airway cells interpret the same messenger differently because different downstream proteins wait for cAMP (the next lesson follows that messenger).

Blocking only beta-1, the reasoning behind beta-1-selective beta-blockers, lowers heartbeat rate and force during adrenaline signaling and leaves airway and vessel receptors alone. A blocker that also hits beta-2 removes adrenaline's airway relaxation, a risk for a person with asthma. This is the assignment the body mainly uses.

</details>

## Ligand efficacy, and why "GPCRs can be unfaithful to trimeric G proteins"

The second film classed ligands by how they move **basal activity**, a receptor population's signaling with no ligand: an **agonist** raises it, a neutral **antagonist** leaves it while blocking other ligands, an **inverse agonist** lowers it. Its meter was an operational test, not a claim that a GPCR has only two rigid structures.

For many receptors only a small fraction is active at any moment, so basal activity can be too low to measure. Many drugs long called antagonists proved to be inverse agonists once tested where it was visible. Carazolol, the beta-blocker bound in the inactive beta-2 receptor structures from 2007, is a partial inverse agonist.

The four-family table also simplified: as the thrombin receptor showed, one receptor can couple to several G-alpha families, and the dominant partner can differ between cell types, so a coupling measured in one cell line is a hypothesis about the next. Efficacy is measured the same way: a ligand classed for one response in one cell may be classed differently for another.

Time adds another partner. Kinases phosphorylate a receptor that has stayed active, then **arrestin** binds its tail, blocks further G-protein coupling and helps pull the receptor into the cell. It also binds other signaling proteins, but whether arrestin signals on its own without G proteins remains debated, a question the lesson on turning signals off develops.

**Predict.** Two cell lines have equal amounts of a Gs-coupled receptor known to undergo beta-arrestin-dependent desensitization, such as the beta-2-adrenergic receptor, but one lacks both beta-arrestins. After prolonged agonist exposure, which line should retain more G-protein signaling?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The line lacking beta-arrestins. Its initial response can be normal, but it loses a major brake on repeated receptor–G-protein coupling, so desensitization is reduced.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How the seven-pass plan was found

Rhodopsin, the light-catching pigment of retinal rod cells, was too greasy to sequence whole. Paul Hargrave's laboratory in the United States and Yuri Ovchinnikov's in Moscow cut it at methionine and sequenced the pieces one amino acid at a time. By 1983 both had published the complete cow-rhodopsin sequence, including seven water-avoiding stretches long enough to span a membrane.

In 1986 Robert Lefkowitz's laboratory at Duke and a Merck group cloned a beta-adrenergic receptor gene. Brian Kobilka, then a cardiology fellow, had worked toward it for almost two years. Its sequence resembled rhodopsin, seven spans included. The only other seven-pass protein then known also used light, and Lefkowitz later recalled speculation that this plan marked light-sensitive molecules. The adrenaline receptor showed instead that it marked receptors that signal through G proteins.

### How many GPCR genes

The human genome holds roughly 800 GPCR genes; Lefkowitz's Nobel lecture says almost a thousand. About half are odorant receptors, while a mouse has about a thousand odorant-receptor genes alone.

### How the pertussis test named CB1

An **orphan receptor** is a receptor gene with no known ligand. In 1990 Lisa Matsuda and colleagues in Tom Bonner's group had a rat-brain gene predicting a seven-pass protein. Allyn Howlett's laboratory had shown that cannabinoids lower cAMP dose-dependently in nerve-cell preparations, that only one mirror-image form works, and that pertussis toxin blocks the effect. Expressed in cells that lacked it, the orphan showed those properties: it was CB1, the cannabinoid receptor, which couples to Gi/o.
