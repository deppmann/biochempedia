## Explain: who lives together and what holds them back

<WordBank terms={['population', 'community', 'ecosystem', 'biotic factor', 'abiotic factor', 'competition', 'cooperation', 'limiting factor', 'exponential growth', 'carrying capacity', 'extinction', 'dynamic equilibrium']} />

Think about one creek flowing into the Bay, with blue crabs, fish, clams, marsh grass and microbes, plus mud, water and sunlight.

A **population** is all the members of one species that live in the same area at the same time. In species that reproduce sexually, they can breed with each other. The blue crabs in the Bay this winter are a population. Its members compete for food, water, shelter and mates.

A **community** is all the populations of different species that live in an area and interact. The crabs, fish, clams and marsh grass of the creek form a community.

An **ecosystem** is a community together with its nonliving surroundings, such as water, mud, air and sunlight.

Two kinds of factors affect that population:

- A **biotic factor** is a living part of the environment, such as predators, food, competitors and disease.
- An **abiotic factor** is a nonliving part of the environment, such as temperature, moisture, air, salinity (salt level) and pH.

Living things interact in many ways, including predation, when one eats another. **Competition** happens when individuals need the same limited resource, such as food or a hiding place. **Cooperation** happens when individuals work together and both gain, as when wolves hunt in a pack.

A **limiting factor** is anything that keeps a population from growing larger. Examples for blue crabs:

| Factor | Kind | How it can limit blue crabs |
|---|---|---|
| Larger crabs | Biotic: predators | Adult blue crabs eat young crabs, so fewer young survive. |
| Clams and fish | Biotic: food | Less food can mean less energy for making eggs. |
| Other crabs | Biotic: competitors | In a crowd, more crabs need the same food and shelter. |
| Disease | Biotic | Sickness can spread faster through a crowded group. |
| Cold water | Abiotic: temperature | Very cold winters can kill crabs. |
| Fresh water | Abiotic: salinity | Low salt in cold water lowered crab survival in lab tests. |

Other species meet other limits. Land plants and animals depend on moisture, land animals take oxygen from the air, and the pH of soil helps decide which plants grow where.

## Explain: J-shaped and S-shaped growth

The first film bent a J-shaped curve into an S. Here are the numbers and names behind it.

Suppose 10 bacteria land in a dish of fresh food, and each one splits in two every 30 minutes. After one step there are 20, then 40, 80, 160 and 320. The population increases by the same fraction each step, but the number added gets larger. This is **exponential growth**, and its graph makes a J shape. It can happen while a population is small and resources are plentiful, but it cannot last: food runs short and waste builds up.

Biology courses name five parts of a population growth curve. The first three make up the simplest S-shaped model, which levels off near the **carrying capacity**, the largest population the environment can support over time with its resources and challenges:

1. **Initial growth.** A small population adds only a few individuals because few are present to have young.
2. **Exponential growth.** Numbers rise faster while resources are plentiful.
3. **Steady state.** Growth slows. The model line approaches carrying capacity, while real populations usually move up and down around it. Births and deaths are about equal if few individuals move in or out. This balance, kept by ongoing change, is a **dynamic equilibrium**.

The last two are possible outcomes if conditions get worse, not required stages:

4. **Decline.** Deaths outnumber births and the population shrinks.
5. **Extinction.** If the decline continues, the population falls to zero.

Many real populations stay near a steady state for a long time, and some briefly overshoot carrying capacity before falling back. When a population in one place reaches zero, it has undergone **local extinction**, which is different from extinction of a whole species.

## Explain: which limits change with crowding

Some limits get stronger as a population becomes crowded. These are **density-dependent** factors, such as competition, disease and predators. Other limits hit a population whatever its size. These are **density-independent** factors.

## Explain: carrying capacity can move

A freeze lowers a crab count whatever the crowding. Does it also lower how many crabs the Bay can support? That depends on what changes and for how long.

Hiding places matter for young crabs. In the Bay, adult crabs do nearly all the killing of young crabs, and in one tank test young crabs survived better among more stand-in grass stems (see Going deeper for both studies and their limits).

People can change carrying capacity and population size too. Crabbing removes crabs from the Bay each year, and shoreline building can remove marsh. [The Chesapeake Under Pressure](/lessons/chesapeake-under-pressure/) examines these pressures.

In every population, living things can produce more young than their environment can support, and the environment's limits push back.

So a hard freeze and a lost marsh can both leave fewer crabs. Can the count alone tell them apart? The second film takes that up.

### Design problem

You want to test whether salt in the water is a limiting factor for duckweed, a tiny floating plant that multiplies by budding. Plan a fair test that shows how salt affects the population's growth and the level it reaches.

**Predict.** Why should the plan use several cups at each salt level and place the cups in random order under the lights?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Repeated cups show whether a pattern is consistent instead of a chance result in one cup. Random order keeps one position under the lights from always going with one salt level.

</details>

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** How does the amount of salt in the water affect the growth of a duckweed population?
- **Independent variable:** salt added to each cup, for example 0, 1, 2 and 4 grams per liter.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of duckweed fronds (the tiny leaf-like parts), counted every two days for three weeks.
- **Control:** cups with no added salt.
- **Constants:** the same pond water and plant food, cup size, water volume, light, temperature and starting number of fronds (for example 10).
- **Repeated trials:** at least four cups for each salt level, placed in a random order under the lights.
- **What results would mean:** if the no-salt cups level off at the highest count and saltier cups grow more slowly, salt slows growth in these cups. If the saltier cups also level off lower, salt lowers the carrying capacity.
- **Safety and ethics:** wash your hands after handling pond water, and do not release the plants into a stream or pond afterwards.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The survey estimates abundance; it does not measure carrying capacity, so it cannot show whether carrying capacity changed. Cold, salinity, predators, food, hiding places and crabbing, which removes adult crabs every year, all change how many crabs are born and survive. In some winters, such as 2025–2026, most of the change came from young crabs; in others, adult females changed more.

**Science practices you used:** asking a question about a real pattern, using a model and naming its limits, reading a data table and finding a mean, planning a fair test with a control and repeated trials, and making a claim backed by evidence and reasoning.

## Going deeper

### Hiding places for young crabs

In a 2023 tank study, young blue crabs survived better when the tank held more wooden rods standing in for marsh grass stems, with adult crabs present as predators. The study was mainly a test of a method, and the authors said the result still needs testing with real plants. A 37-year field study in the Bay found that adult crabs did nearly all the killing of young crabs, and that shallow water near shore gave young crabs partial shelter.

### Winter survival of crabs in the cold and fresh

In a 2021 laboratory study, scientists kept blue crabs at different winter water temperatures and salt levels. In the coldest, freshest condition tested (2 °C and a salinity of 5), only about 10% of the crabs were alive after 40 days. Warmer, saltier tanks had higher survival. At the same low salinity, crabs in water 2 °C warmer took about twice as long to fall to 10% survival. The 10% figure is one treatment's survival after 40 days, not a carrying capacity for the whole Bay.

### The longer decline in the Bay's crabs

Scientists say the Bay's crabs have declined since 2011, and a draft 2026 stock assessment found no clear cause.
