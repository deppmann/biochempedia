## Explain: who lives together and what holds them back

<WordBank terms={['population', 'community', 'ecosystem', 'biotic factor', 'abiotic factor', 'competition', 'cooperation', 'limiting factor', 'exponential growth', 'carrying capacity', 'extinction', 'dynamic equilibrium']} />

Think about one creek that flows into the Bay. It holds blue crabs, fish, clams, marsh grass and microbes, along with mud, water and sunlight.

A **population** is all the members of one species that live in the same area at the same time. In species that reproduce sexually, they can breed with each other. The blue crabs in the Bay this winter are a population. Its members compete for food, water, shelter and mates.

A **community** is all the populations of different species that live in an area and interact. The crabs, fish, clams and marsh grass of the creek form a community.

An **ecosystem** is a community together with its nonliving surroundings, such as water, mud, air and sunlight.

Two kinds of factors shape a population:

- A **biotic factor** is a living part of the environment, such as predators, food, competitors and disease.
- An **abiotic factor** is a nonliving part of the environment, such as temperature, moisture, air, salinity (salt level) and pH.

Living things in a community interact in many ways, such as predation (one eats another). Two important ones are these. **Competition** happens when individuals need the same limited resource, such as food or a hiding place. **Cooperation** happens when individuals work together and both gain, as when wolves hunt in a pack.

A **limiting factor** is anything that keeps a population from growing larger. Here are examples for the blue crab.

| Factor | Kind | How it can limit blue crabs |
|---|---|---|
| Larger crabs | Biotic: predators | Adult blue crabs eat young crabs, so fewer young survive. |
| Clams and fish | Biotic: food | Less food can mean less energy for making eggs. |
| Other crabs | Biotic: competitors | In a crowd, more crabs need the same food and shelter. |
| Disease | Biotic | Sickness can spread faster through a crowded group. |
| Cold water | Abiotic: temperature | Very cold winters can kill crabs. |
| Fresh water | Abiotic: salinity | Low salt in cold water lowered crab survival in lab tests. |

Other species meet other limits. Land plants and animals depend on moisture, land animals take oxygen from the air, and the pH of soil helps decide which plants grow where.

## Explain: J-shaped and S-shaped growth

Suppose 10 bacteria land in a dish of fresh food, and each one splits in two every 30 minutes. After one step there are 20, then 40, 80, 160 and 320. The number added gets bigger every step. This is **exponential growth**, growth in which a population increases by the same fraction in each time period. On a graph it makes a J shape.

Exponential growth happens when a population is small and resources are plentiful. No real population can keep it up for long. Food runs short, waste builds up, and crowding makes competition, predation and disease worse.

When limits set in, the simplest model bends the curve into an S shape. Biology courses name five parts of a population growth curve. The first three make up the S shape. The last two are what can happen afterwards if conditions get worse:

1. **Initial growth.** A small population adds only a few individuals at first, because there are few individuals to have young.
2. **Exponential growth.** Numbers rise faster and faster while resources last.
3. **Steady state.** Growth slows and the line levels off near the carrying capacity.
4. **Decline.** If conditions get worse, deaths outnumber births and the population shrinks.
5. **Extinction.** If the decline continues, the population falls to zero.

**Carrying capacity** is the largest population that an environment can support over time, with its resources and its challenges. At the steady state, births and deaths are about equal, if few individuals move in or out. The numbers still go up and down a little around that level. This balance, kept by constant change, is called **dynamic equilibrium**.

These five parts are a model, and real populations do not have to pass through all of them in order. Many populations stay near a steady state for a long time. Also, **extinction** of a population in one place is different from extinction of a whole species. Blue crabs could vanish from one creek and still live in the rest of the Bay.

## Explain: carrying capacity moves

Carrying capacity is not one number stamped on a species. It changes when the food, space and shelter an area offers change and stay changed. More food or more hiding places can raise it. The loss of marsh grass or other cover can lower it. A single hard freeze is different. It kills crabs and pushes the population below carrying capacity, but it does not change how many crabs the Bay's resources could support, so the population can grow back.

Hiding places matter for young crabs. A 37-year field study in the Bay found that adult crabs did nearly all the killing of young crabs. A tank study found that young crabs survived better with more stand-in marsh grass stems, though the authors said the result still needs testing with real plants. Details of both studies are in Going deeper.

Some limits get stronger as a population gets more crowded. These are called **density-dependent** factors, such as competition, disease and predators. Other limits hit a population whatever its size, such as a storm or a hard freeze. These are **density-independent** factors.

People change carrying capacity and population size too. Crabbing removes crabs from the Bay each year, and building on shorelines can remove marsh. You will look at these pressures in [The Chesapeake Under Pressure](/lessons/chesapeake-under-pressure/).

The tension in every population is the same. Living things can produce more young than their environment can support, and the environment's limits push back.

### Design problem

You want to test whether salt in the water is a limiting factor for duckweed, a tiny floating plant that multiplies by budding. Plan a fair test that shows how salt affects the population's growth and the level it reaches.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** How does the amount of salt in the water affect the growth of a duckweed population?
- **Independent variable:** salt added to each cup, for example 0, 1, 2 and 4 grams per liter.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of duckweed fronds (the tiny leaf-like parts), counted every two days for three weeks.
- **Control:** cups with no added salt.
- **Constants:** the same pond water and plant food, cup size, water volume, light, temperature and starting number of fronds (for example 10).
- **Repeated trials:** at least four cups for each salt level, placed in a random order under the lights.
- **What results would mean:** if the no-salt cups level off at the highest count and saltier cups grow more slowly, salt slows growth in these cups. If the saltier cups also level off lower, salt lowers the carrying capacity.
- **Safety and ethics:** wash your hands after handling pond water, and do not release the plants into a stream or pond afterwards.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Hiding places for young crabs

In a 2023 tank study, young blue crabs survived better when the tank held more wooden rods standing in for marsh grass stems, with adult crabs present as predators. The study was mainly a test of a method, and the authors said the result still needs testing with real plants. The same 37-year field study in the Bay also found that shallow water near shore gave young crabs partial shelter.

### Winter survival of crabs in the cold and fresh

In a 2021 laboratory study, scientists kept blue crabs at different winter water temperatures and salt levels. In the coldest, freshest condition tested (2 °C and a salinity of 5), only about 10% of the crabs were alive after 40 days. Warmer, saltier tanks had higher survival. At the same low salinity, crabs in water 2 °C warmer took about twice as long to fall to 10% survival. The 10% figure is one treatment's survival after 40 days, not a carrying capacity for the whole Bay.

### The longer decline in the Bay's crabs

Scientists say the Bay's crabs have declined since 2011, and a draft 2026 stock assessment found no clear cause.
