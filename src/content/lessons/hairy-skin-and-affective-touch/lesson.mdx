techniques:
  - name: "Microneurography with psychophysics"
    blurb: >-
      A fine electrode inserted through the skin into a peripheral nerve records single afferent
      axons in an awake person while a controlled stimulus is applied. Paired with ratings, it
      lets you compare a fibre's tuning with what people report, though in Löken 2009 the two came
      from separate groups.
  - name: "Intersectional and lineage genetics (Cre, CreER, Flp)"
    blurb: >-
      Recombinase lines give access to a sensory neuron class for labelling, recording, ablation
      or activation. A constitutive Cre permanently labels cells that recombined after expressing the driver, and their descendants (a
      lineage); an inducible CreER given tamoxifen in adulthood marks cells expressing it then;
      combining two drivers narrows the class.
  - name: "In vivo calcium imaging of afferent terminals"
    blurb: >-
      A genetically encoded calcium indicator in a defined afferent population is imaged at its
      spinal terminals in an anaesthetized mouse while the skin is stroked or poked. It shows which
      stimuli drive the population, at the level of the terminals imaged.
  - name: "Fibre photometry with a dopamine sensor"
    blurb: >-
      An optical fibre over a brain region collects fluorescence from a genetically encoded
      dopamine sensor during behaviour. It reports changes in
      extracellular dopamine signal there, not a feeling.

**Stroke the hairy skin of a human forearm with a soft brush at a few centimetres per second, and one class of slow, unmyelinated nerve fibre fires harder than it does for slower or faster strokes. Ask people to rate the same strokes, and they call those middle speeds the most pleasant.**

These C-tactile (CT) afferents are the best-supported part of a system for the pleasant and socially relevant side of gentle touch. The match between their tuning and the ratings is a correspondence, and whether a touch feels pleasant also depends on who is touching, on the situation and on processing in the brain, so CT firing alone does not decide it.

The core lesson on [touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) noted that some C fibres are low-threshold touch afferents rather than nociceptors, and the lesson on [low-threshold mechanoreceptors](/lessons/ltmr-classes/) placed C-LTMRs and Aδ endings on mouse hair follicles. This lesson asks two narrower questions. What detects a slow stroke? And what is known, and not known, about why it can feel good? The short answers: more than one afferent class, and much less than popular accounts suggest.

## The idea: a slow brush is tuned like a stimulus feature

A **C-tactile (CT) afferent** is a human low-threshold mechanoreceptor with an unmyelinated axon. The term is often used interchangeably with the mouse term **C-LTMR** (C-fibre low-threshold mechanoreceptor), but whether the two name the same cells is unresolved, and this lesson ends on that question. Vrontou and colleagues declined to call MrgprB4 neurons C-LTMRs by electrophysiological criteria, because von Frey filaments did not activate them. Unmyelinated touch afferents had been recorded in human hairy skin before 2009 (Johansson 1988, Nordin 1990, Vallbo 1999), but what they were for was still a hypothesis.

Löken, Wessberg, Morrison, McGlone and Olausson (Nature Neuroscience, 2009) treated stroke velocity as a stimulus feature, the way [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/) treats orientation or frequency. In **microneurography**, a fine electrode is passed through the skin into a peripheral nerve to record single axons in an awake person. They recorded single CT afferents from the forearm of healthy volunteers while a soft brush crossed the receptive field at a range of controlled velocities. CT firing traced an inverted U, highest for strokes between about 1 and 10 cm/s. Myelinated afferents recorded in the same study did not show that tuning; their firing rose with velocity. The tuning, not a higher absolute firing rate, is what set CT afferents apart.

In separate sessions, a separate group of volunteers rated how pleasant the same strokes felt. Their ratings traced the same inverted U. Think of two thermometers in different rooms that both read highest at noon: they agree, but neither shows that one causes the other. The 2009 result is a correspondence between two tuning curves measured in different people. It does not show that CT firing is needed for pleasantness, or that it is the only input to it.

## Stroking is not one receptor: field-LTMRs and C fibres in mouse

In mouse hairy skin, at least three afferent populations are candidates for detecting a stroke, and they are not the same cells.

**TH-positive C-LTMRs.** Li and colleagues in the Ginty laboratory (Cell, 2011) recorded from sensory neurons in an ex vivo mouse preparation and then identified them. Neurons marked by tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) were C-LTMRs, and they conducted slowly. Their endings were **lanceolate**: nerve terminals that run lengthwise along a hair follicle, parallel to the hair shaft. They sat on zigzag and awl/auchene follicles (two of the mouse hair types). The TH-positive population did not express MrgprB4. LTMRs innervating the same or neighbouring follicles sent central projections into narrow dorsal horn columns, which the authors read as support for local integration, the topic of [the dorsal horn lesson](/lessons/dorsal-horn-touch/).

**MrgprB4 neurons.** Liu and colleagues in David Anderson's laboratory (Nature Neuroscience, 2007) used a genetic tracer to follow sensory neurons expressing the receptor MrgprB4. The fibres were unmyelinated and nonpeptidergic and innervated hairy skin only. That paper was anatomy. Its link to tactile C fibres was a suggestion, not a recording.

**Aβ field-LTMRs.** Bai, Lehnert and colleagues in the Ginty laboratory (Cell, 2015) used intersectional TrkC and Ret genetics to reach a class whose endings are **circumferential**: they wrap around the follicle instead of running along it. A **field-LTMR** is a large myelinated afferent of this type with a broad receptive field. In vivo recordings from the ganglia showed that these neurons did not respond to deflection of a single hair and needed indentation forces in the noxious range, yet they fired to gentle stroking of the skin. One neuron could form as many as 180 circumferential endings. The authors proposed that the neuron sums many weakly sensitive endings, so a stroke that moves many follicles at once reaches threshold when no single hair can. A single hair is a lever; a field-LTMR is a net. The paper did not test pleasantness or reward.

So gentle stroking is not a C-fibre job alone. Any claim that "the stroking receptor" drives a behaviour has to say which of these classes it manipulated.

## From skin to reward in mice

Four groups have manipulated mouse touch afferents or their central targets and measured behaviour. Keep each outcome as measured.

**Vrontou and colleagues (Nature, 2013)** imaged calcium in the spinal terminals of MrgprB4-positive neurons in anaesthetized mice at least 8 weeks old. The terminals responded to massage-like stroking of hairy skin delivered at about 0.5 to 2 cm/s. That is the protocol's speed, not an estimate of the mouse optimum, and the human 1 to 10 cm/s band should not be carried across. In a separate experiment with male mice about one month old, a **DREADD** (a designer receptor activated only by a synthetic ligand) was used to activate MrgprB4 neurons, and the mice developed a **conditioned place preference**: they spent more time in the chamber paired with activation. The authors read this as positive reinforcement, anxiolysis or both.

**Elias and colleagues in the Abdus-Saboor laboratory (Cell, 2023)** worked with adult female mice and a Cre line that marks the developmental Mrgprb4 lineage, that is, cells that recombined after expressing Mrgprb4 at some point, not only those expressing it in adulthood. Optogenetic activation of these afferents in back skin produced place preference and a dorsiflexion resembling **lordosis**, the arched posture a receptive female mouse takes during mating. Ablating the lineage reduced sexual receptivity. Stimulating the lineage evoked dopamine release in the brain, and in ablated females a dopamine-sensor signal in the nucleus accumbens, measured by fibre photometry, was reduced during mounting. A dopamine signal is a neuromodulator readout tied to reward prediction and motivation ([motivation and reward](/lessons/motivation-and-reward/)), not a measure of pleasure.

**Yu and colleagues (Neuron, 2022)** started from the brain. Touch-like tactile stimulation increased the firing of oxytocin neurons in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVH), and Tac1-positive neurons in the lateral and ventrolateral periaqueductal gray sent monosynaptic excitatory projections onto them. Reducing Tac1-neuron activity abolished the touch-evoked oxytocin firing. The stimulus promoted social interaction and place preference. The study did not test which peripheral afferents are needed; it is not evidence about Mrgprb4 neurons. Oxytocin's wider roles belong to [the hypothalamus lesson](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/).

**Huzard and colleagues (Science Advances, 2022)** deleted the calcium channel CaV3.2 in the Nav1.8 lineage of male mice, a developmental deletion that the authors took to impair C-LTMR function (Nav1.8 is not restricted to C-LTMRs), and the mice showed fewer social and tactile interactions. Transient chemogenetic enhancement of C-LTMR excitability in adults promoted touch seeking. The authors could not tell whether the DREADD activated the neurons directly or only potentiated them.

## What human patients and lesions say

Four human studies test the 2009 picture from different angles, and they do not all point the same way.

**A deafferented patient.** Olausson and colleagues (Nature Neuroscience, 2002) studied a person lacking large myelinated afferents. CT stimulation of hairy skin produced a faint, pleasant sensation, and functional MRI showed insular activation with no detectable activity in primary or secondary somatosensory cortex. The authors proposed that CT input may underlie emotional and affiliative responses to caress-like touch. The insular link is one patient and one imaging method; [emotion and stress](/lessons/emotion-and-stress/) discusses what an insular activation can and cannot mean.

**Glabrous skin.** Watkins and colleagues (Journal of Neurophysiology, 2021) recorded three CT afferents in human glabrous hand skin, about 1 for every 80 myelinated mechanoreceptors recorded, and estimated CT density there at around seven times lower than in hairy skin. Human CTs are sparse in the palm, not absent.

**Reduced C-fibre density.** Morrison and colleagues (Brain, 2011) tested ten carriers of a nerve growth factor beta mutation (hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy type V), whose density of thin and unmyelinated fibres is reduced. They rated slow arm stroking at CT-optimal velocities as less pleasant than matched controls, their rating pattern across velocities differed, and their posterior insular response was not modulated by CT-optimal stroking. That points the other way from the cordotomy result and ties hedonic touch to C-fibre density, although the mutation affects more fibres than CT afferents alone.

**Cordotomy.** An **anterolateral cordotomy** cuts the anterolateral spinal cord to relieve intractable pain. Marshall and colleagues (eLife, 2019) tested adults with refractory cancer pain before and after the operation, rating the pleasantness of brush strokes at three speeds. Pain, temperature and itch were markedly disrupted, but pleasantness ratings were not significantly changed. If CT pleasantness travelled only in the anterolateral tract, the cut should have removed it. The result rules out one obligatory route. The authors proposed that fibres ascending outside the anterolateral funiculus, most likely in the dorsal columns, carry enough information, and they also reported reduced ratings of stroking intensity and of texture after the cut, while monofilament detection thresholds were spared. Mouse work points the other way: Choi and colleagues (Nature, 2020) described Gpr83-positive spinoparabrachial neurons, part of the anterolateral system, that take low-threshold mechanosensory input and carry positive or negative valence. The species gap is unresolved.

**Worked problem: reading the fit.** For the pain-affected side before cordotomy, the authors fitted the group's ratings with a quadratic in log velocity:

> **y = -2.07x² + 1.88x + 6.22, where x = log10(v in cm/s), R² = 0.22**

1. *Find the peak.* dy/dx = -4.14x + 1.88. Set it to zero: x = 1.88 / 4.14 = 0.454. The second derivative is -4.14, so this is a maximum.
2. *Convert to velocity.* v = 10^0.454 = 2.85 cm/s.
3. *Peak rating.* x² = 0.206, so y = -2.07(0.206) + 1.88(0.454) + 6.22 = -0.427 + 0.854 + 6.22 = 6.65.
4. *Predicted ratings at the tested speeds.* At 0.3 cm/s, x = -0.523 and y = -0.566 - 0.983 + 6.22 = 4.67. At 3 cm/s, x = 0.477 and y = -0.471 + 0.897 + 6.22 = 6.65. At 30 cm/s, x = 1.477 and y = -4.517 + 2.777 + 6.22 = 4.48.
5. *Interpret.* The fitted peak, 2.85 cm/s, sits inside Löken's 1 to 10 cm/s band, so the patients' group curve has the familiar shape. But R² = 0.22 means velocity explains about a fifth of the variance in these pooled ratings. Much of the rest is the difference between patients in how pleasant they found touch overall, not noise around the velocity effect: within patients, the negative quadratic fitted better than a line in 14 of the 19 adults in the study. And a fit to ratings cannot show that CT afferents are needed. The same study found that pleasantness survived the lesion.

## Which mouse neuron is a CT afferent, and what "pleasant" can mean in a mouse

The cross-species match is unsettled. TH-positive C-LTMRs and MrgprB4 neurons are different populations, and Huzard and colleagues described the identity of the mouse population best matching human CTs as controversial. A 2025 study by Du and colleagues (iScience) imaged the Mrgprb4 lineage in mice and found responses to both mechanical and thermal stimuli; mice lacking the lineage lost a conditioned preference for mild pressure. A polymodal lineage is a poor fit for a pure "pleasant touch" line, but a lineage is not the adult population: Mrgprb4 expression starts in a broader set of progenitors, so mixed responses in it say little about adult Mrgprb4 neurons. Vrontou and colleagues found that adult MrgprB4 fibres responded to stroking and not to pinch. The [sensory neuron atlas](/lessons/sensory-neuron-atlas/) lesson explains why a transcriptomic class is not yet a function. Settling the match needs human molecular data tied to physiologically identified units, and that work has begun: Yu, Nagi and colleagues (Nature Neuroscience, 2024) sequenced individual human ganglion neuron somata, found divergence from mouse and likely human-specific types, and used the molecular profiles to guide microneurography. It has not yet matched a human CT afferent to a mouse type.

The second gap is the word itself. In people, "pleasant" is a rating. In a mouse it can only be an outcome: time in a chamber, a posture, a dopamine-sensor signal, contact with cage mates. Each is useful; none is a report of feeling, and none licenses a claim about human health or wellbeing. The forward lessons on [touch in autism models](/lessons/touch-in-autism-models/) and [itch](/lessons/itch/) return to these afferents from other angles, and [circuit experiment design](/lessons/circuit-experiment-design/) sets out the logic of necessity and sufficiency used below.

### Design problem

Elias and colleagues used a developmental Mrgprb4 lineage. Design experiments to test whether Mrgprb4-expressing afferents in adult mice are needed for rewarding touch, and say what each result would and would not show.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Access in adults.** Use an inducible driver for Mrgprb4, such as a CreER line if a validated one exists (otherwise it must be built and validated first), and give tamoxifen to adult mice, so that only cells expressing the marker then are targeted. Confirm recombination in the ganglia with a reporter and check which cells are labelled, since the lineage and the adult population may differ. Include tamoxifen-treated mice lacking the driver.

**Two separate arms.** Sufficiency: activate the adult population (optogenetically or chemogenetically) and measure place preference. Necessity: ablate or silence it in adults (diphtheria toxin receptor, or an inhibitory DREADD), then test touch-conditioned place preference, the same reward readout the sufficiency arm uses. Measure receptivity to a male and the accumbens dopamine-sensor signal during mounting by fibre photometry as separate social and neural outcomes. Activation alone cannot show need.

**Controls for other explanations.** Measure locomotion and posture so a motor change is not read as receptivity, and run an anxiety assay so anxiolysis is not read as reward. Test both sexes if the claim is general.

**Check the modality.** Image the targeted neurons to stroking, pressure and temperature (Du and colleagues found polymodality in the lineage). If they respond to more than touch, call the result "required for this behaviour," not "a pleasure line."

**Human counterpart.** A mouse marker cannot stand in for a human CT afferent. A human claim is best tested by relating identified CT activity to pleasantness ratings, ideally within the same participants, and eventually by molecular identification of the recorded fibre class. Necessity in people needs a loss-of-function case instead, such as the reduced C-fibre density studied by Morrison and colleagues.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The cordotomy study in detail

Marshall and colleagues (eLife, 2019) reported on 19 adults with refractory cancer pain tested before and after the operation (a twentieth recruit could not be tested afterwards). A brush crossed a marked 10 cm path on the dorsal forearm at 0.3, 3 and 30 cm/s, and patients rated pleasantness from -10 to +10.

### Preparation details for the mouse populations

Li and colleagues in the Ginty laboratory (Cell, 2011) used an ex vivo preparation of skin, nerve, dorsal root ganglion and spinal cord from mice aged 2 to 3 months. Neurons marked by tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) were C-LTMRs, with a mean conduction velocity of 0.58 ± 0.02 m/s in that preparation.

### Lineage labelling and the human atlas

The 2025 study by Du and colleagues (iScience) used mice of both sexes aged 8 to 16 weeks. About 45% of Mrgprd+ neurons are labelled in the Mrgprb4 line they imaged. Yu, Nagi and colleagues (Nature Neuroscience, 2024) identified 16 types of human ganglion neuron.
