techniques:
  - name: "Substrate channeling in multienzyme complexes"
    blurb: "When several active sites are tethered into one physical complex, an intermediate made at one site is handed directly to the next without diffusing into the cytosol. PDC is the textbook case: the lipoamide arm of E2 carries the acetyl unit from E1 to E2 and then visits E3 to be reoxidized, so reactive intermediates do not escape, side reactions are suppressed, and local concentrations stay high. Cryo-EM of the assembled core shows the architecture; kinetic and biochemical experiments test how the intermediates move between active sites."
  - name: "Tracing the released CO₂ with labeled carbon"
    blurb: "Label pyruvate at C1 (the carboxyl) or at C2/C3, and the CO2 released by PDC tells you which carbon left (in whole cells, later turns of the TCA cycle release the other carbons too, so timing matters). This kind of tracing shows that PDC strips the carboxyl carbon (C1) as CO2 and that the surviving two carbons (C2, C3) become the acetyl group. Scaled up, the same logic underlies research ¹³C-pyruvate breath tests and hyperpolarized ¹³C-pyruvate MRI of metabolism in tumors and the failing heart."
  - name: "Phospho-state assays for covalent regulation"
    blurb: "To see whether PDC is on or off you measure the phosphorylation state of E1's regulatory serines (human E1α has three), using phospho-specific antibodies or activity assays before and after adding PDK, PDP, ATP, or Ca2+. Watching activity fall when PDK phosphorylates E1 and rise when PDP (Ca2+-activated) removes the phosphate is how the kinase/phosphatase switch was mapped, and it is the same readout used to test PDK inhibitors like dichloroacetate."

  - stem: "Which statement correctly describes the net reaction carried out by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex?"
    choices:
      - "Pyruvate + CoA + NAD+ -> acetyl-CoA + CO2 + NADH + H+"
      - "Pyruvate + CoA + FAD -> acetyl-CoA + CO2 + FADH2"
      - "Acetyl-CoA + CO2 + NADH -> pyruvate + CoA + NAD+"
      - "Pyruvate + CoA + NAD+ + ATP -> acetyl-CoA + CO2 + NADH + ADP"
    answer: 0
    rationale: "PDC performs an oxidative decarboxylation: pyruvate loses its carboxyl carbon as CO2, is joined to CoA as acetyl-CoA, and NAD+ is reduced to NADH. FADH2 is only a transient enzyme-bound intermediate at E3, not a net product, so the FAD-based equation is wrong. The reverse reaction does not occur in animals because the step is irreversible. No ATP is consumed or produced by PDC, ruling out the ATP-containing equation."
  - stem: "A medical resident is about to start a glucose IV on a severely malnourished, alcohol-dependent patient. Based on PDC biochemistry, what should be given first and why?"
    choices:
      - "Niacin (B3), because NAD+ must be supplied before the gate can open"
      - "Thiamine (B1), because without TPP the E1 step fails and a glucose load can worsen lactic acidosis"
      - "Lipoic acid, because the swinging arm is depleted in malnutrition"
      - "Acetyl-CoA, because PDC needs its product to prime the reaction"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "Thiamine is the precursor of TPP, the coenzyme E1 requires to decarboxylate pyruvate. In thiamine-deficient patients PDC is already failing; a glucose load floods the cell with pyruvate that cannot be processed, so it backs up into lactate and can worsen or precipitate lactic acidosis. Hence thiamine before or with glucose. Acetyl-CoA is the product, not a primer. Niacin/NAD+ deficiency is not the limiting factor here. Lipoic acid is synthesized endogenously and is not the deficiency at issue."
  - stem: "At which point in the PDC mechanism is NADH actually produced?"
    choices:
      - "When the hydroxyethyl group is oxidized and transferred to lipoamide on E1"
      - "When E2 transfers the acetyl group to coenzyme A"
      - "When E3 passes electrons from FADH2 to NAD+"
      - "When E1 decarboxylates pyruvate and releases CO2"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "NADH is generated only at the final E3 step, where electrons held first on lipoamide and then on E3's bound FAD (as FADH2) are finally transferred to NAD+. The E1 decarboxylation releases CO2 but makes no NADH; the E1 oxidation/transfer reduces lipoamide, not NAD+ (a classic trap); the E2 step makes acetyl-CoA. Only the electron handoff to NAD+ at E3 yields NADH."
  - stem: "Why must gluconeogenesis bypass the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex rather than simply running it in reverse?"
    choices:
      - "PDC is located in the cytosol, where gluconeogenesis cannot reach it"
      - "PDC requires oxygen, which is absent during fasting"
      - "Acetyl-CoA is too small a molecule to re-enter the pathway"
      - "The PDC reaction is essentially irreversible in animals because of its large negative free-energy change"
    answer: 3
    rationale: "The oxidative decarboxylation has a large negative ΔG and no enzyme exists in animals to reverse it, so acetyl-CoA cannot be converted back to pyruvate. Gluconeogenesis therefore starts from pyruvate or other three-carbon precursors, never acetyl-CoA, and uses pyruvate carboxylase and PEPCK to get from pyruvate back to PEP. PDC is in the mitochondrial matrix, not the cytosol, and the irreversibility, not localization or molecule size, is the reason. PDC does not directly consume O2; it depends on aerobic conditions only because the ETC regenerates NAD+."
  - stem: "Which part of the PDC reaction supplies most of its free energy, paying for both the NADH and the thioester of acetyl-CoA?"
    choices:
      - "The loss of CO2 from pyruvate's carboxyl carbon"
      - "Hydrolysis of ATP bound to E1"
      - "The oxidation of the two-carbon unit from aldehyde level to acid level, with the electrons ending on NAD+"
      - "Formation of the thioester bond, which releases energy as it is made"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "The oxidation releases about 50 kJ/mol. Storing the acetyl group as a thioester instead of free acetate spends about 31 of it, and what is left (about 19), together with about 14 from the CO2 loss, gives the overall drop of about 33 kJ/mol. So the CO2 loss contributes the smaller share and pays for neither the NADH nor the thioester. PDC uses no ATP. Making the thioester costs energy rather than releasing it; energy is released when acetyl-CoA is later hydrolyzed."
  - stem: "During vigorous muscle contraction, intracellular Ca2+ rises. How does this affect PDC activity?"
    choices:
      - "Ca2+ activates PDK, phosphorylating E1 to turn PDC off"
      - "Ca2+ activates PDP, which dephosphorylates E1 and turns PDC on"
      - "Ca2+ allosterically binds acetyl-CoA, blocking product inhibition"
      - "Ca2+ inhibits PDP, so E1 stays phosphorylated and PDC turns off"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "Calcium activates pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatase (PDP), which removes the inhibitory phosphates from E1's regulatory serines, switching PDC on. This couples the calcium signal that triggers contraction to a surge of acetyl-CoA for ATP production. PDK is the kinase that turns PDC off (phosphorylation inactivates), and Ca2+ acts on PDP, not PDK. Ca2+ does not bind acetyl-CoA."

  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: "Glycolysis turns glucose into pyruvate in the cytoplasm. To get more energy out, pyruvate moves into the mitochondrion, where the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC) converts it to acetyl-CoA. In the process one carbon leaves as CO2 and the cell makes one NADH. The reaction is one-way (irreversible), so acetyl-CoA can't be turned back into glucose. PDC is a giant machine made of three enzymes and five helper molecules (coenzymes), four of which come from vitamins you eat. A swinging chemical arm hands the carbon from one part of the machine to the next without dropping it. The cell turns PDC up when it needs energy (low ATP) and down when it has plenty (high ATP, NADH, acetyl-CoA)."
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: "PDC catalyzes the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate in the mitochondrial matrix: pyruvate + CoA + NAD+ -> acetyl-CoA + CO2 + NADH + H+, with a large negative ΔG that makes it irreversible (hence gluconeogenesis must bypass it). It is a ~10-MDa complex of three enzymes (E1, E2, E3) and five coenzymes (TPP, lipoamide, CoA, FAD, NAD+). E1 (TPP/Mg2+) decarboxylates pyruvate, the step where the carbon is lost for good (the free-energy drop comes mainly from the oxidation that follows, with the CO2 loss contributing the rest); the hydroxyethyl-TPP is oxidized and transferred to lipoamide; E2 transfers the acetyl to CoA forming the energy-rich thioester acetyl-CoA; E3 reoxidizes the dithiol lipoamide, passing its electrons to bound FAD (forming FADH2) and then to NAD+ (yielding NADH). NADH is produced only at E3. The lipoamide swinging arm enables substrate channeling. Regulation is two-tiered: direct product inhibition by NADH and acetyl-CoA, plus covalent control where PDK (stimulated by a high ATP/ADP ratio, NADH and acetyl-CoA, inhibited by ADP and pyruvate) phosphorylates E1 (off) and Ca2+-activated PDP dephosphorylates it (on). Phosphorylation here inactivates."
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: "PDC is a paradigm of metabolic channeling and integrated control. The thiazolium C2 of TPP, a stabilized ylide, performs nucleophilic addition to pyruvate's 2-oxo carbon; decarboxylation is driven by the thiazolium ring acting as an electron sink, yielding the enamine/hydroxyethylidene-TPP intermediate, which is then oxidatively acylated onto the distal lipoyl-lysine of E2's lipoyl domain. The lipoyl domain shuttles between E1, E2, and E3 active sites (a tethered, swinging-arm channeling that keeps reactive acyl and dithiol intermediates out of bulk solvent and maintains high effective local concentration). E2 acetyltransfer to CoA is a thioester-to-thioester exchange: acetyl-lipoamide and acetyl-CoA both have low resonance stabilization (poor 3p-2p overlap at sulfur), so the transfer is close to even and conserves the acetyl group's high transfer potential (~-31 kJ/mol for acetyl-CoA hydrolysis). E3 is a disulfide-oxidoreductase: dihydrolipoamide reduces an active-site cystine, electrons pass to FAD, then to NAD+, the sole NAD-reducing event; the bound FADH2 is an obligatory internal relay, never a free product. Flux is governed at E1 by reversible Ser phosphorylation (PDK/PDP), nested within direct product inhibition (NADH/CoA and acetyl-CoA/CoA ratios). PDK is activated by high NADH/NAD+, acetyl-CoA/CoA, and ATP/ADP ratios and inhibited by pyruvate; PDP1 is Ca2+- and Mg2+-dependent, coupling excitation-contraction to oxidative fuel selection. The Randle cycle, dichloroacetate (PDK inhibitor) pharmacology, and HIF-1α-driven PDK1 induction in the Warburg phenotype are all readouts of this node."

  - title: "Why a thioester is a wound spring"
    text: "Reach for two images: a wound spring versus a relaxed one, and a boulder perched on a cliff versus one sitting in a valley. The chemistry behind the picture is real. Sulfur is bigger than oxygen, so its orbitals overlap the carbonyl carbon poorly and cannot stabilize the thioester by resonance the way an oxygen ester's lone pairs do. The thioester is left poorly stabilized, while its hydrolysis products, acetate and CoA-SH, are much more stable, which is why hydrolyzing it releases roughly as much energy as splitting ATP. And something has to wind the spring: in PDC it is the oxidation, which spends most of its free energy building the thioester instead of free acetate."
    source: "CH18 lecture notes — 'wound spring vs relaxed spring' and 'boulder on a cliff' for thioester energetics."

**Eat more sugar than you burn and your body stores the surplus as fat. Burn that fat later, and almost none of it can come back as sugar. Why does the road run only one way?**

The answer starts with one reaction. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC) turns pyruvate, the three-carbon end of glycolysis, into acetyl-CoA, releasing a carbon as CO₂ and banking one NADH. Picture an airport: glycolysis in the cytosol is Terminal 1, the citric acid cycle in the mitochondrial matrix is Terminal 2, and PDC is a security gate between them that opens one way. Fat breaks down mostly to acetyl-CoA, which is already on the far side of that gate.

The net reaction is worth memorizing exactly:

> **Pyruvate + CoA + NAD⁺ → Acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH + H⁺**

It is an **oxidative decarboxylation**: pyruvate (3 carbons) loses its carboxyl carbon as CO₂ and is oxidized to a 2-carbon acetyl group carried by coenzyme A. The two halves of that name happen at different moments. It runs in the mitochondrial matrix, which pyruvate enters through the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Follow two things through the machine: the carbons and the electrons. Pyruvate's carboxyl carbon leaves as CO₂ at E1 *before* any oxidation happens. The two acetyl carbons that stay and the two electrons removed from them then ride the same swinging arm, and they get off at different stops: the carbons at E2 (onto CoA), the electrons at E3 (through FAD to NAD⁺).

This two-track picture explains the net reaction, the product brakes and the poisons. The phosphorylation switch at E1 is a separate layer on top.

## Following one pyruvate: two tracks on one arm

Keep the two tallies, **carbons** and **electrons**, as you go. **Oxidation** just means losing electrons; for a carbon, that shows up as trading a bond to hydrogen for a bond to oxygen (or, here, sulfur). Chemists rank a carbon's oxidation level by those bonds: alcohol, then aldehyde, then acid, each with one more.

**TPP, the grabber on E1.** Thiamine pyrophosphate (made from vitamin B1) has an unusual carbon between a nitrogen and a sulfur in a small ring. It can drop its hydrogen, become negatively charged and grab pyruvate's middle carbon. The ring then soaks up electrons, which lets the carboxyl group break away as CO₂.

**Lipoamide, the arm.** Lipoic acid is a short chain ending in a ring that contains two sulfur atoms bonded to each other (an S–S bond, a disulfide). Bolted to a lysine on E2 (by an amide bond), it becomes lipoamide, riding on a floppy lipoyl domain that jiggles at random among the three enzymes, all within reach. Give the S–S two electrons (with two protons) and it breaks into two –SH groups. So the arm can carry two things at once: an acetyl group on one sulfur, and two electrons in the broken S–S.

**Predict.** Step 1 removes pyruvate's carboxyl carbon as CO₂. Does that step remove any electrons from the molecule?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. CO₂ leaves its bonding electron pair behind (TPP's ring soaks it up), so no electrons leave the molecule overall, and the two carbons left behind sit at the oxidation level of an aldehyde (acetaldehyde, in effect). Yeast's pyruvate decarboxylase uses the same TPP chemistry but releases acetaldehyde for fermentation, with no NAD⁺ involved. The carbon is lost for good (it is gone as gas), but the oxidation, the main energy source, is still to come.

</details>

Now the walk. Three steps build the product, and one more resets the machine.

| **4 · Regeneration** | E3, with FAD, then NAD⁺ | — | The arm swings to E3. The electrons pass through E3's bound FAD (FADH₂ for an instant) to NAD⁺, making **NADH**. The S–S is restored. **The electron track is finished.** |

**Predict.** The oxidation happens at E1, in step 2. So why is the NADH not made there?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Because E1 has no NAD⁺ site. The electrons it removes go onto the nearest acceptor, the arm's disulfide, and they ride the arm until it reaches E3, the only enzyme that can hand them to NAD⁺. That is the exam trap in one line: **the oxidation is at E1, but the NADH is made at E3.** The FADH₂ along the way never leaves E3, which is why the net reaction lists NADH, not FADH₂.

</details>

Read down each column and the net reaction writes itself: three carbons in, one CO₂ and one acetyl-CoA out; two electrons out of the carbons, into one NADH. Step 4 makes no product carbon, but it has to happen: an arm still holding electrons cannot accept the next acetyl group.

Lipoic acid is the exception; the body makes it, so it is not a true vitamin.

No step uses molecular O₂. PDC needs oxygen only indirectly: the electron transport chain must turn NADH back into NAD⁺, or step 4 runs out of acceptor and the relay stops.

### One machine, so nothing floats free

PDC is one of the largest enzyme assemblies in the cell, larger than a ribosome. E2 forms the core, and E1 and E3 dock onto it, close enough for the arm to reach every active site. So the intermediates never diffuse into solution, where they could drift away or be lost to side reactions. That is **substrate channeling**.

## Where the energy goes

In animals this reaction is **irreversible**: no enzyme runs it backward, and acetyl-CoA cannot become pyruvate again. **The oxidation is the main energy source: it pays for the NADH and for the thioester, and what is left over, together with the loss of CO₂, makes the gate one-way.** To see it, keep the free-energy books (standard values, pH 7).

| Piece of the reaction | ΔG°′ (kJ/mol) |
| --- | --- |
| The oxidation: the two carbons go from aldehyde level to acid level, and the electrons end on NAD⁺ | about −50 |
| Storing the acetyl group as a thioester, instead of letting it go as free acetate | about +31 (a cost) |
| Losing CO₂ (found by difference, so approximate) | about −14 |
| **Overall** | **about −33** |

Building the thioester spends most of the oxidation's drop, about 31 of the 50. What is left (about −19), plus about −14 from the CO₂ loss, gives about −33 kJ/mol: an equilibrium constant (K = e^(−ΔG°′/RT)) of about 4 × 10⁵ at body temperature, so far toward the products that in practice the reaction runs only forward.

The +31 line is the same number you get back when you hydrolyze acetyl-CoA: about −31 kJ/mol, roughly ATP's −30.5. Hydrolyzing an ordinary oxygen ester gives only about −21. This is not energy stored in the bond; breaking a bond always costs energy. Hydrolysis releases energy because the products, acetate and CoA-SH, are much more stable than the thioester, which gets little resonance stabilization: sulfur is larger than oxygen, so its orbitals overlap the carbonyl carbon poorly, whereas an oxygen ester's lone pairs stabilize its C=O. CoA is the handle, not the valuable part. So acetyl-CoA is a wound spring, and the oxidation wound it. That stored potential lets acetyl-CoA drive the first step of the citric acid cycle and seed fatty-acid synthesis. Acetyl-CoA has two main fates: complete oxidation for ATP, or storage as fat. The liver can also turn it into ketone bodies, and it is the starting point for cholesterol.

Gluconeogenesis must start on the near side: from pyruvate (made from lactate or amino acids such as alanine), with pyruvate carboxylase and PEPCK carrying it back to PEP, or from other entry points. That answers the opening puzzle. Two parts of a fat are the exceptions: its glycerol, and the propionyl-CoA made at the end of odd-chain fatty-acid breakdown.

</details>

## Two layers of regulation

A gate this important is heavily controlled, and PDC reads the cell's **energy charge** (how much ATP it has relative to ADP and AMP) with the same logic as PFK-1 from the glycolysis chapter. The first layer is **product inhibition**.

**Predict.** Acetyl-CoA and NADH, the two products, both slow the complex, each by competing with the substrate it resembles. Which enzyme does each one slow?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Acetyl-CoA competes with CoA at E2, backing up the carbon track. NADH competes with NAD⁺ at E3, backing up the electron track. Either way the relay slows: "we have plenty, slow down."

</details>

The second layer is **covalent**, and it acts at E1. A dedicated kinase, **PDK** (pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase), phosphorylates a serine on E1 and switches the complex OFF. A phosphatase, **PDP**, removes that phosphate and switches it back ON. Both are bound to the complex itself. Note the direction: here phosphorylation **inactivates**, the same convention as glycogen synthase. Much of the energy-charge sensing at this gate runs through PDK: ATP is its substrate and ADP competes with it, so PDK reads the ATP/ADP ratio.

| The cell signals | What happens | E1 | The gate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plenty: high ATP/ADP ratio, high NADH, high acetyl-CoA | PDK is stimulated | phosphorylated | closed |
| Need: ADP and pyruvate; in working muscle, Ca²⁺ | ADP and pyruvate inhibit PDK, so PDP wins; Ca²⁺ activates PDP | dephosphorylated | open |

The plenty signals reinforce the product brake.

The cleanest illustration is muscle. A contracting muscle fills with **Ca²⁺**; some is taken up into the mitochondrial matrix, where it switches on the phosphatase (the PDP1 isoform). PDC switches on, and acetyl-CoA surges into the citric acid cycle exactly when the muscle needs ATP. The calcium that triggers contraction also unlocks the fuel.

## When the gate fails

Each failure below breaks one of the two tracks, and a stall anywhere stops the relay.

**Beriberi** is B1 deficiency: no thiamine, no TPP, so the carbon track stops before it starts. Pyruvate and lactate pile up, and the nervous and cardiovascular systems suffer. TPP-dependent α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and transketolase fail too, and in the brain they may matter as much as PDC. This is why a malnourished or alcohol-dependent patient should get thiamine before or with a glucose drip: in severe deficiency the sugar load can worsen lactic acidosis, and it has been reported to precipitate Wernicke's encephalopathy. (Glucose for dangerously low blood sugar is not delayed to wait for thiamine.) Thiamine deficiency is also one part of refeeding syndrome, which is defined by falling blood phosphate, potassium and magnesium after feeding restarts.

**Predict.** Arsenite grips the arm's two –SH groups, the state the arm is in after step 3. Which track stops, and why does the other stop too?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The electron track stops: the locked arm cannot hand its electrons to E3, so no NADH is made and the S–S is never restored. Then the carbon track stops too, because an arm that cannot be reset cannot accept the next acetyl group. The whole complex freezes. Arsenite binds closely spaced pairs of thiols; in lipoamide these are the sulfurs on carbons 6 and 8.

</details>

Mercury (Hg²⁺) also binds thiols such as lipoamide's, though its toxicity involves many other targets.

**E3 deficiency** stops the electron track in at least three complexes at once, because PDC, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and the branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase share one E3 (it also serves the glycine cleavage system). Patients show lactic acidosis, often with raised branched-chain amino acids and α-ketoglutarate.

The **Warburg effect** in cancer is partly PDC regulation hijacked: in many tumors HIF-1α (a low-oxygen transcription factor that many tumors keep switched on) drives up PDK, E1 is held largely phosphorylated and off, pyruvate is shunted to lactate even when oxygen is abundant, and the tumor gets fast ATP plus glycolytic intermediates for biosynthesis. Other tumors keep substantial PDC activity and mitochondrial oxidation.

## Going deeper

### The energy ledger in numbers

The −50 kJ/mol comes from the reduction potentials of the acetate/acetaldehyde couple (−0.581 V) and NAD⁺ (−0.320 V). The +31.4 thioester cost is 62% of that. The overall −33.4 kJ/mol agrees, within a few kJ, with a cross-check from the acetyl-CoA + CO₂/pyruvate couple (E°′ about −0.48 to −0.50 V, giving −31 to −35 kJ/mol). The −14 for losing CO₂ is found by difference and mixes tables, so it is approximate; the conclusion that the drop comes mainly from the oxidation, with the CO₂ loss contributing the rest, does not hang on it.

### Size and docking of the complex

PDC is roughly 10 megadaltons in eukaryotes, against about 4 for a eukaryotic ribosome. E2 forms a core of 60 subunits arranged as a pentagonal dodecahedron; in mammals some of those positions are taken by a related protein, E3BP. Some bacteria, such as *E. coli*, build a 24-subunit cube instead, which is the film's "24 or 60." E1 docks onto subunit-binding domains of E2, and E3 onto those of E3BP (in bacteria, E2 binds both). The lipoamide arm, carried on a mobile lipoyl domain, is about 14 Ångströms long.

### Hyperglycemia and superoxide

This concerns the electron transport chain, not PDC. In hyperglycemia, one proposed model holds that excess fuel overloads the chain so that more electrons leak out as superoxide. Oxidative stress is thought to contribute to diabetic neuropathy, but its sources are still debated and other pathways are involved too.
