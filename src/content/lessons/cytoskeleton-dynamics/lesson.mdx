      With Marc Kirschner, inferred from the behaviour of microtubule populations in vitro that
      growing and shrinking microtubules coexist and interconvert, and named the behaviour
      dynamic instability (Nature, 1984).
    profileSourceAnchor: "Mitchison & Kirschner, Nature 312:237–242 (1984); Mitchison Lab biography, Harvard Medical School."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Mitchison did his doctoral work at the University of California, San Francisco, and his
      laboratory's biography names Marc Kirschner as his mentor there. The question they took on
      was an old puzzle about microtubules. If tubulin and microtubules simply reached a balance,
      like a crystal and its solution, every microtubule in a test tube should grow or shrink
      together and then sit still. Their measurements on populations of microtubules assembled
      in vitro did not fit that picture. How the number and lengths of microtubules changed over
      time fitted better with two populations side by side, one growing and one shrinking, that
      switched between states rather infrequently. The 1984 paper in Nature called this dynamic
      instability and linked it to the hydrolysis of GTP bound to tubulin. The authors inferred
      the behaviour from populations; they did not watch single microtubules switch. That was
      done in the following years with light microscopy. The idea gave cell biologists a way to
      think about how a cell could explore its interior with microtubules and keep only the
      ones that find a target.

      matures gradually rather than a sharp boundary, but the core claim, that hydrolysis after
      assembly makes the polymer unstable, set the terms for decades of experiments.

techniques:
  - name: "Seeded elongation with electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Seeds whose ends can be told apart, such as Limulus acrosomal bundles for actin or axoneme
      fragments for tubulin, are mixed with purified subunits for a set time, then fixed and
      measured. Plotting growth at each end against subunit concentration gives kon from the
      slope and koff from the intercept.
  - name: "Video-enhanced light microscopy of single microtubules"
    blurb: >-
      Differential interference contrast or dark-field optics with electronic contrast
      enhancement make single microtubules visible in a live preparation. Measuring the length of
      one end in every frame gives a length-versus-time trace, from which growth and shortening
      velocities and catastrophe and rescue frequencies are read.
  - name: "TIRF microscopy of single actin filaments"
    blurb: >-
      Fluorescent actin filaments are tethered to a coverslip and lit only within about a hundred
      nanometres of the glass. Time-lapse images show each end growing or shrinking relative to
      fixed attachment points, which separates barbed-end and pointed-end behaviour on one
      filament.
  - name: "Photobleaching, photoactivation and speckle marking"
    blurb: >-
      A laser bleaches or activates fluorescence in one region of a labelled network, or sparse
      labelling creates random fluorescent speckles. Following the mark shows whether the polymer
      moves, turns over in place or flows from one end to the other, which total intensity alone
      cannot show.

    choices:
      - "Their dimers pair antiparallel in the tetramer, so both ends match"
      - "Their subunits bind no nucleotide, so the two ends cannot differ"
      - "They are capped at both ends by proteins in every cell type"
      - "Their thirteen protofilaments run in alternating directions"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Cytoplasmic intermediate-filament tetramers are built from two dimers aligned antiparallel
      and half staggered, so the building block already points both ways and the filament has no
      structural polarity. Lacking nucleotide is true but is not what removes polarity: a
      polymer of identical head-to-tail subunits would be polar with or without a nucleotide.
      Capping proteins describe how ends are regulated in cells, not the polymer's intrinsic
      structure. Thirteen protofilaments describe a microtubule, and its protofilaments all run
      the same way.
  - stem: "One end of a filament has kon = 10 per micromolar per second and koff = 2 per second. What is its critical concentration, and what does the end do when the free subunit concentration is 0.1 micromolar?"
    choices:
      - "5 micromolar; it shrinks by about 1.9 subunits per second"
      - "20 micromolar; it shrinks by about 1 subunit per second"
      - "0.2 micromolar; it shrinks by about 1 subunit per second"
      - "0.2 micromolar; it grows by about 1 subunit per second"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The critical concentration is koff/kon = 2/10 = 0.2 micromolar. At 0.1 micromolar the net
      rate is (10)(0.1) − 2 = −1 subunit per second, so the end shrinks. Getting 0.2 micromolar
      but saying it grows reverses the sign: below the critical concentration loss beats
      addition. A value of 5 micromolar comes from dividing kon by koff, which inverts the ratio.
      A value of 20 micromolar comes from multiplying the constants instead of dividing.
  - stem: "What does GTP hydrolysis by β-tubulin do for a microtubule?"
    choices:
      - "It releases α-tubulin so that the dimer can enter the lattice"
      - "It supplies the energy that pushes each new dimer onto the end"
      - "It weakens the lattice behind the end after the dimer has joined"
      - "It must happen before a free dimer can bind the growing end"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Dimers bind the end with GTP on β-tubulin, and hydrolysis follows in the lattice; GDP
      lattice is less stable and is held only while a cap of GTP-tubulin remains. Assembly above
      the critical concentration is driven by binding, not by hydrolysis, so the idea that
      hydrolysis pushes dimers on confuses a destabilizing step with a driving one. Hydrolysis
      before binding would give GDP-tubulin, which assembles poorly. α-Tubulin stays in the
      dimer; its GTP is not hydrolysed.
  - stem: "A microtubule plus end grows at 2 micrometres per minute for 4 minutes, then loses 6 micrometres in 15 seconds. What is the shortening velocity, and how does it compare with growth?"
    choices:
      - "24 micrometres per minute, 3 times the growth rate"
      - "24 micrometres per minute, 12 times the growth rate"
      - "6 micrometres per minute, 3 times the growth rate"
      - "0.4 micrometres per minute, one fifth of the growth rate"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Shortening is 6 micrometres in 15 s = 0.4 micrometres per second = 24 micrometres per
      minute, and 24/2 = 12 times the growth rate. Writing 0.4 per minute forgets to convert
      seconds to minutes. Saying 3 times compares 6 micrometres with 2 micrometres per minute,
      which mixes a distance with a rate. A value of 6 per minute treats the 15 s interval as one
      minute.
  - stem: Why does it help a cell that a microtubule end can switch by itself between growth and rapid shortening?
    choices:
    - It keeps every microtubule in the cell the same length
    - The collapse of GDP-tubulin releases the energy that moves organelles along the lattice
    - Ends can search the cell interior, the ones that reach a target can be kept, and the array can be rebuilt when the cell changes
    - It lets each microtubule reach a stable length set by the critical concentration at its two ends
    answer: 2
    rationale: Dynamic instability lets individual ends explore space and be kept or lost depending on what they find, so a cell can remodel its microtubule array and build structures such as the spindle. It does the opposite of making all microtubules alike, because each end switches on its own. Organelles are moved along the lattice by motor proteins, not by the collapse of GDP-tubulin. A steady length set by a critical concentration describes a balance, and the point of the switching is that ends do not settle at one.

    rationale: >-
      Because the filament is tethered, the lattice cannot slide; the barbed end advancing and
      the pointed end retreating by equal amounts at constant length is treadmilling, a steady
      flux of subunits through the polymer. Sliding is excluded by the tethers. Catastrophe is a
      microtubule term for abrupt switching, and the stem describes steady, matched movement.
      Free actin below both critical concentrations would make both ends shrink and the filament
      shorten.

The instability is not a defect. Ends that grow and collapse by themselves let a microtubule array search the cell interior, keep the ends that find a target, and rebuild quickly when the cell changes shape or divides; regulated actin turnover lets a cell build and take down protrusions. This lesson asks what the three filament systems are made of, what decides whether an end grows or shrinks, and why two of them spend nucleotide energy in a way that keeps them unstable.

## Three polymers built for different jobs

The cytoskeleton is the set of protein filaments in the cytosol, the fluid part of the cytoplasm outside organelles. It has three classes.

| | Actin filament | Microtubule | Intermediate filament |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Subunit | globular actin | α/β-tubulin heterodimer (a two-protein unit) | fibrous proteins (vimentin in fibroblasts, keratins in epithelial cells) |
| Width | about 7 nm | about 25 nm, a hollow tube of (usually) 13 protofilaments (rows) | about 10 nm |
| Two different ends? | yes: barbed and pointed | yes: plus (β-tubulin exposed) and minus | no |
| Nucleotide | ATP | GTP | none, and no enzyme activity |
| Bending | in between | stiffest | most flexible |

Actin's barbed and pointed ends are named after the arrowhead pattern seen when fragments of the motor protein myosin decorate a filament in the electron microscope. Actin subunits add head to tail, which is why the filament has two different ends. Tubulin dimers also stack head to tail, into rows called protofilaments, and usually (not always) 13 rows lie side by side to close the tube. Every dimer points the same way, so the tube has a plus end and a minus end. In vimentin and other cytoplasmic intermediate filaments, two rope-like coiled-coil dimers line up antiparallel (in opposite directions) and half staggered (offset) to form a tetramer. A building block that points both ways gives a filament with no polarity. These filaments are not permanent: they exchange subunits slowly, and cells take them apart by phosphorylation, the addition of phosphate tags, as with the nuclear lamins, fibrous proteins of the nuclear scaffold, at mitosis.

In comparisons of purified filaments, microtubules are the stiffest in bending, which suits them to serve as tracks and struts. Intermediate filaments are the most flexible in bending, yet a single filament can be stretched beyond two or three times its resting length without breaking. Think of a rope beside a fishing rod: the rope bends at a touch and is far harder to snap. Flexible is not weak; bending and stretching are different tests.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson**, the one the first film animates. Each filament end, in a given state, has a critical concentration, Cc = koff / kon. Hydrolysis after assembly gives a polymer a **tight** end (fresh nucleotide, low Cc) and a **loose** end (old nucleotide, high Cc). Between the two Ccs the polymer cannot come to rest with nothing moving. **Actin** separates the two kinds in *space* (treadmilling); **a microtubule end** switches between them over *time* (dynamic instability).

This rule is for single ends with purified proteins. It says *whether* a polymer can settle and *which way* each end moves, not the rates in a cell, where accessory proteins (profilin, formins, cofilin, capping protein, EB proteins and others) change them, some by an order of magnitude or more. It does not say *when* a capped end loses its cap. Intermediate filaments, with no nucleotide and one kind of end, are the contrast case: the rule predicts neither treadmilling nor instability.

## One end: the doorway and its critical concentration

Start with a measurement. Let tubes of pure actin at a range of total concentrations settle, then measure how much is in filaments and how much is free, and plot free actin against total actin.

- **Below a certain total**, nothing polymerizes, so free actin rises in step with total actin.
- **Above that total**, free actin stays flat and every extra subunit goes into polymer, one for one.

The flat level of free actin is the critical concentration, Cc, and it is also the total where the line bends. (For real ATP-actin, whose two ends differ, free actin settles between the two ends' values; next section.) Followed over time, one tube shows a lag, then growth, then a plateau. The lag is there because starting a filament is hard: a few subunits must come together before an end exists. So cells control *where* filaments start, with formins and the Arp2/3 complex for actin and γ-tubulin at the centrosome for microtubules. Rho-family GTPases decide where actin assembles ([that lesson](/lessons/integrins-and-rho-gtpases/)).

Why does free actin stop at one value? Treat a filament end as the film's doorway: subunits arrive at a rate proportional to the free subunit concentration C, and leave at a rate that does not depend on C.

## Two ends: why hydrolysis makes them differ

So far, one end. A filament has two, and they are built differently: the barbed end adds subunits much faster than the pointed end does (about nine times faster for ATP-actin). Does that give the two ends different critical concentrations?

**Predict.** Imagine actin that never hydrolysed its ATP. Could the barbed end have a lower critical concentration than the pointed end?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Adding one subunit at the barbed end, or one at the pointed end, gives exactly the same result: the same filament, one subunit longer. The energy change is the same, so the balance point, koff/kon, must be the same. The barbed end can be faster at *both* adding and losing, but the ratio cannot differ. Actin holding ADP, which cannot be hydrolysed further, shows this: its two ends have critical concentrations of about 1.9 and 1.7 µM, equal within measurement error.

</details>

So a difference between the ends needs a step that spends energy, and that step is hydrolysis. An actin subunit joins carrying ATP, hydrolyses it a little later, then releases the phosphate slowly, so the older lattice holds ADP-actin. ATP-actin binds a filament end more tightly than ADP-actin does, meaning it has a lower critical concentration. Hydrolysis by itself barely loosens a subunit; release of the phosphate does.

In the standard, simplified picture:

- The **barbed end** grows fast, so new ATP-actin keeps arriving before the subunits there have hydrolysed. Its fresh tip makes it a **tight** end, with a low critical concentration.
- The **pointed end** adds subunits slowly, and in the window where treadmilling happens it is losing them, so its tip exposes older subunits that have hydrolysed their ATP and released the phosphate. It is a **loose** end, with a higher critical concentration.

Measured with ATP-actin in solution, the critical concentrations are about 0.1 µM at the barbed end and about 0.6 µM at the pointed end (Pollard, 1986; constants in Going deeper). Put them on a line:

> 0 ─── **0.1 µM** (barbed Cc) ─── **0.6 µM** (pointed Cc) ───→ free ATP-actin

**Predict.** What does each end do with free actin at 0.05 µM, at 0.3 µM and at 1 µM?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

- At 0.05 µM, below both: both ends shrink.
- At 1 µM, above both: both ends grow, the barbed end about twenty times faster (+10.2 against +0.5 subunits per s).
- At 0.3 µM, between them: the barbed end grows and the pointed end shrinks.

So "the barbed end grows and the pointed end shrinks" is true only in the window between the two values.

</details>

Only the middle zone is new. Left alone, filaments use up free actin until the barbed end's gain matches the pointed end's loss. With Pollard's constants, that happens at about 0.17 µM, close to the barbed end's value rather than halfway, because the barbed end's ninefold larger kon gives its value more weight. There the filament keeps its length while subunits flow through it, as the first film shows: **treadmilling**. Single purified actin filaments watched by microscopy show barbed-end growth and pointed-end shortening at approximately equal rates at steady state.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/cytoskeleton-dynamics/treadmilling-flux.webp" width={1200} height={646} alt="AI-generated illustration: a ten-bead actin filament with a new bead joining at the barbed end and an older bead leaving the pointed end, a broad arrow showing subunits flowing front to back, and a second strip showing the same length a moment later." caption="Treadmilling: at steady state the barbed end gains subunits as fast as the pointed end loses them, so the filament keeps its length while subunits flow through it." />

Binding drives assembly; hydrolysis afterwards turns the steady state into a flux. Pure treadmilling is slow, a small fraction of a micrometre per minute, so cells that rebuild actin quickly add proteins that start, speed, cap and sever filaments (see the design problem).

## One end, two states: dynamic instability

A microtubule end switches between steady growth and rapid shortening. The switch to shortening is a **catastrophe**; the switch back is a **rescue**. This **dynamic instability**, a property of individual ends, is the rule's *time* version: one end alternates between a tight (capped) and a loose (uncapped) state. (Microtubules can treadmill too, in vitro and in some cells, so treadmilling is not limited to actin.)

The standard explanation is the **GTP cap** that the first film animates. The GTP sits on β-tubulin and is hydrolysed soon after the dimer joins, inside the lattice, so the end stays intact only while a cap of not-yet-hydrolysed dimers remains.

- **Capped, the end is tight.** Its critical concentration is about 5 µM in one purified preparation (the worked example in the box above), so at typical test concentrations it grows.
- **Uncapped, the end is loose.** It sheds dimers so fast that its critical concentration is, by a rough estimate, far above any tubulin concentration tested (Going deeper, "Who first watched the switching").

GDP-tubulin protofilaments are curved, which is why they peel outward once the cap is gone.

Because a cap can be lost by chance even at a steady growth rate, catastrophes are random events. Slowing hydrolysis lengthens the cap, as recombinant human tubulin mutants show (Roostalu and colleagues, 2020). Exactly what the cap looks like, and what final event commits an end to shortening, is still argued.

Mitchison and Kirschner inferred dynamic instability in 1984 from populations of microtubules; microscopy of single ends soon showed the switching directly, at both ends of purified microtubules (Going deeper). In cells, minus ends are often capped or anchored, for example at the centrosome.

**Predict.** A microtubule end has lost its cap and is shortening. Will doubling the free tubulin, say from 7 to 14 µM, slow the shortening?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Barely. An uncapped end's critical concentration is out of reach, so a bit more tubulin cannot turn loss into gain. A 1988 study of purified pig brain tubulin saw exactly this: rapid shortening did not change with tubulin concentration over 7 to 15.5 µM. The cap model also fits its second finding, that more tubulin made catastrophes rarer at both ends: a capped end grows faster with more tubulin and is thought to build a bigger cap that is harder to lose, though catastrophe also depends on the age of the end.

</details>

### A worked problem: reading one trace

The data below are made up for teaching. They describe one plus end grown from a seed in purified tubulin; length is measured from the seed.

- 0 s, 5.0 µm; steady growth to 180 s, 9.5 µm; catastrophe
- 192 s, 3.5 µm; rescue; steady growth to 312 s, 6.5 µm; catastrophe
- 325 s, 0 µm (back to the seed)

**Step 1, growth velocity.** (9.5 − 5.0) µm over 180 s (3 min) = 1.5 µm per min. The second growth phase gives (6.5 − 3.5) µm over 120 s (2 min) = 1.5 µm per min, the same.

**Step 2, shortening velocity.** 6.0 µm in 12 s = 0.5 µm per s = 30 µm per min, twenty times faster than growth. The second shortening phase, 6.5 µm in 13 s, gives the same 0.5 µm per s.

**Step 3, switching frequencies.** Each frequency is the number of switches divided by the time spent in the state they leave. The end spent 180 + 120 = 300 s growing and had 2 catastrophes: 2/300 = 0.0067 per s, × 60 s = 0.4 per min. It spent 12 + 13 = 25 s shortening and had 1 rescue: 1/25 = 0.04 per s.

**Step 4, dimers per second.** Assume 13 protofilaments and 8 nm per dimer, so one micrometre holds 1,000 nm ÷ 8 nm = 125 dimers per protofilament, and 125 × 13 = 1,625 dimers in all. Growth of 1.5 µm per min is 0.025 µm per s, times 1,625 = about 41 dimers per s.

Check against real data. The box's example, the 1988 study's plus-end constants at 10 µM tubulin, gives +45 dimers per s, about 1.7 µm per min by the same conversion. The study's rapid shortening, 733 dimers per s, is about 27 µm per min. The made-up trace is in the right range.

### Design problem

In a cultured fibroblast, the total actin fluorescence in the lamellipodium, the thin sheet at the leading edge, looks constant over 5 minutes. Design an experiment to decide whether the filaments there are static or treadmilling. First ask: why can't the total intensity decide?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

A treadmilling network and a static one can hold the same polymer. Mark part of it and follow the mark (see [microscopy and images](/lessons/microscopy-and-images/) and fluorescence recovery after photobleaching, or FRAP, in [membrane organization](/lessons/membrane-organization-topology/)).

1. Express fluorescent actin and either bleach a narrow stripe across the lamellipodium, photoactivate a small patch of photoactivatable actin, or label sparsely so the network shows random speckles.
2. Image every few seconds while the edge holds still. Treadmilling: the mark moves rearward and fades at the back while fresh fluorescence appears at the front. Static filaments: the mark stays put and does not recover. Exchange all along the filaments: the mark recovers in place without moving.
3. Predict that, with the edge stationary, the mark's rearward speed equals the speed at which new network is added at the front.

Controls: a fixed cell, where the mark should neither move nor recover; an unbleached region, to measure bleaching by imaging alone; and jasplakinolide, which stabilizes actin filaments and should slow the mark. With the edge still, a rearward-moving mark shows that the network flows back while new network is added in front; assembly pushing and myosin pulling both drive that flow. Fading of the mark, after correcting for bleaching, shows polymer being lost as it moves back. A lamellipodium is a branched network of short filaments rather than single long filaments, and in cells its fast turnover depends on accessory proteins such as ADF/cofilin, profilin and capping protein. Neither observation says which proteins drive either step.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Checking the rule against measured constants

Pollard (1986) measured elongation at each end of purified actin at 22 °C, using Limulus acrosomal seeds. Elongation at each end rose linearly with ATP-actin concentration up to 20 micromolar, and the slopes and intercepts gave separate rate constants for each end. For ATP-actin: barbed kon 11.6 per µM per s, koff 1.4 per s (Cc 0.12 µM); pointed 1.3 and 0.8 (Cc 0.62 µM). For ADP-actin: barbed 3.8 and 7.2 (Cc 1.9 µM); pointed 0.16 and 0.27 (Cc 1.7 µM). The ADP-actin pair checks the one-Cc argument. The steady-state concentration, (1.4 + 0.8)/(11.6 + 1.3) ≈ 0.17 µM, is an average of the two critical concentrations weighted by each end's kon. The flux there is about 0.6 subunits per second, under a tenth of a micrometre per minute, likely an upper estimate, since real pointed ends carry older, ADP-containing subunits. (These constants are as tabulated in Pollard and Borisy, 2003.)

### Who first watched the switching, and in what preparation

Mitchison and Kirschner proposed dynamic instability in 1984 from measurements on populations of microtubules in vitro: the way numbers and lengths changed over time fitted growing and shrinking microtubules that coexist and interconvert rather infrequently. Single microtubules were watched switching in real time soon after, by dark-field microscopy (Horio and Hotani, 1986) and by video light microscopy in Ted Salmon's laboratory (Walker and colleagues, 1988). The Walker study used pig brain tubulin free of associated proteins, grown from axoneme fragments at 37 °C, and found both ends dynamic; rapid shortening at the plus end ran at 733 dimers per second.

If an uncapped end accepted dimers no faster than a capped one (kon no more than 8.9 per µM per s), its Cc would be at least 733 / 8.9 ≈ 82 µM plus the tubulin concentration itself, a rough bound far above the 7 to 15.5 µM tested.

### How fast does the cap mature

In the 2020 mutant-tubulin study that lengthened the cap (Roostalu and colleagues), the binding of end-binding (EB) proteins, which recognize the cap region, suggested that the cap matures gradually, with a wild-type rate estimated near 0.2 per second. This is an estimate in one recombinant system.

### Drug effects that depend on the system

**Taxol.** With purified microtubule protein, Schiff and Horwitz found in 1981 that taxol-treated microtubules resisted disassembly on dilution and that taxol drove extra assembly after an apparent steady state. In HeLa cells, taxol did not raise microtubule polymer mass below 10 nM but did at higher doses, and submicromolar taxol suppressed both growing and shortening of purified brain microtubules (Jordan and colleagues, 1993).

**Cytochalasin B.** Brown and Spudich showed in 1981 that its binding follows the number of filament ends, by changing the number of ends on actin-coated beads while holding total actin constant. That experiment shows end binding; it does not by itself show which end. That the bound end is the barbed end came from other experiments.

**Nocodazole and latrunculin A.** In cultured newt lung epithelial cells, 4 to 400 nM nocodazole introduced a paused state, slowed both growth and shortening and made catastrophes more frequent (Vasquez and colleagues, 1997); in BSC-1 monkey kidney cells after an hour the effects were similar, except that catastrophes became less frequent. So the direction of one switching parameter depends on the system. Latrunculin A binds actin monomers with an apparent Kd of about 0.2 µM in pure actin. Single-filament imaging in 2018 found it binds ATP-actin far more tightly than ADP-actin (0.1 versus 4.7 µM) and also speeds disassembly at the ends of freshly assembled ATP-actin filaments and slowly severs them.
