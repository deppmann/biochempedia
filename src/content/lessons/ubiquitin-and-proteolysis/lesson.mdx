      the parts of a pathway without needing to guess in advance what they are.
  - name: "Northern blot"
    blurb: >-
      RNA from cells is separated by size on a gel, transferred to a membrane and probed with
      labeled DNA for one gene. Band intensity reports how much messenger RNA of that gene the cells
      contain. Comparing cells with and without VHL at 21 and 1 percent oxygen showed that VHL is
      needed for the oxygen dependence.
  - name: "Binding assay with a synthetic peptide"
    blurb: >-
      Chemists make a short peptide that carries exactly the modification they want, such as a
      hydroxyproline, and test whether a purified partner such as VHL binds it. With a defined
      peptide and no cell extract, the modification itself is shown to be the signal.
  - name: "X-ray crystallography"
    blurb: >-
      A crystal of a protein complex scatters X-rays, and the pattern gives the positions of the
      atoms. Crystals of TIR1 grown with auxin and a repressor peptide showed the hormone sitting
      between the two proteins.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "A pathway needs to tag one particular protein with ubiquitin and leave thousands of other proteins alone. Which step of the E1, E2 and E3 relay does most of the choosing?"
    choices:
      - "E1, because it uses ATP and so decides which proteins are worth the energy"
      - "E2, because it carries the ubiquitin on a cysteine and so picks the lysine to be modified"
      - "E3, because it binds the target's degron and the ubiquitin-loaded E2 together"
      - "The proteasome, because it reads each protein's sequence before ubiquitin is added"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The E3 ligase carries the specificity: it binds the loaded E2 and the target at once, and
      each of the hundreds of E3s reads its own set of degrons. The answer naming E1 is wrong
      because one E1 loads ubiquitin for nearly the whole system and never meets the target; ATP is
      spent when E1 activates each ubiquitin, whichever protein is chosen. The answer naming E2 is wrong because
      an E2 does not choose the target on its own, there are only a few dozen of them, and most are shared among
      many E3s. The answer naming the proteasome is wrong because it acts after tagging and
      recognizes the ubiquitin chain and does not choose which proteins get tagged.
  - stem: "In the usual case, how does ubiquitin end up joined to the protein it tags?"
    choices:
      - "By an isopeptide bond from the C-terminus of ubiquitin to a lysine of the target"
      - "By a thioester between the C-terminus of ubiquitin and a cysteine of the target"
      - "By a phosphoester between ubiquitin and the hydroxyl of a serine in the target"
      - "By a disulfide bond between a cysteine of ubiquitin and a cysteine of the target"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The isopeptide bond to a lysine side chain is the usual final linkage. The thioester answer
      describes the transient intermediate that ubiquitin forms on E1 and E2 and, for HECT ligases, on
      the E3, so it is a step on the way and not the finished tag. The phosphoester answer confuses
      ubiquitination with phosphorylation, which joins a phosphate to a hydroxyl. The disulfide answer
      is wrong because ubiquitin is attached through its C-terminal carboxyl, not through a cysteine
      side chain.

  - stem: "A protein carries a chain of ubiquitins, each joined to the next through its lysine 63. Which outcome is most likely?"
    choices:
      - "E1 removes the chain at once, because E1 is the enzyme that reverses the tagging reaction"
      - "The chain has no effect, because only a single ubiquitin attached to a protein can be read"
      - "Proteins with ubiquitin-binding domains read the chain as a scaffold or a trafficking signal"
      - "The proteasome destroys the protein, because every polyubiquitin chain is a destruction signal"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Lysine-63 chains are mainly read by proteins that carry ubiquitin-binding domains, which gather
      around the chain and so assemble complexes or direct traffic. The answer that the proteasome
      destroys the protein treats every chain as the lysine-48 kind; the classic proteasome signal is a
      lysine-48 chain of at least four ubiquitins. The answer that the chain has no effect is wrong
      because chains are read, and the type of link changes the reading. The answer involving E1 is
      wrong because E1 only activates ubiquitin; ubiquitin is removed by deubiquitinating enzymes.

  - stem: "A cultured cell has no working VHL protein but a normal HIF1A gene. It still makes a normal prolyl hydroxylase and is grown in air with 21 percent oxygen. What do you expect for HIF-1α in this cell?"
    choices:
      - "It is absent, because without VHL the cell can no longer make the protein at all"
      - "It is destroyed even faster, because nothing is left to protect it from the proteasome"
      - "It is not hydroxylated, because VHL is the enzyme that adds the hydroxyl group to the protein"
      - "It is hydroxylated but not ubiquitinated, so it accumulates and its target genes are on"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The prolyl hydroxylase still works in air, so HIF-1α is hydroxylated, but VHL is the part of the
      ligase that reads that mark, so without it no ubiquitin chain is built and HIF-1α builds up. The
      answer that VHL adds the hydroxyl confuses the substrate receptor of the ligase with the
      oxygen-using enzyme. The answer that HIF-1α is absent confuses a problem of destruction with a
      problem of synthesis, since VHL has no role in making the protein. The answer that destruction
      speeds up reverses the logic, because VHL is what brings HIF-1α to the proteasome.

  - stem: "Imagine an Arabidopsis plant in which TIR1 is the only auxin receptor of its kind, and the pocket of TIR1 that normally holds auxin is altered so that auxin no longer fits. TIR1 still joins the SCF complex and still binds an Aux/IAA repressor weakly. What do you expect when auxin is added to the plant?"
    choices:
      - "The repressor is destroyed anyway, because TIR1 still binds it and belongs to a ligase"
      - "The repressor stays and the genes stay off, because auxin cannot tighten TIR1's grip on it"
      - "A surface protein takes over the sensing job and destroys the repressor in place of TIR1"
      - "The genes switch on without any auxin, because TIR1 is now stuck in a permanently active form"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Auxin works as glue in the joint between TIR1 and the repressor. With the pocket altered the
      grip stays loose, the repressor is not ubiquitinated at a useful rate, and the genes stay off.
      The answer that the repressor is destroyed anyway ignores that the weak, unglued contact is the
      resting state in which the repressor is turned over only slowly. The answer that the genes switch on without auxin
      has the logic backwards, because genes come on only when the repressor is destroyed. The answer
      about a surface protein fails because surface sensing, through ABP1 and TMK kinases, drives fast responses and does not destroy Aux/IAA repressors; the 2005 papers
      and the crystal structure place the sensing in TIR1 itself.

The cells are then moved to low oxygen, which stops destruction completely while synthesis continues unchanged. About how many molecules does each cell hold at the steady state in air, and about how many 20 minutes after the move?"

      - "About 1,000 in air; about 2,000 after 20 minutes"
      - "About 1,000 in air; about 3,000 after 20 minutes"
      - "About 700 in air; about 2,700 after 20 minutes"
      - "About 1,000 in air; still about 1,000 after 20 minutes, because a steady state does not change"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The rate constant is 0.7 divided by 7 minutes, or 0.1 per minute, so the steady state is
      synthesis divided by that constant: 100 / 0.1 = 1,000 molecules. With destruction stopped, 100
      new molecules per minute for 20 minutes adds 2,000 to the existing 1,000, giving 3,000. The
      answer starting at 700 multiplies synthesis by the half-life instead of dividing by the rate
      constant. The answer ending at 2,000 counts only the molecules made after the move and
      forgets the pool already present. The answer that nothing changes treats the steady state as
      a fixed property of the protein, when it is a balance that shifts as soon as destruction stops.

The first film showed oxygen marking one protein, HIF-1α, for destruction. HIF-1α is a transcription factor, a protein that switches genes on, and it turns on the genes a cell needs when oxygen runs short. This lesson is about that one-way switch and how a signal writes it.

## One recognition step can change a protein's fate

**Ubiquitin** is a small protein, 76 amino acids long, attached to other proteins as a tag. The **proteasome** is a barrel-shaped protease that destroys proteins carrying an appropriate ubiquitin signal. A **degron** is the feature the tagging machinery recognizes on a target: a short sequence, a chemical change or both.

> **The one idea.** Signals change protein fate by changing what an E3 can recognize.
>
> *Scope:* this rule covers the regulated ubiquitin pathways in this lesson. A signal may complete a degron or strengthen an E3-target contact. The mark built afterward also helps determine the outcome.

Proteolysis makes the choice costly to reverse. A phosphate can be removed, and a ubiquitin mark can be cut off before degradation. Once a protein is cut into peptides, only a newly made copy replaces it. Destroying an inhibitor can free a pathway until new inhibitor is made; destroying an activator can end a signal until it is replaced. A cell leaves mitosis by destroying cyclin B, which keeps it there.

The earlier lesson on [protein turnover and amino acid catabolism](/lessons/protein-turnover-amino-acid-catabolism/) covers proteasome mechanics and ubiquitin's discovery.

## The bonds and branches behind the relay

The film showed the handoff; here are the bonds. **E1**, the activating enzyme, spends ATP to join ubiquitin's carboxyl end, glycine 76, to one of its own cysteines. That temporary **thioester** is an energy-rich sulfur-carbonyl bond. E1 passes ubiquitin to a cysteine on **E2**, the conjugating enzyme. **E3**, the ligase, completes the transfer, usually by an **isopeptide bond**: an amide between ubiquitin's C-terminus and a target lysine side chain rather than the backbone.

Human cells have two E1s, about forty E2s and roughly six hundred predicted E3s. One E1 supplies nearly the whole system, while each E3 recognizes a set of degrons. For the examples here, most target specificity sits in the E3 or its substrate receptor.

E3s have two main designs. A **RING-type** ligase holds loaded E2 beside the target, so ubiquitin passes directly across. A **HECT-type** ligase first takes ubiquitin onto its own cysteine. **SCF ligases** are RING-type: a cullin scaffold holds a small RING protein that docks E2, the adaptor Skp1 and an interchangeable F-box protein that binds the degron.

**Predict.** Mixed with ubiquitin-loaded E2, an E3 first carries ubiquitin as a short-lived thioester on one of its own cysteines before the target receives it. Is the E3 RING-type or HECT-type?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

HECT-type. A HECT ligase accepts ubiquitin from E2 onto its catalytic cysteine, then transfers it to the target. A RING ligase never holds ubiquitin itself; it promotes direct transfer from E2 to the target.

</details>

After the first ubiquitin, more can be added to a lysine on the preceding ubiquitin. Ubiquitin has seven lysines, and chains can also join through its first amino acid, methionine 1. Linkage, length and branching shape how a mark is read. A chain joined through lysine 48 and at least four ubiquitins long is a usual proteasome signal.

## Signals write recognizable degrons

Some regulated targets carry an incomplete degron that a signal completes.

**Phosphorylation can complete a docking site.** Some inhibitors have a short sequence containing two serines. The F-box protein β-TrCP binds only when both serines are phosphorylated. A signal-activated kinase adds the phosphates, and β-TrCP brings the inhibitor to an SCF for a lysine-48 chain and proteasomal destruction. IκB, inhibitor of NF-κB, is the classic case, followed in the NF-κB lesson. The Wnt lesson uses the same logic for β-catenin, except its signal stops phosphorylation. The reversible phosphate reads the signal; destruction commits that target molecule.

**Predict.** Both degron serines are replaced by alanines, but the kinase and proteasome still work. What step fails first?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Phosphorylation fails first: alanine has no hydroxyl, so the active kinase cannot add phosphates. Without them β-TrCP cannot recognize the degron, so the inhibitor never reaches the SCF.

</details>

**Oxygen-dependent hydroxylation completes another docking site.** Gregg Semenza found HIF-1 while studying erythropoietin, the kidney hormone that tells bone marrow to make red blood cells. Its subunit HIF-1α was made continuously and destroyed rapidly when oxygen was available, so its standing amount changed with oxygen.

VHL is mutated in an inherited cancer syndrome, and cells lacking it made oxygen-regulated messages at high levels in ordinary air. VHL, the substrate receptor of a cullin-RING ligase, plays the role an F-box protein plays in an SCF, so it had to be reading some degron on HIF-1α. The film's peptide test named it: a hydroxylated proline, recognized with no cell extract present.

**Prolyl hydroxylases**, or PHDs, add that hydroxyl. The reaction needs iron, 2-oxoglutarate and one molecule of oxygen. Oxygen is a substrate, used up like flour in bread; no separate oxygen receptor is required. Falling oxygen lowers how often PHDs finish the reaction, so VHL has less to grip and HIF-1α builds up while synthesis continues. It pairs with ARNT in the nucleus and switches on genes including VEGF, a growth factor that drives new blood vessels.

A close relative, HIF-2α, is destroyed through the same hydroxylation and VHL pathway. In the body it is the main factor switching on erythropoietin, and in kidney cancers lacking VHL it is the main tumour driver.

### Production minus destruction

How fast can HIF-1α rise once destruction stops? Let a protein amount, P, be made at constant rate s and destroyed with first-order rate constant k:

> **dP/dt = s − k × P; at steady state P = s/k; half-life = 0.69/k.**

A larger k gives a lower steady state and a shorter response time.

**Predict.** Synthesis stays fixed while k falls to half its original value. What happens to steady-state P and the half-life?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Both double: the steady state becomes s/(k/2) = 2s/k, and the half-life becomes 0.69/(k/2), twice 0.69/k.

</details>

## Ubiquitin marks remain editable

**Deubiquitinating enzymes** cut ubiquitin off and can trim lysine-48 chains.

**Predict.** A deubiquitinating enzyme removes a target's lysine-48 chain before the proteasome engages it. Can the same protein molecule still be rescued? What if proteolysis has begun?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Before engagement, yes: chain removal rescues that molecule from this destruction signal. Once proteolysis has cut the protein, removing ubiquitin cannot rebuild it; a replacement must be made.

</details>

**SUMO**, a small ubiquitin-like modifier of about 100 amino acids, is folded like ubiquitin and attached to lysines by its own E1, E2 and E3. It usually changes binding or location, though some SUMO chains are read by ubiquitin ligases that send the protein to the proteasome.

## Evidence that TIR1 is receptor and selector

The film called auxin a **molecular glue**, a small molecule that strengthens or creates a protein-protein contact. Auxin tells shoots which way to bend and roots where to grow. Botanists long sought its receptor at the cell surface, but the receptor behind its gene response is part of an SCF destruction machine.

Arabidopsis plants with a broken TIR1 gene respond poorly to auxin. TIR1 is an F-box protein, then thought to grab targets rather than sense hormones. In 2005 two laboratories independently showed that TIR1 binds auxin itself (see Going deeper). The relevant targets are Aux/IAA repressors, which hold auxin-responsive genes off.

A 2007 crystal structure from Mark Estelle's group with Ning Zheng confirmed the film's picture: auxin sits where TIR1 and an Aux/IAA repressor meet.

**Predict.** Auxin and TIR1 bind normally, but the proteasome is blocked. Will the repressor disappear and the genes switch on by this route?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Recognition and ubiquitination can occur, but the repressor remains if the proteasome cannot destroy it, so the genes stay off.

</details>

Drug makers use the same principle. Lenalidomide, a thalidomide relative used in multiple myeloma, binds cereblon, a cullin-RING substrate receptor. The drug reshapes cereblon's surface so it grips the transcription factors Ikaros and Aiolos, which it would otherwise ignore, and they are then ubiquitinated and destroyed. The drug was used clinically before this mechanism was known. **PROTACs** are two-headed molecules: one end binds a target, while the other binds a ligase such as VHL, using a small molecule built around the hydroxyproline VHL recognizes in HIF-1α.

### Design problem

A transcription factor must be nearly absent when oxygen is plentiful and enter the nucleus within minutes when oxygen falls. Design the control. Why destroy it continuously rather than make it on demand?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Make the factor continuously and destroy it through an oxygen-dependent tag. Give it a five-minute half-life in air. Then k is about 0.14 per minute and 1/k about seven minutes, so the steady pool equals about seven minutes of synthesis.

In this simple model, when oxygen falls, hydroxylation and destruction stop while synthesis continues. Each minute adds s to the existing s/k pool, about 14 percent of baseline. After 30 minutes the pool is about five times baseline, without waiting for new transcription.

Making it on demand would wait tens of minutes to hours for transcription, RNA processing and translation. Continuous destruction also makes the off-switch as fast as the on-switch: when oxygen returns, the excess above baseline halves every five minutes, and after 25 minutes only about 3 percent of the excess is left. The cost is ATP and amino acids spent on discarded protein.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How VHL was tied to oxygen

Peter Ratcliffe's group showed in 1999 that HIF-1α destruction depends on VHL and occurs efficiently when oxygen is available. William Kaelin reached VHL from the other direction, through the inherited cancer syndrome above.

### The degron and oxygen bookkeeping

In 2001 the two competing laboratories independently identified the hydroxylated degron. Proline 564 in human HIF-1α is one site; proline 402 is modified too. One oxygen atom enters the new hydroxyl. The other enters succinate when 2-oxoglutarate loses carbon dioxide.

### Where target choice really sits

E2-E3 pairing and the rest of the machinery can also influence ubiquitin transfer and chain architecture, so the E3 is the main selector in these pathways, not the only one.

### The auxin receptor papers

On 26 May 2005, Estelle's laboratory at Indiana University and, independently, Ottoline Leyser and Stefan Kepinski at the University of York reported in the same issue of *Nature* that TIR1 binds auxin itself.
