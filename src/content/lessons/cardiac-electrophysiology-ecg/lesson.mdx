scientists:
  - name: "Willem Einthoven"
    contribution: >-
      Built the string galvanometer, which recorded the heart's currents faithfully from the body
      surface, and set out leads I, II and III as the sides of a triangle from which the
      frontal-plane electrical axis can be calculated. He received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or
      Medicine for 1924.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Einthoven's Nobel lecture, The string galvanometer and the measurement of the action currents of the heart (1925), at NobelPrize.org; Barold, Cardiac Electrophysiology Review 7:99-104 (2003), on Einthoven's triangle and the electrical axis."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Einthoven, a physiologist at Leiden in the Netherlands, started from an instrument that was
      too sluggish. In 1887 Augustus Désiré Waller had recorded the human heart's electrical
      activity with a capillary electrometer, in which a mercury meniscus moves with voltage. The
      records were real but distorted by the lag of the mercury.
      Einthoven first analysed such records with mathematical corrections and then
      built an instrument that would not need them. In his string galvanometer a very fine
      conducting thread hung between the poles of a strong electromagnet; current through the
      thread deflected it, and its shadow was photographed on moving paper. Because the thread was
      so light, it followed the heart's fast changes. He then fixed where the electrodes go. Leads
      I, II and III join the right arm, left arm and left leg as the sides of a triangle, and the
      deflections in two leads give the direction of the heart's mean electrical axis in the
      frontal plane. That geometry is still how the frontal axis is read today.
  - name: "G. W. Beeler Jr. and Harald Reuter"
    contribution: >-
      Showed under voltage clamp in dog ventricular muscle that a slow inward current, left after
      the fast sodium current is inactivated, depends on external calcium and not on sodium, and
      concluded that calcium carries the current that sustains the plateau.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Beeler and Reuter, Membrane calcium current in ventricular myocardial fibres, Journal of Physiology 207:191-209 (1970); Beeler and Reuter, Reconstruction of the action potential of ventricular myocardial fibres, Journal of Physiology 268:177-210 (1977)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the 1960s the fast sodium current of heart muscle was known to inactivate within a few
      milliseconds, yet a ventricular action potential lasts hundreds. Something had to keep
      charge flowing inward. Harald Reuter had already reported, in Purkinje fibres in 1967,
      a slow inward current that depended on external calcium. With G. W. Beeler Jr. he turned to
      dog ventricular muscle and the voltage clamp. They held the membrane at a depolarised level
      long enough to inactivate the fast sodium system, then stepped the voltage and measured what
      remained. A slow inward current survived sodium-free solution and tetrodotoxin but rose and
      fell with external calcium, and its inactivation was slow enough to span a plateau. Their
      1970 paper concluded that calcium carries it. Seven years later the same pair used such
      measurements to build a mathematical model of the ventricular action potential. Models of
      that kind, now built from human cells, are how investigators test whether blocking one
      current lengthens the whole action potential.
  - name: "Otto F. Hutter and Wolfgang Trautwein"
    contribution: >-
      Recorded with intracellular microelectrodes from the beating frog heart while stimulating
      its nerves, and showed that the vagus hyperpolarises and flattens the pacemaker potential of
      sinus venosus fibres while sympathetic stimulation steepens it.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Hutter and Trautwein, Vagal and sympathetic effects on the pacemaker fibers in the sinus venosus of the heart, Journal of General Physiology 39:715-733 (1956), open access."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Otto Loewi had shown in the 1920s that the vagus slows the frog heart by releasing a
      chemical. In the 1950s Hutter and Trautwein, and at the same time José del Castillo and Bernard Katz, set out to see what vagal stimulation did to the voltage of a single pacemaker
      cell. Hutter and Trautwein did so with glass microelectrodes, then a young technique,
      pushed into fibres of the isolated, spontaneously beating frog heart. In the sinus venosus,
      the frog's pacemaker region, each fibre showed a slow depolarisation during diastole; in the
      auricle, the voltage between beats stayed flat. When they stimulated the vagus, the sinus
      fibres' ramp was suppressed and their diastolic potential moved more negative, by up to 23
      mV, while auricle fibres did not change. Sympathetic stimulation did the opposite to the
      ramp: it rose faster, with the threshold unchanged. Their 1956 paper put both autonomic
      effects on the pacemaker membrane itself. Which ions were responsible was a further question.
      Later work found a potassium channel that acetylcholine
      opens through a G protein.
  - name: "Dario DiFrancesco"
    contribution: >-
      With H. F. Brown and S. J. Noble described a current in rabbit sinoatrial tissue that is
      activated by hyperpolarisation and increased by adrenaline, later called the funny current,
      and was an author of the 2011 adult mouse Hcn4 knockout that tested its role.
    profileSourceAnchor: "DiFrancesco, The role of the funny current in pacemaker activity, Circulation Research 106:434-446 (2010); Baruscotti and colleagues, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 108:1705-1710 (2011)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In 1979 H. F. Brown, Dario DiFrancesco and S. J. Noble reported in Nature that a current in
      rabbit sinoatrial node tissue was increased by adrenaline, which suggested how sympathetic
      nerves speed the heart. The current behaved oddly. It switched on when the membrane was made
      more negative, the reverse of most voltage-gated currents, and it carried both sodium and
      potassium, so it flowed inward at diastolic voltages. It became known as the funny current,
      If. DiFrancesco, working in Milan, spent the following decades on its properties and its
      control by cyclic AMP. After the HCN channel genes were cloned in the late 1990s, HCN4 was
      identified as the main isoform in the sinoatrial node, and the question became testable in
      an animal. In 2011 Mirko Baruscotti, DiFrancesco and colleagues deleted Hcn4 in the hearts
      of adult mice. Heart rate fell by about half and AV block developed, but the rhythm did not stop at once, a result that keeps If central to rate
      control without making it the only pacemaker mechanism.

techniques:
  - name: "Voltage clamp of cardiac preparations"
    blurb: >-
      Hold the membrane at a chosen voltage with a feedback amplifier and record the current
      needed to keep it there. Changing the voltage protocol, the bath ions or a blocker such as
      tetrodotoxin separates one current from another, as Beeler and Reuter did in dog ventricle.
      In multicellular strips the voltage is not perfectly uniform, which limits precision.
  - name: "Intracellular microelectrode recording"
    blurb: >-
      Push a fine glass pipette filled with salt solution into a single cell and record its
      membrane potential through each beat. It shows the shape of the action potential, the
      maximal diastolic potential and the slope of the pacemaker ramp, and how nerve stimulation
      changes them.
  - name: "Electrocardiography"
    blurb: >-
      Record voltage differences between electrodes on the skin as excitation spreads through the
      heart, on paper moving at a standard speed. The tracing reflects voltage differences between
      groups of cells, so it moves only while excitation or recovery is spreading; its timing gives rate and conduction intervals, and the size and polarity of QRS across the limb leads give the mean electrical axis.
  - name: "Inducible tissue-specific knockout mice"
    blurb: >-
      Flank a gene with recombination sites and delete it only in one tissue, and only when a drug
      such as tamoxifen is given. This avoids embryonic death and tests what the gene does in the
      adult, as in the cardiac Hcn4 knockout.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "In a human ventricular myocyte, which current carries most of the inward charge that holds the plateau (phase 2)?"
    choices:
      - "The fast sodium current through Nav1.5"
      - "The L-type calcium current through Cav1.2"
      - "The funny current through HCN4 channels"
      - "The inward-rectifier potassium current IK1"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The plateau is a balance in which inward L-type calcium current opposes the outward IKr and
      IKs, and Beeler and Reuter's dog ventricular work showed that this slow inward current is
      carried by calcium. The fast sodium current makes the upstroke and inactivates within
      milliseconds, so picking it confuses phase 0 with phase 2. IK1, an inward-rectifier channel,
      carries outward potassium current near rest and holds the resting potential in phase 4. The funny current belongs to pacemaker
      cells and is activated by hyperpolarisation, not by the depolarised plateau.
  - stem: "Why can a mammalian ventricle not be driven into a sustained, fused contraction (tetanus) by rapid stimulation, as a skeletal muscle can?"
    choices:
      - "Gap junctions spread each stimulus to every cell at once"
      - "The sarcoplasmic reticulum empties completely each beat"
      - "The refractory period covers most of the twitch duration"
      - "Cardiac myosin cannot attach to actin twice in one beat"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      While the plateau holds the membrane depolarised, sodium channels stay inactivated, so the
      cell is refractory for nearly as long as its action potential, which spans most of the
      contraction; a new action potential cannot start until the twitch is largely over. Gap
      junctions do spread excitation quickly, but fast spread does not prevent summation. The
      sarcoplasmic reticulum releases only part of its calcium per beat, so complete emptying is
      wrong on the facts. Myosin heads cycle many times during one twitch; nothing limits them to
      one attachment.
  - stem: "Which single change in a sinoatrial node cell would shorten its cycle length, with everything else held constant?"
    choices:
      - "A smaller funny current during phase 4"
      - "A more negative maximal diastolic potential"
      - "A less negative threshold for the upstroke"
      - "A steeper phase 4 diastolic depolarisation"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Cycle length is the time the ramp takes to climb from the maximal diastolic potential to
      threshold, plus the action potential itself. A steeper ramp covers that distance sooner,
      which is how beta-adrenergic stimulation speeds the rate. A more negative maximal diastolic
      potential lengthens the climb, which is one of the vagal effects. A less negative threshold
      also lengthens the climb. A smaller funny current flattens the ramp, as muscarinic
      stimulation or loss of HCN4 does, and slows the rate.
  - stem: "On an ECG recorded at the standard 25 mm per s, consecutive R waves of a regular rhythm are 20 small (1 mm) squares apart. What is the heart rate?"
    choices:
      - "60 beats per min"
      - "15 beats per min"
      - "150 beats per min"
      - "75 beats per min"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Each small square is 0.04 s at 25 mm per s, so R-R = 20 x 0.04 = 0.80 s and the rate is 60 /
      0.80 = 75 beats per min (equivalently 1,500 / 20). Fifteen comes from dividing 300 by the
      count, a rule that applies to large squares, not small ones. Sixty assumes each small square
      is 0.05 s. A rate of 150 assumes a paper speed of 50 mm per s, which halves the time each
      square represents.
  - stem: "In a ventricular myocyte at 37 C with intracellular potassium of 140 mmol per L (Nernst slope 61.5 mV per tenfold change), extracellular potassium is raised from 4 to 7 mmol per L. What is the new potassium equilibrium potential?"
    choices:
      - "-80 mV"
      - "-110 mV"
      - "-95 mV"
      - "-75 mV"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      EK = 61.5 x log10(7/140) = 61.5 x (-1.30) = -80 mV, less negative than the -95 mV at 4 mmol
      per L, so the resting potential, which tracks EK, depolarises. A value of -110 mV moves in
      the wrong direction, as if more potassium outside pulled the membrane more negative. The
      value -95 mV is the starting value, unchanged. The value -75 mV uses a slope of about 58 mV
      per tenfold change, which applies near room temperature, not at 37 C.
  - stem: "Suppose every ventricular cell had the same action potential duration, so the cells excited first also repolarised first. Which way would the T wave in lead II point?"
    choices:
      - "The same way as the QRS complex"
      - "The opposite way to the QRS complex"
      - "Neither way, because repolarisation makes no deflection"
      - "It would merge into the QRS complex"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A repolarisation boundary is the depolarisation boundary turned round: its resting side is
      behind it. If it swept the same way as the QRS front, the electrode that saw the positive side
      of depolarisation would now see the negative side, so T would point opposite to the QRS. Saying
      T points the same way as the QRS describes the healthy ECG, which is the reason repolarisation
      must on balance run the other way. Repolarisation does make a deflection whenever a boundary
      travels, so no deflection is wrong. Equal durations of a few hundred milliseconds keep
      repolarisation well after depolarisation, so the T wave would not merge into the QRS.

      - "A longer plateau and a stronger contraction"
      - "A shorter plateau and a weaker contraction"
      - "A slower upstroke and a wider QRS complex"

    rationale: >-
      The data point to a calcium-carried current (calcium-sensitive, independent of sodium and
      tetrodotoxin): the L-type current that holds the plateau against outward potassium currents
      and triggers calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Blocking part of it lets the
      potassium currents repolarise the cell sooner and lets less calcium in, so the plateau
      shortens and contraction weakens. A slower upstroke and wider QRS would follow block of the
      fast sodium current, which the stem shows is not this current. A longer plateau with a

    choices:

      - "Release is unchanged, because the store holds enough calcium for a full release"
      - "Release stops completely, because it is all-or-none once the trigger falls"

    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Each cluster of ryanodine receptors responds mainly to the L-type channels next to it, so total

  - stem: "A tracing from a made-up patient at rest shows the PR interval lengthening beat by beat (0.16 s, 0.24 s, 0.28 s), then a P wave with no QRS complex after it, after which the PR interval returns to 0.16 s and the cycle repeats. Which description fits best?"
    choices:
      - "First-degree block, since every P wave conducts after a longer delay"
      - "Mobitz I (Wenckebach) block, a progressive delay at the AV node ending in one dropped beat"
      - "Mobitz II block, usually from block below the node, with a constant PR interval"
      - "Third-degree block, with a slower escape pacemaker driving the ventricles"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A PR that lengthens beat by beat until one P wave fails, then restarts, is Mobitz I
      (Wenckebach). Each atrial impulse reaches the AV node earlier in its recovery from the last
      one, so it is conducted more slowly, until one arrives while the node is still refractory and
      fails; the pause lets the node recover. It can be found in healthy endurance athletes and is
      usually attributed to high vagal tone. First-degree block conducts every P wave, so no beat
      would be dropped. Mobitz II keeps the PR constant before the dropped beat, unlike this
      lengthening PR. Third-degree block conducts no P wave at all, so atria and ventricles
      would beat independently.

**Once excited, a ventricular muscle cell stays depolarised for a few hundred milliseconds, roughly a hundred times as long as a nerve spike.** That plateau sets how much calcium enters, keeps beats from fusing, and shows on the skin as the stretch between the QRS complex and the T wave. This lesson goes further than [circulation and the heart](/lessons/circulation-and-heart/), from currents to the tracing.

Here is the puzzle that joins the cell to the tracing. Between the QRS complex and the T wave, nearly every ventricular cell sits on its plateau, depolarised well above its resting level. Yet the ECG at that moment is flat, as flat as it is between beats, when every cell is at rest. Why?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** The ECG never shows a cell's voltage, only differences between cells: it sees the edges of waves. When every ventricular cell is in the same phase, all at rest or all on the plateau, the trace is flat. It moves only while a boundary between excited and resting tissue travels through the heart: upward when depolarisation travels toward the positive electrode, or when repolarisation travels away from it. The more tissue the boundary spans, the bigger the deflection.

The rule is a simplification (a real ST segment is only nearly flat), and it says nothing about what sets the heart rate or holds the plateau. Those come from the cells' own currents, so we build the cells first.

## One ventricular cell: a long plateau

Picture the voltage of one ventricular cell through a single beat. The values are teaching values for a human or other large-mammal ventricle, from recent reviews; mouse and rat ventricular action potentials are much shorter, with little plateau.

- **Phase 4**, rest: flat near −90 mV, held close to the potassium equilibrium potential (E_K, said “E sub K”) by the inward-rectifier potassium current **IK1**.
- **Phase 0**, the upstroke: sodium channels (Nav1.5) open once the membrane is depolarised to threshold, and the voltage overshoots well above zero.
- **Phase 1**, a small notch, as sodium channels inactivate and a transient outward potassium current, **Ito**, flows.
- **Phase 2**, the plateau: the voltage hangs near zero for about 200 ms.
- **Phase 3**, repolarisation: the voltage falls back to rest.

To see what holds the plateau, borrow the rule from [the action potential](/lessons/action-potential/): with no current injected, the voltage moves toward a balance point, the average of the ions' equilibrium potentials weighted by their conductances, and it holds still where inward and outward currents cancel.

During phase 2 the main pull is between two sets of channels. The **L-type calcium current** (ICaL, through Cav1.2) flows in, helped by the sodium-calcium exchanger. The delayed-rectifier potassium currents **IKr** (the hERG channel) and **IKs** flow out. The currents are nearly equal, so the voltage changes only slowly, drifting down as the outward currents gain.

Why doesn't the large resting potassium conductance drag the cell straight back to −90 mV? Because IK1 is an **inward rectifier**: it passes current well near rest but largely shuts at plateau voltages. So during the plateau the total conductance is low, and small currents hold the voltage. That makes the plateau easy to stretch. Block one of the outward currents, as drugs that block hERG do, and the plateau lengthens, and with it the QT interval on the ECG.

**Predict.** Treat the plateau as just these two currents, at about +10 mV, with E_K at −95 mV. The L-type channel's current reverses at about +60 mV (below calcium's own equilibrium potential, partly because the channel also lets some potassium out). Hold them in balance at that instant: the inward calcium current equals the outward potassium current. Which has the larger conductance?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Calcium's. Current is conductance times driving force. Potassium sits 105 mV from its equilibrium potential (+10 minus −95); calcium sits only 50 mV below its reversal (+60 minus +10). To match a strong push with a weak one, calcium needs about twice the conductance (105 / 50 = 2.1). Check with the balance-point rule: (2.1 × 60 + 1 × (−95)) / 3.1 = +10 mV. So the plateau does not sit at the midpoint of −95 and +60 (about −18 mV); the larger calcium conductance pulls it up to +10.

</details>

In **phase 3** the L-type channels **inactivate**, the same word you met for sodium channels: they shut with time at depolarised voltages, and as calcium builds up inside. Calcium's pull fades, the potassium currents win, and the balance point slides down. As the voltage falls, IK1 conducts again and finishes repolarisation. Several potassium currents can repolarise the cell, so losing one to a gene variant or a drug is partly covered, a margin called the **repolarisation reserve**.

While the membrane is depolarised, sodium channels stay inactivated and the cell is **refractory**: a second stimulus cannot fire it. The refractory period lasts nearly as long as the action potential, and in heart muscle the contraction rises and falls within roughly those same few hundred milliseconds, so it covers most of the twitch. A skeletal muscle fibre, with its brief action potential, can be restimulated during a twitch and summed into tetanus ([skeletal muscle mechanics](/lessons/skeletal-muscle-mechanics/)). A ventricular cell cannot, so the ventricle refills between beats.

### The classic experiment: what carries the plateau?

The fast sodium current inactivates within milliseconds, so what keeps a ventricular cell depolarised for hundreds? One candidate was a slowly inactivating sodium current. G. W. Beeler Jr. and Harald Reuter tested it in dog ventricular muscle under voltage clamp (Journal of Physiology, 1970). A conditioning depolarisation first inactivated the fast sodium system; then they recorded the inward current left. A slow inward current remained.

**Predict.** If sodium carried that slow current, what would sodium-free solution do to it? If it ran through the fast sodium channel, what would tetrodotoxin, a fast sodium channel blocker, do? And should external calcium matter?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A sodium current should vanish in sodium-free solution, a current through the fast channel should be blocked by tetrodotoxin, and calcium should not matter. The real current did the opposite on all three. It persisted in sodium-free solution, was unaffected by tetrodotoxin and by magnesium, and was very sensitive to external calcium. It also inactivated slowly enough to span a plateau. They concluded that calcium carries it.

</details>

That ruled out the tetrodotoxin-sensitive sodium channel as the carrier. It did not show that the plateau is calcium current alone.

## Calcium in, more calcium out

The calcium that holds the plateau also starts the contraction. It enters through L-type channels at the **dyad**, where a T tubule meets the junctional sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR). There it raises calcium in a narrow space and opens the SR's **ryanodine receptors (RyR2)**, which release far more: **calcium-induced calcium release (CICR)**. The Fabiatos' skinned-cell evidence is in [muscle and movement](/lessons/muscle-and-movement/).

Positive feedback like that could run away. It does not, because release is under local control. Each cluster of RyR2 responds mainly to the L-type channels next to it, so total release is graded with the L-type current: a smaller trigger gives a smaller release, not none. Release at each site also shuts off before the SR empties, by mechanisms still being studied. The fraction of SR calcium released per beat varies with SR load and the trigger. Calcium then returns to the SR through the pump **SERCA** and leaves the cell through the **sodium-calcium exchanger (NCX)**, and the muscle relaxes.

{/* DIAGRAM: cardiac dyad with L-type channels, RyR2, SERCA and NCX */}

**Contractility**, the strength of contraction at a given filling, rises with sympathetic drive. Protein kinase A phosphorylates Rad, a protein that inhibits the L-type channel, relieving the inhibition so more calcium enters. It phosphorylates phospholamban, releasing its brake on SERCA so the SR holds more, and troponin I, which speeds relaxation. See [cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/) and, for the loop, [the cardiac cycle](/lessons/pressure-volume-loops/). Mutations in RyR2 itself cause an inherited arrhythmia, catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia: Priori and colleagues (2001) found them in four of twelve probands.

## Pacemaker cells have no resting potential

A sinoatrial (SA) node cell never rests. Picture its trace: after each action potential the voltage falls to a **maximal diastolic potential**, the most negative point of the cycle, then drifts upward in **phase 4 diastolic depolarisation** until it reaches threshold and fires. L-type calcium channels carry its slow upstroke, and there is no plateau.

Two clocks drive the drift. The best-known part of the membrane clock is the **funny current (If)**, carried mainly by HCN4 channels. It is activated by hyperpolarisation and carries both sodium and potassium, a net inward current at diastolic voltages. How far If is activated at the end of each action potential helps set the steepness of phase 4 and so the rate. The **calcium clock** is a series of local calcium releases from the SR in late diastole, which add inward current through the sodium-calcium exchanger. In the **coupled-clock model**, built mostly from rabbit cells, the two clocks entrain each other. Other currents also contribute, including a low-threshold L-type calcium current (Cav1.3) and the decay of potassium current left from the action potential. How much each contributes is still debated.

Autonomic nerves act on both clocks. Noradrenaline at beta-adrenergic receptors raises cyclic AMP. Cyclic AMP binds HCN4 directly and increases If, and through PKA it increases L-type calcium current and SR calcium cycling; both steepen phase 4. Acetylcholine at muscarinic M2 receptors lowers cyclic AMP and If. It also opens a G-protein-gated potassium channel. By the balance-point rule, more potassium conductance pulls the balance point toward E_K, so the maximal diastolic potential becomes more negative and the ramp has farther to climb ([GPCRs and G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/); reflex control is in [regulating arterial pressure](/lessons/blood-pressure-regulation/)).

A simple description of the timing shows why a steeper ramp means a faster heart: one beat is the ramp's climb to threshold plus the action potential itself.

In normal sinus rhythm, with every beat conducted, each firing of the SA node becomes one beat on an ECG, so the distance between beats on the paper, the R-R interval (or the P-P interval), is the cycle length.

Is the funny current the whole pacemaker? If it were, removing it should stop the heart. Mirko Baruscotti, Dario DiFrancesco and colleagues (PNAS, 2011) deleted Hcn4 in adult mouse hearts. In the intact mice, they report, heart rate fell by about half and AV block developed, but the rhythm did not stop at once (the mice died within about five days), and beta-adrenergic stimulation still sped the isolated pacemaker cells. So If is a major part of the membrane clock, and these data do not show that it is the whole pacemaker. Another adult knockout gave a different picture (Going deeper), and a mouse heart beats several times faster than a human's.

## The wave spreads, pauses at the AV node, then races

From the SA node, excitation crosses the atria to the **atrioventricular (AV) node**, normally the only electrical path into the ventricles. It pauses there, then runs quickly through the bundle of His, the bundle branches and the Purkinje fibres.

{/* DIAGRAM: human conduction system, SA node to Purkinje fibres */}

Cells of the AV node and the conducting system below it can also pace, at slower intrinsic rates. The SA node is fastest; in a standard textbook account, AV node cells fire about 40 to 60 per min and His-Purkinje cells about 15 to 40. The fastest site leads: its wave reaches each slower site and fires it before that site's own ramp gets to threshold. The resting sinus rate is usually below the node's intrinsic rate because vagal tone slows it.

## What the ECG records: cells that disagree

An electrode on the chest sits centimetres from the nearest heart cell. It cannot record any one cell's action potential, and it records electrical activity, not contraction. What reaches it is current flowing through the fluid around the cells, and that current flows only where neighbouring cells sit at different voltages.

Picture a strip of ventricular muscle with a wave of excitation halfway along it. Behind the wave the cells have fired and sit on their plateau. Ahead of it they are still at rest, near −90 mV. At the boundary, positive charge flows through gap junctions from the excited cells into the resting ones; that is the current that brings the next cells to threshold and moves the wave on. The circuit closes through the fluid outside the cells, where the resting side of the boundary is positive and the excited side negative. So the boundary acts like a small battery lying in the tissue, positive end forward, travelling with the wave.

That is the reason behind the one idea at the top of the lesson: the skin sees these travelling batteries, never the cells' voltages themselves. It also shows why "the ECG is all the action potentials added up" is wrong: that sum stays high right through the plateau, where the real trace is flat.

**Predict.** A lead's positive electrode is on the left leg, below the heart. During ventricular depolarisation the main front travels down and to the left, toward the apex. Does the QRS in this lead point up or down?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Up. The front is coming toward the electrode, so the electrode faces its positive, resting side. This lead is lead II, and in most healthy people its QRS is mostly upright.

</details>

Now read one beat, left to right.

- **Before the P wave**, every cell is at rest. Flat.
- **The P wave** is depolarisation spreading across the atria from the SA node, down and to the left. It is upright in lead II.
- **The PR segment**, between P and QRS, is nearly flat although the AV delay is happening. The atria are depolarised (their repolarisation is just beginning and is small), and the wave is crawling through the AV node and the bundle of His, structures too small for their boundary to register at the skin. So the **PR interval**, from the start of P to the start of QRS, contains the AV delay. In healthy adults at rest it typically runs 0.12 to 0.20 s.
- **The QRS complex** is depolarisation crossing the ventricles through the fast Purkinje network, in under 0.12 s in healthy adults. Its width is how long the crossing takes, so anything that slows ventricular conduction widens it. Atrial repolarisation happens now, hidden by the much larger QRS.
- **The ST segment** comes when nearly every ventricular cell is on its plateau. Flat, or nearly: the opening puzzle, answered.
- **The T wave** comes from phase 3 happening at different times in different regions: a broad, slow repolarisation boundary, set by when each region was excited and how long its action potential lasts.
- **The QT interval**, from the start of QRS to the end of T, runs from the first ventricular cell's phase 0 to the last cell's phase 3. So it tracks the ventricular action potential duration, and anything that lengthens the plateau lengthens QT.

**Predict.** Suppose every ventricular cell had the same action potential duration. Then the cells excited first would also finish first, and repolarisation would sweep the same way depolarisation did. Would the T wave point the same way as the QRS, or the opposite way?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The opposite way. A repolarisation boundary is the depolarisation battery turned round: its resting side is now behind it. Travelling the same way as the QRS front, it would show the electrode its negative side. Yet in most leads of a healthy ECG the T wave points the same way as the QRS. So, on balance, repolarisation must run the opposite way to depolarisation: the regions excited last finish first, because their action potentials are shorter. The textbook account puts that gradient across the wall, with the outer layer (the epicardium) finishing first. Mapping studies find the gradient across the wall small and larger ones along other axes, so which gradients matter most is still debated (numbers in Going deeper).

</details>

### Leads, and why two leads give a direction

{/* DIAGRAM: Einthoven's triangle, leads I, II and III with polarity */}

Willem Einthoven set out the first three leads as the sides of a triangle. Each of these three leads is one voltage difference between two electrodes. **Lead I** is left arm minus right arm, **lead II** is left leg minus right arm, and **lead III** is left leg minus left arm. Seen from the front, each lead looks along one side of the triangle toward its positive electrode. Because left leg minus right arm equals (left arm minus right arm) plus (left leg minus left arm), lead II always equals lead I plus lead III. This is **Einthoven's law**, a check you can run on any tracing.

A lead also cares about direction: a boundary travelling straight toward its positive end gives the largest upward deflection, one at right angles gives none, and in between the size scales with the cosine of the angle. In a real QRS the front's direction rotates; when its average direction is at right angles to a lead, the up and down parts cancel and the complex is called **isoelectric**.

Three more leads fill the gaps. The **augmented leads**, aVR, aVL and aVF, each compare one limb electrode with the average of the other two. ## AV block on the tracing

- **First-degree block:** every P wave conducts, after a longer delay.
- **Mobitz I** (Wenckebach): the PR lengthens beat by beat until one P wave has no QRS after it, then the cycle restarts. Each atrial impulse reaches the AV node earlier in the node's recovery from the last one, so it is conducted more slowly and the PR lengthens. Eventually an impulse arrives while the node is still refractory and fails. The pause lets the node recover. The same rate dependence shows in perfused rabbit hearts, where the delay where atrium meets node grew with rapid atrial pacing.
- **Mobitz II:** the PR stays constant and a P wave suddenly fails, usually from block below the node.
- **Third-degree block:** no P wave conducts, and a slower escape pacemaker, from the hierarchy of rates described earlier, drives the ventricles.

First-degree and Mobitz I block can be found in healthy endurance athletes and are usually attributed to high vagal tone.

So raising outside potassium from 4 to 7 mmol per L moves E_K from −95 to −80 mV, and the resting potential approximately with it.

Now the tracing. In simple models, more potassium outside also raises potassium conductance, so phase 3 is faster and steeper, and the action potential shortens. Two regions that start phase 3 a few milliseconds apart then differ by more millivolts at any instant, because each is falling more steeply, and they differ for a shorter time. By the same logic, that gives a taller, narrower T wave: tall, peaked T waves are the classic early ECG change. A modest depolarisation brings cells nearer threshold, so conduction may first speed. With larger rises, more sodium channels sit inactivated, so phase 0 slows and conduction slows; propagation fails only far higher. Slower conduction widens the QRS, since its width is the crossing time, and the P wave broadens and flattens for the same reason.

In people, the potassium level at which these ECG changes appear varies widely, and their sequence comes mainly from models, isolated hearts and animal preparations. Low extracellular potassium does the reverse to duration, lengthening the action potential by reducing outward current through potassium channels and the sodium-potassium pump. Regulation of plasma potassium is in [water, sodium and potassium](/lessons/water-sodium-potassium/).

### Design problem

Hutter and Trautwein (Journal of General Physiology, 1956) recorded with intracellular microelectrodes from the isolated, beating frog heart while stimulating its nerves. Vagal stimulation stops the beat. Does the vagus (A) make the pacemaker membrane more negative, (B) stop the ramp at the same diastolic potential, or (C) block spread of excitation out of the pacemaker? Which recordings tell these apart, what is the control tissue, and what would sympathetic stimulation do?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Record maximal diastolic potential and ramp slope in sinus venosus fibres (the frog's pacemaker region) during vagal stimulation. A predicts a more negative diastolic potential; B the same potential with a flat ramp; C normal pacemaker activity with a silent atrium. As control, record auricle fibres, which have a stable diastolic potential, to ask whether any hyperpolarisation is general.

Their data: in 28 experiments the sinus fibres' mean maximal diastolic potential went from about −55 to −65.4 mV (largest shift 23 mV), the ramp was suppressed, and auricle fibres did not change. Hypothesis A fits. Sympathetic stimulation steepened the pacemaker potential with threshold unchanged.

Ruled in: both autonomic limbs act directly on the pacemaker potential. Not shown: which ion carries the change, or how a mammalian SA node behaves; mammalian atrial muscle, unlike this frog auricle, does respond to acetylcholine. Extensions: vary external potassium to test the permeability idea, or block muscarinic receptors (Loewi's frog hearts are in [transmitter systems](/lessons/transmitter-systems/)).

</details>

## Going deeper

### Plateau numbers and disagreements

Sources differ on the peak of phase 0: one review gives about +30 mV, another an overshoot of +40 to +60 mV with a maximum rate of rise near 400 V per s. In Beeler and Reuter's experiments the slow inward current's threshold was near −35 mV in Tyrode solution, and its inactivation time constants ran from 40 to 700 ms between −35 and +50 mV. Their work did not show the channel protein (later Cav1.2) or anything about human cells, and voltage in a multicellular strip is never perfectly uniform. Nor was this the first cardiac calcium current: Reuter had reported a calcium-dependent slow inward current in Purkinje fibres in 1967.

### The HCN4 knockouts in detail

Hcn4-mutant mouse embryos lost about 85 percent of If (Stieber and colleagues, 2003) but died, which is why the 2011 study used a tamoxifen-inducible, cardiac-specific system in adults. In its isolated SA node myocytes, If fell by about 70 percent and spontaneous rate by about 60 percent, and the residual If still responded to beta-adrenergic stimulation. An earlier adult knockout (Herrmann and colleagues, EMBO Journal, 2007) also removed most sinoatrial If but gave recurrent sinus pauses, with no slowing of baseline rate and normal acceleration under sympathetic stimulation, so the phenotype differs between models. In humans, Schulze-Bahr and colleagues (2003) found a heterozygous HCN4 deletion (1631delC) in one person with sinus node dysfunction; in COS-7 cells the mutant gave If-like currents insensitive to cyclic AMP.

### Numbers from animal hearts

In the perfused rabbit hearts, optical mapping found a step delay of more than 40 ms at the junction of atrium and node; refractory periods were about 81 ms in atrium, 90 ms in AV node and 185 ms in His bundle. For the T wave: in isolated cells and some cut wedge preparations, mid-wall (M) cells have the longest action potentials, then endocardial, then epicardial, but whether this ordering holds in the intact, well-coupled heart is debated. In nine perfused pig hearts repolarisation differed by only about 14 ms across the wall, against 66 ms from left to right and 51 ms from apex to base.

### Why the T wave peaks in hyperkalaemia

A 2017 review (Weiss, Qu and Shivkumar) explains the peaked T wave by global shortening of the action potential, which makes repolarisation more synchronous across the wall and narrows the T wave. Synchrony alone would lower the T wave, so its height probably comes mainly from the steeper phase 3; which effect dominates in people is not settled.
