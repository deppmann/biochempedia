    story: >-
      In 1979 H. F. Brown, Dario DiFrancesco and S. J. Noble reported in Nature that a current in
      rabbit sinoatrial node tissue was increased by adrenaline, which suggested how sympathetic
      nerves speed the heart. The current behaved oddly. It switched on when the membrane was made

techniques:
  - name: "Voltage clamp of cardiac preparations"
    blurb: >-
      Hold the membrane at a chosen voltage with a feedback amplifier and record the current
      needed to keep it there. Changing the voltage protocol, the bath ions or a blocker such as
      tetrodotoxin separates one current from another, as Beeler and Reuter did in dog ventricle.
      In multicellular strips the voltage is not perfectly uniform, which limits precision.
  - name: "Intracellular microelectrode recording"
    blurb: >-
      Push a fine glass pipette filled with salt solution into a single cell and record its
      membrane potential through each beat. It shows the shape of the action potential, the
      maximal diastolic potential and the slope of the pacemaker ramp, and how nerve stimulation
      changes them.
  - name: "Electrocardiography"
    blurb: >-
      Record voltage differences between electrodes on the skin as excitation spreads through the
      heart, on paper moving at a standard speed. The tracing is a summed extracellular signal of
      many cells; its timing gives rate, conduction intervals and the mean electrical axis.
  - name: "Inducible tissue-specific knockout mice"
    blurb: >-
      Flank a gene with recombination sites and delete it only in one tissue, and only when a drug
      such as tamoxifen is given. This avoids embryonic death and tests what the gene does in the
      adult, as in the cardiac Hcn4 knockout.

**Once excited, a ventricular muscle cell stays depolarised for a few hundred milliseconds, roughly a hundred times as long as a nerve spike.** That plateau sets how much calcium enters, keeps beats from fusing, and shows on the skin as the stretch between the QRS complex and the T wave. This lesson goes further than [circulation and the heart](/lessons/circulation-and-heart/), from currents to the tracing.

## A long plateau keeps the ventricle from summing its twitches

Teaching values for a human or other mammalian ventricular cell, from recent reviews: **phase 4**, rest, sits near -90 mV, held close to the potassium equilibrium potential (EK) by the inward-rectifier current **IK1**. In **phase 0**, sodium channels (Nav1.5) open from a threshold of about -70 to -55 mV. Sources differ on the peak: one review gives about +30 mV, another an overshoot of +40 to +60 mV with a maximum rate of rise near 400 V per s. How the spike is made is in [the action potential](/lessons/action-potential/). **Phase 1** is a small notch as sodium channels inactivate and a transient outward potassium current, **Ito**, flows.

**Phase 2**, the plateau, lasts about 200 ms and is a balance. The **L-type calcium current** (ICaL, through Cav1.2) flows in; the delayed-rectifier potassium currents **IKr** (the hERG channel) and **IKs** flow out, like a tug of war with the rope held near the middle. In **phase 3** the L-type channels close and the potassium currents win. Several potassium currents can repolarise the cell, so losing one to a gene variant or a drug is partly covered, a margin called the **repolarisation reserve**. In isolated tissue, mid-wall cells have the longest action potentials, then endocardial, then epicardial.

While the membrane is depolarised, sodium channels stay inactivated and the cell is **refractory**: a second stimulus cannot fire it. The refractory period lasts nearly as long as the action potential and so covers most of the twitch. A skeletal muscle fibre, with its brief action potential, can be restimulated during a twitch and summed into tetanus ([skeletal muscle mechanics](/lessons/skeletal-muscle-mechanics/)); a ventricular cell cannot, so the ventricle refills between beats.

### The classic experiment: what carries the plateau?

The fast sodium current inactivates within milliseconds, so what keeps a ventricular cell depolarised for hundreds? Perhaps a slowly inactivating sodium current. G. W. Beeler Jr. and Harald Reuter tested this in dog ventricular muscle under voltage clamp (Journal of Physiology, 1970): a conditioning depolarisation inactivated the fast sodium system, and they recorded the inward current left.

A slow inward current remained. It persisted in sodium-free solution, was unaffected by tetrodotoxin (a fast sodium channel blocker) and by magnesium, and was very sensitive to external calcium. Its threshold was near -35 mV in Tyrode solution, and its inactivation time constants ran from 40 to 700 ms between -35 and +50 mV, slow enough to span a plateau. They concluded that calcium carries it.

Ruled out: the tetrodotoxin-sensitive sodium channel as the carrier. Not shown: the channel protein (later Cav1.2), human cells, or that the plateau is calcium current alone; and voltage in a multicellular strip is never perfectly uniform. Nor was this the first cardiac calcium current: Reuter had reported a calcium-dependent slow inward current in Purkinje fibres in 1967.

## Pacemaker cells have no resting potential

A sinoatrial (SA) node cell never rests. After each action potential its voltage falls to a **maximal diastolic potential**, the most negative point of the cycle, then drifts upward in **phase 4 diastolic depolarisation** to threshold. L-type calcium channels carry its slow upstroke, and there is no plateau.

Two mechanisms drive the drift. The **funny current (If)**, carried mainly by HCN4 channels, is activated by hyperpolarisation and carries both sodium and potassium, a net inward current at diastolic voltages. How far If is activated at the end of each action potential sets the steepness of phase 4 and so the rate. A **calcium clock** of local calcium releases from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) in late diastole adds inward current through the sodium-calcium exchanger. In the **coupled-clock model**, built mostly from rabbit cells, the two clocks entrain each other. How much each contributes is still debated.

Intrinsic rates form a hierarchy: in one human review, about 70 to 80 per min for SA node cells and 40 to 60 for AV node cells; His-Purkinje cells are slower, about 15 to 40. The fastest site leads, exciting each slower one before its own ramp reaches threshold.

Autonomic nerves act on the ramp. Noradrenaline at beta-adrenergic receptors raises cyclic AMP, which increases If and steepens phase 4. Acetylcholine at muscarinic M2 receptors lowers cyclic AMP and If, and also opens a G-protein-gated potassium channel that makes the maximal diastolic potential more negative ([GPCRs and G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/); reflex control is in [regulating arterial pressure](/lessons/blood-pressure-regulation/)). A simple description of the timing:

> **cycle length = (threshold - maximal diastolic potential) / slope of phase 4 + action potential duration**

In a made-up example, a ramp from -60 to -40 mV at 0.05 mV per ms takes 400 ms; with a 400 ms action potential the cycle is 800 ms, 75 per min. Double the slope: 600 ms, 100 per min.

### The modern experiment: deleting HCN4 in the adult mouse

If the funny current were the whole pacemaker, removing it should stop the heart. Hcn4-mutant mouse embryos lost about 85 percent of If (Stieber and colleagues, 2003) but died, so Mirko Baruscotti, Dario DiFrancesco and colleagues (PNAS, 2011) deleted Hcn4 in the adult mouse heart with a tamoxifen-inducible, cardiac-specific system. In isolated SA node myocytes, If fell by about 70 percent and spontaneous rate by about 60 percent, and the residual If still responded to beta-adrenergic stimulation. In the intact mice, they report, heart rate fell by about half and AV block developed.

Rate fell by about half and the mice died within about five days; the rhythm did not stop at once. HCN4 is needed for normal adult rate and AV conduction in mice, but the result does not show that If alone makes the rhythm, and a mouse heart beats several times faster than a human's. In humans, Schulze-Bahr and colleagues (2003) found a heterozygous HCN4 deletion (1631delC) in one person with sinus node dysfunction; in COS-7 cells the mutant gave If-like currents insensitive to cyclic AMP.

## Calcium in, more calcium out

Calcium entering through L-type channels at the **dyad**, where a T tubule meets the junctional SR, raises calcium in that narrow space and opens the SR's **ryanodine receptors (RyR2)**, which release far more: **calcium-induced calcium release (CICR)**. The Fabiatos' skinned-cell evidence is in [muscle and movement](/lessons/muscle-and-movement/). Why does this positive feedback not run away? Release is graded with the size of the L-type current, and the SR releases only about half its calcium per beat. Calcium then returns to the SR through the pump **SERCA** and leaves the cell through the **sodium-calcium exchanger (NCX)**, and the muscle relaxes.

{/* DIAGRAM: cardiac dyad with L-type channels, RyR2, SERCA and NCX */}

The release channel matters in people: Priori and colleagues (2001) found RyR2 mutations in four of twelve probands with catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, an inherited arrhythmia. **Contractility**, the strength of contraction at a given filling, rises with sympathetic drive as protein kinase A phosphorylates the L-type channel (more calcium entry), phospholamban (releasing its brake on SERCA, so the SR holds more) and troponin I (faster relaxation); see [cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/) and, for the loop, [the cardiac cycle](/lessons/pressure-volume-loops/).

## The wave pauses at the AV node, and the ECG adds up the wave

Excitation crosses the atria to the **atrioventricular (AV) node**, normally the only electrical path into the ventricles, pauses, then runs quickly through the bundle of His, the bundle branches and the Purkinje fibres. In perfused rabbit hearts, optical mapping found a step delay of more than 40 ms at the junction of atrium and node, a delay that grew with rapid atrial pacing. Refractory periods in the same rabbit work were about 81 ms in atrium, 90 ms in AV node and 185 ms in His bundle.

{/* DIAGRAM: human conduction system, SA node to Purkinje fibres */}

The **electrocardiogram (ECG)** is the summed extracellular field of many cells, recorded from the skin: not one cell's action potential and not contraction. A depolarisation front moving toward a positive electrode gives an upward deflection. Willem Einthoven set out leads I (right arm to left arm), II (right arm to left leg) and III (left arm to left leg) as a triangle. The **P wave** records atrial depolarisation, the **QRS complex** ventricular depolarisation and the **T wave** ventricular repolarisation; atrial repolarisation is hidden in the QRS. The **PR interval**, from the start of P to the start of QRS, contains the AV delay. In healthy adults at rest PR typically runs 0.12 to 0.20 s and QRS lasts under 0.12 s.

{/* DIAGRAM: Einthoven's triangle, leads I, II and III with polarity */}

The textbook account of the upright T wave is that the epicardium repolarises first. Mapping disagrees: in nine perfused pig hearts, repolarisation differed by only about 14 ms across the wall, against 66 ms from left to right and 51 ms from apex to base.

AV block, read as conduction patterns: in **first-degree** block every P wave conducts, after a longer delay. In **Mobitz I** (Wenckebach) the PR lengthens beat by beat until one P fails, then restarts, as a rate-dependent delay like the rabbit step would produce. In **Mobitz II** the PR stays constant and a P suddenly fails, usually from block below the node. In **third-degree** block no P conducts, and a slower escape pacemaker drives the ventricles. First-degree and Mobitz I block are very common in endurance athletes, in whom vagal tone is high.

### Worked problem: reading one tracing

Illustrative numbers, adult human resting ECG at 25 mm per s: 1 small square = 1 mm = 0.04 s; 1 large square = 5 mm = 0.20 s. Assume a regular rhythm.

1. *Rate.* R-R = 4 large squares = 0.80 s. Rate = 60 s per min / 0.80 s = 75 per min (check: 300 / 4 = 75).
2. *Intervals.* PR = 4 small squares = 0.16 s; QRS = 2 small squares = 0.08 s.
3. *QT corrected for rate.* QT = 9 small squares = 0.36 s. QT shortens as rate rises, so it is corrected to a 1 s cycle:

> **Bazett: QTc = QT / square root of RR. Fridericia: QTc = QT / cube root of RR (RR in s)**

Bazett: 0.36 / 0.894 = 0.40 s. Fridericia: 0.36 / 0.928 = 0.39 s. At 120 per min (R-R 0.50 s) with QT 0.30 s: Bazett 0.30 / 0.707 = 0.42 s; Fridericia 0.30 / 0.794 = 0.38 s. Both leave QT unchanged at R-R = 1 s and drift apart as rate rises. Bazett's is widely described as overcorrecting at fast rates; his 1920 formula came from a small group of healthy people.

4. *Axis.* The **mean QRS axis** is the average frontal-plane direction of ventricular depolarisation. With the standard hexaxial angles (lead I 0 degrees, lead II +60, aVF +90, aVL -30), net QRS is positive in I and aVF, so the axis lies between 0 and +90 degrees. QRS is isoelectric in aVL, so the axis is perpendicular to it, at +60 or -120 degrees; the quadrant picks +60. Check: lead II should show the largest positive QRS.

Answer: 75 per min, PR 0.16 s, QRS 0.08 s, QTc 0.40 s (Bazett) or 0.39 s (Fridericia), axis about +60 degrees.

## Potassium moves the resting potential and reshapes the ECG

Because IK1 dominates the resting ventricular membrane, the resting potential follows EK. The Nernst equation ([membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/)) at 37 C gives 61.5 mV per tenfold ratio. With intracellular potassium at 140 mmol per L:

> **EK = 61.5 mV x log10([K]outside / [K]inside)**

At 4 mmol per L outside, EK = 61.5 x (-1.544) = -95 mV. At 7 mmol per L, EK = 61.5 x (-1.301) = -80 mV, assuming the resting potential tracks EK, an approximation.

Predictions, from models, isolated hearts and animal preparations: a modest depolarisation brings cells nearer threshold, so conduction first speeds. With larger rises, more sodium channels sit inactivated, phase 0 slows and conduction slows; propagation fails only far higher. Raised potassium also increases potassium conductance, so action potentials shorten and repolarisation becomes more synchronous: the first ECG change is tall, peaked T waves. Slower conduction then broadens and flattens the P wave and widens the QRS. Low extracellular potassium does the reverse to duration, lengthening the action potential by reducing outward current through potassium channels and the sodium-potassium pump. Regulation of plasma potassium is in [water, sodium and potassium](/lessons/water-sodium-potassium/).

### Design problem

Hutter and Trautwein (Journal of General Physiology, 1956) recorded with intracellular microelectrodes from the isolated, beating frog heart while stimulating its nerves. Vagal stimulation stops the beat. Does the vagus (A) make the pacemaker membrane more negative, (B) stop the ramp at the same diastolic potential, or (C) block spread of excitation out of the pacemaker? Which recordings tell these apart, what is the control tissue, and what would sympathetic stimulation do?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Record maximal diastolic potential and ramp slope in sinus venosus fibres (the frog's pacemaker region) during vagal stimulation. A predicts a more negative diastolic potential; B the same potential with a flat ramp; C normal pacemaker activity with a silent atrium. As control, record auricle fibres, which have a stable diastolic potential, to ask whether any hyperpolarisation is general.

Their data: in 28 experiments the sinus fibres' mean maximal diastolic potential went from 55 to 65.4 mV in magnitude (about -55 to -65 mV; largest shift 23 mV), the ramp was suppressed, and auricle fibres did not change. Hypothesis A fits. Sympathetic stimulation steepened the pacemaker potential with threshold unchanged.

Ruled in: both autonomic limbs act directly on the pacemaker potential. Not shown: which ion carries the change, or how a mammalian SA node behaves; mammalian atrial muscle, unlike this frog auricle, does respond to acetylcholine. Extensions: vary external potassium to test the permeability idea, or block muscarinic receptors (Loewi's frog hearts are in [transmitter systems](/lessons/transmitter-systems/)).
