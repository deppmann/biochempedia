scientists:
  - name: "Max Rubner"
    contribution: >-
      Compared the heat production of resting dogs of different sizes and found that total heat
      rose less steeply than body mass, while heat per unit of body surface stayed roughly
      constant, the pattern later called the surface law.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Rubner, Ueber den Einfluss der Körpergrösse auf Stoff- und Kraftwechsel, Zeitschrift für Biologie 19:535-562 (1883)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Rubner worked on a question that sounds simple: does every gram of an animal cost the same
      to run? If it did, a dog ten times heavier than another should produce ten times as much
      heat each day. Dogs suit the question because one species spans a wide range of body sizes,
      so size can be compared without also changing the kind of animal. He kept the dogs at rest
      and estimated how much heat
      each produced in a day. Total heat rose with size, but much less steeply than mass. The
      small dogs produced more heat per kilogram than the large ones. When he divided heat by
      each dog's estimated body surface instead of by its mass, the values came out roughly
      equal. Since surface grows about as mass to the power 2/3 in animals of similar shape, this
      gave a scaling rule with a definite exponent that later workers could test against other
      species.
  - name: "Max Kleiber"

      Compiled resting metabolic rates for animals from rats to steers and fitted them with a
      power of body mass near 3/4 rather than 2/3, the relation now called Kleiber's rule, which
      is a debated empirical fit rather than a law.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Kleiber, Body size and metabolism, Hilgardia 6:315-353 (1932); UC Davis Library Archives and Special Collections, Kleiber, Max (1893-1976), Papers."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Kleiber was Professor of Animal Physiology at the University of California, Davis, from
      1929 to 1960, according to the university's archival description of his papers. The question
      matters for animal husbandry, because how much feed an animal needs depends on how much
      energy it spends at rest. In his 1932 paper in Hilgardia he gathered resting metabolic rates for animals
      that ranged from rats to steers and asked which power of body mass described them best.
      On a graph of the logarithm of metabolic rate against the logarithm of mass, the points
      fell close to a straight line, and its slope was near 3/4, steeper than Rubner's 2/3. The
      result gave animal scientists a way to compare the energy needs of animals of very different
      sizes on one scale. It also started a long argument, still open, about whether 3/4 is a
      real regularity, an artefact of which animals were chosen, or one value among many.

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the end of the twentieth century the 3/4 exponent had become a fixture of textbooks, and
      in 1997 a group of physicists and ecologists proposed that it arose from the branching
      geometry of supply networks such as blood vessels. White and Seymour, physiologists working
      in Australia, went back to the data. A basal metabolic rate is meant to be measured in an
      adult animal at rest, not digesting a meal, and in a thermoneutral environment, one where it
      spends no extra energy on warming or cooling itself. Many published values did not meet
      those conditions. Large mammals tend to differ from small ones in body temperature, and
      herbivores that ferment food in the gut, such as cattle and kangaroos, may never be fully
      fasted when measured. When White and Seymour restricted and corrected
      the data for these factors, the fitted exponent for mammals came out close to 2/3. Their
      paper did not settle the question. It showed that the exponent depends on how the data are
      chosen, and later work often reports exponents group by group.

      - "It stays at 0.6 per um"

    rationale: >-
      For a cube the ratio is 6/s, so doubling s halves it: 2,400 um2 of area over 8,000 um3 of

      reverses the real relation. Staying the same treats area and volume as growing in step, but
      area rises fourfold and volume eightfold. Dividing by eight applies the volume factor to the
      whole ratio and forgets that area also grew.

A flatworm has no lungs and no heart, yet oxygen reaches all its cells; a mouse needs both. The first film traces why; this text adds the depth limit, plants, and energy use. It builds on [what an organism has to do](/lessons/organism-problems/).

> **The one idea.** When a body grows without changing shape, its middle ends up farther from its outer surface, and diffusion time grows with the square of that distance. In a simple model of a solid ball of tissue fed from air at its surface, oxygen runs out at the centre once the radius passes about 1.5 mm for slow tissue, or a few tenths of a millimetre for busy tissue; other shapes give other limits. So bodies stay thin, add internal exchange surfaces, or move fluid in bulk (**bulk flow**: pumped blood, air or sap), leaving diffusion only the last few micrometres.
>
> *Scope:* oxygen in metabolically active tissue, bodies compared at the same shape and density and supplied from their outer surface. The depth numbers come from an idealized model (Going deeper); low-demand tissue, or a cell with an unusual internal arrangement, shifts the limit.

## A bigger cube has less skin per unit of inside

The **surface-area-to-volume ratio**, outer area divided by enclosed volume, measures the first cost the first film's cubes showed: less surface per bit of inside. For an ideal cube of side s, the area is 6s² (six square faces) and the volume s³.

## Diffusion: fast over micrometres, slow over centimetres

**Diffusion** is the net spread of molecules from where they are more concentrated to where they are less, driven by their random motion. Its speed is set by the **diffusion coefficient**, D. For a small molecule in water, D is about 1,000 um² per s. Crossing membranes is in [membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/).

In a simple model of free diffusion along one direction, the mean squared distance travelled in time t is 2Dt; its square root is the typical distance L. Set L² = 2Dt and divide by 2D:

## How deep can diffusion reach?

The table fills in the middle of the first film's model-ball range, from the same idealized model:

| Oxygen use of the tissue (mL O₂ per g per hour) | Largest radius of a solid ball whose centre still gets oxygen |
|---|---|
| 0.05 (slow) | 1.45 mm |
| 0.3 | 0.59 mm |
| 1.0 | 0.33 mm |
| 3.0 (busy) | 0.19 mm |

For scale, a resting human averages about 0.2 mL O₂ per g per hour over the whole body; on the 3/4 fit later in this lesson, which is in energy units, a resting mouse uses about seven times as much per gram. Depth goes as one over the square root of the rate of use, so tenfold faster use (0.3 to 3.0) cuts reach only about threefold.

**Predict.** Planarians, like many flatworms, are flattened and take oxygen in by diffusion through two faces; a ball takes it in from every direction. In the same model, is a sheet's largest half-thickness (face to middle) bigger or smaller than the ball's largest radius?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Smaller. The sheet's half-thickness limits are 0.84, 0.34, 0.19 and 0.11 mm, less than the ball's radius at every rate by a factor of about 1.7, the square root of 3. Flattening does not allow thicker tissue; it works because length and width can grow while thickness stays under the limit.

</details>

## Four answers in two kingdoms

**Flattening.** Besides the planarian, the blade, or **lamina**, of a typical eudicot leaf (a broad-leaved flowering plant, such as a bean) is a wide, thin sheet in which no cell is far from the surface.

**Folding.** The mammalian lung folds its exchange surface into a vast number of small air sacs, the alveoli. Exchange depends on that area divided by the barrier's thickness, because a thin barrier is a short diffusion distance. Leaves fold surface inside too: their **mesophyll**, the photosynthetic tissue, is full of air spaces. Using X-ray microCT, Mathers and colleagues (2018) measured 21.3 mm² of exposed mesophyll surface per mm² of leaf area in barley and 15.5 in rice. Area over area has no units: the inside surface is fifteen to twenty times the leaf's one-face area, unlike the cube's ratio in per um.

**Branching.** The airways of the mammalian lung divide about 23 times in the standard textbook model of the adult human, from the trachea to the alveolar sacs. In a typical eudicot leaf, veins branch from the **midrib**, the main central vein, into finer and finer veins. Their **xylem** carries water and minerals, and dense fine veins shorten the path water must take from the xylem, through living tissue, to where it evaporates.

**Bulk flow.** Bulk flow moves a whole fluid, and everything in it, by a pressure difference or by motors. Breathing, blood circulation and sap in phloem (sugar-carrying tissue) are bulk flow ([gas exchange](/lessons/gas-exchange/), [circulation](/lessons/circulation-and-heart/), [plant transport](/lessons/plant-transport/)). Even single cells use it: in the giant cells of the alga Chara, motors drive the cytoplasm round at up to about 100 um per s.

None of these removes diffusion. In the mammalian lung, transport shifts from bulk airflow toward diffusion in the finest branches, and oxygen crosses the alveolar wall into the blood by diffusion, not by active transport.

Bulk flow has to deliver whatever the tissue burns. So how much does a body burn, and does every kilogram burn the same? The second film follows the experiments that first asked.

## Body mass and metabolism: Rubner, Kleiber and the debate

**Metabolic rate** is the rate at which an organism uses energy; **basal metabolic rate** is that rate in an adult **endotherm** (an animal that keeps itself warm with its own heat: mammals and birds) at rest, fasted, and at a temperature where it spends nothing extra on heating or cooling.

Rubner's 1883 "surface law" is usually quoted as an exponent near 2/3, but what he reported was heat roughly constant per unit of estimated body surface. That implies 2/3 if the dogs are treated as one shape and one density: surface grows as length², mass as length³, so surface grows as mass to the power 2/3. It ruled out simple proportionality in these dogs; it did not show that surface heat loss is the cause in other animals. Kleiber's 1932 fit, rats to steers, gave about 3/4. Both are written as power laws:

For rate per kilogram, divide the total by M: rate per kg = a × M^b / M. Dividing powers of the same thing subtracts exponents, M^b / M^1 = M^(b − 1), so

> **rate per kg = a × M^(b − 1)**

With b = 0.75 the exponent is −0.25, a negative power, so rate per kilogram falls as M rises.

**Worked problem.** The second film held b at 0.75 on the widely quoted mammal fit, basal metabolic rate of about 70 x M^(0.75) kcal per day with M in kg, whose exponent is debated. Now change b:

1. The mouse's rate per kilogram over the human's is (M_mouse / M_human)^(b − 1).
2. A negative power flips a fraction: that equals (M_human / M_mouse)^(1 − b).
3. The mass ratio is 70 / 0.03 = 2,333. With b = 3/4 the power is 1/4: 2,333^(1/4), about 7. With b = 2/3 the power is 1/3, a cube root: 2,333^(1/3), about 13 (check: 13 × 13 × 13 = 2,197).

The exponent alone nearly doubles the predicted difference. White and Seymour (2003) found a mammalian exponent near 2/3 once body temperature and digestive state were controlled. The exponent depends on the group, the state (basal, field or maximal), temperature and method, and no explanation of it is settled.

### Design problem

Illustrative data: plotting log basal metabolic rate against log body mass gives a slope of 0.70 for 40 rodent species and 0.68 for 40 bird species. Pooling those species with shrews and whales gives a slope of 0.76. A classmate says the pooled slope confirms that metabolic rate is set by the branching geometry of blood vessels, as one well-known network model (West, Brown and Enquist, 1997) proposes. What does each slope tell you, and what would you say to your classmate?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Each slope is an estimate of b for that set of animals. Within rodents, a 100-fold rise in mass predicts about 100^(0.70), roughly 25 times the total rate (100 is 10², so 100^(0.70) = 10^(1.4)); within birds, about 100^(0.68), roughly 23 times. The pooled slope is a different quantity. Adding shrews and whales stretches the mass range, and if the groups differ in their constant a, the line through all of them can be steeper or shallower than the line within any one group. At 0.76, a 100-fold rise predicts about 33 times the rate.

Before trusting any of the three, ask how the rates were measured (basal, field or maximal), at what temperature, and with what regression. Related species are not independent points, so a phylogenetic regression may give a different slope from an ordinary one; see [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/).

To your classmate: a slope near 3/4 is consistent with the network model, but so are other explanations, and the within-group slopes here are nearer 2/3. A fitted number cannot confirm a mechanism. Testing one would need a prediction that rival explanations do not share, checked in data collected under the same conditions.

</details>

## Does a lower rate per kilogram rescue a big body?

**Predict.** On the 3/4 fit, a kilogram of human burns about a seventh of what a kilogram of mouse burns, and the depth table says slower tissue is supplied more deeply. Could that let a human-sized body shaped like a mouse live on diffusion through its skin?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No; it helps, but far too slowly. Reach grows only with the square root of the drop in demand. From mouse to human the body gets about 13 times wider (2,333^(1/3)), but the reach grows only about 2.6-fold on the 3/4 fit (the square root of 7), and about 3.6-fold with b = 2/3 (the square root of 13).

</details>

And the outer surface? For bodies of one shape and one density, b = 2/3 would keep demand per unit of outer surface constant with size, and b = 3/4 would raise it about 1.9-fold from mouse to human. That does not mean skin could supply a mammal, whose oxygen enters through its lungs. Either way, the middle moves away faster than reach grows. The one idea again: for the same shape, a bigger body means a longer path, so bodies stay thin, add inner surface or pump fluid. ## Going deeper

### The sphere's ratio, step by step

For a sphere of radius r, surface area over volume is 4πr² / (4/3 πr³) = 3/r. Here π ("pi") is about 3.14, and it cancels: π over π is 1, 4 divided by 4/3 is 3, and r² over r³ leaves 1/r.

### What t = L² / 2D does and does not measure

The formula gives a typical spread along one coordinate, not the time to fill a cell or the first arrival: some molecules arrive sooner, and evening out a whole region takes longer.

### The depth model behind the table

The table uses an idealized steady-state model: uniform tissue using oxygen at a constant rate Q per unit volume, surface oxygen held at air's P = 0.21 atm, no internal supply or carrier-bound oxygen, and the centre just reaching zero. Then the largest ball radius is √(6KP/Q) and the largest sheet half-thickness √(2KP/Q), so their ratio is √3. K is Krogh's diffusion constant, the diffusion coefficient times oxygen's solubility in tissue: 1.4 × 10⁻⁵ mL O₂ per cm per min per atm. To use the table's rates in mL O₂ per g per hour, take tissue density as 1 g per cm³ and divide by 60 to get Q per cm³ per minute. The human figure of about 0.2 is a resting oxygen use of about 250 mL per minute spread over 70 kg.

Since rate per kilogram goes as M^(b − 1), reach grows as M^((1 − b)/2), against a body radius growing as M^(1/3), assuming one shape, constant density, K and P, and tissue demand in step with the whole body's rate per kilogram.

Stores barely help. Tissue in equilibrium with air would hold about 0.0045 mL of dissolved O₂ per mL, an upper bound, lasting about 1.3 minutes at the resting human rate, about 11 seconds at seven times that, and about 5 minutes at the table's slowest rate. Real tissue oxygen and carrier-bound stores differ.

### More on the exponent

**Allometry** is the study of how a trait changes with body size when it does not change in simple proportion. Griebeler and Werner (2016), comparing ordinary and phylogenetic regression across 17 mammalian orders, found no single exponent for all the groups.

### A limit with exceptions

These limits are conditional. Volland and colleagues (2022) described a bacterium, Candidatus Thiomargarita magnifica, whose cells averaged more than 9,000 um long: shape and internal arrangement can push a single cell far beyond the usual size.
