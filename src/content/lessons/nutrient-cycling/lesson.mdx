techniques:
  - name: "Small-watershed budget"
    blurb: >-
      Choose a watershed on watertight bedrock, gauge precipitation and its chemistry, and measure
      stream flow and chemistry at a weir. Inputs minus outputs gives the net gain or loss through the
      measured routes (gas exchange such as fixation and denitrification needs separate measurement), and a treated watershed can be compared with an untreated reference.
  - name: "Whole-lake manipulation"
    blurb: >-
      Add or withhold nutrients to an entire lake or a divided basin and follow chlorophyll,
      nutrients and nitrogen fixation for years against pre-treatment data and an untreated lake.
      It tests limitation at the scale managers act on, but one lake is not a replicate.
  - name: "15N tracer methods"
    blurb: >-
      Add the heavy isotope 15N and follow it: into biomass for nitrogen fixation, into N2 for
      denitrification or anammox, or as the dilution of a labelled ammonium pool by newly made
      unlabelled ammonium (isotope pool dilution) for gross mineralisation. The answer depends on how well the label mixes with the pool being
      measured.

**Nutrient cycling** is the reuse of a limited supply of elements such as carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus. The first film kept one forest's nitrogen accounts.

> **The one idea.** A store of an element changes only by what crosses its boundary: change in stock = inputs − outputs. Inside, decomposers release the element and plants take it up again. That loop can be several times larger than all the inputs, yet it cancels in the sum. The store grows while inputs exceed outputs and shrinks while outputs exceed inputs. Break the loop, and part of its large flow can cross the line: stores change only across the boundary, and a broken loop can cross it.

## Pools, fluxes and residence time

A **pool** (or stock) is the amount of an element in one compartment at one time, in mass per area (kg N per hectare). A **flux** is a transfer between pools, or across the **boundary** (the line that decides what counts as inside), in mass per area per time: kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, where the −1 exponent reads as "per".

> **change in stock = inputs − outputs   (for a stated boundary and interval)**

> **residence time = stock / flux through the pool**

**Residence time** is the average time an atom spends in a pool when inflow and outflow balance. This is the mass balance from [energy flow](/lessons/energy-flow/), except that energy leaves as heat while atoms are reused.

**Worked problem.** Use the first film's invented watershed (one stream; boundary from canopy to bedrock; fluxes in kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹; steady rates; water and its dissolved nitrogen leave only by the stream). Its net gain of **+9 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹** (inputs 14; outputs 3 by the stream and 2 by denitrification) is stored in wood and soil.

1. *How long does an atom stay?* The soil organic N pool is 4,500 kg N per hectare, and gross mineralisation (decomposers releasing organic N as ammonium) is 90 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹. Residence time = 4,500 / 90 = **50 years**, the average wait before decomposers release an atom. That assumes the pool is near balance: the +9 is a whole-watershed gain, and the budget does not say how much of it reaches the soil, so 50 years is an estimate. That loop is about six times the watershed's inputs (90 / 14 = 6.4).
2. *Disturbance.* Cut the forest and suppress regrowth. Suppose stream export rises to 40 while the other terms stay fixed (an assumption; gas losses could rise too). Outputs = 40 + 2 = 42, and change = 14 − 42 = **−28 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹**, a net loss.

**Predict.** Mineralisation keeps running at about 90. If all the extra stream nitrogen came from that loop, roughly what share of the loop is now leaking out?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

About two fifths: export rose by 40 − 3 = 37, and 37 / 90 = 0.41. That rests on the assumption; a bulk budget cannot tell which atoms left.

The invented rise shows the direction of the real Hubbard Brook result (Likens, Bormann and colleagues, Ecological Monographs, 1970), not its size. Their **small-watershed approach** used valleys on watertight bedrock that lets little water seep deep, so a **weir** (a low dam with a notch that gauges stream flow) measured nearly everything leaving in liquid water. The felled trees stayed on site and herbicide kept regrowth down, so plant uptake fell sharply with no nutrients carried off in wood; an uncut watershed was the reference. Cutting and herbicide also changed drainage, soil temperature and moisture, and microbial activity, so the result is their combined effect, not uptake loss alone. Nitrate is a negative ion and drainage water stays electrically neutral, so positive ions (cations) such as calcium leave with it; and with few leaves, little water returns to the air by **transpiration** (evaporation from leaves), so more drains through.

*Scope.* A budget covers one element, one boundary and one interval, stated before any adding; where the line goes decides which transfers are inputs and outputs. The rule is accounting: it shows *that* a store changed and through which boundary flux, not *which* process controls the system. That needs a causal test, most directly, at this scale, a whole-ecosystem experiment like this one.

## The carbon cycle: fast exchanges laid over slow stores

The large pools of carbon outside carbonate rock are the ocean, the atmosphere, living plants, soil and fossil fuels. Two biological fluxes dominate the fast exchange between them. Photosynthesis by plants and phytoplankton moves CO2 into organic matter ([the Calvin cycle](/lessons/calvin-cycle/) has the chemistry). Respiration by plants, animals and decomposers returns it as CO2; on land, plants respire roughly as much as decomposers do ([oxidative phosphorylation](/lessons/oxidative-phosphorylation/) has the mechanism). In the Northern Hemisphere the two run out of phase: atmospheric CO2 falls each summer, when photosynthesis outpaces respiration, and rises each winter.

The ocean exchanges CO2 with the air across its surface; dissolved CO2 forms bicarbonate and carbonate, so the ocean holds far more carbon than the air. Fossil carbon is organic matter buried over millions of years.

**Predict.** Draw the boundary around the fast cycle (air, ocean, plants, soil). Is burning fossil fuel an internal transfer or a boundary flux?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A boundary flux. Left alone, fossil carbon is a pool with almost no outflow; burning it opens a large flux from a slow pool into the fast ones. Photosynthesis and respiration, however large, stay inside the line.

</details>

## The nitrogen cycle: microbes run most of the transformations

The atmosphere is about 78 percent N2, but its triple bond puts it out of reach of most organisms. Most of the microbial transformations are **redox** reactions: they move electrons (oxidation removes them, reduction adds them). Nitrogen's oxidation state runs from −3 in ammonium through 0 in N2 to +5 in nitrate. Five classic steps:

- **Fixation** reduces N2 to ammonia, using the enzyme nitrogenase. Some bacteria and archaea do it, free-living or in symbioses such as rhizobia in legume root nodules. Lightning and fertiliser manufacture also fix nitrogen.
- **Ammonification** releases ammonium from organic nitrogen as decomposers break down dead matter, with no change in oxidation state (amino-acid nitrogen is covered in [protein turnover and amino acid catabolism](/lessons/protein-turnover-amino-acid-catabolism/)).
- **Nitrification** oxidises ammonia through nitrite to nitrate and requires oxygen. The textbook version uses two groups: ammonia oxidisers (bacteria, and archaea such as the marine isolate Könneke and colleagues reported in 2005) and nitrite oxidisers.
- **Denitrification** reduces nitrate or nitrite, in low-oxygen soils and sediments, to gases that can include NO, N2O and N2.
- **Leaching** carries nitrate in drainage water to streams and rivers; it is transport in water, not a reaction.

Which steps cross the boundary depends on where the line is drawn. For the watershed line, fixation is an input; denitrification and the nitrate leaving at the stream outlet are outputs; ammonification, nitrification, plant uptake and litter fall stay inside as the loop.

Two discoveries have added to that list. One is **anammox** (anaerobic ammonium oxidation), which joins ammonium and nitrite into N2 without oxygen, another gas output:

> **NH4+ + NO2− → N2 + 2 H2O**

First described in wastewater bioreactors, anammox was found by Kuypers and colleagues in the Black Sea, just below the layer of water that still has oxygen, and has been measured in marine sediments: in some oxygen-poor waters and sediments, denitrification is not the only biological route back to N2. The other is **comammox**: Nitrospira bacteria that carry out complete ammonia oxidation to nitrate within one organism, so the two chemical steps of nitrification need not belong to two organisms.

## Phosphorus: the element no microbe can fix

Phosphorus has no major gas, so no organism can pull it from the air as nitrogen fixers pull N2. It enters ecosystems mainly as phosphate-bearing rock weathers, and much of it ends in marine sediments; over geological time, uplift of those sediments is the main route back to land (seabirds and fish that migrate from the sea up rivers return a smaller amount). In soil, phosphate binds to mineral surfaces and comes off again (**sorption** and **desorption**), microbes store some in their biomass and free phosphate from dead matter with enzymes called phosphatases, and plants take up the dissolved form, orthophosphate. Every nucleotide carries it ([nucleotide metabolism](/lessons/nucleotide-metabolism/)). Dust and smoke do carry some through the air on particles (Going deeper).

In budget terms, with the boundary drawn around a lake's water and its sediments, its phosphorus and nitrogen both rise only when inputs exceed outputs, and phosphorus released from sediment is an internal transfer (around the water alone, it would be an input). But nitrogen has an input phosphorus lacks: fixation from the air. A nutrient is **limiting** when adding it on its own increases production, and a lake rich in nutrients and dense with algae is **eutrophic**. Which input sets a lake's algae? ## What a budget can and cannot show

Every result above rests on the one rule: a store changes only by what crosses its boundary. So a budget is only as good as its measured fluxes.

**Worked check.** Global Carbon Budget 2024 estimated, for 2023, emissions E = 11.1 ± 0.9 GtC yr⁻¹ (gigatonnes of carbon per year), atmospheric accumulation A = 5.9 ± 0.2, ocean sink O = 2.9 ± 0.4 and land sink L = 2.3 ± 1.0 (± one standard deviation, a measure of uncertainty). The residual is:

> **B = E − A − O − L = 11.1 − 5.9 − 2.9 − 2.3 = 0.0 GtC yr⁻¹**

**Predict.** The residual is zero. Does that show every term was measured well?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The zero comes from rounding (the unrounded source figure is about −0.02), and the land sink alone is uncertain by ±1.0, nearly half its value and far more than the residual. Errors in two terms could cancel and still give zero.

</details>

Budgets often fail to close. Four common reasons:

- **Unmeasured fluxes.** N2 lost by denitrification is hard to detect against an atmosphere that is mostly N2. Rock can be an input: weathering of nitrogen-rich mica schist supplied nitrogen at forest sites in the Klamath Mountains.
- **Sampling frequency and record length.** Li and colleagues (2019), subsampling a daily watershed record, found that the uncertainty of estimated nutrient export depended on how often samples were taken. In a mid-latitude hardwood forest, Melillo and colleagues (2017) found that over 26 years of soil warming, phases of carbon loss alternated with phases of no detectable loss. A short record would have misled.
- **Tracer bias.** Adding 15N2 (N2 made with the heavy isotope 15N, so it can be traced) as a gas bubble, which does not fully equilibrate with water, can underestimate nitrogen fixation (Mohr and colleagues, 2010).
- **Compensating errors**, as in the carbon check above.

A lake manager has a different lake with summer algal blooms and wants to know whether to control nitrogen or phosphorus. Using the two designs from the second film, plan the test, and say what it can and cannot show.

Manipulate the whole lake: bottle experiments miss slow responses such as rising nitrogen fixation, nutrient release from sediments or changes in grazing. ## Going deeper

### Dates and discovery chronologies

The Hubbard Brook watershed was cut between 18 November and 31 December 1965, and herbicide kept regrowth down for the next three growing seasons.

Kuypers and colleagues published the Black Sea anammox result in 2003. In 2015 two groups, van Kessel and colleagues and Daims and colleagues, each reported in Nature enriched Nitrospira that perform complete ammonia oxidation to nitrate within one organism. In 2017 Kits and colleagues isolated one of them, Nitrospira inopinata, in pure culture and showed that it oxidised ammonia to nitrate stoichiometrically.

The Lake 226 result appeared in Science in 1974. A PNAS profile records that Schindler was invited in 1968 to head the experimental-lakes project.

### A new case of fixation

UCYN-A is a nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterium that lives inside one marine alga, Braarudosphaera bigelowii. In 2024 Coale and colleagues interpreted it as an early-stage nitrogen-fixing organelle; the result is for that alga and its partner.

### Phosphorus: aerosol source and terrestrial limitation figures

Barkley and colleagues (2019), sampling aerosols at Cayenne, French Guiana, and modelling their transport, reported that African aerosols carry phosphorus to the Amazon and tropical Atlantic, and that smoke from biomass burning in southern Africa, not only desert dust, is a substantial source of the soluble phosphorus.

Hou and colleagues' 2020 synthesis found significant phosphorus limitation in 46.2 percent of 652 field phosphorus-addition experiments in natural ecosystems and croplands, including outside the tropics. In the 436 experiments in natural terrestrial ecosystems, phosphorus addition raised aboveground plant production by a mean of 34.9 percent.

### How loose is a zero?

If the four carbon-budget uncertainties were independent, the residual's standard uncertainty would be about ±1.4 GtC yr⁻¹ (the square root of 0.9² + 0.2² + 0.4² + 1.0²), far larger than the unrounded residual of about −0.02.

### Measurement-error case details

Morford and colleagues estimated 1.6 to 10.7 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ from weathering at three Klamath Mountains forest sites on nitrogen-rich mica schist. Mohr and colleagues (2010) found that the bubble method underestimated fixation by 60 percent in a 12-hour incubation of Crocosphaera watsonii cultures.
