## Explain: what succession is

<WordBank terms={['ecological succession', 'community', 'disturbance', 'primary succession', 'secondary succession', 'pioneer species', 'abiotic factors', 'biotic factors', 'climax community', 'biodiversity']} />

A **community** is all the populations of different species living in one area. **Ecological succession** is the change, over years to centuries, in which species live in an area. The change is predictable in a general way: certain kinds of plants tend to come early, and others tend to come late.

Succession often starts after a **disturbance**, an event such as a fire, a flood, a storm or plowing that removes much of a community. What the disturbance leaves behind decides which kind of succession follows.

**Primary succession** is succession on bare rock or new land where there is no soil. Bare rock, sand and silt left by a melting glacier and fresh lava are examples. The first organisms to live there are **pioneer species**, usually lichens and mosses that can grow on rock. As they grow, they help break down the rock, and when they die they add the first thin layer of soil.

**Secondary succession** is succession after a disturbance that leaves the soil in place. Examples are an abandoned farm field, a burned forest and a flooded meadow. The soil already holds nutrients, seeds and roots, so secondary succession usually reaches forest much faster than primary succession.

| | Primary succession | Secondary succession |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Bare rock or new land, no soil | Soil remains after a disturbance |
| Virginia or nearby example | Rare in Virginia; a glacier’s retreat in Alaska | Abandoned Piedmont field, burned forest |
| First arrivals | Lichens and mosses | Weeds, grasses, seeds already in the soil |
| Speed | Usually centuries | Usually decades to a century or more |

## Explain: how a Virginia field becomes a forest

Each stage changes the place it grows in. **Abiotic factors** are the nonliving parts of an ecosystem, such as light, water, temperature and soil. **Biotic factors** are the living parts, such as plants, animals, fungi and microbes. The plants of one stage change the abiotic factors, and the new conditions favor different plants.

Here is the usual path published for old Piedmont fields just south of Virginia.

- **The first years.** Weeds such as horseweed and asters grow fast in full sun. By about the third year, broomsedge, a tall grass, often covers the field.
- **Pines move in.** Where seed trees grow nearby, pine seedlings and blackberry appear after about five years. Pines grow fast in full sun, and within a few decades the field becomes a young pine forest.
- **Shade builds.** A closed pine canopy blocks much of the sunlight. Pine seedlings grow poorly in that shade. Young hardwoods, such as oaks and hickories, cope with shade better and grow slowly underneath.
- **Hardwoods take over.** As the old pines die over the next several decades, the hardwoods below fill the gaps. After a century or more, hardwoods dominate, and one Virginia source puts a mostly hardwood forest at about 200 years.

The late stage that stays fairly stable when no major disturbance strikes is the **climax community**. In most of Virginia, the climax community is a deciduous oak-hickory forest. A USDA Forest Service inventory estimated that in 2021 the oak-hickory forest type covered about 61 percent of Virginia’s forest land, and loblolly-shortleaf pine about 19 percent.

## Explain: disturbance keeps the story open

The climax community is a model. Ecologists now see the late stage as less fixed than older textbooks did. Storms, insects, floods and fires keep opening gaps, so a real landscape is a patchwork of fields, pine stands and hardwood forest of different ages.

Disturbance is not always harmful. In the North Carolina experiment from the opening, only cutting plus fire opened the forest canopy, and young oak saplings were more common where it was open. The same study found no clear difference in the smallest oak seedlings, so the benefit depended on the treatment and on the age of the plant.

Succession also changes **biodiversity**, the variety of living things in an area. One simple measure is the number of species recorded in a place. It can rise or fall from stage to stage, so scientists say which measure they used.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Disturbances reset parts of a landscape, so succession can start over in patches, and fire can sometimes favor certain species.</Support>

### Design problem

The lesson says pine seedlings grow poorly in shade, while young hardwoods cope better. Plan a fair test of whether shade slows the growth of pine seedlings more than oak seedlings, using seedlings in pots, shade cloth and a ruler.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Question and hypothesis: If seedlings grow under shade cloth, then pine seedlings will grow less than in full sun, and oak seedlings will be slowed less, because pines need bright light.
- Independent variables: light (full sun or shade cloth) and kind of seedling (pine or oak).
- Dependent variable: increase in height, in centimeters, after 8 weeks.
- Constants: the same pot size, the same soil from one bag, the same water on the same schedule, seedlings of similar starting height, the same place outdoors.
- Control: the seedlings in full sun are the comparison group for each kind of tree.
- Repeated trials and sample size: at least 10 seedlings in each of the four groups, with the mean height gain calculated for each group.
- Safety and ethics: buy seedlings from a nursery instead of digging them from the wild, wear gloves for soil, and plant the seedlings out afterward.

Bigger gains in sun than in shade, with a larger drop for pines, would support the hypothesis. Height is one measure; dry mass would make the test stronger.

</details>
