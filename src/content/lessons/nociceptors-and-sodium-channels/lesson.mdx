summary: "A sodium-channel change can alter a nociceptor's spike initiation, so what the neuron does depends on how many channels are available at rest, not only on how easily they open. Human sodium-channel genetics gives pain biology its cleanest loss-of-function evidence: loss of SCN9A removes pain, and other variants in the same gene cause pain syndromes. The change can act through channel number, gating and availability, sometimes by shifting the resting potential. This lesson works through that genetics, including the Nav1.9 variants that break the rule that more channel current means more pain, and then takes a Nav1.8 blocker from a fitted channel curve to a phase 3 outcome. It also sorts molecular labels from conduction classes from functional states and reads two complementary mouse lesion experiments. You will be able to calculate residual channel current from a concentration curve, say why that is not neuronal silence, and weigh competing accounts of Nav1.7-null analgesia."
hook: "Three families in which nobody could feel pain pointed to one sodium channel. Twenty years later, the drug that reached patients blocks a different one."

  - name: "C. Geoffrey Woods"
    contribution: >-
      With James Cox and colleagues, mapped congenital inability to experience pain in three
      Pakistani families to recessive nonsense mutations in SCN9A, the gene for Nav1.7, and showed
      the mutant human channels passed no current above background in HEK293 cells.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Cox JJ, Reimann F, Nicholas AK, et al., Nature 444:894 (2006), doi:10.1038/nature05413."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Human genetics can find a channel that no screen in a dish would nominate. Working with
      three consanguineous families from northern Pakistan whose affected members had never
      experienced pain, Woods, James Cox, Frank Reimann and their colleagues combined pedigree
      mapping with sequencing and then tested the candidate directly. Six affected children,
      aged 4 to 14, were examined clinically. Three different homozygous nonsense mutations in
      SCN9A segregated with the condition. When the mutant human Nav1.7 constructs were expressed
      with accessory subunits in HEK293 cells, the currents were no greater than background, so
      the genetic argument and the biophysical one pointed the same way: lose this one channel and
      the painful world goes quiet while touch continues. The loss is not confined to pain: Weiss and colleagues later showed that people with SCN9A loss of function also cannot smell. The children could still tell hot from cold food and drink, although temperature sensation was not rigorously assessed. The 2006 paper turned Nav1.7 into
      a major target for analgesic drug development, and it set the standard that a
      human pain gene should come with a channel measurement attached.
  - name: "Stephen G. Waxman"
    contribution: >-
      With Sulayman Dib-Hajj, Theodore Cummins and colleagues, measured the gating changes caused
      by human Nav1.7 and Nav1.9 variants, including erythermalgia mutants that activate at more
      negative voltages and Nav1.9 variants that depolarize neurons and impair firing.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Cummins TR, Dib-Hajj SD, Waxman SG, J. Neurosci. 24:8232 (2004); Huang J, Vanoye CG, Cutts A, et al., J. Clin. Invest. 127:2805 (2017)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      A variant list is not a mechanism. Waxman's group at Yale built its programme on taking each
      human sodium-channel variant into a recording rig and asking which gating step it changed.
      After Yang and colleagues identified SCN9A missense variants in a Chinese family and a
      sporadic patient with primary erythermalgia in 2004, Cummins, Dib-Hajj and Waxman expressed
      two of those mutants, I848T and L858H, in HEK293 cells and found activation shifted toward
      more negative voltages and slowed deactivation: a channel that opens too easily and closes
      too late. The same approach produced the result that complicates the simple story. In 2017
      Huang, Vanoye, Dib-Hajj, Waxman and colleagues expressed human Nav1.9 variants in rat dorsal
      root ganglion neurons; L1302F and L811P depolarized the cells and impaired action-potential
      generation. Gain of channel function, loss of neuronal output. The lesson for anyone reading
      a new variant is that the behaviour follows from the whole neuron, not the channel alone.

  - name: "Bruce P. Bean"
    contribution: >-
      With Robert Stewart and colleagues, recorded from dissociated adult human dorsal root
      ganglion neurons at 37 °C and showed that potent Nav1.8 inhibition by suzetrigine reshaped
      the action potential while reducing, but generally not eliminating, repetitive firing.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Stewart RG, Osorno T, Fujita A, et al., Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 122:e2503570122 (2025), doi:10.1073/pnas.2503570122."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      When a sodium-channel blocker reached patients in 2025, the obvious question was what it
      does to a human nociceptor. Bean's laboratory answered it in the preparation that matters:
      dorsal root ganglion neurons from adult human organ donors, dissociated and recorded at
      37 °C rather than at room temperature, where sodium-channel kinetics are slower. Fitting a
      1:1 concentration-response model to recombinant human Nav1.8 with the beta-3 subunit in CHO
      cells gave a dissociation constant of 0.38 nM at 37 °C and 0.072 nM at 21 °C, so the
      temperature of the rig changes the number by more than fivefold. In the human neurons,
      suzetrigine generally reduced but did not eliminate repetitive firing; at 10 nM it lowered
      the spike peak and shoulder while largely sparing threshold and the initial upstroke. The
      work put a ceiling on what blocking the channel can be taken to mean.

techniques:
  - name: "Diphtheria toxin receptor cell ablation"
    blurb: >-
      A Cre- or knock-in-driven human diphtheria toxin receptor makes only the targeted cells
      sensitive to injected toxin, so a genetically defined population can be killed in an adult
      animal. The experiment needs histological counts showing how much of
      the population actually went, and a comparison against loss from birth shows whether development compensated.
  - name: "Whole-cell voltage clamp with soma indentation"
    blurb: >-
      A patch pipette clamps a dissociated sensory neuron while a calibrated probe pushes on its
      cell body. The evoked current reports mechanosensitivity of the somatic membrane in that
      culture, which is a proxy for the ending rather than a recording from it.
  - name: "Pedigree linkage with heterologous channel expression"
    blurb: >-
      Sequencing in informative families ties a phenotype to a variant; expressing the wild-type
      and mutant channel in a cell line then asks what the variant does to current and gating.
      Together they give a requirement in people plus a channel-level defect, which further experiments have to connect to the behaviour of the whole neuron.
  - name: "Randomized double-blind trial with SPID48"
    blurb: >-
      Patients report pain on a numeric scale after surgery, and the sum of baseline-relative
      improvement weighted over 48 hours gives one number per patient, in units of point-hours. It measures integrated
      self-reported relief in that setting and over that window, and nothing beyond it.

  - stem: "A patient carries a recessive nonsense SCN9A genotype. Which result from the 2006 study most directly shows that the mutant channels carry no measurable current?"
    choices:
      - "The affected family members reported having never experienced pain"
      - "The mutant constructs passed no current above background in HEK293 cells"
      - "The three mutations were found in families from northern Pakistan"
      - "Six affected children aged 4 to 14 were examined clinically by the authors"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Expressing the mutant channel and finding no current above background is the biophysical
      measurement, which is why the heterologous assay was done at all. The clinical examination
      and the family reports establish the human phenotype rather than channel behaviour, and the
      geographic origin of the families speaks to consanguinity and mapping power, not to whether
      the protein conducts.
  - stem: "Human Nav1.9 L1302F depolarized the average resting potential of transfected rat dorsal root ganglion neurons by about 11.5 mV relative to wild-type Nav1.9 and impaired action-potential generation. Why is this called a gain of channel function?"
    choices:
      - "The variant removes the channel protein, which depolarizes the neuron"
      - "The variant blocks potassium channels, which depolarizes the neuron"
      - "The variant passes less current near rest, which depolarizes the neuron"
      - "The variant passes more current near rest, which depolarizes the neuron"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Nav1.9 carries a persistent current whose activation and inactivation overlap near rest, so a
      variant that increases it holds the membrane depolarized; fewer of the neuron's other sodium
      channels then remain available and firing is impaired. Less current would hyperpolarize rather than depolarize the
      cell, loss of the protein is the opposite of what was measured, and nothing in the experiment
      tested or required an action on potassium channels.
  - stem: "Two nociceptors carry Nav1.7-like channels with an illustrative steady-state availability curve of V½ = −65 mV and k = 7 mV. One rests at −70 mV, the other at −55 mV. Each is depolarized by 5 mV and held there. Which neuron keeps the larger fraction of the availability it started with?"
    choices:
      - "The neuron starting at −70 mV, which keeps about three-quarters of it"
      - "The neuron starting at −55 mV, which keeps about three-quarters of it"
      - "Both keep the same fraction, because both were depolarized by 5 mV"
      - "Neither keeps any, because 5 mV spans the whole availability curve"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Substituting into h(V) = 1/(1 + exp[(V − V½)/k]) gives 0.67 at −70 mV falling to 0.50 at
      −65 mV, which keeps about 74% of the starting value, and 0.19 at −55 mV falling to 0.11 at
      −50 mV, which keeps about 54%. So the neuron starting at −70 mV loses the smaller proportion,
      and the option assigning three-quarters to the neuron starting at −55 mV reverses the two.
      The fraction kept is not the same for both, because the curve is steepest near its midpoint
      and the two neurons sit on different parts of it; and 5 mV is less than one slope factor, so
      it cannot take either neuron across the whole curve. Note what this calculation does not
      settle: both steps also change the distance to a threshold that can itself move, so these
      numbers say which neuron can better afford the step, not whether either one fires more.
  - stem: "PF-05089771 inhibited half-inactivated recombinant human Nav1.7 with an IC50 of 11 nM and resting channels with an IC50 of about 10 μM. What does this difference describe?"
    choices:
      - "A difference between recombinant and native channels of one subtype"
      - "Selectivity between channel subtypes, not state dependence of block"
      - "State dependence of block, not selectivity between channel subtypes"
      - "A difference between human and rodent channels in the same state"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The two numbers come from the same human Nav1.7 channel held at different voltages, so they
      report how much the drug prefers the inactivated conformation. Subtype selectivity would
      require comparing Nav1.7 with other subtypes; no second species was compared here; and both
      protocols used the same recombinant channels in HEK293 cells rather than native ones.
  - stem: "Why is it wrong to treat peptidergic, non-peptidergic, Aδ and mechanoinsensitive as four exclusive bins of nociceptor?"
    choices:
      - "They describe four molecular labels, so none of them can overlap at all"
      - "They describe molecular label, conduction class and functional state, so they overlap"
      - "They describe four conduction classes, which cannot be assigned to one neuron"
      - "They describe four behavioural assays, so none of them refers to neurons"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The categories come from different kinds of measurement: CGRP and MRGPRD are molecular
      markers, Aδ and C are conduction classes, and mechanoinsensitivity is a state that can
      change. Prato and colleagues found CHRNA3-labelled mechanoinsensitive neurons that are
      peptidergic, and Ghitani and colleagues found Calca-labelled Aδ high-threshold
      mechanoreceptors, so labels cut across conduction classes. Calling them four molecular
      labels or four conduction classes misstates what CGRP, Calca, Aδ and C each report, and the
      categories are properties of neurons rather than names of behavioural assays.
  - stem: "In Cavanaugh and colleagues (2009), mice whose Mrgprd-positive neurons were deleted from birth showed no detectable mechanical deficit, while adult ablation raised the von Frey threshold. What does the comparison show?"
    choices:
      - "That developmental loss and adult loss are different experiments"
      - "That Mrgprd-positive neurons carry no mechanical signal at any age"
      - "That von Frey filaments cannot measure mechanical thresholds in mice"
      - "That the adult ablation must have killed additional unrelated neurons"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The same population removed at two different times gave two different outcomes, which is why
      the authors read the early-loss result as compensation and why a developmental knockout
      cannot stand in for an adult one. Off-target killing is not supported, since the histology
      verified loss of more than 98% of Mrgprd-positive somata; the adult result itself shows the
      neurons do contribute to mechanical responses; and the assay detected a change in the adult
      animals, so it was not insensitive.

  - stem: "A fitted 1:1 concentration-response model for recombinant human Nav1.8 gives I(C) = Imax/(1 + C/Kd) with Kd = 0.38 nM at 37 °C. A student wants the drug concentration that leaves 10% of the fitted maximal current. Which value is closest?"
    choices:
      - "0.38 nM"
      - "38 nM"
      - "0.042 nM"
      - "3.4 nM"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Setting 0.10 = 1/(1 + C/0.38) gives 1 + C/0.38 = 10, so C = 9 × 0.38 = 3.42 nM. The smallest
      value is Kd divided by 9 and would leave about 90% of the current; Kd itself leaves 50% by
      definition of the fit; and 38 nM corresponds to about 99% inhibition, which is 99 × Kd rather
      than 9 × Kd.
  - stem: "Two phase 3 trials randomized 1,118 adults after abdominoplasty and 1,073 after bunionectomy to suzetrigine, placebo or hydrocodone/acetaminophen, measuring SPID48. Suzetrigine beat placebo in both; neither trial demonstrated superiority to the active comparator. A reader concludes the drug works as well as the opioid comparator. What is wrong?"
    choices:
      - "A failed superiority test does not establish equivalence"
      - "The trials were too large for a comparator claim to be made"
      - "SPID48 cannot compare two active drugs within one trial"
      - "A failed superiority test establishes inferiority to the comparator"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Superiority, non-inferiority and equivalence are separate hypotheses with separate margins,
      so failing to show one does not establish another, although the confidence interval still shows which differences are compatible with the data. Inferiority was likewise not a prespecified hypothesis, although in the prespecified bunionectomy analysis the SPID48 difference favoured hydrocodone with acetaminophen by 20.2 point-hours (95% CI 7.7 to 32.7);
      SPID48 was in fact the outcome used for the comparator arms; and larger samples make
      comparisons more informative rather than less.

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm five"
    text: >-
      Your skin has tiny alarm wires that tell your brain when something hurts. The alarm travels
      along the wire using little gates that let salt in. Some people are born with a broken gate
      and never feel pain at all. Scientists are building medicines that hold one kind of gate
      shut so the alarm is quieter.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Pain signals start in sensory neurons called nociceptors and travel to the spinal cord as
      action potentials, which need voltage-gated sodium channels. Three of those channels,
      Nav1.7, Nav1.8 and Nav1.9, are concentrated in these neurons. People with two broken copies
      of the Nav1.7 gene cannot feel pain; people with other changes in the same gene feel burning
      pain in the hands and feet. That makes these channels obvious drug targets, and in 2025 a
      Nav1.8 blocker was approved in the United States for moderate-to-severe short-term pain in adults.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      Nociceptors are not a single type. CGRP marks peptidergic afferents, MRGPRD marks an
      IB4-binding non-peptidergic population, Calca also labels Aδ high-threshold mechanoreceptors
      with circumferential hair-follicle endings, and CHRNA3 marks mechanoinsensitive neurons that
      acquire mechanosensitivity after nerve growth factor by a PIEZO2-dependent route. Molecular
      label, conduction class and functional state are three different axes. Their action potentials
      draw on the sodium channels Nav1.7 (SCN9A), Nav1.8 (SCN10A) and Nav1.9 (SCN11A), the last
      two tetrodotoxin resistant. Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 contribute to the spike itself, while Nav1.9 mainly sets the resting potential and subthreshold excitability. Recessive SCN9A nonsense alleles abolish pain, while gain-of-function
      variants shift activation negative or impair fast inactivation and cause pain syndromes.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      The pharmacology has separated from the genetics. Nav1.7 has the cleanest human loss-of-function
      argument, yet selective inhibitors such as PF-05089771, whose IC50 was 11 nM against
      half-inactivated and about 10 μM against resting recombinant human channels, did not improve
      the clinical endpoints tested, with dose, selectivity margin and tissue access all proposed as
      explanations. Nav1.8 inhibition instead produced postoperative trials that were positive against placebo but did not show superiority over an opioid comparator, with one prespecified comparator analysis favouring the opioid,
      while recordings from dissociated adult human dorsal root ganglion neurons at 37 °C show that
      strong block reshapes the spike without silencing repetitive firing. The open question is
      mechanistic: mouse Nav1.7-null analgesia has been attributed to endogenous opioid signalling,
      to opioid-dependent suppression of central-terminal transmission, and to a requirement for
      Nav1.7 in C-fibre spike initiation with the enkephalin change unnecessary. Deletion timing,
      Cre pattern and recording site differ across those studies and have not been matched.

  - title: "One participant, one infusion"
    source: "Minett MS, Pereira V, Sikandar S, et al., Nature Communications 6:8967 (2015)."
    text: >-
      When Minett, Wood and colleagues reported that naloxone substantially reversed analgesia in
      their conditional Nav1.7-null mice, they added a human arm: a blinded saline-versus-naloxone
      comparison in one 39-year-old woman with Nav1.7-null congenital pain insensitivity. During
      the naloxone infusion she detected 80% of brief radiant heat pulses, having detected none at
      baseline or with saline. It is a striking result and a sample of one, and the opioid
      dependence at issue is an endogenous mechanism rather than anything to do with addiction.
  - title: "A blocker that does not silence the neuron"

**Three families in northern Pakistan contained people who had never experienced pain, and the cause was two broken copies of one sodium-channel gene. The sodium-channel blocker that reached patients in 2025 targets a different channel.**

Those two sentences set the problem. Human genetics nominated Nav1.7; the drug that worked blocks Nav1.8. Why the two came apart is the subject of this lesson: the end of the Nav1.8 section returns to this puzzle and explains why a genetic requirement does not guarantee that a particular inhibitor will relieve pain.

You have met the groundwork in [touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/), which separates nociception from pain, sorts fibres by diameter and velocity, follows the spinothalamic crossing, and takes TRPV1, TRPM8, TRPA1 and Piezo2 to the level of necessity. This lesson picks up where that one stops. Two questions run through everything that follows: which molecularly defined nociceptors do what, and what human sodium-channel genetics and pharmacology have actually shown.

## What a sodium-channel result can and cannot predict

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A sodium-channel change reaches behaviour through what it does to the neuron's spike initiation, and that depends on how many sodium channels are **available** at the resting potential — not sitting inactivated, and so free to open — and not only on how easily they open. So more current and less current do not predict more pain and less pain. Ask three questions in order: what did the change do to the channel, what did that do to the resting potential and to channel availability, and only then what did that do to firing.
>
> **Scope.** This is a statement about spike initiation in nociceptors carrying Nav1.7, Nav1.8 and Nav1.9, in the preparations this lesson reads, and it does not say what a person feels. Release at the central terminal is a second route from channel to behaviour. Keep the levels of evidence apart as you read: a channel recording measures current, a neuronal recording measures firing, a mouse withdrawal measures a reflex, and the human studies and clinical phenotypes include reports of pain, which the surgical trials quantify with SPID48. *Where the idea stops*, at the end, lists the rest of the limits.

## Three channels, one spike

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Nociceptor spikes draw on three channels concentrated in these neurons: Nav1.7 (gene SCN9A), Nav1.8 (SCN10A) and Nav1.9 (SCN11A), the last two tetrodotoxin resistant, which is how their currents are isolated in a recording. Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 contribute to the spike itself, while Nav1.9 mainly sets the resting potential and subthreshold excitability. How sodium channels activate and inactivate is the business of [the action potential](/lessons/action-potential/) and [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/).

Two kinds of sodium current matter here, and keeping them apart is most of the lesson. A **transient current** rises when the membrane depolarizes and then inactivates within milliseconds; that is the current a spike is built from. A **persistent current** stays appreciable while the membrane sits still, because its inactivation is slow or incomplete rather than absent. What persists is a population current: individual channels keep opening, closing and inactivating under it. Nav1.9 supplies a persistent current, which is why it pulls on the resting potential rather than on the upstroke.

Now the quantity the rest of the lesson turns on. **Availability** is the fraction of voltage-gated sodium channels not sitting in the inactivated state, and so free to open when the membrane depolarizes. It depends on the voltage the membrane has been resting at, because inactivation accumulates while the membrane sits depolarized and channels recover from it only at negative voltages. Hold the voltage steady long enough and availability settles at a value read off a Boltzmann curve,

> **h(V) = 1 / (1 + exp[(V − V½) / k])**

with V½ the half-inactivation voltage and k about 7 mV for Nav1.7-like channels. Every voltage below is a held, steady-state one. The curve runs downhill: the more depolarized the rest, the fewer channels are left. So depolarization does two opposite things at once. It moves the membrane closer to threshold, which helps a neuron fire, and it costs availability, which removes the current the spike is built from.

One curve does not cover the whole neuron. Nav1.8 half-inactivates at far more depolarized voltages than Nav1.7, so a curve drawn for Nav1.7-like channels describes the channels that depolarization takes out first, not every sodium channel in the cell.

**Predict.** Two nociceptors carry the same channels. One rests at −70 mV, the other at −55 mV. Which has more sodium channels available, and which one stands to gain from a small depolarization? Commit to an answer before reading on.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Take an illustrative V½ of −65 mV and k of 7 mV, a curve in the range reported for Nav1.7-like channels — the TTX-sensitive ones, not the whole mixture the neuron carries.

- at −70 mV: h = 1/(1 + e^(−5/7)) = **0.67**, so about two-thirds of those channels are available;
- at −55 mV: h = 1/(1 + e^(10/7)) = **0.19**, so about one-fifth are.

Now give each neuron the same 5 mV depolarization and hold it there. The neuron starting at −70 mV keeps about three-quarters of the availability it had; the one starting at −55 mV keeps about half. So the same step costs the depolarized neuron proportionally more: it was already low on the curve, where there is less left to lose.

Stop there. You have a cost, in one subtype, for each cell — not what either cell does, because both steps also close the gap to a threshold that can itself move, and firing draws on the rest of the neuron's conductances. The arithmetic says which neuron can better afford the step. Its direction holds whatever midpoint you assume; its size does not, because the midpoint for a real neuron is a measurement.

</details>

## People who cannot feel pain, and people who feel too much

Human sodium-channel genetics gives pain biology its cleanest loss-of-function evidence, because the mutations are inherited, the phenotypes are extreme, and the channel can be put in a recording rig.

Cox, Woods and colleagues (2006) mapped congenital inability to experience pain in three consanguineous families from northern Pakistan and found three different homozygous nonsense mutations in SCN9A.

**Predict.** You have the pedigrees and three different nonsense alleles in one gene. What measurement would you insist on before believing that this gene causes the phenotype?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Put the mutant channel in a cell and look for current. Linkage and segregation tell you the variant travels with the phenotype; they cannot tell you the protein stopped working, and a nonsense allele is not by itself proof of a dead channel.

The authors did exactly that. Expressed with accessory subunits in HEK293 cells — a heterologous system in which wild-type and mutant constructs are compared against the recording background in control-transfected cells — the mutant human channels produced currents no greater than background. Loss of this channel, loss of pain, with the genetic argument and the biophysical one pointing the same way.

Note what the assay does and does not reach. No current above background means no measurable conduction; it does not distinguish an absent protein from a truncated, mislocalized or non-conducting one. Nor does a cDNA assay reproduce native SCN9A transcript processing or measure transcript and protein abundance in the patient's neurons: it tests whether the mutant construct produces functional current in the expression system.

</details>

The phenotype is not pain alone: Weiss and colleagues (2011) showed that people with the same loss of function are also completely anosmic. The pedigree logic behind reading such families is covered in [pedigrees](/lessons/pedigrees/), and why common pain variants need a different design in [complex traits and GWAS](/lessons/complex-traits-and-gwas/).

### The same gene, too much pain

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Variants in the same gene run the other way. These ones alter how the channel gates, and the channel protein they alter is expressed and conducting — unlike the recessive nonsense constructs, which gave no current above background.

**Predict.** The person has burning pain in the hands and feet, a syndrome called primary erythermalgia. In gating terms, what would the channel have to be doing wrong?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Three gating changes are each documented in pain-associated Nav1.7 variants: opening at voltages where the channel should be shut, closing too slowly once it has opened, and failing to inactivate so that current persists. Which ones travel together, and with which clinical pattern, is a separate question.

Yang and colleagues (2004) identified SCN9A missense variants in a Chinese primary erythermalgia family and a sporadic patient; Cummins, Dib-Hajj and Waxman then expressed two of them, I848T and L858H, in HEK293 cells at about 21 °C and found activation shifted toward more negative voltages **and** slowed deactivation. In these variants the first two changes came together: a channel that opens at voltages where it should be shut, and lingers. The third change belongs to a different clinical picture. In paroxysmal extreme pain disorder, Fertleman and colleagues (2006) tested three mutant Nav1.7 channels in vitro and found impaired fast inactivation with persistent sodium current. Same gene, distinct channel lesions, distinct phenotypes — and each of these is a measurement on a channel in a cell line, which still has to pass through a neuron before it reaches a symptom.

</details>

## Where more current breaks: Nav1.9

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

The tempting rule is that more sodium current means more pain. Nav1.9 breaks it, and watching it break is the most useful thing in this lesson.

Nav1.9 is not built like its neighbours. Its activation and steady-state availability curves overlap near the resting potential, and in that overlap — the **window** — some channels are open at any moment, so the population carries a standing inward current. That window current is the persistent current from the last section: not a spike, but a steady depolarizing pull on the resting potential, which is proposed to set subthreshold excitability.

Now two experiments on the same channel, pointing opposite ways.

- Okuda and colleagues (2016) made knock-in mice carrying Nav1.9 **R222S**, a variant found in familial episodic pain. Their dorsal root ganglion neurons showed **increased** evoked firing.
- Huang and colleagues (2017) expressed human Nav1.9 **L1302F** and **L811P**, variants associated with insensitivity to pain, in small dorsal root ganglion neurons from rats aged 4 to 6 weeks. Both **depolarized** the neurons and **impaired** action-potential generation; L1302F shifted the average resting potential by 11.5 mV relative to wild-type Nav1.9.

Both are gain of channel function. One raises firing; the other impairs it, and in a minority of the most depolarized cells abolishes it. A proposed reconciliation, before you work for it: different amounts of depolarization, acting on subtype-specific availability and on the rest of the conductance system, can either raise or impair excitability. These preparations were not matched for availability, so the calculation below illustrates one possible cost rather than the measured cause of their difference.

**Predict.** A variant increases a nociceptor's persistent sodium current, so the resting potential moves 11.5 mV in the positive direction. Does the neuron fire more, or less? Commit before you read on, and notice what you would need to know about the neuron to answer at all.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

You cannot answer from the current alone. You need to know how many of the neuron's **other** sodium channels are still available.

Put a number on the cost. Take a nociceptor resting at −60 mV, with the same illustrative Nav1.7-like curve, V½ = −65 mV and k = 7 mV:

- at −60 mV: h = 1/(1 + e^(5/7)) = **0.33**, so a third of those channels are available;
- after an 11.5 mV depolarization, at −48.5 mV: h = 1/(1 + e^(16.5/7)) = **0.086**.

Availability falls about **four-fold**, and the exact factor depends on the midpoint assumed: across plausible V½ values from −60 to −78 mV the loss runs from 3.1-fold to 4.9-fold. The direction does not depend on the assumption; the size does, which is why this is a rough, back-of-envelope argument rather than a measurement. It is also a cost in one subtype only — Nav1.8 is not on this curve — which is one reason not every depolarized cell went silent.

So both outcomes are open to the same channel. A neuron with availability to spare, as at −70 mV in the earlier prediction, can take a small depolarization, move closer to threshold and fire more; that is the pattern proposed for familial episodic pain, as in R222S. A neuron pushed 11.5 mV from −60 mV has given up three-quarters of the availability it had, and loses the current the upstroke is built from; that is the pattern proposed for insensitivity to pain, as in L1302F, where the most depolarized cells stopped firing. The channel gains; the neuron loses.

None of this is a fixed number of millivolts: the arithmetic gives a direction, not a prediction. And the preparations differ in more than availability — a knock-in mouse neuron against a rat neuron transfected with a human channel.

</details>

A channel measurement fixes what the protein does; the neuron's operating point decides what that means for firing. A variant list is not a mechanism.

## Blocking Nav1.8: from a fitted curve to a spike

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

Here is the opening puzzle in its proper place. Suzetrigine blocks Nav1.8 and reduced pain after surgery versus placebo in both phase 3 trials, while PF-05089771, a selective Nav1.7 blocker, did not beat placebo in painful diabetic neuropathy. Those are different pain conditions, so the two results are not a head-to-head comparison of targets.

Nav1.7 was the obvious target, and the compound tested here has not delivered. It had an IC50 of 11 nM against **half-inactivated** recombinant human Nav1.7 in HEK293 cells, meaning channels held at a voltage where much of the population has already inactivated, and about 10 μM against the same channels held at rest, nearly a thousandfold difference produced by voltage alone. That difference is the **state dependence of block**: how strongly the drug prefers one channel conformation over another, measured on one channel at two voltages.

State dependence is not selectivity. Selectivity would mean comparing subtypes, and this compound was only 11-fold selective over Nav1.2 in the pharmacology summarized by its healthy-volunteer study. In a randomized trial of 135 participants with painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy it did not significantly improve weekly average pain versus placebo at week 4 — which is not a measured absence of effect, only a failure to separate drug from placebo on that endpoint. The authors of the healthy-volunteer study proposed insufficient dose and inadequate access to the central nervous system as candidate explanations, not settled ones. How targets become drugs, and how often they do not, is [drugs against a part](/lessons/drugs-against-a-part/).

Nav1.8 went differently. Suzetrigine binds the Nav1.8 voltage-sensing domain 2 **allosterically**, at a site away from the pore, and stabilizes the closed state; it was highly selective over the other human Nav subtypes tested in a recombinant panel, and selectivity in a recombinant panel is not tissue selectivity in a patient. Two phase 3 trials randomized 1,118 adults after abdominoplasty and 1,073 after bunionectomy, with **SPID48** as the outcome: patients rate their pain repeatedly for 48 hours after surgery, and the baseline-relative improvement is summed over that window to give one number per patient, in point-hours. Note what that makes SPID48 — a measure of what patients reported, which is a different kind of evidence from channel, neuronal and animal-withdrawal measurements.

Suzetrigine beat placebo in both trials. Neither trial demonstrated superiority over hydrocodone with acetaminophen, and in the bunionectomy trial's prespecified comparator analysis SPID48 favoured the opioid. Superiority, non-inferiority and equivalence are three separate hypotheses with separate margins, so a failed superiority test is not equivalence — and here one trial's comparator analysis ran the other way. United States regulatory approval followed in January 2025 for moderate-to-severe acute pain in adults, an indication broader than the two postoperative surgical models in which the pivotal trials tested it.

**Worked problem.** Stewart and colleagues (2025) fitted a 1:1 concentration-response model to recombinant human Nav1.8 with the beta-3 subunit expressed in CHO cells, obtaining a dissociation constant of 0.38 nM at 37 °C.

**Back to the opening puzzle.** These results illustrate the gap between genetic requirement and pharmacological efficacy; the causes of their different clinical outcomes remain unresolved. Human genetics names a channel the behaviour requires. That is a statement about the neuron, not a promise that a drug can occupy enough of that channel, in the right conformation, in the tissue where it matters — and, as the human recordings show, even strong occupancy of the right channel need not silence a nociceptor. A clean genetic requirement and a useful drug are two different achievements.

## Which nociceptors? Labels, classes and states

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

The same discipline applies one level up, to populations. Textbook figures still divide nociceptors into bins: peptidergic, non-peptidergic, Aδ, C. The experiments of the last fifteen years do not support bins, because the categories come from three unrelated kinds of measurement.

A **molecular label** is a gene whose expression marks a population and gives genetic access to it. Cavanaugh and colleagues used CGRP as a marker of peptidergic afferents in mouse tissue, and the Mrgprd-positive population they studied binds the plant lectin IB4, the classical non-peptidergic stain. These are markers, not functions: CGRP is not a heat channel.

A **conduction class** is a velocity measured from an axon. Ghitani and colleagues identified mouse Aδ high-threshold mechanonociceptors whose endings wrap circumferentially around hair follicles, and their medium-diameter neurons were Calca-labelled, with conduction velocity measured in vivo. Calca encodes alpha-CGRP (and calcitonin, by alternative splicing). So a mechanonociceptor carries a peptidergic label, and the two axes cross.

A **functional state** is something a neuron can change. Prato and colleagues identified CHRNA3-positive mechanoinsensitive peptidergic C-fibre nociceptors in cultured lumbar dorsal root ganglion neurons from mice aged 8 to 12 weeks. Take the three conditions one at a time. Untreated, almost none of these neurons gave a mechanically activated current when the soma was indented. After 24 hours with 50 ng/ml nerve growth factor, the currents appeared. With the growth factor plus siRNA knockdown of PIEZO2, they did not. So in a dish, and in the soma rather than an intact ending, silence was a state rather than a property, and PIEZO2 was required for the change.

## Ablate one population, lesion another

The cleanest way to ask what a molecularly defined population does is to remove it in an adult and test behaviour. Cavanaugh and colleagues (2009) did this in mice carrying a diphtheria toxin receptor in Mrgprd-positive neurons, and they did the control that makes the result readable: histology showed that toxin treatment eliminated more than 98% of Mrgprd-positive dorsal root ganglion cell bodies.

Behaviour then moved in one modality. Mechanical withdrawal threshold, measured with calibrated von Frey filaments, rose, while responses to heat and cold were spared within the tested stimulus ranges. The complementary experiment went the other way and used a different tool: a pharmacological lesion of TRPV1-positive central afferent branches abolished noxious heat sensitivity in those assays while baseline mechanical withdrawal thresholds were unchanged. Note that the lesion is defined by TRPV1 expression and placed at the central branches, which transmit the afferent signal rather than transduce heat at the skin. Read the two together and you have a functional dissociation in mice, in those stimulus ranges — not evidence that every individual fibre is unimodal, nor that mouse withdrawal reports human pain.

The third arm is the one most often dropped. Mice whose Mrgprd-positive neurons were deleted from birth lacked a detectable mechanical deficit in the later assays, which the authors read as compensation. Every genetic result in this field inherits that problem: a germline deletion is a developmental experiment, while adult manipulations — induced gene deletion, cell ablation, reversible silencing, acute pharmacology — ask what the adult circuit needs, and the two can disagree completely about the same cells.

### Design problem

Mice lacking Nav1.7 are analgesic, and three groups explain it differently. Minett, Wood and colleagues (2015) found upregulation of Penk, the proenkephalin gene whose product yields endogenous opioid peptides, and substantial naloxone reversal in conditional nulls aged 8 to 12 weeks, plus one 39-year-old woman in whom naloxone raised detection of brief heat pulses from none to 80%. MacDonald and colleagues (2021) reported an opioid-dependent reduction of transmission at nociceptor central terminals, the second route: release rather than spike initiation. Deng and colleagues (2023) localized the enkephalin increase to C-low-threshold mechanoreceptors, the unmyelinated afferents that respond to gentle stroking rather than to noxious stimuli, found it unnecessary for the analgesia, and showed Nav1.7 is required for C-fibre spike initiation. Design experiments to separate these.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Match the variables that differ before testing the mechanism.

1. **Timing.** Run the same assay on a developmental deletion and on a tamoxifen-induced adult deletion in the same driver line. The Mrgprd ablation result above shows how far these can diverge for one population.
2. **Deletion pattern.** Cre drivers determine where, and for inducible drivers when, the floxed gene is recombined, and the three studies did not use the same ones: Advillin-Cre, which targets sensory neurons; Wnt1-Cre, which targets neural-crest derivatives more broadly; and the full-body tamoxifen-inducible ERT-Cre knockout used by Deng and colleagues. Their deletion patterns have to be measured rather than assumed equivalent. Cross each driver to a reporter, score which populations recombine, and repeat the naloxone challenge in matched patterns before comparing outcomes.
3. **Recording site.** Behaviour cannot distinguish a failure to initiate a spike from a failure to release at the terminal. Record at both: in vivo C-fibre recordings for initiation, and dorsal horn synaptic transmission for the terminal, in the same animals.
4. **Opioid arm.** Include genotype-by-antagonist controls, blinding, and a positive control showing naloxone works in that preparation. Endogenous opioid dependence here means a mechanism inside the animal, with nothing to do with addiction.
5. **Scope.** The human evidence is three Nav1.7-null participants: the woman reported in 2015, and two men in MacDonald and colleagues (2021). Naloxone enhanced pain sensitivity in the woman and in one of the two men, and had no apparent effect in the other, so the positive tally is two of three. That is informative and not decisive. A mechanistic claim about people needs a larger cohort with a measured opioid manipulation.

The result that would settle most of it: an adult-induced, nociceptor-restricted deletion with the enkephalin increase blocked, tested for analgesia and recorded at both the initiation site and the terminal.

</details>

### Where the idea stops

- **It is a rule about spike initiation, and spike initiation is not the only route.** Transmitter release at the central terminal can change while peripheral firing is preserved, which is what the design problem turns on.
- **Keep the levels of evidence apart.** The channel recordings, the neuronal recordings and the mouse withdrawal assays do not report what anyone feels; the human studies and clinical phenotypes do include reports of pain, and the surgical trials quantify them with SPID48. Connecting a channel to a report takes every step in between.
- **Availability gives a direction, not an outcome.** It is one subtype's reserve, measured at steady state, and firing also depends on the size of the depolarization, a threshold that can move and the rest of the neuron's conductances.
- **Block is not silence.** A fitted concentration-response curve describes a recombinant current. Whether a neuron stops firing is a separate measurement, and in human neurons it generally did not.
- **A label is not a function, and a deletion carries its timing.** Marker genes give access to populations; what those populations do has to be measured, in adults as well as from birth.

## Going deeper

### Resting potential and spike threshold

Huang and colleagues offer an account that reconciles the two directions of the Nav1.9 story. In non-transfected rat dorsal root ganglion neurons held at a range of membrane potentials, the minimum injected current needed to evoke a spike varied non-monotonically: it fell with a few millivolts of depolarization and rose again with more. That is the measured counterpart of the account above. Note what it was — the current needed to fire a cell in one preparation, not a universal relation between resting potential and voltage threshold. Where the bottom of that curve sits, and whether another neuron shows a useful dip, requires measurement or a model that includes the relevant conductances and the stimulation protocol; starting availability alone does not locate it.

### The suzetrigine trial numbers

In phase 2, only the highest tested regimen reduced postoperative pain versus placebo over 48 hours. In phase 3, the abdominoplasty SPID48 difference versus placebo was 48.4 point-hours (95% CI 33.6 to 63.1); the bunionectomy difference versus placebo was smaller, 29.3 point-hours (95% CI 14.0 to 44.6), and in that trial's prespecified comparator analysis SPID48 favoured hydrocodone with acetaminophen by 20.2 point-hours (95% CI 7.7 to 32.7).

### Suzetrigine's selectivity panel

The reported figure is at least 31,000-fold selectivity over the other tested human Nav subtypes, measured in a recombinant panel.

### PF-05089771 in healthy volunteers

In a crossover study of 25 healthy males aged 18 to 55, a single 300 mg dose did not differ from placebo on the primary endpoints. That study is also the source of the pharmacology summary quoted above.

### The anosmia, and what differs between mice

In mice the olfactory failure is opioid independent while the analgesia is naloxone reversible (MacDonald and colleagues 2021).

### Numbers behind the nociceptor axes

Ghitani and colleagues' medium-diameter Calca-labelled neurons conducted at 3.6 ± 0.47 m/s on average in a sample of six in vivo extracellular recordings, which the authors classified as Aδ in that preparation, against a slower small-diameter comparison group in the C-fibre range. Where the boundary between the classes falls depends on the species and the recording conditions, so a velocity is read against the criteria of the study that measured it. CHRNA3-positive neurons were about 7.8 ± 1.6% of cells in cultured mouse L2 to L5 preparations, and about half of retrogradely traced peptidergic afferents innervating the visceral and deep somatic tissues the authors examined. Denominators travel with their tissue: neither is a count of cutaneous nociceptors, and neither is a human proportion.

### The Mrgprd ablation in numbers

In the mice with adult ablation of Mrgprd-positive neurons, mechanical withdrawal threshold rose from 0.41 ± 0.04 g to 0.99 ± 0.15 g (mean ± SEM).
