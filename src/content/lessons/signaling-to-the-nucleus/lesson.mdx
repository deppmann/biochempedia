techniques:
  - name: "Overexpressing a suspected drug target"
    blurb: >-
      Make cells produce far more of one protein than normal, then add the drug. If the cells
      with extra protein need more drug to be shut down, the drug is probably acting through
      that protein. Clipstone and Crabtree used this to tie cyclosporin A and FK506 to
      calcineurin inside T cells.
  - name: "Reporter gene assay"
    blurb: >-
      Join the DNA site for one transcription factor to a gene whose product is easy to
      measure, and put the construct into cells. The amount of product reports how active that
      factor is. Mutating the factor or drugging the pathway then shows which step the response
      needs.
  - name: "Blocking protein synthesis (the cycloheximide test)"
    blurb: >-
      Treat cells with a drug that stops ribosomes, give the signal, and measure mRNA. A gene
      that still switches on is an immediate early gene, because everything needed to
      transcribe it was already made. A gene that no longer switches on needs a product of the
      first wave.
  - name: "Fos staining to map active cells"
    blurb: >-
      Fix tissue a few hours after a stimulus (Sagar and colleagues fixed at three hours) and stain it with an antibody against the Fos
      protein. Stained nuclei mark cells that were strongly activated shortly before. The
      result is a snapshot of a whole brain at single-cell detail, with no timing finer than
      hours.

  - stem: "In a bZIP protein, what does the leucine zipper do?"
    choices:

      - "It joins two helices side by side into a pair"
      - "It lies in the major groove and reads the DNA bases"
      - "It carries the serine that PKA and CaMKIV phosphorylate"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The zipper is the stretch with a leucine at nearly every seventh position; two such helices pack
      their leucines together and form a pair. Reading the bases in the major groove is the job
      of the basic stretch, not the zipper. The serine that PKA and CaMKIV phosphorylate lies in
      a separate part of CREB, outside the bZIP region. Binding a zinc ion belongs to
      zinc-finger factors, a different family whose name is easy to confuse with this one.
  - stem: "Cells are treated with a drug that stops all protein synthesis and are then given a growth factor. Which result identifies a gene as an immediate early gene?"

      - "Its mRNA still rises within minutes"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      An immediate early gene is transcribed by factors that are already made, so its mRNA

    rationale: >-
      FK506, held by FKBP12, blocks calcineurin. Without calcineurin NFAT keeps the phosphates

  - stem: "A cell makes only a CREB in which serine 133 is changed to alanine. PKA and CaMKIV are both normal. After which signal will this CREB bind tightly to the KIX domain of CBP?"
    choices:

      - "After a calcium rise, but not after a cAMP rise"
      - "After neither a cAMP rise nor a calcium rise"
      - "After a cAMP rise and also after a calcium rise"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      PKA after cAMP and CaMKIV after calcium both act by phosphorylating serine 133, and KIX
      grips CREB far more tightly when that phosphate is present. Alanine has no hydroxyl group to

**Add a growth factor to a resting cell and, within minutes, it transcribes c-fos—even with every ribosome stopped.** The first film showed two phosphate switches flipping waiting factors; below is the four-way map of fast nuclear switches, followed by their chemistry, drug proofs, pairing math, and two-wave timing.

## Four ways a signal flips a waiting factor

A **transcription factor** is a protein that binds a short, specific DNA sequence near a gene and changes how often that gene is copied into RNA. Making more factor from scratch works, but transcription plus translation is slow. Fast pathways modify a pre-made factor in one of four ways:

| What the signal does | Example | Where you met it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adds a phosphate that lets the factor hold a co-activator | CREB | This lesson |
| Removes phosphates so the factor can enter the nucleus | NFAT | This lesson |
| Destroys the factor's inhibitor | NF-κB, freed when IκB is destroyed | The Toll-like receptor and NF-κB lesson |
| Makes the messenger itself the factor | STATs, Smads; the cut piece of Notch joins a DNA-bound partner, CSL | The JAK-STAT, TGF-β and Notch lessons |

Row one has other members—such as Elk-1, which ERK phosphorylates in the Ras-to-MAPK lesson. In all four, synthesis is done in advance, so the signal only flips the switch.

<Support>**The one idea.** Fast signals flip pre-made factors to switch on first-wave genes; paired factors widen gene choices and drive a slower second wave. **Scope:** Applies to rapid, stimulus-driven gene programs (minutes to hours) across growth-factor, immune, and neuronal pathways; slow developmental or steroid-receptor programs can differ, some first-wave genes encode non-factor proteins, and paired factors include both pre-made dimers (CREB) and newly assembled pairs (Fos–Jun).</Support>

## CREB: one serine, two messengers

The first film traced cAMP and calcium to one serine on CREB; here are the DNA site, the co-activator chemistry, and the neuronal channel route.

**CREB** (cAMP response element-binding protein) binds the eight-letter **cAMP response element (CRE)**, `TGACGTCA`, as a pair and sits largely on DNA before any signal arrives. Occupancy alone leaves the gene off.

Picking up from the cAMP and PKA lesson, cAMP frees PKA catalytic subunits, and some enter the nucleus to phosphorylate **serine 133**. A **co-activator** does not bind DNA itself but is brought to a gene by a factor that does. The **KIX** domain of **CBP** (CREB-binding protein) grips CREB far more tightly once serine 133 is phosphorylated, then acetylates nearby histones (loosening packaged DNA) and contacts the RNA polymerase machinery.

From the calcium lesson, calcium–calmodulin activates nuclear **CaMKIV**, which hits the exact same serine 133; growth factors also reach it via ERK-activated kinases (not ERK directly).

**Predict.** A neuron receives glutamate and fires strongly. Trace depolarization at the membrane to phosphorylated CREB on DNA—and predict what happens to CBP if a nuclear phosphatase immediately strips serine 133.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Depolarization opens L-type voltage-gated calcium channels and clears the magnesium ion blocking glutamate-bound NMDA receptors. Calcium enters, binds calmodulin, and activates CaMKIV with help from an upstream kinase, **CaMKK**, that phosphorylates CaMKIV. CaMKIV phosphorylates serine 133 within minutes; after strong or prolonged activity, a slower Ras–ERK kinase route keeps it phosphorylated so CBP stays bound. If a phosphatase strips serine 133 immediately, KIX loses its tight grip, CBP detaches, and transcription stops.

</details>

Many neuronal CRE genes also require a second co-activator, a **CRTC**, sent into the nucleus by calcium (*Going deeper*).

## Calcineurin and NFAT: removing phosphates opens the door

The first film showed **NFAT** (nuclear factor of activated T cells) flipping in reverse—entering the nucleus when **calcineurin** strips about a dozen phosphates that hide its nuclear import signal. Here are the T-cell circuit and the living-cell drug proof.

When the T-cell receptor fires, store-operated entry (from the calcium lesson) sustains high calcium, activating calcium–calmodulin and the phosphatase calcineurin. Once inside the nucleus, NFAT binds DNA beside a Fos–Jun pair (met right after the second film) on genes such as **interleukin-2**, which tells the T cell to divide. When calcium drops, kinases restore the phosphates and NFAT exits.

Two chemically unalike transplant drugs mapped this pathway before anyone knew their target. In 1989 a Merck group and Stuart Schreiber's group each identified **FKBP12** as the small protein binding **FK506**; **cyclosporin A** binds **cyclophilin**. In 1991 Schreiber's group and collaborators showed where they converge: each drug–helper complex binds and inhibits calcineurin. Neither drug nor helper blocks calcineurin alone—the pair sits beside the catalytic site and blocks bulky substrates such as NFAT. Jam calcineurin and NFAT keeps its phosphates and stays in the cytoplasm, so the interleukin-2 gene stays quiet.

**Predict.** FKBP12 and cyclophilin are enzymes themselves, so test-tube inhibition does not prove calcineurin is the drug target in living T cells. If calcineurin is the real target, how should overproducing calcineurin change T-cell sensitivity to FK506 and cyclosporin A?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The cells should become more resistant to both drugs, needing higher doses to silence NFAT, because extra calcineurin leaves more enzyme unbound. In 1992 Neil Clipstone and Gerald Crabtree tested this in T cells: extra calcineurin raised resistance to both drugs and boosted NFAT-driven transcription. If more enzyme means more drug is needed, the enzyme is probably the target.

</details>

### Design problem

Design a transcription factor activated by cAMP or calcium, drawing each pathway from surface to gene. Then modify it to require both messengers at once.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

For **OR**, copy CREB: a DNA-bound factor with one serine hit by two kinases. Pathway 1: hormone → GPCR → Gs → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → nuclear PKA → phosphoserine → co-activator → gene on. Pathway 2: depolarization → calcium channel → calmodulin → CaMKK → CaMKIV → same phosphoserine. Either suffices.

For **AND**, split location and activity. Let calcium/calcineurin unmask a nuclear import signal (as in NFAT), and let cAMP/PKA phosphorylate the co-activator handle. Calcium alone imports an inactive factor; cAMP alone phosphorylates a trapped cytoplasmic factor. Only both switch the gene on. (Composite sites like interleukin-2—NFAT beside Fos–Jun—also compute AND.)

</details>

## Basic leucine zippers: pairing multiplies DNA choices

The second film built the chopstick model of pairing and the two-wave cascade; here are the helix geometry, DNA-site lengths, and pairing ceiling behind that rule—that paired factors widen gene choices and drive a slower second wave.

CREB, Fos, and Jun are **bZIP** (basic leucine zipper) proteins, built from two stretches of one long α-helix:

- **Leucine zipper:** a leucine sits at nearly every seventh position. Because an α-helix turns once every ~3.6 residues, seven residues span `7 / 3.6 ≈ 1.94` turns—just shy of two full turns—so every seventh residue lands on nearly the same side, and the leucines form a gently twisting stripe down the helix. Two helices wind their stripes together to hide the leucines from water.
- **Basic stretch:** rich in positively charged lysine and arginine; beyond the zipper, the helices part like chopsticks so each basic stretch enters the DNA major groove and reads one half-site.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/signaling-to-the-nucleus/bzip-pair-on-dna.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two rods joined by a row of dots at the top and parting like chopsticks to grip a marked stretch of DNA at their tips; an inset shows Jun-Jun and Fos-Jun pairing but Fos-Fos not." caption="A bZIP factor pairs through a leucine zipper so its basic stretches read DNA half-sites like chopsticks, and partner rules let Fos–Jun bind DNA more tightly than Jun–Jun while excluding Fos–Fos." />

Swapping a partner changes DNA preference and output. **AP-1** (activator protein 1) is classically a Fos–Jun or Jun–Jun pair (though both can pair with other bZIPs). Jun pairs with Jun; Fos cannot pair stably with Fos because negatively charged residues flanking its zipper repel each other, so Fos needs a partner (classically Jun), and Fos–Jun grips DNA more tightly than Jun–Jun. AP-1 prefers the seven-letter site `TGACTCA`, one letter shorter than the eight-letter CRE (`TGACGTCA`). Four Fos and three Jun genes yield well over a dozen AP-1 pairs.

Adding `n` same-partner pairs to `n(n − 1) / 2` mixed pairs gives the unrestricted ceiling:

> **n proteins that can all pair with one another make n(n + 1) / 2 different pairs**

Roughly fifty human bZIPs would make `50 × 51 / 2 = 1,275` pairs if all paired freely, but residues beside each leucine stripe restrict partner choice (*Going deeper*).

**Predict.** A cell expresses 3 Fos-family and 2 Jun-family proteins (Jun pairs with any Jun or Fos; Fos never pairs with Fos). How many distinct pairs can form, versus 5 freely pairing proteins?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Five freely pairing proteins form `5 × 6 / 2 = 15` pairs. Under the Fos rule: 2 Jun make `2 × 3 / 2 = 3` Jun–Jun pairs, 3 Fos × 2 Jun make `6` Fos–Jun pairs, and Fos–Fos makes `0`, yielding **9 pairs**—still nearly double the gene count.

</details>

## Immediate early genes: from minutes to hours

An **immediate early gene (IEG)** is transcribed within minutes of a signal without new protein synthesis, defined by the **cycloheximide test**: block ribosomes with cycloheximide, add the signal, and measure mRNA.

For `c-fos`, mRNA still rises because its promoter carries both a **CRE** (for CREB) and a **serum response element (SRE)**—named because growth factors in blood serum trigger it in culture—bound by **serum response factor (SRF)** and partners such as **Elk-1** (phosphorylated by ERK). Thus cAMP, calcium, and growth factors all reach `c-fos` via pre-made factors.

In 1984 Michael Greenberg and Edward Ziff showed that `c-fos` transcription surges within minutes of adding growth factors to resting fibroblasts and soon shuts off again. Without cycloheximide, newly translated Fos pairs with Jun to form AP-1, switching on a slower **second wave** of genes that turns a minutes-long signal into an hours-long program.

**Predict.** Resting fibroblasts receive cycloheximide before growth factor. At 30 minutes you measure `c-fos` mRNA and Fos protein; at 2 hours you measure mRNA of an AP-1–driven second-wave gene. What happens to each?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

At 30 minutes, `c-fos` mRNA rises (pre-made factors need no ribosomes), but Fos protein does not rise (stopped ribosomes cannot translate it). At 2 hours, the second-wave mRNA stays off because it requires newly made Fos and other first-wave products.

</details>

### Mapping active neurons with Fos

Resting neurons make very little Fos. Strong excitation brings calcium in, activating CREB and `c-fos`, so antibody staining a few hours later maps recently active nuclei across a whole brain without electrodes. Two cautions apply: a neuron can fire without making Fos, and the stain is a snapshot with timing no finer than hours.

### From ion channels to CREB and long-term memory

A brief synaptic change modifies existing proteins; a long-lasting change requires new transcription, involving CREB where tested.

**Predict.** Injecting excess short CRE DNA ("decoy CRE DNA") into a sensory neuron's nucleus soaks up CREB away from real genes. After training, what should happen to short-lasting versus long-lasting synaptic strengthening?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Short-lasting strengthening survives (it modifies proteins already at the synapse), whereas long-lasting strengthening fails because decoy DNA sequesters CREB. Eric Kandel's laboratory reported this result in *Aplysia* in 1990.

</details>

In 1994, fruit flies expressing a blocking form of CREB failed to form long-term odor memory, and mice lacking two CREB forms showed a similar loss, with short-term memory intact in both. In *Aplysia* the messenger is cAMP; in mammalian neurons, calcium through NMDA receptors and L-type channels hits the same serine 133. Later mouse results varied with isoforms, gene dosage, training spacing, and compensation by CREM (*Going deeper*). None of this shows that CREB is where a memory is stored.

## Going deeper

### CRTC co-activators and coupled folding on CREB

CREB's kinase-inducible domain is largely disordered alone and folds into two helices upon binding CBP's KIX domain when serine 133 is phosphorylated. Many CRE targets also require **CRTC** co-activators, which enter the nucleus when calcineurin dephosphorylates them and cAMP silences the kinases holding them out—making those genes coincidence detectors for calcium and cAMP.

### bZIP partner specificity across species

Charged residues flanking the hydrophobic leucine stripe set which helices pair stably (as in acidic-residue repulsion blocking Fos–Fos while permitting Fos–Jun). Deppmann, Alvania and Taparowsky (*Molecular Biology and Evolution*, 2006) compile bZIP pairing networks across humans, fruit flies, a plant, and yeast.

### Why later mouse CREB memory studies varied

Later mouse studies proved less uniform than the 1994 knockout: the original mutants still made a third CREB isoform, outcomes depended on gene dosage and training spacing, and CREB-deficient mice upregulated the related factor CREM, which may partly stand in for CREB.
