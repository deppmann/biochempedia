techniques:
  - name: "Isolated-tissue bioassay (organ bath)"
    blurb: >-
      A strip of muscle or a piece of heart is kept alive in warm, oxygenated salt solution and
      connected to a recorder. You add a drug at rising concentrations and measure the contraction
      or relaxation at each one. The result is a dose-response curve, from which EC50 and Emax are
      read.
  - name: "Radioligand binding assay"
    blurb: >-
      Membranes are incubated with a radioactive ligand, then filtered and washed, and the
      radioactivity left on the filter is counted. Repeating the incubation with a large excess of
      unlabelled ligand measures nonspecific sticking, which is subtracted. The specific binding
      that remains saturates, and its curve gives Kd and the number of sites (Bmax).
  - name: "Schild analysis"
    blurb: >-
      You measure agonist dose-response curves at several concentrations of an antagonist and
      calculate the dose ratio for each. For a competitive antagonist, log(dose ratio − 1) plotted
      against log antagonist concentration is a straight line of slope 1. The point where the line
      crosses zero sits at log KB, which gives KB, the antagonist's dissociation constant.
  - name: "Irreversible receptor inactivation"
    blurb: >-
      A tissue is treated with a blocker that bonds covalently to the receptor and is then washed,
      so that a fraction of the receptors is permanently removed. Comparing agonist curves before
      and after shows whether the curve moves right before its maximum falls. That order reveals a
      receptor reserve.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "A ligand is present at a free concentration equal to its Kd for a receptor. At equilibrium, what fraction of the receptors is occupied?"
    choices:
      - "100%"
      - "10%"
      - "90%"
      - "50%"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Putting [L] = Kd into occupancy = [L] / (Kd + [L]) gives Kd / 2Kd = 0.5, so half of the
      receptors are occupied. That is what Kd means. The 100% answer treats Kd as the saturating
      concentration, but the hyperbola only approaches full occupancy at concentrations far above
      Kd. 90% would need [L] = 9 × Kd, and 10% would need [L] = Kd/9.
  - stem: "A hormone binds its receptor with Kd = 9 nM. The free hormone concentration rises from 1 nM to 81 nM. How does receptor occupancy change?"
    choices:
      - "It rises 81-fold, from about 1% to about 81%"
      - "It rises from 10% to 90%"
      - "It rises from about 11% to 100%, because 81 nM is far above Kd"
      - "It rises from 50% to 90%"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      At 1 nM, occupancy = 1 / (9 + 1) = 10%. At 81 nM, occupancy = 81 / (9 + 81) = 90%. An
      81-fold rise in ligand, from Kd/9 to 9 × Kd, takes the receptors from 10% to 90% full. The
      1% to 81% answer assumes occupancy is proportional to concentration, which ignores
      saturation. The 11% to 100% answer divides [L] by Kd and caps the result, but occupancy
      never reaches 100% at a finite concentration. The 50% to 90% answer assumes that any
      concentration near Kd half-fills the receptors, when 50% occurs only at [L] = Kd, which is
      9 nM here.
  - stem: "Drug X and drug Y act at the same receptor in the same tissue. X has an EC50 of 2 nM and produces at most 60% of the tissue's maximum response. Y has an EC50 of 200 nM and produces 100%. Which statement is correct?"
    choices:
      - "X is more potent, and Y has the greater efficacy"
      - "Y is more potent, because it produces the larger response"
      - "X is more potent and therefore has the greater efficacy"
      - "X must bind more weakly than Y, because its maximum response is lower"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Potency is read from EC50, and X has the lower one, so X is more potent. Efficacy is read,
      below the tissue's ceiling, from the maximum response; X stops at 60% where Y reaches 100%,
      so Y has the greater efficacy and X is a partial agonist. Saying that X's potency gives it the greater efficacy
      merges two independent properties. Calling Y more potent because its response is larger
      confuses the height of a curve with its position along the dose axis. The claim that X must
      bind more weakly cannot be drawn from a lower maximum, because a partial agonist can have
      very high affinity.
  - stem: "In one tissue, a partial agonist given alone produces at most 40% of the maximum response. The tissue is first made to respond maximally with a full agonist that acts at the same receptor. You then add a high concentration of the partial agonist, which binds with high affinity. What happens to the response?"
    choices:
      - "It stays at the maximum, because the receptors were already saturated with the full agonist"
      - "It rises above the previous maximum, because the effects of the two agonists add together"
      - "It falls toward 40% of the maximum, because occupied receptors now signal more weakly"
      - "It falls to zero, because a partial agonist blocks the receptor like an antagonist"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The partial agonist competes for the same receptors and displaces the full agonist. Each
      receptor it takes now signals weakly instead of strongly, so the response falls toward the
      partial agonist's own ceiling of 40%. The response cannot rise above the maximum, because both
      drugs share one pool of receptors and the full agonist already had it. It does not stay at the
      maximum, because saturation by one ligand does not stop a second ligand from competing for the
      sites. It does not fall to zero, because a partial agonist has some efficacy, and only an
      antagonist with none would abolish the response.

  - stem: "You run an agonist dose-response curve on a strip of muscle, treat the strip with a blocker, wash thoroughly and run the curve again. After a low dose of blocker the curve has moved to the right with the same maximum. After a higher dose it has moved further right and the maximum has dropped to 40%. Washing never restores the original curve. What is the best explanation?"
    choices:
      - "The blocker is a competitive antagonist, and the higher dose was too large for the agonist to overcome"
      - "The blocker binds irreversibly, and the tissue has a receptor reserve for this agonist"
      - "The blocker reacts with the agonist in the bath, so less agonist reaches the receptors"
      - "The blocker is a partial agonist with a maximum of 40%"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A blocker that survives washing is acting irreversibly. Removing some receptors moved the
      curve right without lowering the maximum, so the tissue did not need all of its receptors
      for a full response, which is a receptor reserve. Removing more receptors than the reserve
      could cover then lowered the maximum. A competitive antagonist is reversible, so washing
      would restore the curve, and, at equilibrium, no dose of it lowers the maximum, because more agonist always
      overcomes it. A partial agonist would produce a response of its own, which the data do not show.
      A blocker that reacted with the agonist in the bath would be removed by washing and would
      not lower the maximum, since extra agonist would make up the loss.

  - stem: "In a binding assay on membranes from a cultured cell line, a radiolabelled agonist shows saturable binding with Kd = 100 nM. In the intact cells, the same agonist produces a response with EC50 = 5 nM and reaches the same maximum as other full agonists. Approximately what fraction of receptors is occupied when the response is half-maximal, and what does that imply?"
    choices:
      - "About 95%; the agonist binds tightly, so nearly all receptors are filled"
      - "About 5%; the binding assay must be in error, because EC50 cannot be lower than Kd"
      - "About 50%; EC50 is by definition the concentration that half-fills the receptors"
      - "About 5%; the cells have a large receptor reserve for this agonist"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Occupancy at 5 nM = 5 / (100 + 5), which is about 5%. Half of the maximum response with
      about 5% of receptors occupied means the pathway after the receptor saturates long before
      the receptors do, which is a receptor reserve. The 50% answer assumes EC50 and Kd are the
      same quantity, and they coincide only when response is proportional to occupancy. The 95%
      answer inverts the ratio, as if 5 nM were far above a Kd of 100 nM. Rejecting the binding
      assay assumes EC50 can never lie below Kd, but amplification after the receptor produces
      exactly that.
  - stem: "An agonist contracts isolated gut muscle with EC50 = 10 nM. With 100 nM of drug B in the bath, the curve is parallel to the original, reaches the same maximum and has EC50 = 110 nM. With 1,000 nM of drug B, the EC50 is 1,010 nM and the maximum is again unchanged. Which conclusion do these data support?"
    choices:
      - "Drug B is a competitive antagonist with KB of about 100 nM"
      - "Drug B is a partial agonist, because the agonist still reaches its full maximum"
      - "Drug B is a competitive antagonist with KB of about 10 nM"
      - "Drug B is a non-competitive antagonist, because a tenfold increase in B raised the EC50 about tenfold"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The curves move right in parallel with no loss of maximum, the signature of a competitive
      antagonist. The dose ratio at 100 nM is 110 / 10 = 11, and dose ratio = 1 + [B] / KB gives
      [B] / KB = 10, so KB = 10 nM. The 1,000 nM curve gives 101 = 1 + 1,000 / KB, again 10 nM, as
      simple competition requires. A KB of 100 nM mistakes the concentration that was added for
      the constant, when at [B] = KB the dose ratio would be only 2. A non-competitive antagonist
      usually lowers the maximum and would not raise the EC50 in proportion to [B] across a
      tenfold range; a proportional rise is what competition predicts. A partial agonist would raise the response at low agonist doses, so the curves
      would not be parallel, and an unchanged agonist maximum is what any surmountable blocker
      gives.
  - stem: "Brain membranes are incubated with increasing concentrations of a tritiated antagonist, then filtered, washed and counted; total binding rises steeply at first and then keeps climbing in a straight line. With a 1,000-fold excess of unlabelled antagonist in every tube, binding is a straight line through the origin. Subtracting the second curve from the first leaves a curve that is half-maximal at 2 nM and levels off above about 40 nM at 25 pmol per gram of tissue. Which interpretation is best supported?"
    choices:
      - "The tissue has a finite set of sites, about 25 pmol per gram, with a Kd of about 2 nM"
      - "The ligand binds only nonspecifically, because total binding never levels off"
      - "The Kd is about 40 nM, the concentration at which the subtracted curve levels off"
      - "The straight-line component is the receptor, because it holds most of the radioactivity at high concentrations"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Binding that persists in a large excess of unlabelled ligand is the nonspecific component.
      The labelled drug has been competed off any finite set of sites, and what remains rises in
      proportion to concentration across the range tested. The difference between the two curves is specific
      binding. It saturates at 25 pmol per gram, which is the number of sites (Bmax), and it is
      half-maximal at 2 nM, which is Kd. Concluding that binding is only nonspecific reads the
      total curve alone and misses the saturable part inside it. A Kd of 40 nM confuses the
      concentration that nearly fills the sites with the one that half-fills them. Taking the
      straight-line component as the receptor assumes the largest signal is the meaningful one,
      but a receptor population is finite and must saturate.

  - level: "college"

    text: >-
      Binding of a ligand to one class of independent sites follows the law of mass action:
      fractional occupancy = [L] / (Kd + [L]), a hyperbola with the same form as the
      Michaelis-Menten equation. Kd, the concentration that gives half occupancy, measures
      affinity. A dose-response curve reports two other things, potency (EC50) and efficacy
      (Emax). A full agonist reaches the tissue maximum, a partial agonist does not even at full

      amplifies and saturates, many tissues respond fully with a minority of receptors occupied (a

      the maximum alone. An irreversible antagonist can move the curve right before the maximum

      shift depends on its mechanism. Radioligand binding measures Kd and the

  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      For one class of non-interacting sites at equilibrium, occupancy follows the Hill-Langmuir
      equation with a Hill coefficient of 1, which is the 81-fold rule for 10% to 90%. Response is
      a further, saturating function of occupancy. In the operational model of Black and Leff
      (1983) with a hyperbolic transducer, E = Em × τ[A] / (KA + (1 + τ)[A]), where τ is receptor
      density divided by the concentration of occupied receptor that gives half of the system
      maximum Em. It follows that EC50 = KA / (1 + τ) and that the observed maximum is
      Em × τ / (1 + τ). A high-τ agonist looks full, with EC50 far below KA, and irreversible
      alkylation of receptors lowers τ, which moves EC50 toward KA before the maximum collapses.
      Furchgott's receptor-inactivation method (1966) uses that behaviour to estimate agonist KA.
      For competitive antagonists the Gaddum-Schild relation gives log(dose ratio − 1) =
      log [B] − log KB. A Schild plot with unit slope over a wide range of [B] is the test of
      simple competition, and slopes away from 1 point to non-equilibrium conditions, agonist
      uptake, allosteric interaction or more than one receptor.


      An open question is how far these equilibrium constants predict effect in a living tissue.
      Concentrations in vivo can change faster than binding equilibrates, so the time a drug stays
      on its receptor may matter more than Kd. A single efficacy value also fails to describe a
      ligand that activates different downstream pathways to different degrees, and how to measure
      efficacy in a way that transfers between tissues is still argued over.

**Naloxone can pull a person out of a morphine overdose within minutes, and it never reacts with a single molecule of morphine.** The two drugs compete for the same place on the same cells. This lesson asks how much of that place a given dose fills, how filling it becomes a response, and how two response curves can measure a drug's grip on a receptor nobody has isolated.

## A drug acts at a site it has to occupy

In 1905 John Langley, in Cambridge, tested two drugs on skeletal muscle. Nicotine made the muscle contract and curare prevented it, and both still worked after the nerve to the muscle had been cut and had degenerated. So the drugs were acting on the muscle itself. Langley called the thing they acted on the **receptive substance**. The shorter word, **receptor**, came from Paul Ehrlich, who had coined it a few years earlier while working on antitoxins, for toxins and nutrients rather than drugs.

An **agonist** binds a receptor and switches it on. An **antagonist** binds the same receptor, does nothing there, and keeps the agonist out. In Emil Fischer's lock-and-key picture, both keys fit and only the agonist turns. Some receptors are partly active with nothing bound, and some blockers, called **inverse agonists**, turn that background activity down.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson. In the equilibrium, single-receptor model used here, the tissue sees the agonist's occupancy.** For one agonist acting on one unchanged receptor population in one tissue, the response is treated as a monotonic function of the fraction of receptors that agonist occupies. Binding sets that fraction, with Kd the only drug number in it; the agonist's efficacy and the tissue's amplifier turn it into a response that rises and then saturates, in a shape you usually do not know. Two results follow. **EC50 is Kd seen through the agonist's efficacy and the tissue's amplifier.** **Equal responses on the monotonic, submaximal part of a curve mean equal occupancy** (at the ceiling many occupancies give the same response), so when you compare two curves for the same agonist in one tissue below the maximum, the amplifier cancels, and the shift a competitive blocker causes measures the blocker's own affinity. ## Occupancy follows mass action

Picture a room with a fixed number of coat hooks. Coats go in, catch on hooks and drop off again. The hooks are receptors (R). The coats are **ligand** (L), any molecule that binds the receptor. The fraction of hooks holding a coat is the **fractional occupancy**.

The real mechanism is a reversible reaction, L + R ⇌ LR. Binding runs at a rate k<sub>on</sub>[L][R] and release at k<sub>off</sub>[LR]; at equilibrium the two are equal. The ratio of the rate constants is the **dissociation constant**, Kd = k<sub>off</sub>/k<sub>on</sub>, which also equals [L][R]/[LR]. Add the fact that every receptor is either empty or occupied, and you get the lesson's binding equation:

<EquationCard
  purpose="The fraction of receptors a ligand occupies at equilibrium."
  eq="fractional occupancy = [L] / (Kd + [L])"
  say="The fraction of receptors occupied equals the ligand concentration divided by the dissociation constant plus the ligand concentration."
  symbols={[
    ["[L]", "L", "the free ligand concentration, in solution and not bound. When there are far fewer receptors than ligand molecules, it is close to the concentration you added.", "nM"],
    ["Kd", "K d", "the dissociation constant, k_off / k_on: the free ligand concentration that occupies half of the receptors", "nM"],
  ]}
  toolkit={["read", "log-scales"]}
  check="At [L] = Kd, occupancy is Kd / 2Kd = 1/2. Far above Kd, occupancy approaches 1 but never reaches it."
>
  <Fragment slot="example">
    <p>A ligand at three times its Kd: occupancy = 3Kd / (Kd + 3Kd) = 3/4.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="derivation">
    <p>Step 1, equilibrium: k_on[L][R] = k_off[LR], so Kd = k_off / k_on = [L][R] / [LR].</p>
    <p>Step 2, every receptor is empty (R) or occupied (LR): occupancy = [LR] / ([R] + [LR]).</p>
    <p>Step 3, divide the top and bottom by [LR], and use [R]/[LR] = Kd/[L] from step 1: occupancy = 1 / (Kd/[L] + 1) = [L] / (Kd + [L]).</p>
  </Fragment>
</EquationCard>

So Kd, the free ligand concentration that half-fills the receptors, measures **affinity**: a small Kd means a tight grip. This is the same hyperbola as the Michaelis–Menten equation from the enzyme kinetics lesson, with occupancy in place of v/Vmax and Kd in place of Km, for the same reason: a fixed number of sites is being filled. One caution: Km combines several rate constants, while Kd is a pure binding constant.

**Predict.** What fraction of the receptors is occupied at [L] = Kd/9, and at [L] = 9 × Kd? How many times more ligand separates the two?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

At Kd/9, occupancy = (1/9) / (1 + 1/9) = 10%. At 9 × Kd, it is 9/(1 + 9) = 90%. Getting from 10% to 90% takes an 81-fold increase in ligand, nearly two powers of ten.

</details>

That is why these curves are drawn with concentration on a logarithmic axis. On a linear axis the low-dose end is crushed against the origin. On a log axis the hyperbola becomes a symmetric S-shaped curve with Kd at its midpoint. Try it in the explorer below.

A binding curve that climbs from 10% to 90% in much less than 81-fold means the sites are not acting independently. That is cooperativity (see the lesson on protein binding and allostery). The rule is for binding curves: steps after the receptor can steepen a response curve, so a steep one says nothing about receptor cooperativity by itself.

## Measuring occupancy directly

For most of the century after Langley, pharmacologists inferred receptors from responses. Direct binding measurements began in the 1960s with radioactive atropine on gut muscle, and by the early 1970s they had reached the nicotinic receptor and hormone receptors such as insulin's.

In 1973 Candace Pert and Solomon Snyder at Johns Hopkins measured binding to the opiate receptor, as did two other groups the same year. They mixed radioactive naloxone with ground-up brain tissue, pulled the mixture through a filter, washed the filter and counted the radioactivity that stayed behind.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/binding-and-dose-response/counting-bound-naloxone.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: a three-step strip showing ground-up brain tissue mixed with radioactive naloxone, the mixture pulled through a filter, and the washed filter shown next to a counter, schematically representing measurement of retained radioactivity. A small inset contrasts a finite row of filled receptor pockets with scattered sticking to greasy tissue." caption="In 1973 Pert and Snyder mixed radioactive naloxone with ground-up brain, filtered it, washed the filter and counted the radioactivity that stayed (in practice by liquid scintillation counting of the filter). A finite set of sites that fills up is one sign of a receptor, whereas sticking to grease keeps rising; counts include nonspecific sticking, so counting alone does not identify a receptor." />

Brain is greasy and holds on to almost any small molecule. How do you show that what stuck is a receptor?

The binding has to be **saturable**. A finite set of sites fills up along the occupancy curve, while sticking to grease keeps rising with concentration. To see the saturable part, measure total binding, then measure it again with a large excess of unlabelled drug, which keeps the label off the specific sites and leaves only the nonspecific sticking. Total minus nonspecific is **specific binding**, the curve that saturates.

It has to be **specific**: unlabelled opiates pushed the radioactive naloxone off in the order of their known potency as drugs. And it has to be **stereospecific**. Levorphanol, an active opiate, displaced the label. Dextrorphan, its mirror image, has almost no opiate action and was far weaker. Grease cannot tell a molecule from its mirror image. A shaped pocket can.

The saturation curve hands you the numbers in the occupancy equation, measured on the site itself: a Kd, and the total count of sites, called **Bmax**. Langley's receptive substance had become something you could count.

## Response is not occupancy

Occupancy counts the receptors that have ligand bound. Response is what the tissue does: a muscle shortens, a gland secretes. A dose–response curve plots the response and has two numbers of its own. **EC50** is the concentration that gives half of the drug's maximum response; it measures **potency**: the lower the EC50, the less drug you need. **Emax** is the largest response the drug can produce, however much you add.

Four quantities lie behind those two numbers. Two belong to the drug at the receptor: **affinity** (Kd) and **efficacy**, how well the bound drug switches the receptor on. Two belong to the tissue: the **number of receptors** and the **amplifier**. EC50 mixes all four, which is why one agonist's potency can differ between tissues. Emax shows efficacy only below the tissue's ceiling: two full agonists with different efficacies can both reach the ceiling, and their difference then shows up in how far each EC50 sits below its Kd, or after receptors are removed.

A **full agonist** can drive the tissue to its maximum. A **partial agonist** levels off below it even when it occupies every receptor, like a key that turns the lock only part of the way. Whether a drug looks full or partial depends on the tissue too: in a tissue with many receptors, a weak key can still reach the maximum.

So a drug with an EC50 of 100 nM that reaches 100% is 100-fold less potent than one with an EC50 of 1 nM, with the same maximum; a drug with an EC50 of 1 nM that tops out at 40% is as potent, and less efficacious.

**Predict.** A tissue is responding fully to a full agonist. You add a partial agonist that acts at the same receptor. Does the response rise, stay or fall?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It falls. Each receptor the weak key takes is one the strong key has lost, so the partial agonist behaves as an antagonist and pulls the response toward its own ceiling. It gets close to that ceiling only when it is concentrated enough to win most of the receptors.

</details>

## The gap between the curves: receptor reserve

In 1926 Alfred Clark fitted the occupancy hyperbola, which A. V. Hill had used for nicotine in 1909 and Irving Langmuir for gas sticking to a surface, to the response of frog heart muscle to acetylcholine. He assumed that response is proportional to occupancy, which would make EC50 equal to Kd. In 1956 Mark Nickerson published a short paper in Nature called "Receptor occupancy and tissue response." He reported that a tissue can reach its full response before all of its receptors are occupied.

The reason is amplification after the receptor. Picture a bottling line where the capper is the bottleneck: once it runs at full speed, extra filling machines add nothing. Likewise, once enough receptors are occupied to saturate the slowest step downstream, more occupied receptors change nothing you can measure. This is the sharp version of an amplifier: proportional below the ceiling, flat at it. The receptors not needed are **spare receptors**, together the **receptor reserve**. They are ordinary receptors; the tissue has more copies than the pathway can use at once.

How far apart can EC50 and Kd be? Call p the fraction of receptors occupied when the response is half its maximum. At the EC50 the occupancy equation has to give p. Rearranged:

**Predict.** A radioligand assay gives an agonist a Kd of 100 nM; in the intact cells its EC50 is 5 nM. What fraction of the receptors is occupied at half the maximum response? Does an EC50 below Kd prove a receptor reserve?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

About 4.8%: 5 / (100 + 5). But an EC50 below Kd is not proof by itself. A saturating, hyperbolic-type amplifier works on every agonist and puts its EC50 below its Kd, even for a partial agonist with no spare receptors (example in Going deeper). A reserve is present when the tissue reaches its full maximum with less than full receptor occupancy; an EC50 below Kd for an agonist that reaches that ceiling is evidence for it, and the receptor-inactivation experiment below tests it.

</details>

A common alternative to the sharp ceiling is a smooth amplifier. Give a smooth one the same EC50, Kd/39, and it agrees with the sharp one there and at the two ends but not in between: 5% occupancy gives about two thirds of the maximum, and 90% of the maximum needs about 19% occupancy. The film and the design problem below use the sharp picture; with the smooth one the maximum falls earlier (numbers in Going deeper).

Treating an EC50 as a binding affinity is a common error in the research literature. The gap is also a tool. If a treatment changes a drug's EC50 and leaves its Kd alone, either the number of receptors or something after binding has changed, and you can look for it.

### Design problem

A drug gives a full response when only about 5% of its receptors are occupied. State what that tells you, then design an experiment that would prove it.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

What it tells you: for this drug in this tissue, about 95% of the receptors are spare. The reserve belongs to the pair of drug and tissue. A weaker agonist on the same tissue might need every receptor.

The experiment: take receptors away and watch the curve. Use an irreversible antagonist, a blocker that bonds covalently to the receptor and cannot be washed off or outcompeted. Treat strips of tissue so that different fractions of the receptors are destroyed, wash, then run a full agonist dose–response curve on each strip. In matched samples, count with a radioactive ligand what fraction of the receptors each treatment removed.

The predictions, in the sharp-ceiling picture and taking the 5% figure at face value:

- Half of the receptors gone. The 5% of the original total it needs is now 10% of the survivors. You still get a full response at a higher dose, so the curve moves right.
- 90% gone. That 5% is now half of the survivors. Still a full response, further right.
- More than 95% gone. Even with every survivor occupied, the tissue is short of the number it needs. Now the maximum falls.

A rightward shift first and a lower maximum afterwards is the proof. With no reserve, the maximum would start to fall with the first receptors lost. Real tissues have no sharp threshold, so the maximum sags earlier and more gradually, but the order of events is the same.

As a control, a second agonist acting through a different receptor should keep its full maximum, which shows that the blocker left the downstream pathway undamaged.

</details>

## Blocking the receptor: the shape of the shift names the blocker

Go back to the coat hooks and throw in decoy coats. They take up hooks and produce nothing.

A **competitive antagonist** binds reversibly at the agonist's own site. A receptor can hold the agonist or the antagonist, never both. Add enough agonist and it wins the hooks back, so the maximum is unchanged. What changes is the dose you need: on a log axis the whole curve slides right, parallel to itself. Naloxone does this to morphine. The size of the slide is the **dose ratio**: the agonist concentration needed for a given response with the antagonist present, divided by the concentration needed without it.

**Predict.** You work on a tissue you know almost nothing about: not how many receptors it has, how much reserve, or how many steps lie between receptor and contraction. You measure the agonist's dose–response curve with and without the antagonist. Can those two curves tell you how tightly the *antagonist* binds?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes. Pick a response level, say 30% of the maximum: without the antagonist the agonist needs concentration A to give it, with the antagonist A′. In the scoped model above, equal responses on the monotonic, submaximal part of the curve mean equal agonist occupancy, so the amplifier cancels and only binding is left.

</details>

Write the agonist's occupancy without and with the antagonist (whose share of the receptors adds [B]/KB to the bottom of the fraction), set the two equal, and solve:

So an antagonist at ten times its KB moves *every* agonist's curve for that receptor elevenfold, in *every* tissue that carries it, as long as the assumptions at the end of the lesson hold. That is why antagonist affinities became the fingerprints that told one receptor type from another, long before any receptor was isolated.

### Schild's test: is it really competition?

J. H. Gaddum wrote down the competition equation in 1937. Heinz Schild turned it into a measurement in 1947 and, with Arunlakshana in 1959, into a test. Take logs of the rearranged equation:

> **log(dose ratio − 1) = log [B] − log KB**

Plot log(dose ratio − 1) against log [B] for several antagonist concentrations. Simple competition at equilibrium predicts a straight line of slope 1 that crosses zero at log KB. Schild's **pA2** is the negative log of the antagonist concentration that gives a dose ratio of 2. At that point [B] = KB, so pA2 equals −log KB, but only when the slope is 1, because only then does the competition equation hold at every [B].

A slope other than 1 means an assumption failed: the antagonist had not reached equilibrium, the tissue was removing the agonist, the antagonist bound elsewhere and changed the agonist's affinity, or more than one receptor contributed. A plot that curves at high [B] signals the same, and a slope of 1 over a narrow range of [B] does not rule these out. The test tells you the simple reading is unsafe even when it cannot say which assumption broke.

### Blockers that more agonist cannot overcome

Such blockers are called **insurmountable**; a competitive antagonist, which more agonist does overcome, is surmountable. A **non-competitive antagonist** binds somewhere else on the receptor, or acts after it, and stops the receptor from responding. An **irreversible antagonist**, the covalent blocker of the design problem, binds at the agonist's own site. The signature of both is a lower maximum, but not always at once:

| Blocker | What happens to the agonist curve |
| --- | --- |
| Competitive antagonist | Parallel shift right, same maximum; Schild slope 1 |
| Irreversible (such as β-funaltrexamine) | With a reserve, shift right first, then the maximum falls; with none, the maximum falls from the start |
| Insurmountable allosteric | Varies with how the blocker changes the agonist's affinity and efficacy |
| Non-competitive, acting after the receptor (such as a block of a step in the contraction itself) | Lower maximum; any shift depends on the step blocked |
| Partial agonist added with the full agonist | Response raised at low full-agonist doses by the partial agonist's own effect; above the partial agonist's ceiling, competition shifts the curve right, but enough full agonist still reaches the full maximum |

### Potency orders as fingerprints

Agonist potency rankings sorted receptors into types too. Blood delivers adrenaline to every organ at much the same concentration, yet it narrows blood vessels in the skin and relaxes airways, so the difference lies in the tissues. In 1948 Raymond Ahlquist ranked six adrenaline-like drugs by potency on many tissues. One kind of pocket should give much the same order everywhere, he reasoned. For vasoconstriction, adrenaline and noradrenaline ranked high and isoproterenol low. For vasodilation, relaxation of the uterus and stimulation of the heart, the ranking ran nearly the other way. No third order appeared, so he proposed two kinds and named them alpha and beta.

## Back to the opening question

Naloxone and morphine compete for the opiate receptor Pert's assay counted. In a simple single-receptor preparation at equilibrium, naloxone takes receptors without switching them on and moves morphine's concentration-response curve right by a dose ratio of 1 + [naloxone]/KB. This competition reduces the mu-opioid-receptor signalling that drives respiratory depression, but clinical reversal is time-dependent and is not predicted by that equilibrium ratio alone. Naloxone never touches a morphine molecule; it changes how many receptors morphine occupies. Buprenorphine, a partial agonist at the same receptor, has a maximum set by its efficacy and by each tissue's receptor reserve, and against a full agonist it acts partly as a blocker.

## Where the idea stops

The rule assumes equilibrium, one class of independent sites, a response that is monotonic with agonist occupancy below its ceiling, free concentrations close to those added (no depletion or uptake), no desensitization during the curve, an antagonist that acts only at the receptor with no efficacy of its own (neither a partial nor an inverse agonist), and unchanged receptor number.

- **Across agonists.** The amplifier's input includes efficacy, so two agonists at equal response are not at equal occupancy. The rule compares one agonist with itself.
- **Irreversible antagonists** change receptor number, and so what the tissue does with a given occupancy. Furchgott's method uses exactly this.
- **Allosteric modulators, slow equilibration, uptake, more than one receptor.** These can make a Schild plot curved or give it a non-unit slope, but a unit-slope plot does not exclude them.
- **Pathway-selective (biased) agonists.** The amplifier depends on which response you measure, so one efficacy value does not transfer.

## Going deeper

### A smooth amplifier: the operational model

In the operational model of Black and Leff (1983), response = Em × τ[A] / (KA + (1 + τ)[A]), where Em is the system maximum and τ is receptor density divided by the concentration of occupied receptor that gives half of Em. Then EC50 = KA/(1 + τ) and the observed maximum is Em × τ/(1 + τ). With τ = 38, EC50 = KA/39 and occupancy at the EC50 is 2.5%, as with the sharp 5% ceiling: same p, same EC50, different amplifiers. Removing receptors lowers τ:

| Receptors left | Smooth (τ = 38): maximum as % of untreated, EC50 | Sharp ceiling at 5%: maximum, EC50 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100% | 100%, KA/39 | 100%, Kd/39 |
| 50% | 97.5%, KA/20 | 100%, Kd/19 |
| 10% | 81%, KA/4.8 | 100%, Kd/3 |
| 5% | 67%, KA/2.9 | 100%, Kd |
| 2% | 44%, KA/1.8 | 40%, Kd |

EC50 moves toward KA before the maximum collapses, and Furchgott's receptor-inactivation method (1966) uses that behaviour to estimate an agonist's KA. The model also shows why EC50 below Kd is not proof of a reserve: a partial agonist with τ = 1 reaches half of Em and still has EC50 = KA/2.

### Who named efficacy

Nickerson's paper is the usual citation for spare receptors. The idea that response is not proportional to occupancy, and the term efficacy, are usually credited to Stephenson (1956), building on Ariëns's "intrinsic activity" (1954).

### Equilibrium constants in a living tissue

Concentrations in a living tissue can change faster than binding equilibrates, so the time a drug stays on its receptor, set by k<sub>off</sub> rather than Kd, may matter more than Kd. How far equilibrium constants predict effect in vivo is still argued over.
