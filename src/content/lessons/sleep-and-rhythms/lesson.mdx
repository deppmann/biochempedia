techniques:
  - name: "Lesion and transplant studies"
    blurb: >-
      Destroy a region and see what is lost; then replace it with donor tissue and see what
      returns. A lesion tests necessity, but an electrolytic lesion also destroys passing fibres and every
      cell type in its path, and a
      transplant with a genetically marked donor shows which tissue sets a property such as period.
  - name: "Bioluminescence reporters (PER2::LUCIFERASE)"
    blurb: >-
      Fuse a clock protein to firefly luciferase in a knock-in mouse, culture a tissue with
      luciferin and count the photons it emits over days. The glow rises and falls with the clock
      protein, so each tissue's rhythm can be followed in real time.
  - name: "Polysomnography (EEG, EOG and EMG)"
    blurb: >-
      Record scalp EEG, eye movements (EOG) and chin muscle tone (EMG) through the night, then
      assign one stage to each 30-second epoch. EEG alone cannot tell REM sleep from waking; the
      muscle and eye channels can.
  - name: "Optogenetics"
    blurb: >-
      Express a light-gated channel such as channelrhodopsin-2 in one genetically defined cell type
      and drive it through an optical fibre; silencing needs a different, inhibitory opsin. Driving cells tests sufficiency; only
      silencing or removing them tests necessity, and either holds only for the cells, timing and
      behaviour tested.

**When you sleep depends on two things: a sleep pressure that builds the longer you are awake, and a circadian clock that light keeps on time.**

Sleep comes most easily when the pressure is high and the clock's wake signal is low. The clock is the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** (SCN), a **nucleus** being a compact group of neuron cell bodies in the brain. It is the body's master clock, and other tissues keep clocks of their own. In 1972 Friedrich Stephan and Irving Zucker destroyed this small paired cluster of neurons just above the optic chiasm in rats, and the animals stopped drinking on a daily schedule. The rats still drank, but no longer in a 24-hour pattern. How does a group of cells keep time, how does light keep it on time, and how does the brain switch between waking and sleep?

## The idea: a clock you can cut out and transplant

A **circadian** rhythm keeps a period of about a day with every time cue removed, so the timer is inside the animal. The SCN sits in the anterior hypothalamus (see [the hypothalamus and autonomic control](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/)).

Two experiments made the SCN the master clock. Stephan and Zucker made bilateral electrolytic lesions in albino rats; SCN lesions eliminated circadian drinking and locomotor rhythms. That shows the region is needed, not that it generates time: a lesion also cuts passing fibres and could remove a relay.

Martin Ralph, Russell Foster, Fred Davis and Michael Menaker answered that in hamsters in 1990. They used the tau mutation, which shortens the hamster's free-running period, as a label. They destroyed the host's SCN, then grafted in SCN-region tissue from a donor of a different genotype. The restored rhythms ran with the donor genotype's period, whatever the host's genotype. A relay would have passed on the host's period. A pacemaker imposes its own.

The SCN is not the only clock. Yoo and colleagues (2004) made knock-in mice in which the clock protein PER2 is fused to luciferase, so tissues glow in time with the clock. Isolated peripheral tissues such as liver sustained circadian oscillations for more than 20 cycles in culture. After SCN lesions, those tissue rhythms continued but drifted out of phase with one another. So the SCN keeps the body's clocks in step. It does not supply their ticking.

## The molecular loop and how light resets it

The genes came first from flies. Ronald Konopka and Seymour Benzer (1971) found mutations in one Drosophila gene, period, that made flies arrhythmic or gave them short or long periods (see [CRISPR and functional genomics](/lessons/crispr-and-functional-genomics/) for how such screens work). Mammals use related genes in a loop.

In mouse cells, the transcription factors CLOCK and BMAL1 bind as a pair to **E-boxes**, short DNA sequences near their target genes, and activate Per1 (Gekakis and colleagues, 1998; see [transcription](/lessons/transcription/)). Later work showed the same pair drives the other Per genes and the Cry genes. PER and CRY proteins build up, bind each other and enter the nucleus. As PER and CRY are degraded, the brake comes off and a new cycle begins.

Why does that oscillate instead of settling at a steady level? Think of a shower with a long pipe: you turn toward hot, nothing changes, you turn further, and when the hot water arrives you have overshot. Negative feedback with enough delay, and repression that is steep enough, keeps overshooting in both directions. In the clock, transcription, translation, modification, nuclear entry and timed degradation supply hours of delay. [Signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/) treats delayed negative feedback as a circuit motif.

The loop runs near 24 hours, not exactly 24, so light must reset it each day. Light reaches the SCN through the **retinohypothalamic tract**, a **tract** being a bundle of axons within the central nervous system. Berson, Dunn and Takao (2002) recorded from SCN-projecting retinal ganglion cells in isolated rat retina and found that they still depolarized to light with rod and cone input blocked. Those cells express **melanopsin** (Hattar and colleagues, 2002), a photopigment that signals through a different G-protein cascade from the rod's cGMP pathway (see [the senses and cGMP](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/) and [the retina](/lessons/retina/)). Rods and cones also contribute: mice failed to entrain to light-dark cycles only when melanopsin and rod and cone phototransduction were all removed (Hattar and colleagues, 2003). In the mouse SCN, light pulses strong enough to reset the clock rapidly induced Per1 transcripts.

Timing matters. In healthy people under controlled conditions, bright light before the core-body-temperature minimum (usually in the early morning hours) delayed the clock, and light after it advanced the clock.

**Worked problem: free-running drift.** Czeisler and colleagues (1999) measured a mean intrinsic period of 24.18 h in healthy young and older adults under controlled dim light. That is a study mean; individuals vary. Assume an idealized clock with exactly that period, no time cues, starting in phase with a 24.00-h day.

*Step 1: excess per cycle.* 24.18 − 24.00 = 0.18 h, which is 0.18 × 60 = 10.8 min per cycle.

*Step 2: seven internal cycles.* 7 × 0.18 h = 1.26 h, which is 75.6 min. The clock's marker now falls 75.6 min later than on the 24-h day.

*Step 3: check.* Internal time is 7 × 24.18 = 169.26 h. External time is 7 × 24.00 = 168.00 h. The difference is 1.26 h, as before.

*Step 4: what entrainment needs.* To stay at 24.00 h, this clock needs an average net light-driven advance of 10.8 min per day.

## Reading a night

Sleep is scored by **polysomnography**: scalp EEG, the electro-oculogram (EOG) for eye movements and the chin electromyogram (EMG) for muscle tone. The night is cut into 30-second **epochs**, and each epoch gets one stage. In adults:

- **N1**: the alpha rhythm of relaxed waking gives way to theta activity at 4 to 7 Hz.
- **N2**: **sleep spindles**, trains of 11 to 16 Hz waves lasting at least 0.5 s, and **K-complexes**, well-defined biphasic waves lasting at least 0.5 s.
- **N3**: slow waves of 0.5 to 2 Hz with peak-to-peak amplitude above 75 µV in frontal leads, filling at least 20% of the epoch. N3 merges what older scoring rules called stages 3 and 4.
- **REM**: an EEG that can look much like waking, rapid eye movements on the EOG, and the lowest muscle tone of the night on the EMG.

So the EOG and EMG separate REM from waking. In rodents, glutamatergic neurons of the sublaterodorsal nucleus are thought to produce REM atonia by recruiting inhibitory neurons in the medulla and spinal cord. Dreams are not limited to REM: people woken from NREM sleep also report them (Siclari and colleagues, 2017).

**Worked problem: scoring a hypnogram.** In this made-up recording, a subject is in bed for 960 epochs. The scorer counts 60 epochs of wake, 48 of N1, 432 of N2, 192 of N3 and 228 of REM.

*Step 1: total time.* 960 × 30 s = 28,800 s = 8.0 h. The counts add to 60 + 48 + 432 + 192 + 228 = 960.

*Step 2: sleep.* 960 − 60 = 900 epochs, or 900 × 30 s = 27,000 s = 7.5 h.

*Step 3: sleep efficiency*, sleep divided by time in bed: 900 / 960 = 0.9375, or 93.75%.

*Step 4: stage shares of sleep.* REM 228 / 900 = 25.3%. N3 192 / 900 = 21.3%. N2 432 / 900 = 48.0%. N1 48 / 900 = 5.3%. Rounded, they add to 99.9%; the unrounded shares add to 100%. These are practice numbers, not norms.

In the standard two-process model, when you sleep depends on two processes. A homeostatic pressure, often called Process S, accumulates during waking and dissipates during sleep. A circadian signal from the SCN, Process C, rises and falls with the clock. Sleep comes most easily when pressure is high and the circadian wake signal is low.

How does the brain switch? In the ventrolateral preoptic area (VLPO), sleep-active neurons release GABA and galanin and inhibit wake-promoting cell groups. Those groups (histamine neurons of the tuberomammillary nucleus, noradrenaline neurons of the locus coeruleus, serotonin neurons of the raphe and cholinergic neurons of the brainstem and basal forebrain; see [transmitter systems](/lessons/transmitter-systems/)) are inhibited by the VLPO and in the model inhibit it in return. One model calls this mutual inhibition a **flip-flop**: whichever side is ahead silences the other, so transitions are quick and intermediate states are rare. It is a model. Its details have exceptions, listed under Going deeper.

## Orexin and stable waking

A flip-flop switches quickly but can flip too easily. Neurons in the lateral hypothalamus that make the peptides **orexin A and B** (also called hypocretin 1 and 2) excite many of the wake-promoting groups, and are thought to hold the switch on the wake side.

Three lines of evidence tie orexin to narcolepsy, and each comes from a different species. In a familial dog model, Lin and colleagues (1999) found an autosomal recessive mutation in the hypocretin receptor 2 gene. In mice, conditional loss of orexin neurons in adults fragmented waking and increased transitions between sleep and wake states (Tabuchi and colleagues, 2014). In people, Thannickal and colleagues (2000) counted 85 to 95% fewer hypocretin neurons in four postmortem narcolepsy brains than in twelve controls, while neighbouring neurons making melanin-concentrating hormone were not reduced. Most human patients do not carry the dog's receptor mutation; narcolepsy type 1 is associated with severe loss of brain orexin. An autoimmune attack on these neurons is hypothesized, not established. **Cataplexy**, the sudden muscle weakness many patients have, occurs with consciousness preserved, so it is not falling asleep.

Adamantidis and colleagues (2007) expressed channelrhodopsin-2 in hypocretin neurons of mice and delivered light through an optical fibre into the lateral hypothalamus of freely moving animals. Stimulation increased transitions from slow-wave and REM sleep to waking.

What the sleeping brain does with its hours, including replay of hippocampal activity, is the subject of [memory consolidation](/lessons/memory-consolidation/).

### Design problem

A colleague says the optogenetic result and the human cell counts prove orexin neurons are needed for waking. Design a mouse experiment that asks whether they are needed for waking itself or for stable waking: what you measure, your controls, and your prediction.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Driving the neurons shows that their activity is enough to wake a mouse under that stimulation: sufficiency, not necessity. The human series is correlational: far fewer cells stain for hypocretin in four patients, but the counts cannot say when or why that happened.

To test necessity, silence or ablate the neurons with an inhibitory opsin or a toxin expressed only in orexin neurons, and do it in adult mice so development cannot compensate. Include control mice that lack the opsin or toxin but receive the same light or the same induction, to control for heat, light, handling and the treatment itself. Record EEG and EMG continuously and have blind scorers assign stages in fixed epochs.

Measure total wake per 24 h, the mean duration of wake bouts, the number of transitions between states, and episodes of abrupt atonia that come straight out of sustained waking. If the neurons are needed for stable waking, you predict much shorter wake bouts and many more transitions with little loss of total wake, plus cataplexy-like episodes, scored as abrupt atonia lasting at least 10 s with a theta-dominated EEG after at least 40 s of waking, with video showing immobility. If they were needed for waking itself, total wake would fall sharply.

Two limits remain. Orexin neurons release other transmitters, so silencing the cells does not isolate the peptide; a receptor antagonist or peptide knockout tests that. And the result holds for these mice and this timing, not for human patients.

## Going deeper

### Reporter assays and kinases in the clock loop

In reporter assays either CRY1 or CRY2 abolished CLOCK-BMAL1-driven transcription (Kume and colleagues, 1999). Kinases set part of the loop's timing: casein kinase 1 phosphorylates PER, and the hamster tau mutation is an allele of casein kinase 1 epsilon (Lowrey and colleagues, 2000).

### Qualifications to the flip-flop model

In slice recordings, putative sleep-promoting VLPO cells were inhibited by noradrenaline, acetylcholine and mostly serotonin, not directly by histamine (Gallopin and colleagues, 2000). In mice, Chung and colleagues (2017) found that activating preoptic GABA neurons that project to the tuberomammillary nucleus increased NREM sleep, while activating preoptic GABA neurons in general increased waking. Projection target together with transmitter, not location alone, defines a sleep-promoting cell: glutamatergic preoptic neurons with the same target promoted waking.

### Orexin experiment details

In the Tabuchi experiment, dark-period wake time was significantly reduced from 3 to 10 weeks after induction, with recovery by 11 weeks (Tabuchi and colleagues, 2014). In the Adamantidis experiment, trains at 5 to 30 Hz shortened the latency to wake, while 1 Hz did not (Adamantidis and colleagues, 2007).
