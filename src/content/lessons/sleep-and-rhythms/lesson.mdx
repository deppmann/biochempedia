    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In the late 1960s many biologists doubted that a behaviour as complex as a daily rhythm
      could be changed by a single gene. Seymour Benzer's laboratory at Caltech was using
      chemical mutagenesis in fruit flies to find genes behind behaviour, and Ronald Konopka, a
      graduate student there, chose the circadian clock. He mutagenized flies and screened their
      offspring for altered timing of adult emergence from the pupal case, then tested the
      locomotor activity of the mutants he found. Three mutants stood out: one was arrhythmic, one ran with a period near 19
      hours and one near 28 hours. Genetic mapping put all three on the X chromosome at what
      behaved as a single locus, which they named period. Konopka and Benzer published the
      result in PNAS in 1971. The gene itself was cloned in 1984 by two other groups, the
      laboratories of Jeffrey Hall and Michael Rosbash and of Michael Young. Their work on how
      period and its partners keep time earned those three the 2017 Nobel Prize. The screen
      made the clock a problem that genetics could take apart.

    story: >-
      By 1970 physiologists knew that mammals keep circadian rhythms in constant conditions, so
      something inside the animal had to keep time, and light through the eyes had to reset it.
      Where in the brain was the clock? Friedrich Stephan and Irving Zucker made electrolytic lesions in the
      hypothalamus of albino rats and recorded drinking and running over many days. Rats with bilateral lesions
      of the suprachiasmatic nucleus, a small paired cluster of neurons just above the optic
      chiasm, lost their circadian drinking and activity rhythms. Lesions of the medial

      corticosterone in rats. An electrolytic lesion destroys every cell and fibre in its path,
      so these studies showed that the region was needed, not how it kept time. They pointed
      the search for the mammalian master clock at the SCN.

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      A lesion shows that a region is needed, but the SCN might only relay a rhythm made
      elsewhere. Martin Ralph, working with Michael Menaker, had an unusual tool: a hamster
      carrying a mutation they named tau, reported in 1988, which shortened the free-running
      period. Hamsters with one copy ran fast and those with two ran faster still. That gave a
      label for whose clock was running. Ralph, Russell Foster, Fred Davis and Menaker destroyed
      the SCN of hosts, which became arrhythmic, and implanted SCN-region tissue from donors of
      a different genotype. In animals whose rhythms came back, the period matched the donor's
      genotype, whatever the host's own genotype was. They published the result in Science in
      1990. The tissue transplanted was a region, not a single cell type, and the measure was
      locomotor activity, so the experiment did not show which cells keep time. It showed that
      the SCN sets the period of the rhythm it restores, which is what a pacemaker does.

    story: >-
      Narcolepsy causes overwhelming daytime sleepiness and, in many patients, cataplexy:
      sudden loss of muscle tone, often set off by strong emotion, while the person stays
      conscious. Some dogs inherit a similar disorder, and Stanford researchers kept affected
      lines for study. Emmanuel Mignot's group mapped the canine gene by positional cloning,
      following the disorder through families until the region narrowed to one gene. In 1999
      Lin and colleagues reported in Cell that canine narcolepsy in their model is caused by

techniques:
  - name: "Lesion and transplant studies"
    blurb: >-
      Destroy a region and see what is lost; then replace it with donor tissue and see what
      returns. A lesion tests necessity, but an electrolytic lesion also destroys passing fibres and every
      cell type in its path, and a
      transplant with a genetically marked donor shows which tissue sets a property such as period.
  - name: "Bioluminescence reporters (PER2::LUCIFERASE)"
    blurb: >-
      Fuse a clock protein to firefly luciferase in a knock-in mouse, culture a tissue with
      luciferin and count the photons it emits over days. The glow rises and falls with the clock
      protein, so each tissue's rhythm can be followed in real time.
  - name: "Polysomnography (EEG, EOG and EMG)"
    blurb: >-
      Record scalp EEG, eye movements (EOG) and chin muscle tone (EMG) through the night, then
      assign one stage to each 30-second epoch. EEG alone cannot tell REM sleep from waking; the
      muscle and eye channels can.
  - name: "Optogenetics"
    blurb: >-
      Express a light-gated channel such as channelrhodopsin-2 in one genetically defined cell type
      and drive it through an optical fibre; silencing needs a different, inhibitory opsin. Driving cells tests sufficiency; only
      silencing or removing them tests necessity, and either holds only for the cells, timing and
      behaviour tested.

      - "Sleep pressure is high, but the circadian signal opposes sleep, so sleep comes less easily than at the signal's low point"
      - "Sleep pressure has dissipated during waking, so sleep comes easily"
      - "The SCN signal adds to the sleep pressure, so sleep comes most easily"
      - "Light resets the sleep pressure to zero, so the circadian signal no longer matters"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      In the standard two-process model a homeostatic pressure (Process S) accumulates during waking and dissipates during sleep, while a circadian signal from the SCN (Process C) rises and falls with the clock. Sleep comes most easily when pressure is high and the circadian wake signal is low. Here the pressure is high, but the wake signal is at its peak and opposes it: in the picture of the model, the clock has raised the level that pressure must reach before sleep can start. Pressure builds, not dissipates, during waking. The circadian wake signal does not add to the pressure; sleep is easiest when it is low. Light resets the clock, not the sleep pressure.
  - stem: "Suppose a drug blocked the degradation of PER and CRY proteins in mouse SCN cells without changing their synthesis. What happens to the clock loop?"
    choices:
      - "BMAL1 is degraded instead and the period is unchanged"
      - "E-box output stays repressed; the cycle slows or stops"
      - "CLOCK-BMAL1 drive rises and the cycle speeds up"
      - "Per transcription becomes light-independent"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      PER-CRY complexes repress CLOCK-BMAL1, and their degradation is what releases the brake for
      the next cycle; if they persist, E-box transcription stays low and the oscillation lengthens
      or stops. Rising CLOCK-BMAL1 drive is the opposite of what more repressor does. Light
      resets the clock but is not what makes Per transcription cycle, so stopping degradation
      does not make it light-independent. Nothing in the loop predicts that BMAL1 would be
      degraded in its place with no effect on period.

**Stay up all night and something odd happens. Near dawn you can barely keep your eyes open. By early afternoon, still without sleep, you feel better, though you have been awake hours longer.**

If sleepiness depended only on how long you had been awake, the afternoon should feel worse than the dawn. Something besides time awake changed between them. That something is a clock.

## Two processes decide when you sleep

The first process is **sleep pressure**, often called **Process S**. It builds all the time you are awake and drains while you sleep. It builds quickly at first, then more slowly as it nears a ceiling. In people, one sign of it is the strength of the slow brain waves of deep sleep, measured on the EEG, at the start of the night, which is larger the longer you were awake beforehand.

The second process is the **circadian clock**, **Process C**. *Circadian* means "about a day": the clock keeps a cycle near 24 hours even in a room with no windows or clocks. The clock's main push is toward waking, and that push rises and falls once a day. In people it is strongest in the late afternoon and evening, a few hours before your usual bedtime, and weakest in the early morning, near the daily low point of body temperature.

Put the two together in a picture, with time of day along the bottom and sleep pressure up the side.

- During the day, pressure climbs as a rising curve.
- Above it runs a wavy line: the level pressure must reach before you fall asleep.
- Below it runs a second wavy line: the level pressure must drain to before you wake.
- Both wavy lines rise and fall with the clock.

Over several days the pressure curve is a sawtooth: a slow climb by day to the upper line, then a faster fall by night to the lower one.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** In the simple model of an adult's undisturbed sleep: sleep pressure climbs while you are awake and drains while you sleep. You fall asleep when it climbs to an upper level, and you wake when it drains to a lower one. The clock raises both levels by day and lowers them by night.

Two features of this rule do the work.

- **There are two levels, with a gap between them.** Suppose one level, set midway between the two, did both jobs. In the model you would fall asleep the moment pressure touched it, drain a little, wake, refill a little and fall asleep again, flickering at the line. The gap keeps the model's night of sleep in one long block.
- **The clock moves the levels against the pressure.** As pressure climbs through the afternoon, the clock lifts the upper level ahead of it, so sleep is harder to start and you stay alert into the evening. After you fall asleep, the clock lowers the lower level through the early morning, so you stay asleep while pressure drains. Toward morning the lower level rises again and meets the falling pressure, and you wake.

**Predict.** Use the rule to explain the all-nighter: why is dawn worse than early afternoon? Then predict: when you finally sleep, will you get back every hour you missed?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Staying up means preventing sleep even after pressure crosses the upper level. Here we use the excess above that level as a rough model measure of sleepiness, not a measurement of how a person feels. Near dawn, pressure is high and the clock has pulled the upper level close to its lowest, so pressure sits far above it. By early afternoon, pressure is higher still, but the clock has lifted the upper level, so the excess has shrunk. You are less sleepy without having slept at all.

Recovery sleep is longer than usual, but nowhere near hour for hour. In the simple model (The two-process model in numbers, under Going deeper), pressure drains faster than it built and the rising morning level still ends the night, so a whole night missed buys under an hour of extra sleep. In real recovery nights, much of the payback comes as deeper slow-wave sleep rather than as extra hours.

</details>

### Can the two processes be pulled apart?

The clock sits in the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** (SCN), a small paired cluster of neurons just above the optic chiasm, where some fibres from each optic nerve cross to the opposite side. A **nucleus** here is a compact group of neuron cell bodies in the brain. If the rule is right, removing the clock should take away the timing of sleep but leave the pressure in place.

**Predict.** Destroy the SCN in a rat, then keep the rat awake for some hours. Will it make up the lost sleep afterwards?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It does. In rats whose SCN had been destroyed, sleep was no longer gathered into the daily rest phase but was scattered across day and night. After sleep deprivation, these rats still slept more, and more deeply, as intact rats do (Tobler, Borbély and Groos, 1983; Mistlberger and colleagues, 1983). Without the clock, sleep loses its timing, but the pressure still builds and is still repaid.

The simple model shares the key feature: with the clock's daily swing set to zero, it still alternates, one block of sleep about every 22 hours, out of step with the day.

</details>

**What the rule covers.** It describes when the main sleep episode starts and ends in adults, and why it comes as one block. Its numbers are illustrative, and it does not show the many short bouts in which rats and mice sleep. It does not say which stage of sleep you are in, and it does not say how neurons make the flip. S and C are quantities in a model: the slow-wave activity of the EEG is a marker of S, not S itself, and what S is made of in the brain is not settled.

The rest of the lesson takes the rule's questions in turn. Where is the clock, and is it really a clock? How does a cell keep time near 24 hours? How does light keep it on the 24-hour day? What does pressure look like across a night? And what holds the switch?

## Where the clock is

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Four terms first.

- A rhythm's **period** is the time one cycle takes.
- Its **phase** is where in the cycle it is at a given moment, such as the hour daily activity starts.
- A rhythm is **free-running** when every time cue is removed. The period it keeps then is its free-running period.
- A clock is **entrained** when a repeating cue, usually daily light, locks it to that cycle with a steady phase. For the light-dark day, that means a period of exactly 24 hours.

A circadian rhythm free-runs near a day, so the timer is inside the animal. The SCN sits in the anterior hypothalamus (see [the hypothalamus and autonomic control](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/)).

Two experiments made the SCN the master clock. In 1972 Friedrich Stephan and Irving Zucker made bilateral electrolytic lesions of the SCN in albino rats, destroying it on both sides with an electric current. The rats still drank, but no longer on a daily schedule: SCN lesions eliminated circadian drinking and locomotor rhythms. That shows the region is needed, not that it generates time. A lesion also cuts passing fibres and could remove a relay.

Martin Ralph, Russell Foster, Fred Davis and Michael Menaker answered that in hamsters in 1990. They used the **tau** mutation as a label. It shortens the hamster's free-running period: wild-type hamsters run near 24 hours, hamsters with one copy of tau near 22 hours, and hamsters with two copies near 20 hours. The team destroyed the host's SCN, so its activity rhythm was gone, then grafted in SCN-region tissue from a donor of a different genotype.

**Predict.** The donor's clock runs fast. If the host's rhythms come back, whose period will they have?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The restored rhythms ran with the donor genotype's period, whatever the host's genotype. A relay would have passed on the host's period. A pacemaker imposes its own.

</details>

The SCN is not the only clock. Yoo and colleagues (2004) made knock-in mice in which the clock protein PER2 is fused to luciferase, so tissues glow in time with the clock. Isolated peripheral tissues such as liver sustained circadian oscillations for more than 20 cycles in culture. After SCN lesions, those tissue rhythms continued but drifted out of phase with one another. So the SCN keeps the body's clocks in step. It does not supply their ticking.

So Process C has a home: the SCN is the clock whose daily rise and fall the rule's two levels follow.

## How the clock ticks

The genes came first from flies. Ronald Konopka and Seymour Benzer (1971) found mutations in one Drosophila gene, period, that made flies arrhythmic or gave them short or long periods (see [CRISPR and functional genomics](/lessons/crispr-and-functional-genomics/) for how such screens work). Mammals use related genes in a loop.

1. In mouse cells, the transcription factors CLOCK and BMAL1 bind as a pair to **E-boxes**, short DNA sequences near their target genes. They activate Per1 and, as later work showed, the other Per genes and the Cry genes (see [transcription](/lessons/transcription/)).
2. PER and CRY proteins build up, bind each other and enter the nucleus.
3. In the nucleus they bind CLOCK-BMAL1 and block it, so PER and CRY switch off their own genes. That is the brake.
4. Only as PER and CRY are degraded does the brake come off, and a new cycle begins.

Why does that oscillate instead of settling at a steady level? Think of a shower with a long pipe: you turn toward hot, nothing changes, you turn further, and when the hot water arrives you have overshot. Negative feedback with enough delay, and repression that is steep enough, keeps overshooting in both directions. In the clock, the long pipe is the hours of delay supplied by transcription, translation, modification, nuclear entry and timed degradation. [Signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/) treats delayed negative feedback as a circuit motif.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/sleep-and-rhythms/clock-feedback-loop.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a cell diagram shows CLOCK and BMAL1 bound to an E-box in the nucleus switching on Per and Cry genes; PER and CRY proteins build up in the cytoplasm, return to the nucleus and block CLOCK-BMAL1 at the E-box, and degraded PER-CRY fragments mark the release of the brake." caption="The clock is a delayed feedback loop: CLOCK-BMAL1 switches on Per and Cry, PER and CRY build up and return to the nucleus to shut CLOCK-BMAL1 off, and only their degradation lets a new cycle begin." />

The delays, steep enough repression and the rate at which PER and CRY are broken down together set a period near 24 hours, not exactly 24. That is why the clock needs a daily reset.

## How light keeps the clock on time

Light reaches the SCN through the **retinohypothalamic tract**, a **tract** being a bundle of axons within the central nervous system.

Many of the retinal ganglion cells that send axons to the SCN sense light themselves. They express **melanopsin**, a photopigment that signals through a different G-protein cascade from the rod's cGMP pathway (see [the senses and cGMP](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/) and [the retina](/lessons/retina/)). Rods and cones help too. In the mouse SCN, light pulses strong enough to reset the clock rapidly induced Per1 transcripts (Shigeyoshi and colleagues, 1997), so light reaches into the gene loop itself.

### Which way light moves the clock

Whether light pushes the clock earlier or later depends on when it arrives.

- Light in your biological evening tells the clock that the day is not over yet, so the clock runs later. That is a **phase delay**.
- Light in your biological morning tells the clock that the day began early, so the clock runs earlier. That is a **phase advance**.

In healthy people under controlled conditions, bright light before the daily low point of core body temperature delayed the clock and light after it advanced the clock. That low point usually falls in the early morning, a couple of hours before habitual waking.

**Predict.** A typical sleeper goes to bed near midnight and wakes near 8 in the morning, so the temperature low point falls around 6. Bright light at 23:00, or at 07:00 on a morning they are already up: which way does each move the clock?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Light at 23:00 comes before the temperature low point, in the biological evening, so it delays the clock. Light at 07:00 comes after it, in the biological morning, so it advances the clock.

</details>

**Worked problem: free-running drift.** Czeisler and colleagues (1999) measured a mean intrinsic period of 24.18 h in healthy young and older adults under controlled dim light. That is a study mean; individuals vary. Assume an idealized clock with exactly that period, no time cues, starting in phase with a 24.00-h day.

*Step 1: excess per cycle.* 24.18 − 24.00 = 0.18 h, which is 0.18 × 60 = 10.8 min per cycle.

*Step 2: seven internal cycles.* 7 × 0.18 h = 1.26 h, which is 75.6 min. The clock's marker now falls 75.6 min later than on the 24-h day.

*Step 3: check.* Internal time is 7 × 24.18 = 169.26 h. External time is 7 × 24.00 = 168.00 h. The difference is 1.26 h, as before.

*Step 4: what entrainment needs.* To stay at 24.00 h, this clock needs an average net light-driven advance of 10.8 min per day. Each day the morning advance must outweigh the evening delay by that much.

Without that daily light, this clock would drift later each day. Because the rule's levels ride on the clock, the timing of sleep would drift with it.

## Reading a night

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

The rule says pressure is highest when you fall asleep and drains through the night. What does that look like in a recording?

Sleep is scored by **polysomnography**: scalp EEG, the electro-oculogram (EOG) for eye movements and the chin electromyogram (EMG) for muscle tone. The night is cut into 30-second **epochs**, and each epoch gets one stage.

EEG rhythms are named by frequency: beta at about 13 to 30 Hz (common in alert waking), alpha at 8 to 13 Hz (common in relaxed waking with eyes closed), theta at 4 to 7 Hz and delta below 4 Hz. Slower, larger waves mean that many cortical neurons are active and silent in step, which happens as NREM sleep deepens. In adults:

- **N1**: alpha gives way to theta.
- **N2**: **sleep spindles**, trains of 11 to 16 Hz waves lasting at least 0.5 s, and **K-complexes**, well-defined biphasic waves lasting at least 0.5 s.
- **N3**: slow waves of 0.5 to 2 Hz with peak-to-peak amplitude above 75 µV in frontal leads, filling at least 20% of the epoch. N3 merges what older scoring rules called stages 3 and 4.
- **REM**: an EEG that can look much like waking, rapid eye movements on the EOG, and the lowest muscle tone of the night on the EMG.

So the EOG and EMG separate REM from waking. Through a night, NREM and REM sleep alternate in several cycles.

**Predict.** While pressure drains, the clock heads toward its low point in the early morning. In which half of the night will most N3 fall? And most REM?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Most N3 falls in the first half of the night, when pressure is highest. In the simple model, about 70% of the sleep-onset pressure is left after an hour and a half of sleep, and about a fifth after six and a half hours. REM grows toward morning. Deep sleep mainly follows the pressure; the rise of REM toward morning follows both the clock and the time already slept.

</details>

**Worked problem: scoring a hypnogram.** In this made-up recording, a subject is in bed for 960 epochs. The scorer counts 60 epochs of wake, 48 of N1, 432 of N2, 192 of N3 and 228 of REM.

*Step 1: total time.* 960 × 30 s = 28,800 s = 8.0 h. The counts add to 60 + 48 + 432 + 192 + 228 = 960.

*Step 2: sleep.* 960 − 60 = 900 epochs, or 900 × 30 s = 27,000 s = 7.5 h.

*Step 3: sleep efficiency*, sleep divided by time in bed: 900 / 960 = 0.9375, or 93.75%.

*Step 4: stage shares of sleep.* REM 228 / 900 = 25.3%. N3 192 / 900 = 21.3%. N2 432 / 900 = 48.0%. N1 48 / 900 = 5.3%. Rounded, they add to 99.9%; the unrounded shares add to 100%. These are practice numbers, not norms.

## The switch: why sleep comes in one block

The rule's two levels need a switch that flips to sleep at a high pressure and back only at a much lower one. How could the brain do that?

In the ventrolateral preoptic area (VLPO), sleep-active neurons release the inhibitory transmitter GABA and inhibit wake-promoting cell groups: histamine neurons of the tuberomammillary nucleus, noradrenaline neurons of the locus coeruleus, serotonin neurons of the raphe and cholinergic neurons of the brainstem (see [transmitter systems](/lessons/transmitter-systems/)). In one model, those groups inhibit the VLPO in return, a mutual inhibition called a **flip-flop**: whichever side is ahead silences the other, so transitions are quick and intermediate states are rare.

Mutual inhibition also gives the switch a memory, which engineers call **hysteresis**. Once the sleep side wins, it silences the wake side, which can then no longer push back. Pressure has to drain well below the level at which sleep began before waking can win again. That memory is one way to explain the gap between the rule's two levels. It is a model, and its details have exceptions (see Going deeper).

## Orexin holds the switch

A flip-flop switches quickly but can flip too easily, because near the balance point a small push from either side tips it. Neurons in the lateral hypothalamus that make the peptides **orexin A and B** (also called hypocretin 1 and 2) excite many of the wake-promoting groups, and are thought to hold the switch on the wake side, like a finger holding a light switch up. Remove the finger and the switch still works, but it flips at the slightest nudge: waking breaks into short bouts.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/sleep-and-rhythms/flipflop-orexin.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a circuit diagram shows sleep-active VLPO neurons and a cluster of wake-promoting cell groups inhibiting each other, with orexin neurons above sending excitatory lines to the wake-promoting groups." caption="In the flip-flop model, sleep-active VLPO neurons and wake-promoting groups inhibit each other, and orexin neurons, which excite the wake side, are thought to hold the switch steady in waking." />

Three lines of evidence tie orexin to narcolepsy, and each comes from a different species.

- **Dogs.** In a familial dog model, Lin and colleagues (1999) found an autosomal recessive mutation in the hypocretin receptor 2 gene.
- **Mice.** Conditional loss of orexin neurons in adults fragmented waking and increased transitions between sleep and wake states (Tabuchi and colleagues, 2014).
- **People.** Thannickal and colleagues (2000) counted 85 to 95% fewer hypocretin neurons in four postmortem narcolepsy brains than in twelve controls, while neighbouring neurons making melanin-concentrating hormone were not reduced.

Most human patients do not carry the dog's receptor mutation; narcolepsy type 1 is associated with severe loss of brain orexin. An autoimmune attack on these neurons is hypothesized, not established. **Cataplexy**, the sudden muscle weakness many patients have, occurs with consciousness preserved, so it is not falling asleep. It is thought to be REM sleep's loss of muscle tone breaking into waking.

Adamantidis and colleagues (2007) expressed channelrhodopsin-2 in hypocretin neurons of mice and delivered light through an optical fibre into the lateral hypothalamus of freely moving animals. Stimulation increased transitions from slow-wave and REM sleep to waking.

What the sleeping brain does with its hours, including replay of hippocampal activity, is the subject of [memory consolidation](/lessons/memory-consolidation/).

### Design problem

A colleague says the optogenetic result and the human cell counts prove orexin neurons are needed for waking. Design a mouse experiment that asks whether they are needed for waking itself or for stable waking: what you measure, your controls, and your prediction.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Driving the neurons shows that their activity is enough to wake a mouse under that stimulation: sufficiency, not necessity. The human series is correlational: far fewer cells stain for hypocretin in four patients, but the counts cannot say when or why that happened.

To test necessity, silence or ablate the neurons with an inhibitory opsin or a toxin expressed only in orexin neurons, and do it in adult mice so development cannot compensate. Include control mice that lack the opsin or toxin but receive the same light or the same induction, to control for heat, light, handling and the treatment itself. Record EEG and EMG continuously and have blind scorers assign stages in fixed epochs.

Measure total wake per 24 h, the mean duration of wake bouts, the number of transitions between states, and episodes of abrupt atonia that come straight out of sustained waking. If the neurons are needed for stable waking, you predict much shorter wake bouts and many more transitions with little loss of total wake, plus cataplexy-like episodes, scored as abrupt atonia lasting at least 10 s with a theta-dominated EEG after at least 40 s of waking, with video showing immobility. If they were needed for waking itself, total wake would fall sharply.

Two limits remain. Orexin neurons release other transmitters, so silencing the cells does not isolate the peptide; a receptor antagonist or peptide knockout tests that. And the result holds for these mice and this timing, not for human patients.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The two-process model in numbers

A simple version of the rule, in the style of the model of Daan, Beersma and Borbély (1984), uses illustrative values, not measurements. S rises toward 1 with a time constant of 18 h while awake and falls toward 0 with a time constant of 4 h while asleep. The upper level is 0.67 and the lower level 0.17, each plus the same daily cosine of amplitude 0.12, highest at about 17:00. Run with these values, the model:

- sleeps from about 00:20 to about 08:10, about 7.8 h, and stays locked to the 24-hour day;
- if both levels are replaced by one midway between them, flips back and forth at that line once pressure reaches it, because the waking and sleeping curves both point toward it all day; how short those episodes get is set by the simulation's time step, not by biology, and other single lines can allow longer bouts;
- with the clock's swing set to zero, alternates about 16.6 h awake and 5.5 h asleep, a cycle of about 22.1 h;
- if kept awake from 08:10 until midnight the next night, about 40 h, sleeps about 8.7 h instead of 7.8 h.

These outputs show the rule hangs together; they are not measurements of people.

### Who showed each step of the clock loop

Gekakis and colleagues (1998) showed that CLOCK and BMAL1 bind E-boxes as a pair and activate Per1 in mouse cells. In reporter assays either CRY1 or CRY2 strongly inhibited CLOCK-BMAL1-driven transcription (Kume and colleagues, 1999). Kinases set part of the loop's timing: casein kinase 1 phosphorylates PER, and the hamster tau mutation is an allele of casein kinase 1 epsilon (Lowrey and colleagues, 2000).

### How the light-sensing ganglion cells were found

Berson, Dunn and Takao (2002) recorded from SCN-projecting retinal ganglion cells in isolated rat retina and found that they still depolarized to light with rod and cone input blocked. Those cells express melanopsin (Hattar and colleagues, 2002). Mice failed to entrain to light-dark cycles only when melanopsin and rod and cone phototransduction were all removed (Hattar and colleagues, 2003).

### REM atonia and dreaming

In rodents, glutamatergic neurons of the sublaterodorsal nucleus are thought to produce REM atonia by recruiting inhibitory neurons in the medulla and spinal cord. Dreams are not limited to REM: people woken from NREM sleep also report them (Siclari and colleagues, 2017).

### Qualifications to the flip-flop model

The sleep-active VLPO neurons release galanin as well as GABA. The flip-flop scheme also lists cholinergic neurons of the basal forebrain among the groups the VLPO inhibits. Each link in the scheme has to be tested on its own: a slice response to a transmitter does not show which neurons supply it.

In slice recordings, putative sleep-promoting VLPO cells were inhibited by noradrenaline, acetylcholine and mostly serotonin, not directly by histamine (Gallopin and colleagues, 2000). In mice, Chung and colleagues (2017) found that activating preoptic GABA neurons that project to the tuberomammillary nucleus increased NREM sleep, while activating preoptic GABA neurons in general increased waking. Projection target together with transmitter, not location alone, defines a sleep-promoting cell: glutamatergic preoptic neurons with the same target promoted waking.

### Why an autoimmune cause is suspected

Narcolepsy type 1 is strongly associated with the immune-system gene variant HLA-DQB1\*06:02, and T-cell findings point the same way. Neither shows that an immune attack destroys the orexin neurons, so the hypothesis remains unproven.

### Orexin experiment details

In the Tabuchi experiment, dark-period wake time was significantly reduced from 3 to 10 weeks after induction, with recovery by 11 weeks (Tabuchi and colleagues, 2014). In the Adamantidis experiment, trains at 5 to 30 Hz shortened the latency to wake, while 1 Hz did not (Adamantidis and colleagues, 2007).
