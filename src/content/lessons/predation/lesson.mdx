techniques:
  - name: "Predator removal with an adjacent control"
    blurb: >-
      Keep a predator out of one area and leave a neighboring area untouched, then survey both
      over time, for example along line transects. A difference that appears after removal points
      to the predator, but with one pair of plots it says nothing about how widely the effect holds.
  - name: "Risk-only predator manipulation"
    blurb: >-
      Disable the predator's ability to kill, here by gluing a spider's mouthparts, while leaving
      it present to be sensed. Comparing risk-only, normal-predator and no-predator treatments
      separates effects carried by killing from effects carried by prey behavior.
  - name: "Comparison of sites with and without a predator"
    blurb: >-
      Survey prey and plants at places where a predator is present and places where it is absent,
      as Estes and Palmisano did on Aleutian islands. It is quick and large in scale, but the
      places may differ in other ways, so it supports a cascade without proving one.
  - name: "Factorial exclosure experiment"
    blurb: >-
      Cross two treatments, such as fencing out browsers and building simulated beaver dams, so
      every combination is present at each site. This lets you estimate each factor's effect and
      whether one matters only when the other is present.

    choices:
      - "Each orbit is neutral, so the starting numbers set its amplitude"
      - "Predator numbers peak a little before prey numbers in each cycle"
      - "Every starting point is drawn onto one cycle of a fixed amplitude"
      - "The oscillations always damp out, settling at the equilibrium point"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The classical model has a family of closed orbits around the equilibrium; each start with
      both populations present and away from the equilibrium keeps its own orbit, so the amplitude is set by where it
      starts and a disturbance moves the system to a different orbit. A single attracting cycle of
      fixed amplitude, a limit cycle, needs a modified model with prey self-limitation and a

  - stem: "A predator-prey system follows the classical Lotka-Volterra model with r = 2 per day, a = 0.02 per predator per day, e = 0.2 predators per prey eaten and m = 0.4 per day. What are the equilibrium numbers?"
    choices:
      - "N* = 20 prey and P* = 100 predators"
      - "N* = 50 prey and P* = 100 predators"
      - "N* = 100 prey and P* = 100 predators"
      - "N* = 100 prey and P* = 20 predators"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      N* = m/(ea) = 0.4/(0.2 x 0.02) = 0.4/0.004 = 100 prey, and P* = r/a = 2/0.02 = 100

      conversion efficiency where the death rate belongs.

The first film followed kills into two **ledgers**, each population's running account of births and deaths, and found the lag in that bookkeeping. This text starts from the equations it left out.

> **The one idea.** Every kill is entered in two ledgers: a death in the prey's, and food that, on average, the predators turn into births. So each population's **per capita** (per individual) growth rate is set by the other's numbers: r - aP for each prey, eaN - m for each predator. And a predator can affect species it rarely or never eats, even helping some it also eats, by changing the numbers or behaviour of the species in between.
>
> *Scope:* the two rates belong to the classical Lotka-Volterra model below, and a population's total change is its per capita rate times its own numbers. The reach to other species comes from field experiments, and its size and even its sign depend on the community.

## Two ledgers, written as equations

In [population growth](/lessons/population-growth/) each population changed by its own births and deaths. A **predator** is a consumer that kills and eats other organisms, its **prey**. Alfred Lotka (1925, in his book *Elements of Physical Biology*) and Vito Volterra (1926, in *Nature*) independently wrote the two ledgers as equations. With N prey and P predators:

> **dN/dt = rN - aNP      dP/dt = eaNP - mP**

Here r is the prey's per capita growth rate without predators (per day). The **attack rate** a is the risk of being caught that one predator adds to each prey (units: per predator per day), so aP is each prey's death rate from predation and aNP is the number of prey eaten per day. The conversion e is the average number of new predators made per prey eaten, and m is the predators' per capita death rate (per day). The ledgers are linked by rates, not kill by kill: aNP prey deaths per day feed eaNP predator births per day. Divided by N and by P, the equations give the one idea's per capita rates.

The assumptions are strong. Without predators the prey grow exponentially, with no carrying capacity. Without prey the predators decline exponentially. Each predator's intake rises in proportion to prey numbers with no **handling time** (the time spent catching and eating each prey), so a predator never gets full. Populations are well mixed (every predator equally likely to meet every prey), with no age structure, no space and constant parameters.

The first film's two threshold lines are **isoclines**: the sets of numbers at which one population stops changing. Set each per capita rate to zero: r - aP = 0 gives P = r/a, and eaN - m = 0 gives N = m/(ea). Where the two lines cross is the equilibrium:

> **N* = m/(ea)      P* = r/a**

**Predict.** The prey start breeding twice as fast (r doubles) and nothing else changes. At the new balance point, which population is larger than before: the prey, the predators, or both?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only the predators. N* = m/(ea) contains no r, so the prey equilibrium does not move; P* = r/a doubles. With the worked numbers below, (500, 100) becomes (500, 200). Each equilibrium is set by the *other* population's parameters: faster prey breeding feeds more predators, and the extra predators eat the extra prey.

</details>

**Worked problem.** These numbers are made up for illustration: r = 1 per day, a = 0.01 per predator per day, e = 0.1 predators per prey eaten, m = 0.5 per day. Today there are N = 400 prey and P = 50 predators.

1. *Prey rate.* Prey births are rN = 1 x 400 = 400 prey per day. Prey eaten are aNP = 0.01 x 400 x 50 = 200 prey per day. So dN/dt = 400 - 200 = +200 prey per day.
2. *Predator rate.* Predator production is eaNP = 0.1 x 200 = 20 predators per day. Predator deaths are mP = 0.5 x 50 = 25 predators per day. So dP/dt = 20 - 25 = -5 predators per day.
3. *Equilibrium.* N* = 0.5 / (0.1 x 0.01) = 0.5 / 0.001 = 500 prey. P* = 1 / 0.01 = 100 predators. Check: at (500, 100), prey births are 500 and prey eaten are 0.01 x 500 x 100 = 500, so dN/dt = 0; predator production is 0.1 x 500 = 50 and deaths are 0.5 x 100 = 50, so dP/dt = 0.
4. *Read the state.* Prey are below 500 and predators below 100: the bottom-left zone, where prey rise and predators fall. The simulation below starts here, at (400, 50); run it and note which curve turns first.

## What the classical cycle leaves out

With both populations present and away from the equilibrium, the loops are **neutrally stable**, like a marble on one of many nested, frictionless tracks: a nudge moves it to another track, and it circles that one instead, with no single cycle pulling it back.

Averaged over any full loop, the numbers equal N* and P*. Over one loop each population ends where it began, so its per capita rate averages zero, which requires the average of P to be r/a (and of N, m/(ea)). On the loop through (400, 50) that gives 500 prey and 100 predators, to within rounding.

Hence Volterra's fishing argument: harvest both populations at the same per capita rate h, and the averages become (m + h)/(ea) prey and (r - h)/a predators, prey up and predators down, as long as h stays below r so that predators persist.

**Predict.** Give the worked-problem prey a carrying capacity, K = 400, so that without predators they settle at 400. Can the predators persist?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Each predator replaces itself only when prey exceed N* = 500. Even at 400 prey, a rare predator's per capita rate is eaN - m = 0.001 x 400 - 0.5 = -0.1 per day, so predators decline to extinction and the prey settle at 400.

</details>

Two changes to the assumptions matter most:

| Change to the model | What happens to the cycles |
|---|---|
| Prey self-limitation (logistic growth with carrying capacity K) | The neutral cycles disappear. If K is above N*, the populations settle at the equilibrium, often through damped oscillations, and the prey line tilts to P = (r/a)(1 - N/K), so the predator equilibrium falls below r/a: with the worked numbers and K = 2000, 100 x (1 - 500/2000) = 75 predators at 500 prey. If K is below N*, predators die out. |
| Handling time as well, so predators get full | For some parameter values the model settles onto one cycle whose size does not depend on the start (a limit cycle); for others it settles at an equilibrium, or the predators die out. |

Real records rarely fit the classical model either (Going deeper).

## What one shore can and cannot show

The film's count of 15 to 8 covered only species holding space on the rock. A mussel bed is itself a habitat: count the species living among the mussels and the result can reverse (Going deeper).

The keystone label compares effect with abundance, so the most common species is not a keystone by default: at Mukkaw Bay the mussels were the **dominant competitor** (the winner of the contest for space), and Pisaster was the keystone.

The chiton's two arrows are the general case. A predator's net effect on a species combines its direct effects (killing it, or changing how it behaves) with effects passed through other species, and the two can pull in opposite directions. Larger limpets left the patch as well as the chitons, so there the indirect help won. A weaker species gains only if release from the dominant competitor outweighs what the predator costs it directly, and heavier predation can reverse that balance. So a keystone predator *can* raise **richness** (the number of species), not always does. Why space on a rock is a **limiting resource** (one whose supply caps the populations that use it) is the subject of [competition](/lessons/competition/).

## Cascades through killing and through fear

A **trophic level** is a feeding position in a food chain: plants, herbivores, carnivores. A **trophic cascade** is an indirect predator effect that extends through several trophic levels: predator to herbivore to plant.

In a 1974 paper, James Estes and John Palmisano compared islands in the western Aleutians, Alaska: Amchitka, where a dense otter population had recovered after fur hunting, and Shemya, where otters had not come back (figure). Otters eat urchins, and urchins graze kelp, so the urchin and kelp pattern fits a two-link cascade, but no one removed otters. Later evidence came from a change over time: when sea otters declined across western Alaska in the 1990s, probably because killer whales ate more of them, urchins increased and kelp beds were lost.

A **density-mediated** cascade works by killing herbivores, so fewer are left to eat plants. A **trait-mediated** cascade works by changing what surviving herbivores do, through the risk of being eaten: fear. In enclosures in a Connecticut old field, Andrew Beckerman, Maria Uriarte and Oswald Schmitz (1997) pulled them apart. They kept grasshoppers with no spiders, with hunting spiders, or with spiders whose mouthparts were glued so they could not kill.

Landscape-scale cascades are harder to pin down. Wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in the mid-1990s, and willow recovery has often been credited to them. In an experiment by Kristin Marshall, Thompson Hobbs and David Cooper (2013), reducing browsing alone did not bring the willows to the 2-metre height they used as their recovery criterion (design under Going deeper). Wolves were never an experimental treatment. A 2024 extension of the ten-year experiment to about two decades, and published disagreement over how willow recovery should be measured, leave the park-wide question open.

## How far one system generalizes

Paine extended the removals himself: by 1974 he reported that after Pisaster was removed, mussels moved down the shore at both Mukkaw Bay and Tatoosh Island, with no change at control sites. A 2025 review by Anthony Gillis and colleagues found 25 publications that explicitly manipulated predators to test keystone predation: 10 reported consistent support, 7 found the effect context-dependent and 8 found none. Their search relied on the term itself, so it could miss studies that used other words, and a vote count is not an estimate of how often keystone predation occurs. So a keystone result on one shore is a hypothesis for the next shore, not a given.

Sea-star wasting disease, which killed Pisaster along much of the Pacific coast from 2013, has since run a coast-scale removal (Going deeper).

### Design problem

Design a study that tests whether keystone predation by Pisaster generalizes across the coast. Say what you would measure, how you would replicate, and what result would count against generality.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Choose many sites that differ in wave exposure and mussel recruitment, since both could change how fast mussels take space. At each site assign several plots to removal or control at random. Survey every plot before removal and yearly for several years after (a before-after-control-impact design), to separate a change from what was already happening.

Fix the list of taxa you count before you start, so richness means the same thing everywhere. Measure mussel cover in every plot: if removal lowers richness, mussel cover should rise first, which tests the mechanism.

Use the sea-star wasting die-off from 2013 as a natural experiment, comparing shores that lost most of their Pisaster with shores that lost fewer; the die-off was not randomly assigned.

Expect context dependence. If richness falls where mussel recruitment is high and changes little where it is low, keystone predation is real but conditional, which fits the split in the Gillis review above.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Real data rarely fit the classical model

The famous hare and lynx cycle comes from fur-trade records, which reflect trapping effort as well as animal numbers. In the 1950s Carl Huffaker could keep predatory and plant-eating mites coexisting on oranges only when he made the arena patchy, a story taken up in [movement and spatial populations](/lessons/spatial-populations/). And rapid evolution of prey defenses can reshape cycles, as Takehito Yoshida and colleagues found with rotifers and algae, the subject of [mutualism, parasitism and coevolution](/lessons/mutualism-parasitism-coevolution/).

### The period of the cycle

Near the equilibrium the cycle takes about 2 pi / square root of (rm) = 6.283 / square root of 0.5 = 6.283 / 0.707 = 8.89 days. This is a small-amplitude approximation. The larger orbit that passes through (400, 50) takes about 9.3 days, computed by solving the equations numerically.

### How the patch filled, and when the word keystone appeared

In the film's sequence, the acorn barnacles took most of the open rock within months. Kevin Lafferty and Thomas Suchanek pointed out in 2016 that mussel beds shelter over 300 associated species. Counting those, they argued, removing Pisaster would raise community-wide richness. The memorial to Paine in PNAS dates the keystone term to a short note he published in 1969. Keep the three dates apart: removal began in the early 1960s, the paper is from 1966, and the term is from 1969.

### The Yellowstone experiment in detail

Marshall, Hobbs and Cooper ran a ten-year factorial experiment at four small-stream sites on the northern range, crossing fences that excluded browsers such as elk with simulated beaver dams, which raised July water tables by 33 cm on average.

### What the wasting die-off showed

Monica Moritsch and Peter Raimondi (2018) found that sea-star counts at monitoring sites could recover while biomass, a proxy for predation potential, stayed below 40% of its pre-outbreak level in 2015 to 2017 at most sites outside North Central California, because recovering populations were made of small stars. Near Monterey, Joshua Smith and colleagues (2025) reported that mussels grew larger and spread lower on the shore after Pisaster collapsed, and they inferred that eating more mussels contributed in part to a local rise in sea otters.
