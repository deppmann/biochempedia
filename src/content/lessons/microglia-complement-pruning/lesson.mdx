    profileSourceAnchor: "Stevens et al., The classical complement cascade mediates CNS synapse elimination, Cell 131:1164-1178 (2007)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Barres's laboratory at Stanford studied what glia do for neurons. Work on purified retinal ganglion cells, the neurons that send the eye's output to the brain,
      held a surprise: when the neurons were grown with astrocytes, they raised their messenger RNA
      for C1q, the first protein of the classical complement cascade. Complement was known as part
      of the immune system, a set of blood proteins that mark microbes and debris for removal. Why
      would a developing neuron make it? Stevens and her colleagues looked in the young mouse brain
      and found C1q in the retina and in the visual thalamus during the first postnatal weeks,
      much of it near small synaptic contacts. Mice lacking C1q, and mice lacking C3, kept too much
      overlap between the inputs from the two eyes, and their relay neurons kept extra inputs. The
      2007 paper proposed that unwanted synapses are tagged by complement for elimination. That
      model left one question open, which her later laboratory took up: which cell reads the tag?

    story: >-
      The 2007 knockouts showed that complement was needed, but no one had shown which cell
      carried the dLGN inputs away. Schafer, then a postdoctoral fellow in the Stevens laboratory, set out to catch
      the removal in fixed tissue. Her team injected differently coloured tracers into the two eyes
      of mice four days after birth and examined the visual thalamus a day later. In microglia

techniques:
  - name: "Eye-specific tracer injection"
    blurb: >-
      Two cholera toxin fragments carrying different fluorescent dyes are injected into the two
      eyes. Retinal axons carry them to the visual thalamus, where the overlap between the two
      colours measures how far eye-specific segregation has gone, and dye found inside microglia
      shows whose inputs they took.
  - name: "Engulfment quantification by 3D reconstruction"
    blurb: >-
      Confocal image stacks of fluorescently labelled microglia are rebuilt in three dimensions, and
      labelled material outside the cell's volume is removed. The volume of internalized material is
      divided by the volume of the microglial cell, so a large cell does not appear to engulf more
      merely because it is large.
  - name: "Knockout mice"
    blurb: >-
      In the whole-body knockout mice used here, both copies of one gene are disrupted in every cell. Comparing knockout and normal
      littermates for C1q, C3, complement receptor 3 or CX3CR1 asks whether that protein is needed
      for normal pruning, though not where or when it acts.
  - name: "Whole-cell recording of relay neurons"
    blurb: >-
      In a brain slice, the optic tract is stimulated with increasing strength while the current in
      one thalamic relay neuron is recorded. Each new step in the response marks one more retinal
      axon recruited, which gives an estimate of how many inputs the cell still has.

    rationale: >-

      CX3CR1 or ATP from damaged cells can. An eat-me signal sits on the target and tells an
      arriving phagocyte to engulf it, as exposed phosphatidylserine or bound complement does. The
      reversed pairing puts the membrane-bound lipid as the attractant. Pairing C3b with ATP also
      reverses the roles. C1q and iC3b are both eat-me tags on the target, so neither is a find-me
      signal.
  - stem: "In the classical complement cascade, what links C1q binding to a target with the deposit that complement receptor 3 recognizes?"
    choices:
      - "C1q opens a pore in the synaptic membrane that lets C3 into the cell"
      - "C1q itself is cleaved into iC3b by proteases on the microglia"
      - "C1q-activated enzymes build a convertase that cuts C3 into C3a and C3b"
      - "C1q phosphorylates C3, which then binds complement receptor 3 at once"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Bound C1q activates its partner proteases, C1r and then C1s, and C1s cuts C4 and C2 to build a C3 convertase; this
      cuts C3 into C3a and C3b, and C3b attaches to the surface and is trimmed to iC3b, the ligand
      of complement receptor 3. C1q is not itself converted into iC3b; the two are different
      proteins. The pore that complement can form comes from late components, not from C1q, and it
      does not carry C3 into the cell. C1q is not a kinase and does not phosphorylate C3.

**A developing retinal neuron can make C1q, the first protein of an immune cascade that marks bacteria for destruction.**

Complement is a set of proteins best known from the blood. They bind microbes, dead cells and clumps of antibody, and they coat them so that phagocytes, the cells that eat other cells, will swallow them. Finding complement in a young neuron looked like a category mistake. It turned out to be a clue to one way a developing brain removes connections it has made too many of. This lesson follows that clue from a gene list to the inside of a microglial lysosome.

## Two eyes, one relay: which inputs lose?

The best-studied case is the dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus (dLGN), the visual relay in the thalamus where retinal axons end. Its relay neurons receive the retinal input and pass it on to the visual cortex. Ages here are written in postnatal days: **P5** means the fifth day after birth.

In a newborn mouse, inputs from the two eyes overlap in the dLGN, and many relay neurons receive inputs from both. Over the first postnatal week and a half, mostly between about P3 and P10, they sort into separate territories, a process called eye-specific segregation. Each dLGN ends up mostly the territory of the opposite eye, with a small patch for the eye on the same side. A single relay neuron also goes from many retinal inputs to a few, a refinement that continues for weeks after the territories have sorted.

An inset shows one relay neuron going from many inputs of both colours to a few." caption="Over the first postnatal week and a half, inputs from the two eyes sort into separate territories in the relay, each nucleus ending up mostly the territory of the opposite eye with a small patch for the eye on the same side. A single relay neuron also goes from many retinal inputs to a few." />

The sorting is driven partly by spontaneous retinal waves, bursts of activity that sweep the young retina before the eyes open (see [the lesson on spontaneous retinal waves](/lessons/spontaneous-activity/)). Inputs that lose are removed. Two questions run through this lesson: which inputs lose, and what carries them away? For the first, eye-manipulation and retinal-wave experiments point to the less active inputs losing (Penn et al., 1998; Stellwagen and Shatz, 2002); this lesson tests whether microglia follow the same bias. The obvious suspects for the second are microglia, the brain's resident immune cells and its main phagocytes.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Relative activity biases which retinal inputs lose, and microglia take up relatively more material from the less active eye; complement helps them take up part of it.

**Scope.** Mouse dLGN, mainly during eye-specific segregation (about P3 to P10), with some readouts extending to about a month. The limits come with the evidence.

## A tag from the immune system

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The classical complement pathway has three steps that matter here.

1. **C1q binds.** C1q is shaped like a bunch of six tulips, each head able to grip a surface.
2. **C3 is cut, and the large piece sticks.** Binding activates a protease in C1q's complex, C1r, which activates a second, C1s. C1s cuts C4 and C2, and the pieces assemble into an enzyme, a C3 convertase, that cuts C3 into a small fragment, C3a, and a large one, C3b. Cutting exposes a reactive bond in C3b that can fix it to a nearby surface.
3. **The tag is read.** C3b is trimmed to iC3b. A phagocyte carrying complement receptor 3 (CR3) binds iC3b, which promotes engulfment of what it coats. CR3 is an integrin, the CD11b and CD18 pair, of the adhesion-receptor family taught in [the lesson on integrins and Rho GTPases](/lessons/integrins-and-rho-gtpases/).

<LessonFigure src="/figures/microglia-complement-pruning/complement-tag-three-steps.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: three steps on the same presynaptic terminal. C1q, drawn as a bunch of six tulips, grips the surface; C3 is cut and the large piece sticks to the terminal; a microglial process carrying a receptor clasps that piece." caption="The three steps of the classical pathway that matter here: C1q binds a surface, C3 is cut and its large piece fixes to that surface, and a phagocyte carrying complement receptor 3 binds the tag, which promotes engulfment." />

Beth Stevens, working in Ben Barres's laboratory at Stanford, found that purified rat retinal ganglion cells, the neurons that carry the eye's output to the brain, raised their expression of C1q when grown with astrocytes. That finding sent the team looking at synapses in the brain. Later work found that microglia make most of the C1q in the mouse brain, so which cell supplies the C1q at dLGN synapses is not settled.

In the young mouse, Stevens and her colleagues found C1q in the retina and dLGN early in postnatal life; neuronal C1q is normally turned down in the adult. In the dLGN, C1q and C3 lay at developing synapses. C1q often sat at small synaptic contacts, which the team took to be immature or disappearing ones. That was a proposal: fixed images could not show which contacts later disappeared.

The test was genetic. In a whole-body knockout mouse, both copies of one gene are disrupted in every cell. To estimate how many retinal inputs one relay neuron has, stimulate the optic tract in a brain slice harder and harder while recording the cell's current: each new step in the current marks one more retinal axon recruited. Weak inputs can be missed, so this is an estimate, not a full count.

**Predict.** If complement helps remove losing inputs, what should the dLGN of a mouse without C1q look like, in its eye territories and in single relay neurons?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In mice lacking C1q, the two eyes' territories failed to refine normally and still overlapped more than in normal littermates. In recordings, most normal relay cells had pruned down to few inputs, while most C1q-knockout cells still received several. Mice lacking C3 showed a similar overlap. The authors proposed that unwanted synapses are tagged by complement for elimination.

</details>

Notice what the knockouts did not show: which synapse got a tag, what read it, or which cell carried the input away.

## Caught in the act: microglia take the quieter eye

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

In 2012 Dorothy Schafer, then a postdoctoral fellow in the Stevens laboratory, went after the missing step with Barres and others. Seeing a fluorescent speck beside a microglial cell does not show that the cell ate it, so engulfment has to be measured inside the cell:

> **engulfment index = (volume of retinal label inside a microglial cell) ÷ (volume of that microglial cell) × 100**

Dividing by the cell's volume means a large cell does not seem to eat more merely because it is large. The team injected red and blue tracers, dyes that retinal axons carry from the eye to their terminals, into the two eyes of mice at P4 and looked in the dLGN a day later, in mice whose microglia were marked green. They rebuilt each microglial cell in three dimensions and kept only the label inside it. Retinal material sat inside microglial lysosomes, the cell's digestive compartments. A dye that glows only in acid, and a genetic label of retinal neurons, gave the same result. Electron microscopy and staining for VGlut2, a protein of retinal terminals, showed that the engulfed material was mainly presynaptic.

**When, and through what.** Engulfment had a clock. It was high at P5, when segregation is under way, and much lower by P9. It also had a receptor. In mice lacking CR3, the microglial reader, or lacking C3 and so the tags cut from it (C3b, iC3b), microglia took up roughly half as much retinal material as normal microglia. Those mice later had more overlap between the eye territories, and more retinal synapses about a month after birth.

**Predict.** Without CR3, microglia ate about half as much. Should a CR3 knockout keep every input it would normally have lost, or only some of them?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only some, as it turned out. Residual uptake shows that microglia still engulf material by routes other than CR3, and astrocytes eat dLGN synapses too, but a halved P5 index cannot by itself predict how much refinement is lost. The index is a snapshot of cargo at P5, not a count of inputs removed, and these experiments do not say whether eating starts the removal or clears material that neurons shed. The wiring answered: refinement fell short rather than failing, and the excess of structural synapses was modest, of the order of 1.3-fold (about 30 percent more) by array tomography, an imaging method that counts synapses in ultrathin serial sections.

</details>

Now the question that matters most: does the eater choose?

**Predict.** Two drugs change one eye's activity. Tetrodotoxin (TTX) blocks sodium channels and silences neurons. Forskolin raises cAMP, a messenger inside the cell, and increases their activity. At P4 you treat one eye of each mouse and give the other eye a matched control injection; each eye gets its own tracer. At P5 you measure how much of each eye's material dLGN microglia hold. If microglia preferentially remove less active inputs, what should each drug do? And before you compare the two eyes, what must you correct for?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

With one eye silenced, microglia took relatively more material from that eye. With one eye boosted, they took relatively more from the untreated eye. The two drugs push in opposite directions, which is stronger evidence than either alone: a drug that simply damaged axons would raise engulfment of its own eye both times.

The correction is for how much of each eye's input is there, around the microglia you sample. Take an invented example. Suppose a cell's neighbourhood holds 90 units of labelled input from one eye and 10 from the other. An eater with no preference that takes a tenth of everything holds 9 units and 1 unit. Now silence the minor eye and let the eater take a fifth of its input: it holds 2 units against 9. The silenced eye is still the smaller raw share, yet per unit of its own input it was eaten twice as much (2 ÷ 10 = 0.2, against 9 ÷ 90 = 0.1).

So compare each eye with its own input, matched or measured where the microglia are sampled. A whole-nucleus territory map cannot give those local numbers. Dividing by microglial volume, as the index does, is needed too, but it does not fix this.

</details>

So the bias is between whole eyes: in this circuit, microglia engulf relatively more of the less active eye's material.

### Design problem

You want to test whether the activity bias needs complement. Using the TTX experiment above, design the comparison that would answer it, name the result that would mean "yes," and list the controls.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Repeat one-eye TTX in normal mice and in CR3 (or C3) knockouts. In each mouse, measure the bias: the silenced eye's eaten fraction against the control eye's, each taken relative to that eye's locally available labelled input. Then ask whether the bias differs by genotype (in statistical terms, a genotype-by-treatment interaction).

- If the bias is lost in the knockout, the deleted protein is needed for the bias to show.
- If the bias survives, the bias can be expressed without that protein.

Neither result says which molecule encodes relative activity. A knockout could lose the bias because it cannot eat tagged targets, not because the tag carries the activity information. A surviving bias could ride on another receptor, and losing CR3 leaves the complement tags in place. Rescue experiments, or deleting the gene only in microglia and only at this age, would sharpen the answer.

Controls:

- Give the other eye a matched saline or vehicle injection, so both eyes are handled the same.
- Measure each dLGN separately, because the silenced eye is the main input to the opposite nucleus and the minor one on its own side.
- Treat the left eye in some mice and the right in others, so a left-right difference cannot pose as an activity effect.
- Check that TTX silences the eye equally well in both genotypes, and that it does not kill retinal ganglion cells, since dying cells would be eaten for a different reason.
- Correct for each eye's local input within each genotype, because the knockouts keep more overlap.
- Check that enough uptake remains in the knockouts to measure a bias.
- Normalize to microglial volume, and score the images blind to genotype and treatment.

</details>

## Find me, eat me

Any pruning claim raises two questions: how does the eater get there, and what marks the target? "Find me" signals draw the eater in; "eat me" signals mark the target. Complement is on the eat-me side; none of the dLGN experiments above tested a find-me signal.

Find-me signals act at a distance. ATP released from damaged cells draws microglial processes toward the damage. Fractalkine, also called CX3CL1, is a membrane protein on neurons that can be cut free; its receptor, CX3CR1, is on microglia. In 2011 Rosa Paolicelli, in Cornelius Gross's laboratory, reported that mice lacking CX3CR1 had fewer microglia and more dendritic spines in the developing hippocampus, with signs of immature connections.

**Predict.** The extra spines in the CX3CR1 knockouts were gone by about P40. Does that mean pruning failed, or something else?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Something else. The excess was transient, so the result is best read as delayed pruning in that region, not its absence. Later studies in other circuits have given mixed answers about whether CX3CR1 is required. In mouse barrel cortex, the part of the cortex that maps the whiskers, its loss blocked engulfment without blocking recruitment, so there the find-me label does not hold: the receptor was needed for eating, not for arrival.

</details>

Eat-me signals sit on the target: complement tags, and phosphatidylserine (PS), a membrane lipid normally kept on the inner, cytoplasmic face of the membrane (see [the lesson on membranes](/lessons/membrane-structure-function/)). A dying cell moves it to the outer face, where phagocytes recognize it. In 2020 a study from Stevens's and Michela Matteoli's groups, jointly led by Nicole Scott-Hewitt and Fabio Perrucci, injected a PS-binding probe into the brain ventricles of P4 mice. At P5, PS turned up on the presynaptic side of a small fraction of dLGN synapses, a fraction that fell between P5 and P10 (in the hippocampus the signal peaked later), and mice lacking caspase-3, an enzyme of apoptosis (programmed cell death), showed no significant difference in the signal. So a synapse can display a death signal locally while its neuron lives. A synapse may then carry two kinds of eat-me mark, a lipid and a protein, possibly read by different receptors. C1q can bind PS, so the two tags may act in series, with the lipid recruiting the protein, or in parallel; which is open.

Two other forms of removal belong elsewhere. Axons that prune themselves using caspases and p75 are in [the lesson on axons that cut themselves back](/lessons/axon-pruning/), and the loss of extra inputs at the neuromuscular junction is in [the lesson on synapse elimination](/lessons/synapse-elimination/).

## When pruning goes wrong: links, not causes

If pruning shapes circuits, too much or too little might matter for disease. Changes in synapse number have been proposed to contribute to autism and to schizophrenia. These are hypotheses, supported by associations, and the disorders themselves are covered in [the lesson on disorders of the synapse](/lessons/synapse-disorders/).

The strongest human evidence linking complement to a disorder is genetic. In 2016, Aswin Sekar, Steven McCarroll and colleagues, including the Stevens laboratory, studied C4, the next protein in the cascade, cut by C1q's enzymes. Humans have two C4 genes, C4A and C4B, in varying copy numbers. Variants predicted to raise C4A expression in the brain were associated with a higher risk of schizophrenia. In mice, loss of C4 reduced pruning in the dLGN.

**Predict.** Put the human and mouse results together. Which way do they suggest pruning might be changed in schizophrenia, and how strong is the case?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Together they suggest that more complement activity might mean more pruning, which fits the long-standing observation of fewer dendritic spines in some schizophrenia brains. The case is a hypothesis, not a cause. The human result is an association, the common C4 variants each change risk only modestly, and the mouse result comes from the visual thalamus, not the human cortex.

</details>

Within this scope the experiments support a narrower statement than the guiding idea: relative activity biases how much of each eye's retinal material microglia engulf, and complement helps microglia engulf about half of it. That this engulfment accounts for which inputs lose remains the working idea, not a result. Its limits, gathered in one place:

- **A bias, eye by eye.** The activity evidence compares whole eyes; no choice was seen at single identified synapses.
- **Needed, not shown to carry the information.** Without C1q, C3 or CR3 refinement falls short, so they are needed in the sense of [the lesson on the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/): without one, the normal outcome is not reached. These studies did not show that complement carries the activity information or marks the quieter inputs.
- **Part means part.** About half the microglial eating remains without CR3, and astrocytes eat dLGN synapses too. Later imaging, in hippocampal slice cultures and in the living Xenopus retinotectal system, suggests that microglia often take partial bites of presynaptic membrane (trogocytosis) rather than whole synapses. Mice that lack microglia from birth still reach normal synapse and spine numbers in the hippocampus, so other cells can compensate there.
- **One circuit.** Microglia prune in other regions too, but which signals they use varies by circuit and age.

## Going deeper

### Who does the eating

The origin of microglia, outside the neural tube, belongs to [the lesson on where glia come from](/lessons/origins-of-glia/). They carry many of the sensors of an immune cell, including Toll-like receptors that switch on NF-κB, which [the lesson on innate immunity](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb/) covers. Astrocytes, the star-shaped glia that wrap synapses, eat too: in the mouse visual thalamus they engulf synapses through the receptors MEGF10 and MERTK.

So a synapse is not only a meeting of two neurons. The classical picture is bipartite, a presynaptic terminal and a postsynaptic cell. Adding the astrocyte that enwraps it gives the tripartite synapse. Adding the microglial process that touches it and can remove it gives what can be called the "quadripartite" synapse.

### Candidates for the mark

How relative activity becomes a molecular mark is the central open question. Candidate protective "don't eat me" signals such as CD47 and its receptor SIRPα (shown to limit engulfment in the mouse dLGN), local regulators of complement, and activity-dependent exposure of phosphatidylserine have all been proposed. Which operate in a given circuit at a given age, and how far the dLGN rules generalise, remains to be shown.
