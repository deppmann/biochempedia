scientists:
  - name: "Lucy Palmer and Greg Stuart"
    contribution: >-
      Imaged membrane voltage along the axons of rat layer 5 pyramidal neurons and found that
      the action potential begins in the distal axon initial segment, about 35 µm from the
      hillock, rather than at the hillock or the first node.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Palmer & Stuart, J. Neurosci. 26:1854–1863 (2006)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Textbooks long placed the start of the action potential at the axon hillock, the cone
      where the axon leaves the cell body. Recording with one electrode at the soma cannot settle
      this, because a spike that starts a few tens of micrometres away arrives there almost at
      once. Lucy Palmer and Greg Stuart attacked the question with voltage-sensitive dyes, which
      change their fluorescence with membrane potential and so let them watch the voltage at
      several points along the same axon at the same time. They worked in acute slices of
      somatosensory cortex from rats, usually three to five weeks old, and studied layer 5
      pyramidal neurons. The earliest signal appeared about 35 µm from the hillock, in the distal
      part of an axon initial segment about 40 µm long. Experiments targeting the first node
      suggested it is not involved in initiation. Their 2006 paper in the
      Journal of Neuroscience made the trigger zone a measured place in one cell type, which
      others could then study as a structure with its own channels and geometry.
  - name: "Axel Nimmerjahn"
    contribution: >-
      With Frank Kirchhoff and Fritjof Helmchen, imaged microglia in the living adult mouse
      neocortex and showed that cells in their presumed resting state continually move their
      processes through the tissue around them.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Nimmerjahn, Kirchhoff & Helmchen, Science 308:1314–1318 (2005)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Microglia, the immune cells of the brain, were long described as resting in healthy
      tissue and activated only by injury or disease. That picture came from fixed
      tissue, which shows a cell's shape at one moment and nothing about how it moves, and from slices or cultures, whose preparation itself injures the tissue. Axel
      Nimmerjahn, Frank Kirchhoff and Fritjof Helmchen looked instead at living tissue. They used
      two-photon microscopy, which can image fluorescent cells some distance below the surface of
      the brain, and took time-lapse images of microglia in the neocortex of adult mice. The cell
      bodies generally stayed where they were. Their fine processes did not: they extended and withdrew
      continually, so that over time they sampled the space around each cell. Their 2005 paper
      in Science described microglia in the presumed resting state as active surveyors. The
      result changed the question from whether microglia do anything in healthy brain to what
      they detect while they survey, which later work on synapses and on injury has pursued.
  - name: "Ken McCarthy"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory deleted the potassium channel Kir4.1 from glia and found impaired potassium
      and glutamate uptake by hippocampal astrocytes, and later reported that manipulating
      astrocyte calcium did not change hippocampal transmission or plasticity.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Djukic, Casper, Philpot, Chin & McCarthy, J. Neurosci. 27:11354–11365 (2007); Agulhon, Fiacco & McCarthy, Science 327:1250–1254 (2010)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Ken McCarthy's laboratory used genetics to ask what astrocytes do, and its results land on
      both sides of this lesson's line between settled and argued. With Biljana Djukic as first
      author, the group removed Kir4.1, an inwardly rectifying potassium channel, from glia with
      a conditional knockout driven by a glial promoter. In hippocampal slices from mice 15 to 20
      days old, astrocytes in CA1 lacking the channel took up potassium poorly and, more
      surprisingly, took up glutamate poorly too. The deletion also reached oligodendrocytes, a
      limit the paper's design made unavoidable. A few years later, with Cendra Agulhon and Todd
      Fiacco, the laboratory turned to gliotransmission, the idea that astrocytes release
      transmitters that act on synapses. Raising or removing astrocyte Gq-receptor calcium signalling in
      mouse hippocampus left excitatory transmission and plasticity unchanged in their tests.
      The two papers together gave the field a firm homeostatic function and a sharp challenge to
      a signalling one.
  - name: "Roberta de Ceglia and Andrea Volterra"
    contribution: >-
      Identified a subgroup of adult mouse hippocampal astrocytes that expresses synaptic-like
      glutamate-release machinery, and showed that deleting VGLUT1 from astrocytes suppressed the
      glutamate hotspots linked to that subgroup.
    profileSourceAnchor: "de Ceglia, Ledonne, Litvin, Lind, Carriero, Latagliata et al., Nature 622:120–129 (2023)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the 2010s, gliotransmission had become a standoff. Some studies reported that
      astrocytes release glutamate onto neurons, while others, using different manipulations,
      found no effect on transmission. One possibility was that both were right about different
      astrocytes. Roberta de Ceglia, Andrea Volterra and colleagues asked whether some astrocytes
      carry the molecular kit for fast, vesicle-based release and others do not. Working in
      adult mice, they combined analyses of gene expression in single astrocytes with imaging of
      extracellular glutamate using a fluorescent sensor. A specialized subgroup of hippocampal
      astrocytes expressed synaptic-like release machinery, including the vesicular glutamate
      transporter VGLUT1. When VGLUT1 was deleted from astrocytes, the optical glutamate hotspots
      associated with that subgroup were suppressed. The 2023 paper in Nature did not show that
      every astrocyte releases glutamate. It turned an all-or-none argument into a narrower
      question that a well-designed experiment can answer: which astrocytes, where, and with
      what effect.

techniques:
  - name: "Voltage-sensitive dye imaging"
    blurb: >-
      A dye in the membrane changes its fluorescence with voltage, so a camera can follow the
      membrane potential at many points along a dendrite or axon at once. Comparing when the
      signal appears at each point shows where an action potential starts.
  - name: "In vivo two-photon time-lapse imaging"
    blurb: >-
      A laser that excites fluorescence only at its focus images labelled cells below the brain
      surface of a living animal. Imaging the same cells repeatedly shows how processes move over
      minutes, or how myelin sheaths are added and change over weeks.
  - name: "Conditional (cell-type-specific) gene deletion"
    blurb: >-
      A recombinase such as Cre, expressed from a promoter active in one cell type, removes a
      floxed gene (one flanked by loxP sites that Cre cuts out) only in those cells, sometimes only after a drug is given. The method is only as
      specific as the recombination: you must check which cells actually lost the gene, and when.
  - name: "Whole-cell recording and genetically encoded sensors"
    blurb: >-
      A patch pipette on an astrocyte records the currents that flow when nearby axons fire,
      and blockers separate the potassium and transporter components. Fluorescent sensors such
      as GCaMP report calcium inside a cell, and sensors of the iGluSnFR family report glutamate
      outside it.

  - stem: "During intense firing, potassium rises in the extracellular space near active neurons. What does spatial buffering by astrocytes do with that potassium?"
    choices:
      - "Converts it into sodium through the action of the ATPase pump"
      - "Binds it to the myelin sheath so that it can no longer diffuse"
      - "Redistributes it via the astrocyte network to low-potassium areas"
      - "Pumps all of it back into the same neurons that released it"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      In spatial buffering potassium enters astrocytes where it is high and, at the same time,
      leaves where it is low, as one loop of current through the gap-junction-coupled network. So
      it is redistributed: still outside, but spread thinner. Pumping it back into neurons is a
      different process, and net uptake by the sodium-potassium ATPase is also distinct from
      redistribution. Myelin does not bind potassium. No pump converts one element into another;
      the ATPase exchanges sodium and potassium across the membrane.
  - stem: "A glial Kir4.1 conditional knockout, made with a glial promoter active from early development, impaired potassium and glutamate uptake by CA1 astrocytes in slices from mice 15 to 20 days old. Recombination also occurred in oligodendrocytes. Which conclusion goes beyond these data?"
    choices:
      - "Glial Kir4.1 is needed for normal potassium uptake in these slices"
      - "Kir4.1 in mature astrocytes alone sets potassium handling in adults"
      - "Losing Kir4.1 can impair astrocyte glutamate uptake in these slices"
      - "Kir4.1 deletion affected more than one glial cell type in these mice"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The deletion was made during development, examined in juvenile slices, and also hit
      oligodendrocytes, so it cannot assign the result to mature astrocytes alone or to adults.
      Impaired potassium uptake in the slices is what was measured. Impaired glutamate uptake was
      measured too, without implying that Kir4.1 carries glutamate. Recombination in
      oligodendrocytes is a stated property of the knockout.

  - stem: "In a model with fixed, well-mixed volumes, the extracellular space is a fifth of the tissue and the firing neurons fill a third of the volume inside cells. [K+]o starts near 3 mM and [K+]i near 140 mM. Why does the shift in E_K come mainly from the outside concentration?"
    choices:
      - "The Nernst equation for potassium uses only the outside concentration"
      - "Potassium that has left a neuron can never cross back into a cell"
      - "Pumps fix the inside concentration so that it cannot change at all"
      - "The outside starts much lower, so it changes far more in proportion"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      E_K depends on the ratio [K+]o/[K+]i. In this model each 1 mM added outside costs the firing
      neurons about 0.75 mM: a rise of a third outside against about half a percent inside, so the
      outside contribution to the shift is about 60 times larger. The equation contains both concentrations, so saying it uses
      only the outside one misreads it. Potassium does re-enter cells, through astrocyte channels
      and through pumps. Pumps do not hold the inside concentration perfectly fixed; the inside
      change is simply small in proportion.

    choices:
      - "It becomes 74 mV more positive, since the ratio quadruples on both sides"
      - "It becomes 12 mV more positive, the same as the rise in millimolar K+"
      - "It becomes 37 mV more negative, pulling the cell further from threshold"
      - "It becomes 37 mV more positive, moving from about −93 to −56 mV"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      E_K = 61.5 × log10([K+]o/[K+]i). At 4 mM it is 61.5 × log10(4/130) = −93.0 mV; at 16 mM it
      is 61.5 × log10(16/130) = −56.0 mV, a shift of 61.5 × log10(4) = +37.0 mV. A more negative
      E_K has the sign backwards, since more potassium outside favours less outward flow. The
      74 mV value doubles the logarithm of a fourfold change. Equating the shift with the 12 mM
      rise confuses concentration with a logarithm of a ratio.
  - stem: "Petr and colleagues deleted the glutamate transporter GLT-1 from neurons or from astrocytes in mice. Neuronal deletion reduced glutamate uptake capacity in crude forebrain synaptosomes by about 40 percent. Astrocytic deletion removed about 80 percent of GLT-1 protein and was associated with seizures. What best reconciles these results?"
    choices:
      - "Seizures after astrocytic deletion must have come from neuronal loss"

      - "Neurons, not astrocytes, carry out most glutamate clearance in vivo"

      Crude synaptosomes are pinched-off terminals, so an assay of uptake into them reports the

    source: "Orkand, Nicholls & Kuffler, J. Neurophysiol. 29:788–806 (1966); Kofuji & Newman, Neuroscience 129:1045–1056 (2004)."
    text: >-
      In the 1960s Stephen Kuffler, with John Nicholls and Richard Orkand, recorded from glial
      cells in the optic nerve of an amphibian, the mudpuppy Necturus. The glial membrane
      potential behaved like a potassium electrode: it changed with the potassium concentration
      outside, and it depolarized when the neighbouring axons fired trains of impulses, as the
      potassium those axons released built up. That glial response was early direct evidence
      that glia sense the potassium that neurons release, and it led to the idea of spatial
      buffering that later work tied to Kir4.1 channels. The lesson's section on the evidence
      walks through the record.
  - title: "The ten-to-one glia myth"
    source: "Azevedo, Carvalho, Grinberg, Farfel, Ferretti, Leite, Jacob Filho, Lent & Herculano-Houzel, J. Comp. Neurol. 513:532–541 (2009)."
    text: >-
      Many books once said that glia outnumber neurons ten to one in the human brain. Frederico
      Azevedo, Suzana Herculano-Houzel and colleagues tested this with the isotropic
      fractionator, which dissolves tissue into a suspension of nuclei and counts neuronal and
      non-neuronal nuclei separately. In the brains of four adult men they counted about 86
      billion neurons and about 85 billion non-neuronal cells, so the two are close to equal over
      the whole brain. The ratio varies by region, with far more neurons than other cells in the
      cerebellum. The count is one estimate from a small sample, with its own range.
  - title: "Lactate in the dish, not in the clinic"
    source: "Lee, Morrison, Li, Lengacher, Farah, Hoffman et al., Nature 487:443–448 (2012)."
    text: >-
      Lee and colleagues knocked down the lactate transporter MCT1 in oligodendrocytes of the
      optic nerve with a lentivirus and saw axon damage. In a separate
      experiment, in organotypic slice cultures of rodent spinal cord, an MCT1 inhibitor combined
      with glucose deprivation caused neurodegeneration, and adding L-lactate to the medium
      prevented it. The rescue supports the idea that oligodendrocytes pass energy substrates to
      axons. The lactate concentration was a condition chosen for a culture experiment, not a measured
      concentration in the brain or a dose for anyone.

**When a small patch of brain fires very hard, as in a seizure, potassium leaking from its neurons can pile up outside them fast enough to shift E_K, the voltage potassium pulls the cell toward, by tens of millivolts. Ordinary firing adds less, but it adds up. Something has to put it back.**

Astrocytes, a kind of glial cell, do a large share of that work. How these cells were discovered and built belongs to Developmental Neuroscience ([the neuron doctrine](/lessons/neuron-doctrine/), [origins of glia](/lessons/origins-of-glia/)); here you meet them at work in the mature brain.

## The puzzle: a small space outside

In a typical neuron, each action potential ends as potassium flows out and sodium current declines; [the action potential](/lessons/action-potential/) shows why. Where does that potassium go? Into the space between cells, and that space is small. In the adult brain it is about a fifth of the tissue volume, a web of gaps tens of nanometres wide. The other four fifths is inside cells: one part outside for every four parts inside. A neuron's resting voltage depends largely on E_K, and E_K depends on the *ratio* of potassium outside to inside.

**Predict.** Firing neurons lose some potassium into that space. Which concentration changes more, the one inside or the one outside? And which change matters more for the cell's voltage?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In millimolar, it depends on how much cell volume is exchanging potassium; in proportion, the outside, by a wide margin. Take an illustrative model with fixed, well-mixed volumes: the space outside is a fifth of the tissue, and the firing neurons fill a third of the four fifths inside. The inside-to-outside volume ratio is (0.8/3)/0.2 = 1.33, so for each 1 mM added outside the firing neurons lose about 0.75 mM. The bigger factor is the starting point, about 3 mM outside against about 140 mM inside. Adding 1 mM outside raises it by a third; losing 0.75 mM inside changes it by about half a percent. Since E_K depends on the ratio, the outside contribution to its shift is about (140/3) × 1.33 = 62 times the inside one. In many ordinary firing conditions the outside dominates like this, but the actual ratio depends on which volumes take part (Going deeper).

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/neurons-and-glia-at-work/extracellular-space.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: brain cells packed tightly together with only a thin, connected web of space between them, where potassium released by firing collects; an inset bar of five equal blocks shows one block of space outside cells for every four blocks inside." caption="The space between brain cells is a thin web, about a fifth of the tissue, so potassium released into it by firing can change its concentration there strongly." />

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Small space outside, big change outside. The space between brain cells is small and holds little potassium, so activity can change extracellular potassium strongly, and the shift in E_K usually comes mostly from the outside; how much depends on the volumes taking part. A neuron's resting potential therefore depends on cells that keep clearing that space.

The rule covers the potassium and glutamate that activity releases into grey matter. It explains why astrocytes buffer, pump and transport, and why deleting astrocytic GLT-1 caused seizures in mice. It does not explain myelin, metabolic support or microglia, other jobs glia do for the axon and the tissue, and it says nothing about whether astrocytes also send signals of their own, which is still argued.

## A map of the neuron

Before the glia, the neuron they look after. Its five compartments each mainly do one thing to the signal, and some leave something in the space outside. Read the last column: what a compartment leaves tells you which glial cell to look for.

| Compartment | What it does to the signal | What it leaves outside | Who looks after it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dendrites | receive input as graded potentials | not followed here | — |
| Soma | sums inputs; holds the nucleus | not followed here | — |
| Axon initial segment | usually starts the spike | potassium, as each spike ends | astrocytes (as for the axon) |
| Axon | carries the spike, regenerating it | potassium, after every spike | astrocytes; myelinating cells insulate and fuel it |
| Terminal | turns the spike into transmitter release | glutamate, at excitatory synapses | astrocytes, through GLT-1 |
| The tissue around them all | — | — | microglia, which survey it |

**Dendrites** receive input. A synapse onto a dendrite produces a **postsynaptic potential**, a local change in membrane voltage whose size depends on how much transmitter arrived. It is **graded**: bigger input, bigger potential, with no threshold. An excitatory one is an **EPSP**, a depolarization that moves the cell toward firing. In a passive dendrite it shrinks as it spreads, the way a ripple fades from where a stone fell. The next lesson, on [the resting membrane and passive signals](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/), puts numbers on how far it travels.

The **soma**, or cell body, holds the nucleus and most of the machinery that makes proteins. It is also a place where inputs meet. **Spatial summation** adds potentials arriving at different synapses at about the same time; **temporal summation** adds potentials arriving at one synapse in quick succession. Dendrites also do some of this adding and transform inputs before they arrive.

The **axon initial segment** (AIS) is the first stretch of axon. Packed with voltage-gated sodium channels a little way out from the large cell body, it is where the action potential usually starts (density is only part of the reason; see Going deeper).

The **axon** carries the spike away, regenerating it as it goes (in myelinated axons, only at the gaps in the myelin; see below). It also carries cargo both ways on microtubule tracks, described in [molecular motors](/lessons/molecular-motors/).

The **terminal** turns the spike back into chemistry. Depolarization opens voltage-gated calcium channels, and calcium entry triggers fusion of transmitter-filled vesicles with the membrane. At fast synapses this release can follow the spike in less than a millisecond, though the delay is not the same at every synapse. [Synaptic transmission](/lessons/synaptic-transmission/) takes this apart.

The picture is the canonical one, drawn from cortical and hippocampal pyramidal neurons and cerebellar Purkinje cells, and it has exceptions: some mammalian neurons fire spikes in their dendrites, and the trigger zone is not always where textbooks put it.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/neurons-and-glia-at-work/neuron-compartments.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a neuron laid out left to right: dendrites, a cell body, the cone-shaped hillock, a highlighted axon initial segment just beyond it, a sheathed axon and terminals with vesicles." caption="Each compartment of the canonical neuron mainly does one job, from dendrites receiving input to terminals releasing transmitter, and the spike usually starts at the initial segment just beyond the hillock." />

### Where the spike starts

Many textbooks say the action potential begins at the **axon hillock**, the cone where the axon leaves the soma. A somatic recording cannot test this, since a spike starting tens of micrometres away arrives almost at once. So Lucy Palmer and Greg Stuart imaged the axon at several points simultaneously with a voltage-sensitive dye. They worked in layer 5 pyramidal neurons in acute slices of rat somatosensory cortex, from rats usually three to five weeks old.

Picture the record: one voltage trace for each point along the axon, stacked on a shared time axis. The traces look much alike, but one begins slightly before the rest. That earliest signal appeared about 35 µm from the hillock, in the distal part of an initial segment about 40 µm long. Experiments targeting the first node suggested it is not involved in initiation. This is a measurement in one cell type, not a rule for every neuron.

How the AIS is assembled is covered in [neuronal polarity](/lessons/neuronal-polarity/); what it does, in [the action potential](/lessons/action-potential/).

## How far E_K moves

Back to the potassium. An ion's **equilibrium potential** is the voltage at which its concentration gradient and the electrical force on it balance. For potassium at 37 °C the Nernst equation, derived in [membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/), gives

> **E_K = 61.5 mV × log10([K+]o / [K+]i)**

Read it as a rule of thumb: every tenfold change in the ratio moves E_K by about 61.5 mV.

**Worked problem.** Take [K+]i = 140 mM, hold it steady, and treat potassium on its own. The values are illustrative.

*At rest*, [K+]o = 3 mM: E_K = 61.5 × log10(3/140) = −102.6 mV.

*After intense firing.* In cat cortex, even strong repetitive stimulation and seizure-like afterdischarges raised extracellular potassium only to a ceiling of about 10 mM, and ordinary activity raises it much less. At 10 mM, E_K = 61.5 × log10(10/140) = −70.5 mV, about 32 mV more positive.

Check it with the rule of thumb. Going from 3 to 10 mM is a little more than a threefold rise, which on the log scale is about half a tenfold step (the square root of 10 is about 3.2). So E_K should move by about half of 61.5 mV. It does.

<PotassiumClearanceEk />

The simulation starts at a fourfold rise, to 12 mM, an extreme case beyond the measured ceiling, and lets you vary how fast the excess clears. Its clearance time is illustrative, not measured.

**Predict.** When E_K moves 32 mV, does the neuron's resting potential move 32 mV, more, or less?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Less, but in the same direction. A neuron at rest is mostly but not only permeable to potassium; sodium and other ions also pull on its voltage. In a simple model of the resting membrane (Going deeper), it moves about half as far as E_K: about 16 mV here, and roughly 10 to 21 mV across plausible sodium permeabilities. The next lessons make this precise. At steady rest with no current injected, the total membrane current, pump current included, is zero. In a simplified model with fixed conductances and no other net current, the resting voltage is a conductance-weighted average of the ions' equilibrium potentials; with two ions it lies closer to the one with the larger conductance.

</details>

What does the neuron feel? A modest rise brings it closer to threshold, so it fires more easily, and more firing releases more potassium: a loop that can feed a seizure. A large, sustained rise can do the opposite. Held depolarized, sodium channels inactivate, threshold moves up, and the neuron can stop firing altogether (see "threshold moves" in [the action potential](/lessons/action-potential/)). Either way, something has to clear the space.

## Astrocytes clear the potassium

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

### The evidence: glia that read potassium

Early direct evidence that glia sense the potassium released by active axons came in 1966, when Stephen Kuffler, John Nicholls and Richard Orkand recorded from glial cells in the optic nerve of an amphibian, the mudpuppy *Necturus*.

Picture the record: a glial cell's membrane potential, steady and very negative, drawn under a train of impulses in the neighbouring axons. During the train the glial cell slowly depolarizes, more for longer and faster trains, and it recovers over seconds once the axons stop.

Changing the potassium in the bath moved the glial voltage as a potassium electrode's would, so the glia were sensing the potassium the axons released as it built up outside them. That record led to the idea of spatial buffering.

### The mechanism: a current loop through the astrocyte network

Astrocytes are joined to one another by gap junctions, channels that link the insides of neighbouring cells, and their membranes are almost purely permeable to potassium. In a simplified, strongly coupled network the astrocytes therefore sit at nearly the same voltage, near a conductance-weighted average of the local E_K values (real networks can have voltage gradients and pump or transporter currents). What differs from place to place is the local E_K. Now let one spot fire hard.

1. **Where potassium is high**, the local E_K is more positive than the astrocyte's voltage, so potassium flows *in*, in this model through the potassium channel Kir4.1. (Kir4.1 is inwardly rectifying by name, but it rectifies only weakly compared with other Kir channels, so it can also carry the outward limb in step 3.)
2. That entry depolarizes the connected network slightly.
3. **Where potassium is still low**, the network's voltage is now more positive than the local E_K, so potassium flows *out*.

Entry and exit happen at the same time, as one loop of current, so potassium can leave far away without waiting for the ions that entered to travel there. This is **spatial buffering**: it moves potassium from where it is high to where it is low. On balance it leaves the potassium outside, only spread thinner, where it does less harm. Pumps instead put it back inside cells: sodium-potassium ATPases in astrocytes and in neurons take potassium up. How much each mechanism contributes is still debated. In rat hippocampal slices, Kir4.1 buffered potassium while it was rising, and blocking the pumps slowed clearance after stimulation ended.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/neurons-and-glia-at-work/astrocyte-buffering-loop.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: three linked astrocytes over a band of extracellular space: potassium crowds the space beside firing neurons at the left and enters the first astrocyte, a loop of current runs through the gap junctions, and potassium leaves the last astrocyte where the space holds little." caption="In spatial buffering, potassium enters astrocytes through Kir4.1 where it is high and leaves where it is low, as one loop of current through astrocytes joined by gap junctions." />

**Predict.** Ken McCarthy's laboratory deleted Kir4.1 from glia in mice. What should happen to astrocytes' potassium uptake? Is there any reason their glutamate uptake might change too?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In hippocampal slices from mice 15 to 20 days old, CA1 astrocytes without Kir4.1 took up potassium poorly, and glutamate poorly too (Djukic and colleagues, 2007). That does not mean Kir4.1 carries glutamate. Glutamate transport is driven by ion gradients and carries net positive charge into the cell, so it depends on the astrocyte's voltage, which Kir4.1 helps hold negative. Two limits: the deletion began in development and also reached oligodendrocytes, so the result cannot be pinned on mature astrocytes alone.

</details>

## Glutamate: the same rule, more sharply

Glutamate, the main fast excitatory transmitter, must be cleared from outside synapses too, and the lesson's rule applies even more sharply: transporters keep glutamate outside cells very low between releases, so each release makes a large, brief rise near the synapse. The transporter **GLT-1** (also called EAAT2) is mostly astrocytic, with a smaller pool in excitatory terminals.

**Predict.** Petr and colleagues deleted GLT-1 in mice, from astrocytes in one line and from neurons in another. Which deletion should matter more for the animal? And which should look bigger in an uptake assay in synaptosomes, the pinched-off nerve terminals made from brain tissue?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

For the animal, the astrocytic deletion. Made with an inducible Cre (a gene deletion switched on by a drug), it removed about 80 percent of GLT-1 protein, and the mice had seizures and early death. In the assay, the neuronal one: deleting GLT-1 from neurons cut uptake into crude forebrain synaptosomes by about 40 percent. The two fit once you see what the assay samples: made from terminals, it over-samples the small neuronal pool of GLT-1. Most GLT-1 is astrocytic, and the seizures point to that pool for clearance in the intact brain.

</details>

## The axon's other partners: myelin, fuel and microglia

**Myelin** is a membrane wrapped many times around an axon. It gives the axon a high-resistance, low-capacitance sheath, so current spreads farther between the gaps called **nodes of Ranvier**. One myelinated stretch between two nodes is an **internode**. The spike is regenerated at each node, and current flows through the axon from one node to the next: excitation skips the internode, but current does not. In the peripheral nervous system a myelinating **Schwann cell** makes exactly one internode. In the CNS one **oligodendrocyte** can make several, often on different axons, with no fixed count. Some Schwann cells never myelinate: Remak cells hold small unmyelinated axons. How myelin is made is in [myelination](/lessons/myelination/).

Myelin is not frozen in adults. Hughes and colleagues imaged adult mouse somatosensory cortex for weeks and saw newly generated oligodendrocytes add new sheaths, while in mice 10 to 14 months old only about 1 percent of existing sheaths changed length over seven weeks.

Oligodendrocytes also feed axons. Lee and colleagues knocked down the lactate transporter MCT1 in oligodendrocytes of the optic nerve, and the axons were damaged; in slice culture, added lactate prevented injury when MCT1 was blocked and glucose withheld. The fuel may differ by region: in mouse corpus callosum slices, Meyer and colleagues found that oligodendrocytes kept axons working mainly by delivering glucose, and lactate rescued them less well. The fuller story is in [glia as metabolic partners](/lessons/glia-metabolic-partners/).

**Microglia** are the brain's resident immune cells. Using two-photon imaging in healthy adult mouse neocortex, Nimmerjahn, Kirchhoff and Helmchen saw them in their presumed resting state with cell bodies in place but processes that extended and retracted continually, surveying the tissue. Most such imaging has been done in anesthetized mice; in awake mice, Stowell and colleagues found less process surveillance, because noradrenaline acts on β2-adrenergic receptors on microglia. Their role in removing synapses during development is told in [microglia and complement pruning](/lessons/microglia-complement-pruning/).

## What is still argued: gliotransmission

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

**Gliotransmission** is the proposal that astrocytes release transmitters, such as glutamate, that act quickly on synapses. Astrocytes show calcium signals (see [calcium signalling](/lessons/calcium-signaling/)), and a calcium rise could in principle trigger vesicle release, as it does in a terminal.

The evidence conflicts. Agulhon and colleagues raised or disrupted astrocyte calcium signalling in mouse hippocampus, including by deleting the IP3 receptor type 2, which releases calcium from internal stores, and found no change in excitatory transmission or plasticity.

**Predict.** Does that null result show that astrocytes do not release transmitter that acts on synapses? What would you want to check first?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not on its own. A null result can speak only to what the manipulation actually removed, under the conditions tested, so check whether the deletion really removed astrocyte calcium. Srinivasan and colleagues later showed that adult mouse astrocytes lacking that receptor still have calcium fluctuations, mostly in their processes, in slices and in vivo. So the null result does not exclude effects driven by the remaining calcium.

</details>

On the other side, de Ceglia, Volterra and colleagues found a specialized subgroup of adult mouse hippocampal astrocytes expressing synaptic-like release machinery, including VGLUT1, and showed that deleting VGLUT1 from astrocytes suppressed the glutamate hotspots linked to that subgroup. That is positive evidence, restricted to one subgroup, in one species. Neither study refutes the other: they used different manipulations and asked different questions.

### Design problem

Do adult hippocampal astrocytes release glutamate that changes synaptic transmission? Starting from the IP3R2 null result and the VGLUT1 subgroup above, design an experiment in mice that could show whether release from those astrocytes changes transmission, and say what your result could and could not establish. Plan three things: what you remove and when, what you compare against, and what readout would change.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Manipulation.** Delete VGLUT1 from astrocytes only in adults, with a tamoxifen-inducible Cre driven by an astrocyte promoter. This avoids a developmental confound and targets the release machinery rather than one calcium pathway.

**Check the deletion.** Confirm that VGLUT1 is lost in astrocytes and that neurons and oligodendrocytes did not recombine. Many excitatory neurons in hippocampus and cortex are full of VGLUT1, so even sparse neuronal recombination could confound the interpretation.

**Measure release and its effect.** A glutamate sensor reports glutamate from any source, so first image it with neuronal firing and synaptic release blocked, to see the astrocyte signal on its own. Then, with transmission intact, record from CA1 pyramidal neurons in slices: evoked EPSPs, spontaneous events and, if predicted, plasticity. Compare with littermates given tamoxifen but lacking the floxed allele.

**Test sufficiency.** In separate animals, drive only astrocytes (for example with a light-gated or chemically gated receptor expressed in them) and ask whether a glutamate signal and a synaptic change appear, and whether they vanish after VGLUT1 deletion. Check that the deletion did not change glutamate uptake or ion handling, which could explain a loss of signal without any vesicular release.

**State the scope.** A positive result would show that VGLUT1-dependent release from this astrocyte subgroup can change transmission in adult mouse hippocampus under the conditions tested. It would not show that all astrocytes do this, in other regions or species, or that it matters for behaviour. A negative result would hold only for the readouts and activity patterns tested.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Why the distal initial segment

Sodium-channel density is only part of the usual explanation for why the spike starts in the distal part of the initial segment. Reviews also point to the sodium-channel subtype concentrated there, which opens at less depolarized voltages, and to the segment's electrical distance from the soma's large membrane.

### Watching saltatory conduction

In the myelinated axons of mouse layer 5 pyramidal neurons, voltage imaging by Popovic and colleagues (2011) showed the spike propagating saltatorily, from node to node, toward the axon's branches.

### How much the outside wins

For small changes near rest, with [K+]o = 3 mM, [K+]i = 140 mM and 61.5 mV per tenfold change, each extra millimolar outside moves E_K by about 8.9 mV and each millimolar lost inside by about 0.19 mV: a ratio of 140/3, about 47. (These are slopes at rest; a full step from 3 to 4 mM moves E_K by 7.7 mV.) Multiply by the participating inside-to-outside volume ratio to get the outside's lead, as in the opening prediction. A separate, conditional example: if all the intracellular volume, four fifths of the tissue, exchanged potassium with the fifth outside, the volume ratio would be (1 − 0.2)/0.2 = 4 and the outside would lead by roughly 190-fold, or about 140- to 260-fold if the space is anywhere from 0.25 to 0.15 of the tissue. Within that fixed model, with the space at 0.20, 190 is an upper value. In the same shared-pool model the outside's millimolar change is the larger one only if the participating cells fill more than a quarter of the inside volume, and its contribution to E_K is the larger one unless they fill less than about 0.5 percent of it.

### Any fourfold rise

If [K+]i stays constant, a fourfold rise in [K+]o shifts E_K by 61.5 × log10(4) = 37.0 mV, whatever that fixed [K+]i is: from 3 to 12 mM, for example, E_K goes from −102.6 to −65.6 mV. That is the simulation's starting case.

### How far the resting potential follows

The body's figures use the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation with [K+]i held at 140 mM and [Na+]o at 145 mM, chloride and the pump ignored, and a fixed sodium-to-potassium permeability ratio. For a rise from 3 to 10 mM, ratios of 0.02 to 0.08 give the 21-to-10 mV range, two-thirds to a third of E_K's shift; 0.04 gives 15.6 mV. Other choices give other fractions.

### When the inside starts to matter

In very thin axons during long, high-frequency trains, potassium lost from the inside and sodium gained stop being negligible. Then the outside no longer automatically dominates, and you have to compare the actual changes on each side.

### A clearance time constant

Suppose astrocytes remove the excess potassium with a single-exponential time constant of 2 s (an illustrative value, not a measured one). The excess falls to 10 percent when e^(−t/2 s) = 0.1, so t = 2 s × ln(10) = 2 × 2.303 = 4.6 s.

### Read a real number correctly

In mouse hippocampal slices, Tyurikova and colleagues (2025) measured the decay of the potassium current recorded in astrocytes after a train of five stimuli at 50 Hz: 1.85 s in control astrocytes and 1.13 s in astrocytes overexpressing Kir4.1. The decay is (1.85 − 1.13)/1.85 = 38.9 percent shorter. That is a property of a current, not a time constant for extracellular potassium, so it cannot stand in for the simulation's clearance time or the calculation above.
