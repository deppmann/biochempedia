      - "Phosphodiesterase activity at the membrane"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The channels are held open by cGMP, which is why ions flow all the time in the dark and
      why destroying cGMP closes channels. Active rhodopsin is made almost only by light (thermal activation is rare) and acts on
      transducin, not on the channels, so it cannot be what keeps them open in the dark. GTP
      bound to transducin is the on-state of the G protein in the middle of the chain, and it
      is nearly absent from a resting rod. Phosphodiesterase activity is what removes cGMP, so more of
      it closes channels instead of holding them open.
  - stem: "In a cascade, one active enzyme A switches on 50 copies of B. Each active B switches on exactly one copy of C. Each active C switches on 20 copies of D. How many copies of D does one active A produce?"
    choices:
      - "20"
      - "1,000"
      - "71"
      - "50"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Gain is the product of the steps: 50 × 1 × 20 = 1,000. Adding the steps to get 71 treats the
      cascade like a sum, but each active molecule at one step multiplies the whole downstream chain.
      Answering 50 counts only the first catalytic step and ignores the later one. Answering 20 counts
      only the last step. The one-to-one step contributes a factor of one, so it neither adds nor
      removes amplification.

    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Activity that rises and then falls while the ligand is still present most likely means something the
      pathway makes is turning the pathway down after a delay, which is negative feedback. A
      positive loop would hold the kinase on or make it more complete, not switch it off. High
      gain makes the peak larger but gives no way to end the signal. Insulation limits which

  - stem: "Pooled extracts from groups of frog oocytes show MAP kinase activation rising smoothly with progesterone dose. Which experiment can tell whether each oocyte responds in a graded way or all-or-none?"
    choices:

      - "Assay MAP kinase in each oocyte separately at each dose"
      - "Measure the pooled extract at more closely spaced doses"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Only a single-cell measurement separates the two stories, because a pooled average looks

  - stem: "In a bacterium, CheR adds methyl groups to the receptor and CheB removes them, and the pair keeps adjusting until the receptor's signaling output is back at its resting value.       - "The same final tumbling rate, reached in the same amount of time, because a robust circuit ignores enzyme amounts"
      - "A different final tumbling rate, reached in the same amount of time"
      - "A different final tumbling rate, reached in a different amount of time"
      - "The same final tumbling rate, reached in a different amount of time"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Methylation keeps changing until output equals its resting value, so after adaptation each cell is back at the output it had before the attractant, across the range of enzyme levels where adaptation works, while the amount of enzyme sets

One photon, absorbed by one rhodopsin molecule in a rod cell, changes the current through that cell by about one picoamp, a trillionth of an ampere. One rhodopsin cannot carry that current. How does its signal become large enough to register, yet remain local and stop?

<Support>**The one idea.** Circuit architecture and kinetics shape a signal's size, duration, and reversibility. **Scope:** Use this rule for a defined pathway, cell and input. Wiring alone is not enough: component abundance, catalytic rate, active lifetime, saturation, localization and delays matter. Step gains multiply in the simple unsaturated cascades used here, where each active molecule independently reaches a fixed number downstream.</Support>

A pathway diagram is only a wiring list. To predict behaviour, ask how much output one input produces, how output changes with time, and whether the response can reverse.

## Gain is a product, within a simple model

The first film turned one photon's path through rhodopsin, transducin and phosphodiesterase (PDE) into a product of step gains. Here are the measured numbers.

In darkness a rod carries a **dark current** of about 20 picoamps through channels that cGMP holds open. Denis Baylor, Trevor Lamb and King-Wai Yau recorded the single-photon dip from one toad rod in 1979: about one picoamp, a few percent of that current.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/signaling-circuits/rod-photon-chain.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a chain of rhodopsin hit by a photon, then transducin, then PDE, then falling cGMP; an inset compares a membrane in the dark with channels open to one after the photon with some of them closed." caption="One photon activates a multiplying relay that lowers cGMP, closes some channels and reduces the dark current." />

**Gain** is output molecules per input molecule. Rhodopsin to transducin and PDE to cGMP are **catalytic** steps, where one active molecule changes many downstream molecules while it stays active; the transducin-to-PDE handoff is **one-to-one** and multiplies nothing.

Leskov and colleagues measured the first step in isolated rod membranes as a rate: one active rhodopsin switches on transducin, and through it PDE, at 120 to 150 molecules per second. Rhodopsin is switched off quickly, though, so in an intact rod one photon activates only about 16 transducins in mouse and about 60 in frog. Those PDE molecules then destroy about 2,000 cGMP molecules in mouse and about 72,000 in frog.

## Amplification sends a bill

The first film named three costs: noise, shutoff and spread. The rod shows how the first two are paid; other pathways show the third.

First, noise: amplification can magnify upstream fluctuations, and a one-picoamp dip sits on a baseline that never stops moving. Baylor's group could trust the dip only because they counted. They dimmed flashes until most trials were blanks; if each response is one photon caught at random, photons caught per flash follow a Poisson distribution, the signature of rare independent events, and that fixes the blank fraction at each flash strength. It did.

Second, shutoff. Rhodopsin kinase and arrestin shut down rhodopsin. The RGS9 complex speeds transducin's self-shutoff by GTP splitting. The rod also uses **negative feedback**, output acting back through the circuit to oppose its own change: closing channels lowers calcium inside the cell, and calcium-sensing GCAP proteins speed the enzyme that makes cGMP.

Third, a diffusible messenger made in many copies risks reaching unintended targets. One way a cell answers different receptors differently is to keep each pool of messenger near where it was made. mAKAP, for example, holds a kinase, a messenger-destroying phosphodiesterase and the kinase's target within nanometres, so the cyclic AMP that matters stays in that small neighbourhood. That is **insulation**, keeping one pathway's signal away from another pathway's targets, and it has to be built.

Amplification creates output; shutoff and feedback loops, like the rod's calcium loop, decide how that output changes with time. The second film compares three feedback designs under a continuing input.

## Negative feedback can set how long a signal lasts

The second film drew a delayed brake; here is a molecular design for one.

**Predict.** You have a growth factor, its receptor, a kinase and one protein X. How could X make kinase activity fall while the growth factor remains present? What must X sense, what must it act on, and what timing must it have?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Make X a phosphatase whose amount rises when the kinase is active and which removes the kinase's phosphates.

- X senses the kinase's output, so it appears only after the pathway has fired, and it acts on the kinase, so activity falls while the growth factor is still there.
- X acts late. If it worked as fast as the kinase, the signal would barely rise. If it must be made from scratch, the delay is minutes, and that delay sets the window.
- X is short-lived. If it lingered once the growth factor was gone, the cell could respond only weakly a second time.

Cells use this design: ERK drives production of DUSP-family phosphatases that remove ERK's own phosphates.

</details>

The brake's delay, strength and lifetime help set the activity window, but this is one design, not a universal timer: receptor traffic, component degradation and other loops can also set duration.

## Positive feedback turns a dial into a switch

The second film drew an idealized switch. The experimental question is whether a smooth population average can hide individual switches.

James Ferrell and Eric Machleder incubated *Xenopus* oocytes, immature frog eggs, in different concentrations of progesterone, the hormone that starts their maturation. Each egg is about 1.2 millimetres across, large enough to assay alone by immunoblot, a gel-based protein detection method in which active MAP kinase runs slightly slower. Pooling eggs at each dose produced a smooth S-shaped average.

**Predict.** Which experiment distinguishes two possibilities: every egg is partly active, or some eggs are fully active while the rest are off?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Assay one egg per lane. A pooled average cannot distinguish intermediate cells from a mixture of fully on and off cells.

</details>

The single-oocyte assay supported the second story. Ferrell and Machleder reported on 8 May 1998 that the response had a Hill coefficient of at least 35. The **Hill coefficient**, *n*, measures how abruptly output rises with dose. Hemoglobin binding oxygen, with *n* about 3, is the textbook comparison.

> **response = dose^n / (K^n + dose^n)**, where K is the dose that gives half the maximal response
>
> **dose at 90% of maximum ÷ dose at 10% of maximum = 81^(1/n)**

**Predict.** Use the second relation. How wide is the 10-to-90 percent dose span for *n* = 3 and *n* = 35?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The spans are 81^(1/3), about 4.3-fold, and 81^(1/35), about 1.13-fold. For *n* = 1 the span is 81-fold. Larger *n* means a narrower, more switch-like response.

</details>

ERK, a MAP kinase, turns on only after MEK adds two phosphates, one to threonine and one to tyrosine one residue apart. One tag leaves ERK mostly off. In test tubes, MEK adds the phosphates in separate encounters, releasing ERK between them. (In HeLa cells the two additions come close to a single encounter.) This multisite process can make the response **ultrasensitive**: steeper than proportional, an S-shaped curve rather than a true threshold, and still reversible. Stacking three relay tiers sharpens it further. On 17 September 1996, Chi-Ying Huang and Ferrell published a calculation, confirmed in oocyte extracts, with a Hill coefficient about 4 to 5.

Thirty-five needs more. **Positive feedback** means that output acts back to reinforce its own change. Ferrell and Machleder proposed that a loop around the cascade drives an egg that has started switching the remaining distance. Sufficiently strong positive feedback can add **bistability**, two stable output states under the same conditions, and memory. Xiong and Ferrell showed in 2003 that an oocyte committed to maturation stays matured after hormone removal.

Eggs differ in switching threshold, so at an intermediate dose a homogenate reports the fraction that has flipped as if every egg responded partway.

## Adaptation: returning to rest while the signal is still on

**Adaptation** is a return toward rest while the stimulus remains. It lets *E. coli* respond to a climb in attractant rather than stay saturated by the level already reached. The second film showed receptor methylation acting as a running total of the error, which engineers call **integral feedback**, a negative feedback that keeps correcting until the error is zero. Here is the molecular wiring.

The receptor controls CheA, which transfers a phosphate to CheY; phosphorylated CheY drives tumbling. CheR adds methyl groups to the receptor and CheB removes them. The idealized model works because CheB acts mainly on active receptors and CheR works near its top speed. Like turning a shower knob until the water feels right, correction speed affects arrival time, while "right" sets the stopping point.

In most networks, changing an enzyme's amount shifts the steady state the network settles into. Barkai and Leibler derived in 1997 that this loop should instead return each cell to its own pre-stimulus output across wide ranges of enzyme levels. On 14 January 1999, Alon, Surette, Barkai and Leibler tested *E. coli* carrying different amounts of one circuit protein at a time. Adaptation time and steady-state behaviour varied strongly with protein concentration. The precision of adaptation did not. **Robustness** means that a specified output property holds when component amounts or rates vary; it never means that every property is protected.

With an overloaded correcting enzyme, a cell can still return within experimental error to its own pre-attractant rate while differing from normal neighbours in resting rate and return time.

**Predict.** Each receptor has a fixed number of methylation sites. What should happen at very high attractant levels?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Free methylation sites run out before the error reaches zero, so real cells adapt measurably short of their starting rate. Exact adaptation is a limit of the model, not a guarantee.

</details>

## Put the circuit words to work

| Circuit word | Question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| **Gain** | How many output molecules does one input produce? |
| **Negative feedback** | Does output act back to oppose its own change? |
| **Positive feedback** | Does output act back to reinforce its own change? |
| **Feed-forward** | Does one input reach an output by direct and indirect routes at once? |
| **Ultrasensitive response** | Does output climb from near zero to near maximum over a narrow input range? |
| **Adaptation** | Does output return toward rest while stimulus remains? |
| **Robustness** | Which specified output holds as parts or rates vary? |
| **Insulation** | How is one pathway's signal kept from another's targets? |

**Cross-talk** is signal passing between pathways. A cell may use that connection, or it may be an unwanted leak. Chris's lectures borrow a picture from process control: water, malt and hops flow into a fermenter, a threshold indicator trips, and product goes to a storage tank. Two of his questions apply to every case here: "Insulation versus connectivity?" and "Does a signaling pathway have to be the most logical or efficient of all possible scenarios?" The chemotaxis circuit answers the second. It is not the simplest wiring that responds; its architecture and kinetics protect one output, how precisely each cell returns to its own starting rate, and not everything.

## Going deeper

### Different inputs, different activity windows

Activity windows shaped by feedback can run from minutes to hours, depending on the signal, the cell and the readout. Feedback is not the only possible timer.

### What the simple gain product leaves out

The product rule counts independently multiplying steps before saturation. Real pathways may share substrates, saturate enzymes, deactivate components while transmission continues, and feed output back into earlier steps. Rate multiplied by active lifetime helps set a step's gain, but amplitude and duration are not locked together: downstream shutoff or feedback can shorten a high-gain output.

### Exact adaptation is a model limit

Experiments show return within measurement limits over a range of protein amounts, not unlimited perfection.
