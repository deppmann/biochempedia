summary: >-
  A developing neuron that loses its survival signal does not simply run down: in the best-studied cases it switches on a
  program that needs new gene expression and specific proteins. One idea carries the lesson: the
  killers are already built, and a survival signal keeps brakes on them. You trace the program
  from the worm, where cells die on schedule and, in the core pathway, one brake, CED-9, holds back the killers, to the
  NGF-deprived sympathetic neuron, which has two brakes: a gate at the mitochondrion, opened when
  c-Jun switches on pro-death genes and Bax releases cytochrome c, and XIAP on the caspases.
  Afterwards you can predict what removing each part does, and read the Bax-null mouse for what it
  shows about survival and normal development.
hook: "Stop a starving neuron from making new proteins, and it lives longer. Why?"

scientists:
  - name: "H. Robert Horvitz"
    contribution: >-
      With his colleagues, identified the worm genes ced-3 and ced-4, which are needed for
      programmed cell death, and ced-9, which prevents it, and showed that ced-3 encodes a protein
      similar to a mammalian protease and ced-9 a relative of Bcl-2.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Ellis & Horvitz, Cell 44:817 (1986); Yuan, Shaham, Ledoux, Ellis & Horvitz, Cell 75:641 (1993); Horvitz, Nobel Lecture, 8 December 2002."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Horvitz began on cell death with a map. With John Sulston he traced the cell divisions in
      the worm after hatching, outside the gonad, and saw that particular cells died at particular places in the
      family tree, in every animal. A death that happens to a known cell is a phenotype, and a
      phenotype can be screened for. In his laboratory Hilary Ellis used a mutant in which corpses
      linger, and searched for mutants in which the corpses never formed. In 1986 they reported
      ced-3 and ced-4, genes the doomed cells need in order to die. In 1992 Michael Hengartner,
      Ronald Ellis and Horvitz reported ced-9, which protects cells, and double mutants put it
      upstream of the other two. Cloning followed: in 1993 Junying Yuan and colleagues showed that
      ced-3 resembles a mammalian protease, and in 1994 Hengartner showed that ced-9 is related to
      human Bcl-2. A worm pathway had become a guide to the mammalian one. Horvitz shared the 2002
      Nobel Prize with Sydney Brenner and Sulston.
  - name: "John Sulston"
    contribution: >-
      With Horvitz, traced the postembryonic cell lineage of C. elegans, and with colleagues the
      embryonic lineage, showing that the same identified cells die on schedule in every animal.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Sulston & Horvitz, Dev. Biol. 56:110 (1977); Sulston, Schierenberg, White & Thomson, Dev. Biol. 100:64 (1983); Sulston, Nobel Lecture, 8 December 2002."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Sulston wanted to watch cells develop inside a living animal. The worm C. elegans is
      transparent, and Nomarski optics, a kind of microscopy that turns small differences in the
      sample into visible relief, made its nuclei stand out without any staining. He and Horvitz
      followed nuclei as they divided, moved and disappeared, and in 1977 published the lineage of
      every cell born after hatching outside the gonad. The embryo was harder, because late
      embryos keep rotating inside the eggshell, and Sulston, Einhard Schierenberg, John White and
      Nichol Thomson finished the embryonic lineage in 1983. In the lineage diagrams a cell that
      died was marked with an X. Because the same cells received the X in every animal, a
      geneticist could ask why one named cell failed to die in a mutant. That question is where
      the death genes came from. Sulston later helped lead the sequencing of the worm genome.
  - name: "Eugene M. Johnson Jr."
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory showed that NGF-deprived sympathetic neurons need RNA and protein synthesis to
      die, took part in showing that they need Bax, and with Mohanish Deshmukh found that deprived
      neurons acquire a separate "competence-to-die".
    profileSourceAnchor: "Martin et al., J. Cell Biol. 106:829 (1988); Deshmukh & Johnson, Neuron 21:695 (1998)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Johnson's laboratory at Washington University in St. Louis used cultured rat sympathetic
      neurons, which depend on NGF and die when it is taken away. In 1988, with David Martin and
      colleagues, it reported that inhibitors of RNA synthesis or of protein synthesis kept these
      neurons alive after NGF was removed. A neuron that must make new molecules in order to die
      is running a program, and the result sent the field looking for the genes involved. In 1996,
      with Thomas Deckwerth, William Snider, Stanley Korsmeyer and others, Johnson reported that
      sympathetic neurons from mice lacking Bax survive NGF deprivation. In 1998, with Deshmukh,
      he showed that cytochrome c injected into the cytoplasm kills deprived neurons but spares
      neurons kept in NGF, and that deprived neurons gain this sensitivity even when protein
      synthesis is blocked. NGF, the work implied, holds the death program back at more than one
      step.
  - name: "Stanley J. Korsmeyer"
    contribution: >-
      Studied the BCL-2 family from its origin in lymphoma genetics, identified Bax as a
      Bcl-2-binding protein that promotes death, and with colleagues made the Bax-null mouse used
      to test which neurons need Bax to die.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Oltvai, Milliman & Korsmeyer, Cell 74:609 (1993); Knudson et al., Science 270:96 (1995); White et al., J. Neurosci. 18:1428 (1998)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Korsmeyer came to neurons from cancer. Many follicular lymphomas carry a chromosome
      translocation that joins chromosomes 14 and 18, and his group was among those that cloned the
      breakpoint and the gene beside it, BCL2. Bcl-2 turned out to be unusual among cancer genes:
      it did not drive division but kept cells from dying. In 1993, with Zoltan Oltvai and Curt
      Milliman, he reported a protein that binds Bcl-2 and speeds cell death, and called it Bax. In
      1995 Michael Knudson and colleagues in his group reported mice lacking Bax. Those mice became
      a tool for neuroscientists. Sympathetic neurons from them survived NGF deprivation, and in
      1998 Fletcher White, William Snider, Korsmeyer and colleagues reported that naturally
      occurring death was largely eliminated in many neuron populations of Bax-null mice. Asking
      what those surplus neurons became turned a cancer gene into a way to study what developmental
      death is for.

techniques:
  - name: "Lineage tracing by direct observation"
    blurb: >-
      In a transparent animal, nuclei are followed under the microscope as they divide, move and
      die, and every event is recorded in a family tree. It tells you which identified cells die,
      and when, so that a missing death in a mutant can be pinned to one cell.
  - name: "Forward genetic screen"
    blurb: >-
      Animals are treated with a chemical that causes random mutations, and their descendants are
      searched for a phenotype, here the absence of cell corpses. Finding the gene comes after
      finding the mutant, so the screen makes no guess about which proteins are involved.
  - name: "Microinjection into cultured neurons"
    blurb: >-
      A fine glass needle delivers a protein, such as cytochrome c, straight into the cytoplasm of
      single neurons, with a marker dye to identify the injected cells. Skipping the upstream steps
      this way shows whether a cell can still respond to a later step in a pathway.
  - name: "Knockout and double-knockout mice"
    blurb: >-
      A gene is deleted from the mouse germ line, and two deletions can be bred together. Deleting
      Bax keeps neurons alive, and adding a second deletion, such as NGF, shows what that factor
      does once survival no longer depends on it.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "Which observation indicated that the death of NGF-deprived sympathetic neurons is an active program rather than passive decay?"
    choices:
      - "Deprived neurons lost all their mitochondria before any other change"
      - "Deprived neurons swelled and burst, spilling their contents into the dish"
      - "Adding extra glucose to the medium kept the deprived neurons alive"
      - "Inhibitors of RNA or protein synthesis delayed the death after NGF removal"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      If a neuron must make new RNA and protein in order to die, the death is something the cell
      does, and blocking synthesis delays it. Swelling and bursting describe necrosis, classically
      treated as the passive kind of death. Extra glucose would test whether the neurons simply ran out of
      fuel, and that passive explanation is not what the synthesis-inhibitor result shows. Mitochondria
      are not lost first; they stay in place and release cytochrome c later in the program.
  - stem: "In the worm C. elegans, what happens to the cells that normally die during development in an animal with a strong loss-of-function mutation in ced-3?"
    choices:
      - "They die earlier than normal, before their sisters have finished dividing"
      - "They survive, and some take on features of differentiated neurons"
      - "They die on schedule, but their corpses are never engulfed by neighbours"
      - "They keep dividing, so each one forms a small extra clone of cells"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      CED-3 is needed to carry out the death, so without it the doomed cells live, and the cells that live can differentiate; some take on features of neurons, such as making serotonin. Corpses that are never
      engulfed describe engulfment mutants such as ced-1, in which death happens but cleanup fails.
      Dying early would mean the gene restrains death, which is the role of ced-9, not ced-3. The
      surviving cells did not resume division; the mutation changed the fate at the end of the
      branch, not the branch itself.
  - stem: "Which pairing of a worm cell-death protein with its mammalian counterpart is correct?"
    choices:
      - "CED-4 with Bax, the protein that lets cytochrome c escape"
      - "CED-3 with the caspases, proteases that cut after aspartate"
      - "CED-9 with caspase-9, the protease that starts the cascade"
      - "CED-3 with Bcl-2, the protein that keeps the cell alive"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      CED-3 resembles the mammalian protease family later named caspases, which cut their targets
      after an aspartate. CED-9 protects cells and is related to Bcl-2, not to a caspase; the
      shared number 9 is a coincidence of naming. CED-4 is related to Apaf-1, the scaffold of the
      apoptosome; the worm has no clear counterpart of Bax. CED-3 cannot be the Bcl-2 counterpart,
      because loss of CED-3 keeps cells alive while Bcl-2 is itself the protective protein.
  - stem: "Sympathetic neurons from Bax-null mice are cultured, and NGF is then removed from the medium. Which outcome is expected?"
    choices:
      - "They die faster, since Bax normally holds the caspases in check"
      - "They survive and grow larger, since Bax normally limits cell size"
      - "They die on the normal schedule, since Bax acts only in living animals"
      - "They survive but stay small, since NGF also supports their growth"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Bax is needed for cytochrome c release after NGF deprivation, so without it the neurons
      survive, and Deckwerth and colleagues reported that such neurons are atrophic, because NGF
      supports growth as well as survival. Bax works in culture as well as in the animal, so the
      neurons do not die on schedule. Bax promotes death; it does not restrain caspases, so they do
      not die faster. Nothing indicates that Bax limits cell size; the surviving neurons are smaller,
      not larger.
  - stem: "In mice lacking both NGF and Bax, sympathetic neurons survive, but at birth the salivary glands have no sympathetic axons. In mice lacking only Bax, the salivary glands are innervated. What does this comparison show?"
    choices:
      - "NGF is needed for axons to innervate this target, apart from survival"
      - "The salivary glands make no NGF, so they are never innervated at all"
      - "The surviving neurons are not sympathetic, so they ignore the glands"
      - "Bax is needed for sympathetic axons to grow into the salivary glands"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Bax deletion keeps the neurons alive in both kinds of mice, so the only difference is NGF,
      and without it the axons fail to innervate this target. Bax cannot be the requirement, because
      mice lacking only Bax innervate the glands. The glands are innervated in mice with NGF, and
      they are known NGF-producing targets. The surviving neurons are sympathetic; deleting Bax
      changes whether they die, not what kind of neuron they are.

mcatQuestions:
  - stem: "In a hypothetical study modelled on published work, cultured sympathetic neurons from Bax-null mice, which do not release their own cytochrome c after NGF withdrawal, are deprived of NGF for 0, 8, 16 or 24 hours, then injected with cytochrome c. Survival 3 hours later is 95%, 90%, 60% and 10%. In wild-type neurons kept alive through a 24-hour deprivation by blocking protein synthesis, survival after cytochrome c injection is 15%. Neurons injected with a control protein stay at least 90% alive in every group. When a caspase inhibitor is injected along with cytochrome c into the Bax-null neurons at 24 hours, survival is 90%. Which conclusion do these data best support?"
    choices:
      - "NGF keeps neurons alive only by keeping cytochrome c inside the mitochondria"
      - "Injected cytochrome c kills these neurons by a route that needs no caspase activity"
      - "Deprivation slowly removes a restraint on caspases without needing new proteins"
      - "Deprivation kills because cytochrome c must be newly made during the deprivation period"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Bax-null neurons do not release their own cytochrome c, and control injections do not kill
      them, so the deaths come from the injected cytochrome c. Neurons in NGF survive it, and it
      becomes lethal only after many hours of deprivation (survival is still 90% after 8 hours),
      so something below cytochrome c release changes; because blocking
      protein synthesis does not stop the change and a caspase inhibitor prevents the death, the
      change is the loss of a restraint on caspases. Cytochrome c is supplied by the injection, so
      the death cannot depend on making it. The caspase inhibitor rescues the neurons, so the route
      needs caspases. If NGF acted only by keeping cytochrome c in the mitochondria, injected
      cytochrome c would kill neurons kept in NGF, and it does not.
  - stem: "Suppose a mouse sympathetic ganglion contains 30,000 neurons at its peak in both wild-type and Bax-null mice. Three weeks after birth the wild-type ganglion contains 18,000 neurons and the Bax-null ganglion 27,000. Assuming no neurons are added after the peak, what percentage of the naturally occurring death in the wild type did Bax deletion prevent?"
    choices:
      - "33%, because 9,000 extra neurons are a third of 27,000"
      - "50%, because the Bax-null ganglion is 1.5 times larger"
      - "75%, because 9,000 of the 12,000 deaths did not happen"
      - "90%, because the Bax-null ganglion keeps 90% of its neurons"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The wild type loses 30,000 minus 18,000, or 12,000 neurons, and the Bax-null loses 3,000, so
      9,000 of the 12,000 deaths were prevented, which is 75%. The ratio of 27,000 to 18,000 is 1.5,
      but that compares final sizes and does not measure the share of death prevented. Keeping 90%
      of neurons describes survival in the Bax-null alone, not how much of the wild-type death was
      removed. Dividing 9,000 by 27,000 uses the wrong denominator; the question asks about the
      deaths, which number 12,000.
  - stem: "In C. elegans, embryos lacking ced-9 function show many deaths of cells that normally survive, and severe mutants die. Embryos lacking both ced-9 and ced-3, or both ced-9 and ced-4, survive and show few cell deaths. Embryos lacking only ced-3 or only ced-4 also show few deaths. Which model best fits these genetic results?"
    choices:
      - "CED-9 acts upstream to restrain the CED-4 and CED-3 activities"
      - "CED-9 acts downstream of CED-3 to carry out the killing step"
      - "CED-3 and CED-4 act only in the cells that engulf the corpses"
      - "CED-9 and CED-3 act in two parallel and separate death pathways"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The extra deaths caused by losing ced-9 need ced-3 and ced-4, so CED-9 works by restraining
      them, upstream in the genetic order; this is a genetic order and does not by itself show
      which proteins touch. If CED-9 carried out the killing downstream, losing it would prevent
      deaths, not cause them. If ced-3 and ced-4 acted only in engulfing cells, losing them would
      leave unengulfed corpses, not live cells. If the pathways were separate, removing ced-3 would
      not stop the deaths caused by losing ced-9.

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm five"
    text: >-
      A young nerve cell stays alive while it keeps getting a special message from the body part
      it connects to. If the message stops, the cell does not just fade away. It turns on a set of
      tiny scissors inside itself that cut it apart, and then a cleaner cell eats the pieces. If a
      scientist takes away the switch that turns the scissors on, the nerve cell stays alive, but it often stays small.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Many developing neurons die, and most of them die by apoptosis, a tidy form of death in which the cell
      shrinks, packs up its DNA and is eaten by its neighbours. Apoptosis is a program: in the best-studied cases, a neuron that loses its survival signal has
      to make new proteins to do it, and it uses enzymes called caspases to cut itself apart. The
      genes for this program were found in a tiny worm in which the same cells die on schedule in
      every animal. Mammals use related genes. When young sympathetic neurons lose their survival factor, NGF, a
      protein called Bax lets cytochrome c leak out of the mitochondria, and that switches the
      caspases on. Mice without Bax keep many extra neurons, but many of those neurons are smaller than
      normal, and NGF is still needed for their nerves to fully innervate many organs.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      Most naturally occurring neuron death that has been studied is apoptosis, though non-apoptotic forms also occur. In cultured sympathetic neurons deprived of
      NGF, inhibitors of RNA or protein synthesis delay death, so the program needs gene
      expression. Worm genetics supplied its core: ced-3 encodes a caspase-like protease, ced-4 the
      Apaf-1-like adaptor, and ced-9 a Bcl-2 relative that restrains them. In the deprived neuron,
      loss of TrkA signalling weakens Akt-dependent survival signals, JNK phosphorylates c-Jun, and
      BH3-only proteins such as Bim are induced. Bax then permeabilizes the outer mitochondrial
      membrane, cytochrome c binds Apaf-1 to form the apoptosome, caspase-9 activates caspase-3,
      and the cell exposes phosphatidylserine for engulfment by phagocytes such as microglia.
      Bax-null neurons survive NGF deprivation, and Bax deletion largely prevents developmental
      death in several populations, including sympathetic, sensory and facial motor neurons, but
      many survivors are smaller and NGF/Bax double nulls show organ-specific failures of
      innervation.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      NGF guards the intrinsic pathway at two levels in sympathetic neurons. Upstream, it prevents
      the c-Jun-dependent induction of BH3-only proteins and the Bax-dependent release of
      cytochrome c. Downstream, it keeps caspase activation restrained even when cytochrome c is in
      the cytoplasm: Deshmukh and Johnson found that injected cytochrome c kills deprived neurons
      whose own release is blocked, a "competence-to-die" that builds up gradually over roughly
      the first day without NGF, without new protein synthesis and without Bax. Later work by Deshmukh and colleagues tied this to XIAP, whose
      level falls on deprivation, and to low Apaf-1 in differentiated neurons, which makes XIAP
      sufficient to hold caspases off. Genetic ablation of the pathway also shows its limits.
      Caspase-3 or caspase-9 null mice can have forebrain overgrowth, and in caspase-3 nulls its severity depends strongly
      on strain. Yet in these mice postmitotic spinal and peripheral neurons still die in normal numbers, later and by a non-apoptotic route (Oppenheim and colleagues, 2001), whereas Bax deletion rescues many, not all, developing populations.

      An open question is what the surplus neurons in Bax-null mice tell us. Many are smaller and
      some lose target contact, which fits limited trophic support, and in rescued spinal motor
      neurons extra GDNF reversed the shrinkage (Sun, Oppenheim and colleagues, 2003). Whether Bax
      also acts in neurons that survive, in other populations or at other times, would need
      deletion of Bax in only some neurons of a population, at a defined time.

anecdotes:
  - title: "A word for falling leaves"
    source: "Kerr, Wyllie & Currie, Br. J. Cancer 26:239 (1972)."
    text: >-
      In 1972 the pathologists John Kerr, Andrew Wyllie and Alastair Currie described a form of cell
      death with a consistent appearance: the cell shrank, its chromatin condensed, and it broke
      into fragments that neighbouring cells took up. They argued that it was an active, controlled
      process that balances cell division in tissues, and they named it apoptosis, from a Greek word
      for the dropping of leaves from a tree. The paper credits a colleague in Greek at the
      University of Aberdeen with suggesting the term. The image fits: a loss that is part of the
      tree's normal life.
  - title: "An X on the family tree"
    source: "Sulston, Schierenberg, White & Thomson, Dev. Biol. 100:64 (1983)."
    text: >-
      In the worm lineage diagrams of John Sulston and his colleagues, a cell that died was marked
      with an X. The mark recorded when the dying nucleus looked brightest under the microscope,
      not what caused the death, which was unknown. Most embryonic cells that died did so soon
      after they were born, and neighbouring cells engulfed the remains. The authors set a strict
      rule for the chart: each unique event rested on at least two direct observations. Those
      repeated X marks gave geneticists a precise list of deaths to look for in mutants.
  - title: "Caspase, letter by letter"
    source: "Deppmann, developmental neuroscience lecture, Neural Survival and Apoptosis; Alnemri et al., Cell 87:171 (1996)."
    text: >-
      The proteases of apoptosis were first known by many names, after the enzyme ICE and the worm
      gene ced-3. In 1996 a group of researchers proposed one family name, caspase, with numbers
      for each member. In this course's lecture the name is unpacked as "Cysteine requiring
      Aspartate Protease." The standard expansion is cysteine-dependent aspartate-directed
      protease. Either way, the name tells you the chemistry: a cysteine in the active site does
      the cutting, and the cut falls just after an aspartate in the target protein.

**Take nerve growth factor (NGF) away from a young sympathetic neuron in a dish, and most such neurons die within a day or two.**

**Predict.** Now add a drug that stops the neuron from making new proteins. Should the deprived neuron die sooner, later, or on the same schedule?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Later. The death is delayed. In 1988 David Martin, Eugene Johnson and colleagues reported that inhibitors of RNA synthesis or of protein synthesis kept rat sympathetic neurons alive after NGF was withdrawn.

</details>

That answer is the strange one. A cell starved of what it needs should simply run down, and stopping protein synthesis should, if anything, make it run down faster. Instead the neuron has to make something in order to die. The [previous lessons](/lessons/naturally-occurring-death/) showed that developing nervous systems lose a large share of their neurons. This one asks what machinery carries out those deaths, where it was found, and what you learn when you take it away.

## Two ways to die

Pathologists first described two broad ways cells die. In **necrosis**, after trauma or loss of blood supply, the cell swells, its membranes fail and its contents spill out, inflaming the tissue around it. In **apoptosis**, a term introduced by John Kerr, Andrew Wyllie and Alastair Currie in 1972, the cell shrinks, its chromatin condenses into dense clumps, and it breaks into membrane-wrapped pieces that other cells swallow. A nucleus condensed this way is called **pyknotic**, and pyknotic nuclei were what researchers counted in early studies of neuron death in embryos. Several other regulated forms of death, such as necroptosis and pyroptosis, have since been recognised; this lesson follows apoptosis.

The Martin experiment raises three puzzles, which the rest of the lesson answers in order.

1. **What must be made?** Which genes must a deprived neuron switch on in order to die?
2. **What are the parts?** Which proteins carry out the death?
3. **What keeps them off** while NGF is present?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** The killers are already built; a survival signal keeps brakes on them. In the worm's core pathway there is one brake. A young sympathetic neuron that depends on NGF has two, and it dies by apoptosis only when both are off.

## Cells marked with an X

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans* is about a millimetre long and transparent. With Nomarski optics, which make nuclei visible in a living animal without staining, John Sulston and H. Robert Horvitz followed the cell divisions after hatching, outside the gonad, and in 1977 published the postembryonic cell lineage, the full family tree of those cells. Sulston and colleagues completed the embryonic lineage in 1983. Some branches ended in a death, marked on the chart with an X, and the same identified cells died in every animal, at the same place in the tree. Counting both lineages, 131 of the 1,090 somatic cells produced in the hermaphrodite die during development.

A death that happens to a named cell on schedule is a phenotype you can screen for. Hilary Ellis and Horvitz started from a mutant, *ced-1*, in which corpses are not engulfed and so stay visible, then searched for new mutations in which the corpses never appeared. In 1986 they reported two genes, *ced-3* and *ced-4*, that are needed for programmed cell death. In strong mutants the cells that should have died lived, and some took on features of neurons, such as making serotonin or dopamine; the best-documented case is the sister of the serotonergic NSM neuron. The animals looked grossly normal in the tests used.

Then came the gene that works the other way. In 1992 Michael Hengartner, Ronald Ellis and Horvitz reported that *ced-9* protects cells: a gain-of-function mutation kept doomed cells alive, and loss of *ced-9* killed cells that should have lived. Worms with severe loss of *ced-9* die as embryos. Read the results as a table. "Loss" means the gene's function is removed; "gain of function" means a mutation keeps the gene's protein active when it would normally be switched off.

| Worm genotype | Cells that normally die | Cells that normally live |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wild type | die | live |
| Loss of *ced-3* (or of *ced-4*) | live | live |
| Gain of function in *ced-9* | live | live |
| Loss of *ced-9* | die | many die |
| Loss of *ced-9* and of *ced-3* | ? | ? |

**Predict.** Fill in the last row. If CED-9 protects cells by holding back CED-3, what happens when both genes are lost?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Few cells die, in either column, and the embryos survive. The same is true when *ced-9* and *ced-4* are both lost. Removing *ced-3* or *ced-4* stopped the extra deaths caused by losing *ced-9*.

</details>

That is a double-mutant argument, and it has a one-sentence rule: if removing the killer cancels the effect of removing the brake, the brake works through the killer. So in genetic order CED-9 acts upstream, as a brake on CED-4 and CED-3. Genetic order does not by itself show which proteins touch.

One more gene sits above the brake. In 1998 Barbara Conradt and Horvitz found EGL-1. Its only resemblance to the BCL-2 family is a short stretch called a BH3 domain; it binds CED-9 to release the brake, and is needed for most somatic cell deaths in the worm. Without EGL-1, most doomed cells live.

## The same parts in us

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Cloning connected the worm to mammals. In 1993 Junying Yuan, Horvitz and colleagues reported that *ced-3* encodes a protein similar to mammalian interleukin-1β-converting enzyme, a protease of the family later named **caspases**.

Caspases cut with a cysteine in their active site, just after an aspartate in their target. They are made as inactive precursors. **Initiator** caspases switch on when they are gathered onto a platform, and they then cut and activate **executioner** caspases, which cut hundreds of cellular proteins and take the cell apart.

In 1994 Hengartner and Horvitz reported that *ced-9* encodes a relative of human Bcl-2, a protein that keeps cells alive. Bcl-2 belongs to the **BCL-2 family**, whose members decide whether mitochondria release cytochrome c; released cytochrome c sets off the caspase cascade. Some family members protect, and some, such as Bax, kill. The BH3-only members, named for the one domain they share with the family, promote death, as EGL-1 does. CED-4 later proved related to mammalian Apaf-1, described by Xiaodong Wang's laboratory in 1997; once bound to cytochrome c, Apaf-1 gathers and switches on initiator caspases (Step 4 below). The Cell Signaling course teaches this biochemistry in [that lesson](/lessons/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases/).

The match is real but imperfect, so read the last column as carefully as the others.

| Worm | Job | Mammalian counterpart | Where the match fails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EGL-1 | releases the brake | BH3-only proteins, such as Bim | the resemblance is confined to the BH3 domain |
| CED-9 | the brake | Bcl-2 and relatives, such as Bcl-xL | the worm brake holds CED-4 directly; the mammalian brake guards the mitochondria |
| CED-4 | gathers and activates the killer | Apaf-1 | Apaf-1 needs cytochrome c; there is no clear role for cytochrome c in worm apoptosis |
| CED-3 | the killer, a protease | caspases, including caspase-9 and caspase-3 | CED-3 is the main worm caspase in this pathway; mammals split initiator and executioner roles |
| (none) | a gate in the outer mitochondrial membrane | Bax (and its relative Bak) | the worm has no clear counterpart of Bax |

So EGL-1 is best called a functional counterpart of mammalian BH3-only proteins. In 2002 Sydney Brenner, Horvitz and Sulston shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries concerning the genetic regulation of organ development and programmed cell death.

That answers the second puzzle: the parts are the worm's, plus a gate the worm lacks.

## The first brake: from a lost signal to cytochrome c

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

Return to the sympathetic neuron that has lost NGF. In it, the counterpart of CED-9 is **brake 1**, a gate at the mitochondrion: protective BCL-2 proteins hold Bax shut, so cytochrome c, the partner the caspase activator needs, stays inside. A second brake, on the caspases themselves, has no counterpart in the core worm pathway; it comes in the next section. Follow NGF loss through the gate step by step. Each step has been tested by blocking it.

**Step 1. The survival signal falls.** NGF made by the target acts through its receptor TrkA on the neuron's axon terminals ([that lesson](/lessons/trk-and-p75/)). The signal keeps kinases such as Akt active ([that lesson](/lessons/pi3k-akt-insulin/)). Akt adds phosphates to several pro-death proteins and so holds them back; among them are FOXO transcription factors. Without NGF, that restraint weakens and those proteins are freed.

**Step 2. Pro-death genes are switched on.** At the same time a stress pathway turns on. The kinase JNK becomes active and adds phosphates to the transcription factor c-Jun ([that lesson](/lessons/stress-signals/)). In the mid-1990s groups including Johnson's reported that c-Jun rises in deprived sympathetic neurons and that blocking its function protects them. Among the genes then induced are BH3-only proteins such as **Bim**; freed FOXO factors are thought to help switch Bim on. Other BH3-only proteins share the work, and neurons lacking Bim alone die more slowly rather than not at all. This is part of the new gene expression that the 1988 inhibitors blocked, and it is one major part of the answer to the first puzzle.

**Step 3. The gate opens.** A BH3 domain is a short helix. It fits a groove on protective proteins such as Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, which is what EGL-1 does to CED-9 in the worm. Protectors with their grooves filled can no longer hold **Bax** back, and Bim can also activate Bax directly.

Inactive Bax sits in the cytoplasm with its last helix tucked into its own groove. Active Bax moves to the mitochondria, inserts that helix into the outer membrane and, with other Bax molecules, makes the membrane leaky. Cytochrome c, which normally carries electrons inside the mitochondrion, escapes into the cytoplasm. In 1996 Thomas Deckwerth, Johnson and colleagues reported that sympathetic neurons from mice lacking Bax survive NGF deprivation. Later work showed that in these neurons Bax is needed for cytochrome c to leave the mitochondria.

**Step 4. The killers assemble.** In the cytoplasm, cytochrome c binds Apaf-1, the counterpart of the worm's CED-4. Apaf-1 assembles into a wheel-shaped complex, the **apoptosome**, which gathers and activates the initiator caspase-9. Caspase-9 cuts and activates the executioner caspase-3, which takes the cell apart.

**Predict.** Delete Bax and take NGF away. Which of the four steps still happen, and does the neuron live?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Steps 1 and 2 lie upstream of Bax, so they should still happen: Akt activity falls, and c-Jun and Bim rise. Step 3 cannot happen. With no Bax the gate stays shut, cytochrome c stays in the mitochondria, step 4 never starts, and the neuron survives. It survives as a shrunken cell, though: Deckwerth and colleagues reported that such neurons are atrophic, because NGF supports growth as well as survival.

</details>

That is the first brake, and NGF keeps the genes that would open it switched off. Is it the only one?

## The second brake: competence to die

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

Mohanish Deshmukh, working in Johnson's laboratory, skipped the gate. With a fine glass needle he injected cytochrome c straight into the cytoplasm of cultured sympathetic neurons, with a marker dye to identify the injected cells, and published the results in 1998.

**Predict.** Inject cytochrome c into neurons kept in NGF. If the mitochondrial gate were the only step NGF controlled, what should happen?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

On the one-brake view they should die, because the injection skips the gate. They survived.

</details>

A second group had lost NGF for about a day. A deprived neuron opens its own gate and dies anyway, so Deshmukh kept each neuron's gate shut, in two separate ways: by blocking protein synthesis, which stops the gate opening, or by using neurons lacking Bax. These neurons were alive when injected. Cytochrome c killed them within hours, a control injection did not, and a caspase inhibitor prevented the death.

So something below cytochrome c release also changes when NGF is taken away. Deshmukh called this acquired susceptibility **competence-to-die**. It built up gradually over roughly the first day without NGF: injection began to kill many neurons after about 15 to 20 hours, sooner in some than in others.

How the neurons were kept alive shows what the change does not need. Neurons making no new protein became competent, and so did neurons lacking Bax. It is a separate brake, and deprivation removes it.

Later work by Deshmukh and colleagues identified the brake. It is the caspase inhibitor **XIAP**, whose level falls during NGF deprivation. XIAP can hold caspases off in these neurons partly because they have little Apaf-1: with few apoptosomes, the caspase signal is small enough to block. When cytochrome c was injected along with **Smac**, a mitochondrial protein that removes inhibitors from caspases, neurons kept in NGF died too.

Now the whole rule can be stated.

> **The two brakes.** **Brake 1 is a gate at the mitochondrion:** protective BCL-2 proteins keep Bax shut, and BH3-only proteins such as Bim, switched on by c-Jun when NGF is lost, open it. Opening it needs new gene expression. **Brake 2 sits on the caspases:** XIAP holds them back, and it is decisive partly because these neurons have little Apaf-1. Deprivation lifts it without any new protein. The neuron dies by apoptosis only when the gate is open (or skipped by injection) and the caspase brake is off.

**What the rule covers.** Apoptosis after trophic deprivation in young NGF-dependent sympathetic neurons, where both brakes were measured. It may extend to other Bax-dependent peripheral populations, but brake 2 has been mapped mainly in sympathetic neurons. Other regulated deaths are outside it. The strength of brake 2 depends on how much Apaf-1 a cell has, and axon breakdown after injury and the local use of caspases inside axons are separate matters (Going deeper).

The table puts every result so far against the two brakes.

| Condition | Brake 1: the gate | Brake 2: XIAP on caspases | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NGF present | shut | on | lives |
| NGF removed | opens | off | dies |
| NGF removed, protein synthesis blocked | stays shut | off | death delayed |
| NGF removed, no Bax | stays shut | off | lives |
| NGF present, cytochrome c injected | skipped | on | lives |
| NGF removed for a day, gate held shut, cytochrome c injected | skipped | off | dies within hours |
| NGF present, cytochrome c and Smac injected | skipped | removed by Smac | dies |

A single-brake model gets the fifth row wrong, and it cannot explain why the injection kills only after many hours without NGF. The second brake is the simplest addition that fits the data, and it answers the third puzzle.

## Cleaning up: the corpse sends a signal

A developing brain kills many cells, yet at any moment few dead cells are visible, because they are removed quickly. In the worm, neighbouring cells engulf the corpses. In the vertebrate brain and spinal cord the main phagocytes are microglia, the brain's resident immune cells; in the developing dorsal root ganglia of mice, glial precursors, not macrophages, do most of the eating.

The dying cell helps. A healthy cell keeps the lipid phosphatidylserine on the inner face of its plasma membrane. In the cells where this has been studied, caspases cut the enzymes that hold it there and activate a scramblase that mixes the two faces, so phosphatidylserine appears outside, where receptors on phagocytes recognize it as an "eat me" signal. The same exposure lets researchers label apoptotic cells with annexin V, a protein that binds phosphatidylserine. Because the cell is normally eaten before its membrane fails, its contents do not spill, and the tissue is not inflamed. Microglia also remove synapses, which [another lesson](/lessons/microglia-complement-pruning/) covers.

## Take the machinery away

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

Culture shows how the program runs. Whole animals ask what it does. Mice can be made lacking a caspase or lacking Bax (knockouts, in which a gene is deleted from the germ line).

**Predict.** Delete caspase-3 or caspase-9 from a mouse. Do the spinal and peripheral neurons that normally die during development survive?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. In 2001 Ronald Oppenheim and colleagues counted spinal interneurons and motor, sensory and autonomic neurons in these mice, and the same number died as in normal mice. The deaths came later and did not look apoptotic: chromatin condensation was absent or reduced, and the cytoplasm filled with vacuoles.

</details>

That is the first limit of the two-brake rule: it gates the apoptotic route, not every way a neuron can die. Deleting a caspase changes how these neurons die; in the populations that depend on Bax, deleting Bax stops the death. There, caspases decide *how*; Bax decides *whether*.

If Bax is the gate, what happens in an animal that lacks it? In 1998 Fletcher White, William Snider, Stanley Korsmeyer and colleagues reported that naturally occurring death was largely eliminated in many neuron populations of Bax-null mice. Across these and later studies, Bax deletion rescues many developing populations, among them sympathetic and sensory neurons, facial motor neurons and retinal ganglion cells. It does not rescue every developing neuron.

The saved neurons were not normal. In the lumbar dorsal and ventral roots and the facial nerve of Bax-null mice, myelinated axons were about 30 to 55 percent more numerous than in normal mice (put the other way, 24 to 35 percent of the Bax-null axons were the extra ones), and many were very small in cross section. Later work from Oppenheim's laboratory found that the extra spinal motor neurons of Bax-null mice had begun to innervate their targets, after which a subset shrank and lost target contact. A neuron saved from death may survive as a smaller cell, which fits limited target support.

Natalia Glebova and David Ginty used Bax deletion to ask a different question. Mice lacking NGF lose their sympathetic neurons, so you cannot ask what else NGF does for them. In mice lacking both NGF and Bax, reported in 2004, the sympathetic neurons survived.

**Predict.** Bax deletion keeps these neurons alive without NGF. If NGF did nothing but keep neurons alive, what would their innervation of organs look like?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It would look like innervation in mice lacking only Bax, which keep their NGF. It did not. In embryos and newborns, the axons failed to fully innervate many targets, and the failure differed by organ.

| Innervation in NGF/Bax double nulls, compared with Bax-null mice | Example |
| --- | --- |
| absent or nearly absent | salivary glands |
| greatly reduced | heart |
| somewhat reduced | stomach, kidneys |
| normal | trachea |

</details>

NGF is therefore needed for axons to grow into many targets, as well as for survival.

The logic is general. A population that survives because the death machinery was deleted gives clues to what the death was for, by showing what is still wrong once dying is no longer an option, though the deleted gene may have other jobs too.

### Design problem

Many surviving neurons in Bax-null mice are small, and some lose target contact. Design an experiment to tell whether that is because they lack trophic support or because Bax has a job in neurons that survive.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Delete Bax in only some neurons of a population, at a defined time, and compare them with their normal neighbours in the same ganglion. Run two versions, each on its own.

- **Late deletion.** Delete Bax only after the period of naturally occurring death, in neurons that have already survived with normal support. If they now shrink or lose target contact, that suggests Bax has a job in neurons that survive, to be confirmed by restoring Bax. If they stay normal, this test found no such job at that age; it cannot rule out an earlier one.
- **Sparse early deletion.** Delete Bax in a scattered few neurons before the death period, so that their neighbours compete normally. If limited support alone explains the size, roughly the share that would normally have died (about half in many peripheral populations) should be small and the rest should match their neighbours. If the neurons that would have survived anyway are smaller too, that points to a job for Bax itself; late deletion tests that group more cleanly.

In either version, adding trophic factor is a useful last check: if it restores size, limited support is a sufficient explanation. For rescued spinal motor neurons that check has been made. Oppenheim's laboratory reported in 2003 that the trophic factor GDNF reversed their shrinkage in Bax-null mice and promoted axon regrowth. Whether Bax has other jobs in other populations is still open.

</details>

## Putting it together

The three puzzles have one answer: the killers are already built, and NGF keeps two brakes on them. What must be made includes c-Jun's targets, BH3-only proteins such as Bim, which open the gate. The parts are the worm's, found again as caspases, Apaf-1 and the BCL-2 family, with Bax as the mammalian gate. What keeps them off is the closed gate and XIAP on the caspases. Delete the caspases and these neurons still die, another way; delete Bax and many live, smaller.

Which neurons get enough NGF in the first place is a question about targets, and how targets choose among their neurons is the subject of [competition](/lessons/competition-for-targets/).

## Going deeper

### Caspase knockouts and the forebrain

Mice lacking caspase-3 or caspase-9 can have enlarged, malformed forebrains with extra cells. This reflects reduced death early in neurogenesis, largely among progenitors, not a block of the trophic deaths this lesson follows. In caspase-3 knockouts the severity depends heavily on the genetic background, the mouse strain the deletion is bred into; on the C57BL/6J strain the brain develops almost normally.

### Apaf-1 as neurons mature

In fully mature neurons Apaf-1 is largely silenced, which is one reason brake 2 is a property of particular cells at particular ages, not of all cells. One prediction of the rule is that young sympathetic neurons lacking XIAP and kept in NGF should be killed by injected cytochrome c alone; later work from Deshmukh's group reported this. In mature neurons, with little Apaf-1, removing XIAP need not be enough.

### Caspases inside axons

Caspases are also used locally, inside axons. As the degeneration lecture puts it, "Caspases required for developmental degeneration but not injury." The firm result is for axons that lose trophic support, which use caspases, including caspase-9 and caspase-3, to degenerate, while an axon cut by injury breaks down by a different route. Pruning in the living animal is less uniform. The point is taken up in [Pruning Without Dying](/lessons/axon-pruning/).
