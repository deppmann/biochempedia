scientists:
  - name: "Raymond Rappaport"
    contribution: >-
      Reshaped sand-dollar eggs mechanically to ask what part of the mitotic apparatus tells the
      cell surface where to furrow, showing that a furrow can form between two asters that are not
      joined by a spindle.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Rappaport, J. Exp. Zool. 148:81–89 (1961), on the cleavage stimulus in sand dollar eggs."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Rappaport worked with the eggs of sand dollars, which are large, clear and divide on
      schedule, so a change in shape can be followed by eye under the microscope. In the 1961
      paper usually cited for this work, he reshaped a fertilized egg into a ring (a torus)
      before its first division. At the first division
      the cell furrowed only on one side, leaving a horseshoe-shaped cell with two nuclei. At the
      next division that cell held two spindles side by side. Furrows formed across each spindle,
      as expected, and also in the gap between the two neighbouring asters, where no spindle
      joined them. Two asters were enough to start a furrow in this egg. The experiment turned a
      vague question, what tells the surface where to cut, into a geometric one that could be
      tested by moving parts of the cell around, the approach the RhoA-zone studies used decades
      later.
  - name: "William M. Bement"
    contribution: >-
      With Hélène Benink and George von Dassow, imaged active RhoA in echinoderm and frog embryos
      and showed that it forms an equatorial zone before the furrow, positioned by microtubules
      and moved when the spindle is moved.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Bement, Benink & von Dassow, J. Cell Biol. 170:91–101 (2005), doi:10.1083/jcb.200501131."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Biochemists knew that the small GTPase RhoA was needed for animal cytokinesis, but knowing a
      protein is needed does not tell you where or when it acts. Bement, Benink and von Dassow
      made that visible. They expressed eGFP fused to the Rho-binding domain of rhotekin, a probe
      that sticks only to RhoA in its active, GTP-bound form, in embryos of two sea urchin species
      and of the frog Xenopus laevis. Active RhoA gathered in a bounded band at the equator one to
      four minutes before the furrow appeared. It still formed when actin assembly was blocked
      with cytochalasin D or latrunculin B in sea urchin embryos, so it lies upstream of the actin
      ring. Then, in green sea urchin blastomeres already beginning to furrow, they pushed the spindle aside with a blunt needle.
      The RhoA zone relocated over three to five minutes, and the furrow followed it. The work
      gave the field a live readout of the signal that places the furrow.
  - name: "Gerd Jürgens"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory showed that the Arabidopsis gene KNOLLE encodes a syntaxin-related protein
      that sits at the division plane, and that in knolle embryos vesicles reach the plane but
      appear to fail to fuse into a complete cell plate.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Lauber et al., J. Cell Biol. 139:1485–1493 (1997), doi:10.1083/jcb.139.6.1485; Lukowitz, Mayer & Jürgens, Cell 84:61–71 (1996)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The knolle mutant was studied in the Jürgens laboratory to track down a gene needed for
      cytokinesis in the Arabidopsis embryo. Embryos lacking the gene had enlarged cells with incomplete walls and several
      nuclei, a sign that division of the cell had failed while division of the nucleus had
      not. In 1996, Wolfgang Lukowitz, Ulrike Mayer and Jürgens reported that the gene encodes a
      protein related to syntaxins, the membrane proteins that pair with partners on vesicles to
      drive membrane fusion. In 1997, Martina Lauber and colleagues in the same laboratory raised
      antibodies against KNOLLE and found it at the forming cell plate. Serial-section electron
      microscopy of mutant embryos showed bands of vesicles in the division plane and walls left
      incomplete. The vesicles had arrived; the step that failed appeared to be fusion. That
      pointed to fusion rather than delivery and gave plant cytokinesis a named fusion protein to study.
  - name: "Joachim Seemann"
    contribution: >-
      With Jen-Hsuan Wei, forced cultured PtK1 cells to divide into a product holding the whole
      spindle and a spindle-free cytoplast, and showed that the cytoplast still inherited working
      Golgi stacks, while rebuilding the Golgi ribbon needed both Golgi extract and tubulin.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Wei & Seemann, J. Cell Biol. 184:391–397 (2009), doi:10.1083/jcb.200809090."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In most mammalian cells the Golgi apparatus is one ribbon of linked stacks beside the nucleus.
      During mitosis the ribbon comes apart and its membranes collect around the spindle poles,
      which raised a simple question: do Golgi membranes reach the daughters by riding on the
      spindle? Wei and Seemann tested it in PtK1 cells, a rat-kangaroo kidney line with flat,
      clear cytoplasm. They induced asymmetric divisions in which one product, a karyoplast, took
      the entire spindle and the chromosomes, while the other, a cytoplast, took none. If the
      spindle carried the Golgi, the cytoplast should have none that worked. Instead it held
      scattered Golgi stacks, and a reporter protein made from injected CD8 mRNA reached its
      surface, so the stacks could secrete. What the cytoplast lacked was the ribbon. Tubulin
      injected alone did not restore it; Golgi extract plus tubulin did, and so did a cooler division that let part of the spindle into the cytoplast. Stacks and ribbon turned out to be inherited by different rules: working stacks without the spindle, the factors for rebuilding the ribbon with it.

  - name: "Microneedle spindle displacement"
    blurb: >-
      A blunt glass needle pressed on a large embryonic cell pushes the spindle to a new position

      whether the spindle instructs the cortex or merely sits near it.
  - name: "Serial-section electron microscopy of mutants"
    blurb: >-
      A tissue is cut into a series of thin sections and each is imaged by electron microscopy, so
      that a structure can be followed in three dimensions. In knolle embryos it showed vesicles
      piled in the division plane beside incomplete walls, which points to a fusion defect rather than a delivery defect.
  - name: "Induced asymmetric division with a secretion assay"
    blurb: >-
      A dividing cell is manipulated so that the whole spindle goes to one product. The other
      product is then examined for organelles and tested for function, for example by injecting
      mRNA for a membrane protein and asking whether it reaches the cell surface.

      cutting the bridge at abscission, long after RhoA has been activated.

      - "A contractile ring of actin and nonmuscle myosin II"

    rationale: >-
      The phragmoplast forms between the two daughter nuclei and guides Golgi vesicles to the
      centre of the plane, where they fuse into the cell plate. The preprophase band marks, before
      mitosis, where the plate will later meet the parental wall, and it has disappeared by the
      time the plate is built; losing it can make plate orientation less precise. A contractile
      ring of actin and myosin II pinches an animal cell from the outside in, which a walled plant
      cell cannot do. ESCRT-III cuts the bridge at the end of animal cytokinesis; it does not
      deliver vesicles.

  - stem: "A cultured human cell completes furrow ingression, and its two daughters are left joined by a thin intercellular bridge. If ESCRT-III cannot assemble in that bridge, what is the expected result?"
    choices:
      - "The daughters separate normally once the furrow closes"
      - "The furrow never forms because RhoA is not activated"
      - "The bridge persists and the final cut is delayed or fails"
      - "The spindle fails to separate the two sets of chromosomes"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Abscission, the cut of the bridge, is a separate step after ingression, and ESCRT-III does
      the constriction and scission, so without it the bridge stays and the cut is delayed or
      fails. The furrow has already formed in this cell, and RhoA activation does not depend on
      ESCRT-III. Daughters do not separate on furrow closure alone; that is the misconception
      that ingression equals separation. Chromosome segregation is the spindle's job in anaphase and is normally finished before abscission begins; chromatin left in the bridge can delay the cut.

Mitosis has sorted the chromosomes into two sets, and anaphase, the stage in which they move apart to the two poles, is under way. The cell has three jobs left: place the cut, make the cut, and give each daughter a working share of what it contains. The first film followed the first job and ended on its rule.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** In the embryo cells studied, the cut goes where the anaphase microtubules place a band of active RhoA. Move the microtubules, and the band moves first; the furrow follows. Other animal cells can add cues of their own (Going deeper).

The first film left a question open: how do microtubules deep inside the cell switch on a protein at the membrane? The first section answers it. The next two follow the second job, making the cut, in an animal cell and then in a plant cell boxed in by a wall. The second film takes the third job, sharing the organelles. Microtubules keep turning up: the furrow leaves a bridge packed with them, a plant guides its new wall along them, and the spindle carries some of what the daughters inherit.

## From microtubules to the band

The first film showed *that* microtubules place the band, not *how*. Start with *which* microtubules.

**Predict.** Raymond Rappaport reshaped a fertilized sand dollar egg into a ring, like a doughnut. Its first division cut the ring on one side only, leaving one horseshoe-shaped cell with two nuclei. At the next division that cell held two spindles side by side, each with an aster at both ends. In the gap between them, an aster of one spindle faced an aster of the other, with no spindle joining them. Does a furrow form in that gap?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes. Furrows formed across each spindle and also in the gap between the facing asters (Rappaport, 1961). That is why the one idea says *microtubules*, not *the spindle*: in this egg, two facing asters were enough to mark a furrow site.

</details>

Now the *how*. RhoA is a small GTPase, a switch whose on-off cycle belongs to [GTPase switches](/lessons/gtpase-switches/) and [integrins and Rho GTPases](/lessons/integrins-and-rho-gtpases/). Two proteins relay the microtubules' position to it.

- **Ect2** is the enzyme that switches RhoA on. It is a guanine-nucleotide exchange factor (GEF): it makes RhoA let go of its GDP, and GTP, which is plentiful in the cell, takes its place. On its own, Ect2 stays folded in an inactive form.
- **Centralspindlin** is a protein complex that gathers where microtubules from the two poles overlap. The most prominent overlap is the **spindle midzone**, the dense bundle of overlapping microtubules between the separating chromosomes (see [mitosis](/lessons/mitosis/)).

At anaphase, centralspindlin binds Ect2 and helps activate it. In large echinoderm embryo cells the two collect on overlapping microtubules at the equator, including where astral microtubules from the two poles meet, with or without a midzone between them (Su and colleagues, 2014): the molecular side of Rappaport's result. In cultured human cells, ECT2 must also associate with the plasma membrane during anaphase to switch RhoA on at the equator; how it reaches the cortex in echinoderm embryos is less clear. The band reflects this microtubule cue, but it is not a simple print of where microtubules overlap, and how a sharply bounded band forms is still debated (Going deeper).

**Predict.** In cultured human cells such as HeLa cells, Ect2 is depleted and the cells go through mitosis. Their chromosomes separate normally. What do you expect to find once division should have finished?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In the cultured human cells used in these experiments, many cells fail to concentrate RhoA, actin filaments and myosin at the equator, their furrows fail, and they are left with two nuclei even though the chromosomes were separated. One cell with doubled DNA is a tetraploid cell. The next section shows a second route to the same result.

## Making the cut

The one idea says *where* the furrow forms, not *when* the daughters finally part. The ring of formin-made actin filaments and nonmuscle myosin II pulls the furrow in (see [molecular motors](/lessons/molecular-motors/) and [cytoskeleton dynamics](/lessons/cytoskeleton-dynamics/)). The first film drew it as a drawstring, the simplest case; in fuller view, how the filaments are arranged in the ring differs between systems, and no one geometry is settled for all cells.

Ingression does not finish the job. It leaves the two daughters joined by a thin intercellular bridge packed with spindle microtubules. At its centre sits the **midbody**, a dense plug of overlapping microtubules and proteins left from the spindle midzone. Abscission, the final cut, is a separate, later step. In the several animal-cell systems covered by a 2022 review, ESCRT-III, a set of proteins that polymerize into spirals on membranes, assembles in the bridge, constricts it and cuts it. Chromatin left in the bridge can delay the cut.

If the cut fails, the bridge can regress and leave one cell with two nuclei and doubled DNA. That is the second route to the tetraploid cell of the Ect2 prediction, and one source of the tetraploid cells discussed in [chromosome changes](/lessons/chromosome-changes/).

## Plants build a wall from the inside out

In a plant, the first film's question has a different answer. Microtubule arrays still place and build the division, but not through a RhoA band, and a plant cell cannot pinch, because it sits inside a rigid wall. In the prevalent mode of somatic plant cytokinesis there is no furrow at all. The new membrane starts in the middle of the cell and grows outward.

The builder is the phragmoplast, an array of microtubules and actin filaments that forms between the two daughter nuclei. It guides vesicles from the Golgi to the centre of the division plane. There they fuse with one another to form the cell plate, a flat compartment of new membrane filled with wall material. The plate expands sideways toward the parental cell surface, and cytokinesis is complete when it joins the old wall.

Fusion needs machinery. KNOLLE, an Arabidopsis protein related to syntaxins, the target-membrane SNAREs you met in [vesicle traffic](/lessons/vesicle-traffic/), sits at the division plane.

**Predict.** Arabidopsis embryos lacking KNOLLE have enlarged cells, incomplete walls and several nuclei. A plate could fail because vesicles never reach the plane, or because they arrive and cannot fuse. What would electron micrographs of the plane show in each case?

A block in delivery along the phragmoplast, or in Golgi production, would leave few vesicles in the plane; a fusion block would leave a crowd. Where the plate meets the old wall is often decided earlier. Before mitosis, many plant cells build a preprophase band, a cortical ring of microtubules that marks the future division plane and then disappears. It often helps orient the division but is not indispensable everywhere; Going deeper compares two tissues.

Animal or plant, the cut falls between the two sets of chromosomes, and everything else in the cytoplasm lands on one side or the other. The second film picks up here: does the cell count its organelles out, or is chance good enough?

## Sharing the contents: chance or control

The second film ran the coin-toss model in pictures; here are its numbers, to check by hand. A cell does not count its organelles out one by one, so when is chance good enough? To tell when a cell must step in, first work out what pure chance would give.

### Worked problem: partition by chance

The numbers are illustrative. Suppose each unit of an organelle goes to either daughter independently with probability 0.5, like a coin toss. The count in one daughter then follows a binomial distribution. This is one no-control null model, under the stated assumptions: independent units, equal chance of entering either daughter.

> mean = n × 0.5; standard deviation = square root of (n × 0.5 × 0.5); P(one daughter gets none) = 2 × 0.5^n

**Case 1: n = 100 units.** Mean = 50. Standard deviation = square root of 25 = 5. About 95% of divisions fall within two standard deviations, so most daughters get 40 to 60 units. P(one daughter gets none) = 2 × 0.5^100, about 1.6 × 10^-30. In practice that never happens.

**Case 2: n = 10.** P = 2 × 0.5^10 = 2/1024, about 0.002, or 0.2%. One division in five hundred leaves a daughter with none.

**Case 3: n = 2.** P = 2 × 0.25 = 0.5. Half of all divisions leave one daughter with none.

Check: case 2 by counting. All ten to daughter A is one outcome of 1,024, all ten to daughter B is another, so 2 of 1,024. The factor of 2 is there because either daughter can be the empty one. Each added unit halves the risk, so it falls below one in a thousand at n = 11 and below one in a million at n = 21.

Conclusion: chance is safe only for many independent units. A single connected structure, such as the Golgi ribbon of a mammalian cell or a tubular mitochondrial network, is one unit, not many. It must be broken up first or segregated actively, as the two centrosomes, the microtubule-organizing centres at the spindle poles, are when the spindle carries one to each pole. Chance contributes to sharing many organelles, often alongside active control, and some connected organelles are fragmented before division. In PtK1 cells, a rat-kangaroo kidney line, the Golgi ribbon (see [the secretory pathway](/lessons/secretory-pathway/)) disassembles during mitosis, its membranes cluster around the spindle poles, and the ribbon reforms after division. In HeLa cells, mitochondria fragmented in early mitosis, and cells depleted of Drp1, a dynamin-related GTPase that drives mitochondrial fission, kept tubular mitochondria through mitosis, as Taguchi and colleagues reported in 2007. These are results from single cell lines, so treat them as strong leads rather than general rules.

Moving an organelle and changing its shares are separate claims (Going deeper). Organelles also differ in how they divide (see [organelles as environments](/lessons/organelles-as-environments/)), and in some cells division is unequal on purpose, as in [asymmetric division](/lessons/asymmetric-division/).

### Design problem

The Golgi's mitotic membranes cluster around the spindle poles. Is the Golgi inherited by riding on the spindle? Design an experiment in cultured cells that could tell, and say what each model predicts before you look at any data.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Follow Jen-Hsuan Wei and Joachim Seemann, who used PtK1 cells in 2009. Induce asymmetric divisions that send the entire spindle, with the chromosomes, into one product (a karyoplast) and none into the other (a cytoplast).

Predictions. If Golgi membranes reach daughters only on the spindle, the cytoplast should have no working Golgi. If stacks are partitioned independently, the cytoplast should have them.

Tests. Look for Golgi stacks in the cytoplast by electron microscopy and with Golgi markers. Test function: inject mRNA for a membrane reporter such as CD8 and ask whether the protein reaches the cell surface, which requires a working secretory pathway. Then ask about the ribbon separately: inject tubulin alone, Golgi extract alone, and both together, and score ribbon reassembly.

Result. The cytoplasts held scattered Golgi stacks, and CD8 reached their surface, so working stacks were inherited without the spindle. Tubulin alone did not restore a ribbon; Golgi extract plus tubulin did. A cooler division that let part of the spindle into the cytoplast also restored the ribbon, and the authors concluded that the factors needed to rebuild the ribbon travel with the spindle. So the answer to the opening question is split: working stacks do not ride on the spindle, but the ribbon's assembly factors do.

Limits. This is an engineered division in one cell line. It does not show that no Golgi membrane ever uses the spindle, and it does not settle which Golgi components pass through the ER during mitosis.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The numbers behind the first film

In the 2005 work of Bement, Benink and von Dassow, the band appeared one to four minutes before the furrow in two sea urchin species and the frog *Xenopus laevis*; the actin drugs were cytochalasin D and latrunculin B. In green sea urchin blastomeres (early embryo cells) already furrowing, after the needle moved the spindle, the RhoA zone relocated over three to five minutes, and the furrow followed. These timings come from large embryonic cells; do not assume the same minutes in a human cell.

### Keeping the band narrow

Three mechanisms may all contribute to the sharp edge, in different proportions in different cells: midzone delivery of Ect2, inhibition of cortical activity at the poles by astral microtubules, and RhoA's own fast cycling.

### A furrow placed without the spindle

Outside the embryo cells of the first film, other cues can place the cut. In *Drosophila* neuroblasts, which divide asymmetrically, a polarity pathway can position a furrow independently of the spindle (Cabernard, Prehoda and Doe, 2010).

### Moving organelles versus sharing them

In dividing HeLa cells mitochondria gather toward the furrow, and a dominant-negative Miro-1 (a mutant form that blocks the normal Miro-1, an adaptor linking mitochondria to motors) reduced that gathering, yet the authors saw no obvious inheritance defect, possibly because the mislocalization was only partial. In other HeLa experiments, depleting the motor Myo19 sent mitochondria toward the spindle poles and disturbed how they were partitioned at division, so position can matter for shares.

### The preprophase band in two tissues

After it disappears, the preprophase band leaves a cortical division zone behind. How much the band matters varies with the cell type. In Arabidopsis roots, disrupting the preprophase band reduced the precision of division orientation without abolishing the division zone. In Arabidopsis cambium stem cells, trm678 mutants that make no band kept regular division planes, while the kinesins POK1 and POK2 were needed for ordered planes. Keep each result in its tissue.

### Timing the cell plate

In Arabidopsis root cells filmed live in three dimensions by lattice light sheet microscopy, untreated plates developed over about 20 to 30 minutes, and their diameter grew at about 4.09 μm per minute early on. Callose, a polysaccharide of the forming plate, helps the plate switch from collecting vesicles to maturing: blocking its deposition with Endosidin 7 disrupted that switch. The growth rate is for the early interval in this experiment, not a constant of cytokinesis.
