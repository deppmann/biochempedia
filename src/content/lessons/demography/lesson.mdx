      idealized shapes of survivorship curve, including the three that textbooks now number Type I,
      II and III.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Pearl & Miner, Experimental studies on the duration of life. XIV. The comparative mortality of certain lower organisms, Quarterly Review of Biology 10:60-79 (1935)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Pearl worked at Johns Hopkins University on a question that sounds simple: how long do
      organisms live, and how does the chance of dying change with age? Much of his evidence came
      from laboratory cultures, where every individual could be counted from birth to death, and
      from human mortality records. In a 1935 paper in the Quarterly Review of Biology, written
      with John Rice Miner, Pearl set the survivorship of several organisms side by side
      and sorted the curves into idealized shapes, three of them later numbered Type I, II and III: deaths
      concentrated late in life, deaths spread evenly in proportion, and deaths concentrated early.
      Such idealized shapes are a shorthand, not a claim that real species fall neatly into boxes, and real
      curves rarely do. What the classification made possible was a shared vocabulary, so that a
      field biologist with a new table could say which idealized shape it most resembled and
      where it departed from it.

      from birth to death. In 1947 Deevey published a long review in the Quarterly Review of

techniques:
  - name: "Cohort follow-up (individual census)"
    blurb: >-
      Mark or identify every individual born in a period and record, at fixed intervals, who is
      alive and how many offspring each has produced. It gives l_x and m_x directly, but only for
      the years and conditions the cohort lived through.
  - name: "Censored life-table estimation (actuarial and Kaplan-Meier)"
    blurb: >-
      When some individuals are still alive or lost when the study ends, their records are
      censored: they count as at risk for the time they were seen and are not counted as deaths.
      This gives fair estimates only if being censored is unrelated to an animal's risk of dying.
      Actuarial tables adjust the number at risk in each interval, and Kaplan-Meier curves with
      log-rank tests compare survival between groups.
  - name: "Capture-mark-recapture (Cormack-Jolly-Seber models)"
    blurb: >-
      Mark animals and resight or recapture them on repeated occasions. Patterns of sightings and
      gaps across many encounter histories let the model estimate detection probability and apparent
      survival separately; permanent emigration still has to be estimated with extra information, such as encounters
      outside the study area.

      - "200, the number of females still alive at age 3"
      - "0.60, the fraction of the newborns that died before age 3"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Survivorship l_x is the fraction of the original cohort alive at exact age x, n_x/n_0 =
      200/500 = 0.40. The value 0.60 is the cumulative fraction that died, which is 1 - l_3, not
      l_3. The count 200 is n_3, the number alive, which has not been divided by the starting
      cohort. The value 0.20 spreads the 0.60 that died evenly over three years, an average that
      has no meaning here, which is neither l_x nor a correct q_x, because q_x is calculated interval by
      interval from the number alive at its start.
  - stem: "A cohort of 1,000 shows a Type II survivorship pattern with an annual death probability of 0.5. How many die during the third year of life (between ages 2 and 3)?"
    choices:
      - "125, because half of the 250 alive at age 2 die"
      - "333, because a third of the cohort dies in each of three years"
      - "250, because half of the 500 alive at age 1 die"
      - "500, because Type II means equal numbers die each year"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      With a constant q of 0.5, survivors fall 1,000, 500, 250, 125, so the third year starts
      with 250 alive and half of them, 125, die. Answering 500 is the common misreading that Type
      II means a constant number of deaths; it is a constant probability, so deaths shrink as
      the cohort shrinks. The figure 250 is the number dying in the second year, an off-by-one
      reading of the intervals. Dividing the cohort into three equal shares is the same constant
      numbers error in another form and ignores the stated probability.
  - stem: "In a life table, n_2 = 40 and n_3 = 10. What are q_2 and p_2?"
    choices:
      - "q_2 = 0.25 and p_2 = 0.75"
      - "q_2 = 30 and p_2 = 10"
      - "q_2 = 0.75 and p_2 = 0.25"
      - "q_2 = 0.30 and p_2 = 0.70"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Deaths in the interval are d_2 = 40 - 10 = 30, so q_2 = 30/40 = 0.75 and p_2 = 1 - 0.75 =
      0.25. Swapping the two values to 0.25 and 0.75 confuses the fraction surviving with the
      fraction dying. The pair 0.30 and 0.70 divides the 30 deaths by 100, as if the starting
      cohort rather than the number alive at age 2 were at risk, which gives d_x/n_0, not q_x.
      The pair 30 and 10 gives counts, d_2 and n_3, rather than probabilities.
  - stem: "An ecologist builds a static life table by ageing all deer carcasses found in one winter and treating the counts at each age as if they came from a single cohort. Which assumption does this method require that a cohort follow-up does not?"
    choices:
      - "That the population was closed to immigration and emigration"
      - "That females bear daughters and sons in a 50:50 ratio"
      - "That every animal in the population was marked or identified at birth"
      - "That births and death rates were steady over the past years"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      A static table compares animals born in different years, so it reads as a cohort only if
      the population was stationary: similar numbers born each year and similar age-specific
      death rates over time. Closure matters for a cohort follow-up too, since animals that leave
      look like deaths in either design, so it is not the extra assumption. Marking every animal at
      birth is what a cohort follow-up needs, and a static table avoids it. Static tables also need
      accurate ageing and fair recovery of remains at every age, but steady births and death rates
      are what let animals born in different years stand in for one cohort. A 50:50 sex
      ratio concerns converting births to daughters for m_x, not the survivorship schedule built
      from carcasses.
  - stem: "A country's age pyramid has a broad base, and its fertility has just fallen to the level that would exactly replace each generation in the long run. What does the age structure predict for the next few decades, if mortality and migration stay as they are?"
    choices:
      - "The pyramid's shape shows that survivorship is Type III in this country"
      - "Births stop increasing at once, so the total size stays flat"
      - "The total keeps growing while the large young cohorts reach reproductive ages"
      - "The total falls at once, because replacement fertility means fewer births"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Replacement fertility balances births and deaths only after the age structure settles.
      With many young people about to enter reproductive ages, the number of women of reproductive age keeps rising
      for a time even though each woman has fewer children, so the population keeps growing; this
      is demographic momentum. A flat total at once ignores the age structure. An immediate fall
      confuses replacement with below-replacement fertility. Reading the pyramid as Type III
      survivorship treats present composition as an l_x curve, but a broad base mainly reflects
      past births, which need not have been equal across years.

The first film built R0 = 1.7 for a **cohort**, a group of females born together. Here you add the columns it skipped, turn R0 into a generation time and a yearly rate, and ask how far real counts can be trusted.

> **The one idea.** Whether a population can replace itself depends on the **net reproductive rate, R0**: at each age, the chance a newborn female is alive (l_x) times the average number of daughters per live female at that age (m_x), added over her life. Above 1, each generation is larger than the last; below 1, smaller.
>
> *Scope:* a closed female population (no migration), with rates that stay fixed, and R0 counted per generation, not per year. Wherever this page changes one rate, the others are held the same. It is a prediction from assumptions, not an observation: real vital rates change with density, weather and year.

## The worked cohort, column by column

A **life table** summarizes how a cohort's survival and reproduction change with age. Read the subscript x as "at age x": **n_x** ("n sub x") is the number alive at exact age x, **l_x = n_x / n_0** ("ell sub x") is the film's survivorship, and **m_x** its daughters per live female.

**Worked problem.** In this made-up data set, a closed female cohort is followed with complete records: no migration, no censoring. Each surviving female gives birth in one pulse at exact age x, before that year's deaths, so her daughters are weighted by l_x.

| x (years) | n_x | l_x | d_x | q_x | p_x | m_x | l_x m_x | x l_x m_x |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 100 | 1.0 | 20 | 0.20 | 0.80 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 80 | 0.8 | 40 | 0.50 | 0.50 | 1 | 0.8 | 0.8 |
| 2 | 40 | 0.4 | 30 | 0.75 | 0.25 | 2 | 0.8 | 1.6 |
| 3 | 10 | 0.1 | 10 | 1.00 | 0 | 1 | 0.1 | 0.3 |
| 4 | 0 | 0 | none | undefined | undefined | 0 | 0 | 0 |

The film used n_x, l_x and m_x; each new survival column answers one question:

- **d_x**: how many die between age x and age x + 1? **d_x = n_x − n_(x+1)**, here 20, 40, 30, 10. They add to 100: every death is counted.
- **q_x**: what is the chance of dying in that interval *given that you reached its start*? (This q is a death probability, not the allele frequency q of population genetics.)

**Predict.** Of the 40 females alive at age 2, 30 die before age 3. Is q_2 30 out of 40, or 30 out of 100?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

30 out of 40, so q_2 = 0.75. Only the 40 alive at age 2 were at risk. So **q_x = d_x / n_x**, deaths divided by the number alive at the *start* of the interval (q_0 = 20/100 = 0.20, q_1 = 40/80 = 0.50, q_3 = 10/10 = 1.00). Dividing by the original 100 gives d_x/n_0, not q_x.

</details>

- **p_x**: what is the chance of surviving the interval? Whatever does not die survives: **p_x = 1 − q_x**, here 0.80, 0.50, 0.25, 0.

Reaching age 3 means surviving year 0, then year 1, then year 2, so l_x is a product of the p_x before it: l_3 = p_0 × p_1 × p_2 (check: l_2 = 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.4). That is the product rule from [probability and crosses](/lessons/probability-and-crosses/), each stage conditional on the one before; it is why the film's hard winter reached every later age. How births are divided among ages and offspring belongs to [life histories](/lessons/life-histories/); here m_x is taken as measured. The film's sum, in symbols:

## Survivorship curves and age structure

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Real species mix the film's three ideal shapes, often with Type III juvenile losses followed by Type I adult survival, and the worked cohort, with q_x rising, sits nearer Type I.

In symbols, a constant q gives l_x = (1 − q)^x, and taking logs turns repeated multiplication into repeated addition: log l_x = x × log(1 − q), a straight line falling by the same step each year (see [log scales](/math-toolkit/#log-scales)).

A single average can hide where deaths fall, too. **Life expectancy at birth** is the mean length of life over all newborns, so heavy infant mortality drags it far down. In an invented population where 30% of newborns die within days of birth and the rest all live to exactly 50, life expectancy at birth is about 35 years (0.3 × about 0 years + 0.7 × 50 years), yet no adult dies at 35.

An **age-structure diagram** (an age pyramid) is not a survivorship curve: it shows the proportion of a population in each age class *now*, which depends on past births as well as survival. A pyramid with a broad base is not a Type III curve; it may be a growing population with good juvenile survival. Under the usual conditions (fixed rates, no migration, and births spread over ages so the age classes mix rather than cycle), a population converges toward a **stable age distribution**: the shares in each age class stop changing while the total grows or shrinks. Those shares depend on both survival and the growth rate. In a growing population each year's newborns outnumber the last year's, so the young are a larger share than survival alone would give.

**Predict.** A human population has many young people, and its fertility falls today to **replacement**, the level at which each generation exactly replaces itself in the long run. Does it stop growing today?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. It can keep growing for decades, because the number of women of reproductive age is still rising as the large young cohorts grow up. This is **demographic momentum**. The often quoted replacement figure of about 2.1 children per woman applies only where few children die before adulthood.

</details>

## Where the numbers come from, and what they miss

**Of 100 adult birds banded at a colony, 60 are seen there the next year. Did 40 die?** Not necessarily: some may be alive and missed, some alive elsewhere. R0 is only as good as the l_x and m_x behind it, and there are three ways to get them.

**Cohort follow-up**, the method the worked table assumed, marks every individual born in one period and checks, at each age, who is alive and how many daughters each has. It gives l_x and m_x directly, but slowly: for a long-lived species, the last member of one cohort can outlive a research career.

**A static table** is the shortcut: one snapshot. Age the living animals and read the number at each age as a cohort's survivors, or age the carcasses and read the ages at death as a cohort's deaths. Either way, animals born in different years stand in for one cohort. The simplest case where that is fair is a **stationary** population: about the same number born each year, and the same death rates at each age, for many years (a population growing at a known rate can be corrected; see Going deeper). Every age must also be sampled fairly, in proportion to its numbers. Young remains are fragile and easily missed, so a carcass table can undercount early deaths, and ages read from remains carry error too.

**Mark-recapture**, for animals you cannot watch continuously, records on repeated visits which marked animals are seen again. An animal seen after a gap was alive and missed, so across many animals the sightings and gaps let a model estimate detection separately from survival. After an animal's last sighting, though, it usually cannot tell whether that animal died, left for good or was missed.

Each method has weak points. Some bias the estimates; others make them less certain or hide variation:

- **Censoring.** An animal still alive when the study ends, or lost to an accident in a study of natural deaths, is **right-censored** (figure below): at risk while seen, never a death. That is fair only if being censored is unrelated to the risk of dying; animals lost because they were sick or weak bias survival upward. The 2016 primate tables removed half the censored animals from the number at risk in their interval.
- **Few old individuals.** Small samples at old ages hide gradual changes in mortality, so the oldest ages are usually among the least certain.
- **Pooled years.** Tables pooled across years, like the primate tables, give average rates and hide good and bad years.
- **Missing is not dead.** Even with detection modelled, a survivor that left looks like a death: **apparent survival** is true survival multiplied by **site fidelity**, the chance a surviving animal stays in the study area, so permanent emigration pulls it below true survival ([spatial populations](/lessons/spatial-populations/) takes this further).

### Design problem

Return to the colony that opened this section: 60 of 100 banded adults were seen there the next year. A student concludes that adult mortality is 40% a year. Evaluate the conclusion and design a better study of adult survival.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The 0.60 is an apparent return rate, lowered by three things besides death: birds alive at the colony but not seen, birds alive that moved elsewhere for good, and birds that lost their bands.

1. **Detection.** Search the colony on several occasions each season and for several years, and fit a Cormack-Jolly-Seber model, the standard model for such encounter histories, to each bird's record, so that detection probability and apparent survival are estimated separately.
2. **Emigration.** Search neighbouring colonies, or fit a subset of birds with tracking devices, to estimate permanent emigration, the fidelity term in apparent survival. In Bonelli's eagles in Catalonia, modelling dispersal jointly shifted survival estimates for non-breeding females more than for males, by a few hundredths with wide uncertainty; breeder survival did not change (Badia-Boher and colleagues, 2023).
3. **Band loss.** Double-mark some birds with two bands, or a band and a second mark, to estimate how often bands are lost.
4. **Unknown fates.** Record birds never seen again as unknown fates, keeping their later missed searches in their encounter histories, not as dead.

If detection is 0.9 and fidelity 0.8, an apparent return of 0.60 would point to true survival near 0.6/(0.9 x 0.8), about 0.83, and annual mortality nearer 17% than 40%. Both values are invented to show the logic; only the study can supply the real ones.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The exact yearly rate

With fixed rates, the exact r solves the Euler-Lotka equation; with births in a pulse at each exact age, as in the worked table, it reads sum of λ^(−x) l_x m_x = 1, where λ = e^r is the yearly multiplier. For the worked table λ = 1.415 and r = 0.347 per year, against 1.40 and 0.334 from the shortcut, so the shortcut r is about 4% low here (and the yearly multiplier about 1% low). Scaling every m_x up or down moves λ across 1 exactly where R0 crosses 1, so the sign rule in the one idea holds exactly; only the size of r is approximate.

### Census conventions

Other treatments census just before or just after the yearly breeding pulse, which changes where survival enters the formulas. Matrix models (Leslie matrices) make that choice explicit, and the exact λ is the dominant eigenvalue of such a matrix built with a consistent convention.

### Static tables when a population grows

Stationarity is the simplest requirement. Strictly, a population at a stable age distribution, growing at a known rate r, can also be corrected for that growth: its older age classes were born when the population was smaller.
