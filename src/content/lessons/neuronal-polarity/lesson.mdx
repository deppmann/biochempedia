  - name: "Hook decoration of microtubules"
    blurb: >-
      Permeabilized, unfixed neurons are bathed in extra tubulin, which adds curved sheets to the

      curve one way or the other depending on which way the microtubule points, so each
      microtubule's orientation can be scored.
  - name: "RNA interference knockdown"
    blurb: >-
      A short hairpin RNA that matches one messenger RNA is expressed in a neuron, and the cell
      destroys that messenger, lowering the protein. Starting the knockdown after a structure has
      formed separates what a protein does to build the structure from what it does to keep it.
  - name: "Axotomy in culture"
    blurb: >-
      A single axon is cut with a fine glass needle or a focused laser at a measured distance from
      the cell body. Which process grows out afterwards, and how fast, shows where axon identity
      is stored and whether it can be reassigned.

**In a dish, cut a neuron's axon close to the cell body, and one of its dendrites can grow out as a new axon. Cut it farther away, and the old axon regrows.**

A neuron takes in signals on its dendrites and sends them out along its axon, so the two kinds of process must stay different. The cut-axon result tells you that "axon" is not a permanent label stamped on a process when it first sprouts. It is a state the cell keeps, carried by the axon itself, and it can be handed to a different process if enough of the axon is lost. This lesson asks how axons are formed, and then a second question: once formed, how does an axon stay an axon?

## Special cell biology of the neuron: one cell, two kinds of process

Most neurons in a vertebrate brain have two kinds of process. **Dendrites** are usually several, short, branched and tapering; they receive input from other cells. The **axon** is usually single, of even width and often very long; it carries the neuron's output, the action potential, to other cells. **Polarity** is the word for a cell having two distinct ends or domains that differ in structure and molecules. Some neurons break the pattern. Anaxonic neurons, such as some amacrine cells of the retina, have no clear axon, and many sensory neurons have a single process that splits into two axon branches.

The difference is molecular as well as visible. Dendrites contain the microtubule-associated protein MAP2, receptors for neurotransmitters, and, in many neurons, spines where synapses form. The axon contains other proteins, such as the microtubule-associated protein tau, and its own channels and vesicle machinery. A neuron must sort thousands of proteins to the right side and keep them there for decades. That is what makes the cell biology special.

## Establishing polarity during development: a race among equal neurites

The clearest view of how a neuron polarizes comes from a dish. In 1988 Carlos Dotti, C. A. Sullivan and Gary Banker followed single neurons taken from the embryonic rat hippocampus and grown at low density. They described five stages.

1. **Lamellipodia.** Within hours of plating, the round cell spreads thin, flat veils of membrane.
2. **Minor neurites.** The veils condense into several short neurites, all alike.
3. **The axon.** After a day or two, one neurite starts to grow much faster than the rest. It becomes the axon.
4. **Dendrites.** Over the next few days the other neurites grow, taper and branch as dendrites.
5. **Maturation.** In the second week and later, dendritic spines form and synapses, first seen within days, multiply and mature. (Synapses get their own lesson later: [that lesson](/lessons/cns-synapses/).)

Notice the logic of stage 2. The neurites look equal, and Dotti and Banker had shown in 1987 that cutting the axon of a young neuron within a day of its appearance can let a different neurite become the axon. The choice behaves like a race.

A race with one winner needs two kinds of feedback. **Positive feedback** in the leading neurite makes it grow faster the more it grows. **Negative feedback** holds the others back. The players reported in cultured neurons are these:

- **PI3-kinase and PIP3.** PI3-kinase makes the lipid PIP3 in the membrane, which proteins with PH domains read as an address (see [that lesson](/lessons/pi3k-akt-insulin/) and [that lesson](/lessons/lipid-anchors-and-addresses/)). PIP3 builds up at the tip of the future axon, and blocking PI3-kinase is reported to stop any neurite from becoming an axon.
- **The Par3/Par6/aPKC complex.** The same polarity complex that marks the top of an epithelial cell gathers at the tip of the future axon.
- **Rho-family GTPases.** Cdc42 and Rac, molecular switches on when bound to GTP ([that lesson](/lessons/gtpase-switches/)), reorganize actin at the tip, while RhoA tends to oppose neurite growth ([that lesson](/lessons/integrins-and-rho-gtpases/)).
- **Actin instability and microtubule stabilization.** Frank Bradke and Dotti reported in 1999 that the growth cone of the future axon has a looser, more dynamic actin network. A growth cone is the motile tip of a growing neurite ([that lesson](/lessons/growth-cone/)). Loosened actin is proposed to let microtubules push in, and microtubules in the future axon are more stable than in its siblings. Locally loosening actin with cytochalasin D, or locally stabilizing microtubules with taxol, can make a chosen neurite the axon.

Part of the loop is thought to be physical: a neurite that grows faster draws more building material and more transport, and then grows faster still. How the losers are held back is less settled. Proposals include competition for a limited supply of material and a spreading inhibitory signal.

In tissue, cues from the environment help orient the same machinery.

One caution. These five stages describe dissociated neurons in culture, which have lost their neighbours and their history. In the brain many neurons arrive with polarity already set by their migration. In the developing cortex, for example, the trailing process of a migrating neuron often becomes its axon. The molecules overlap, but the starting point differs. One pathway is clearly required in the living cortex: the kinase LKB1 and the SAD-A and SAD-B kinases it activates. Cortical neurons lacking LKB1, or both SAD kinases, fail to form axons.

## Development of microtubule polarity: the tracks point different ways

A microtubule has a fast-growing plus end and a slower minus end. Motor proteins read that direction. Most kinesins walk toward plus ends; dynein walks toward minus ends. So the orientation of microtubules in a process limits which motors can carry cargo outward into it.

In 1988 Peter Baas and colleagues used hook decoration to score microtubule orientation in cultured rat hippocampal neurons. In the axon, nearly all microtubules point their plus ends away from the cell body. In dendrites the orientation is mixed: a large fraction point each way. In fly neurons, dendrites are instead mostly minus-end-out, so the details depend on the animal. In both, axon and dendrites offer different tracks.

The difference gives the neuron a sorting rule. Cargo carried by a kinesin that prefers stable plus-end-out microtubules can be steered into the axon. Cargo carried by dynein can travel outward only on minus-end-out tracks, which vertebrate dendrites have and the axon lacks. Hook decoration in 1989 found mixed tracks appearing only as dendrites matured, after the axon was chosen. Live imaging in 2016 found mixed tracks already in the young, equal neurites, with the uniform plus-end-out array forming as the axon forms. So this rule may help choose the axon as well as keep it.

## Setting up the axon initial segment: a gate that holds identity

The first few tens of micrometres of the axon form a specialized zone, the **axon initial segment (AIS)**. Under its membrane sits a dense coat built on the scaffold **ankyrin G**. Ankyrin G binds **beta-IV spectrin**, which links it to rings of actin under the membrane. Through its ANK repeats it binds voltage-gated **sodium channels**, **KCNQ2/3** potassium channels, and the adhesion molecules **neurofascin-186** and NrCAM. In many neurons, such as cortical pyramidal cells, Kv1 potassium channels gather there too.

The AIS has two jobs. The first is electrical. Its high density of sodium channels gives it a low threshold, so in many neurons it is where inputs summed from the dendrites and cell body start the action potential. The second is to hold polarity.

How does the AIS hold polarity? In 1999 Bettina Winckler, Paul Forscher and Ira Mellman dragged membrane proteins with a laser trap and found that they moved freely in the cell body and dendrites but were held back at the initial segment, and that the hold depended on actin. Several models of the barrier are proposed and may all contribute: a fence of anchored membrane proteins that slows the diffusion of lipids and proteins; an actin-based filter that blocks vesicles carrying dendritic cargo; and sorting by motors that read microtubule orientation and modifications. Which matters most is still argued.

**Ankyrin G is required to maintain neuronal polarity.** In 2008 Kristian Hedstrom, Yasuhiro Ogawa and Matthew Rasband lowered ankyrin G in cultured hippocampal neurons after the AIS had formed. The AIS fell apart, and MAP2, normally excluded, spread into the axon. Near the cell body the axon began to look like a dendrite, with dendritic spines, though farther out it kept axonal proteins. The axon was already chosen when the scaffold was removed, so the experiment tests maintenance, not choice: identity has to be actively held.

The cut-axon result in the opening points the same way. In a 2008 study from Frank Bradke's laboratory, with Susana Gomis-Rüth and Corette Wierenga, cutting the axon so that only a short stump remained let a dendrite become the axon, while longer stumps regrew. The authors linked the difference to stable microtubules in the distal axon and did not test the initial segment. Myelin and nodes of Ranvier, which repeat much of the AIS machinery along the axon, come in [that lesson](/lessons/myelination/).

### Design problem

You grow mouse hippocampal neurons until they are fully polarized. In one neuron you cut the axon so close to the cell body that only a short stump remains (the experiment used less than 35 micrometres; see Going deeper). In another you cut it much farther out, leaving a long stump. Predict what grows out in each case, and say what the pair of results tells you about where axon identity is stored.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with what each cut removes. The distant cut leaves the cell body, the dendrites and a long axon stump, with its stable microtubules, untouched. You expect the original axon to regrow from its stump, because nothing about its identity has been lost.

The close cut leaves only a short stump, with little of the axon's stable microtubules. Now no process has a clear axonal advantage, which is a little like stage 2, though the dendrites are mature. You expect a dendrite to take over and grow out as the axon. That is what Gomis-Rüth, Wierenga and Bradke reported.

The pair tells you that identity is not a permanent stamp on a process. Remove nearly all of the axon and the label can be reassigned; leave enough of it and the axon simply repairs itself. A follow-up test: lower ankyrin G first, then make the distant cut, and ask whether a dendrite now competes with the regrowing stump.

## Going deeper

### Length thresholds in the cut-axon experiments

Goslin and Banker later cut axons to different lengths and found that a process more than about 10 micrometres longer than the others became the axon. They proposed that uncut neurons choose the same way. In the 2008 study from Bradke's laboratory, a stump shorter than 35 micrometres let a dendrite become the axon, while longer stumps regrew. These thresholds belong to these experiments in cultured neurons; they are not field-wide rules.

### Is the AIS ancient?

The AIS may also be old. Comparative sequence work has been read as suggesting that the ankyrin-G binding motif on sodium channels arose in early chordates, and that the motif on KCNQ2/3 channels arose later, around the time myelin appeared, though that dating rests on which animals have been sequenced. The ankyrin scaffold may be older still: fly neurons have a diffusion barrier in the proximal axon that needs a giant ankyrin.
