techniques:
  - name: "Indicator dilution"
    blurb: >-
      Give a known amount of a marker, let it mix, and measure its concentration in plasma. The
      amount retained divided by the concentration gives the volume of the space the marker
      reaches: heavy water for total body water, inulin or bromide for extracellular fluid,
      Evans blue or labelled albumin for plasma.
  - name: "Radioisotope dilution of exchangeable cations"
    blurb: >-
      Inject sodium-24 or potassium-42, wait for it to exchange with the body's pool, and divide
      the retained dose by the plasma specific activity. The result is exchangeable sodium or
      potassium, the part of the body's store that mixes with the tracer in that time, which
      leaves out much of the sodium in bone.
  - name: "Freezing-point osmometry"
    blurb: >-
      Measure how far below 0 degrees C a sample freezes. Each osmole per kilogram of water
      lowers the freezing point by about 1.86 degrees C, so the reading measures osmolality
      directly instead of estimating it from three analytes.
  - name: "Bioelectrical impedance"
    blurb: >-
      Pass a small alternating current through the body and measure its resistance and
      reactance, from which fluid volumes are estimated. Because the reading depends on
      electrolyte content as well as volume, it can mislead after infusions of different fluids.

**In the textbook model of an adult man, retain one litre of solute-free water and about two-thirds of it ends up inside cells. Retain one litre of isotonic saline and, once it has spread, none of it does.**

Water crosses cell membranes freely and settles wherever the solute that cannot cross is. Claude Bernard's internal environment ([tissues and homeostasis](/lessons/tissues-and-homeostasis/)) is, in the reference man, about 14 litres. This lesson measures the compartments, counts their particles, and predicts how they change.

## Sixty percent is a convention, not a constant

Physiology texts use a **70-kg reference man** whose body is 60 percent water: **total body water (TBW)** of about 42 L, a convention for arithmetic, not a measurement of anyone. It divides like this:

- **Intracellular fluid (ICF)**, the water inside cells: about two-thirds of TBW, 28 L.
- **Extracellular fluid (ECF)**, all the water outside cells: about one-third of TBW, 14 L. About three-quarters of it is **interstitial fluid (ISF)**, the fluid that bathes cells (10.5 L), and one-quarter is **plasma**, the fluid part of blood (3.5 L).

These are fractions of water, not of body mass: two-thirds of 70 kg would be 47 L, more than all the body's water. **Transcellular fluid** (cerebrospinal, synovial and pleural fluid) is usually counted with the ECF. The 3.5 L of plasma is a convention; many measured adult plasma volumes are lower, so do not derive a blood volume from it (blood cells and hemostasis).

People differ, mostly because of fat: lean tissue is roughly 73 percent water, adipose tissue little. The water fraction is about 75 percent of body mass in infants by one textbook estimate and can fall to about 45 percent in old age. The Institute of Medicine's 2005 water report, citing earlier measurements, gives young-adult averages of about 59 percent for men and 50 percent for women, who carry more fat on average, with wide individual ranges. Treat 60 percent as the middle of a range.

## A volume is a dose divided by a concentration

To size a tank you cannot see into, add a known amount of dye, let it mix and measure its concentration. **Indicator dilution** does this in a person:

> **V = (amount given - amount lost) / (equilibrated concentration - baseline concentration)**

This assumes the marker mixes evenly through its space and no further, is not metabolized, and its urinary loss is measured. Each marker reports the space bounded by barriers it cannot cross. **Heavy water** (deuterium oxide) crosses capillary walls and cell membranes: TBW. **Inulin** or **bromide** crosses capillary walls but mostly not cell membranes: ECF. **Evans blue**, a dye that binds albumin, or labelled albumin stays mostly in the vessels: plasma. ICF and ISF have no marker of their own: ICF = TBW - ECF and ISF = ECF - plasma, and each subtraction inherits both errors.

These spaces are operational, not anatomical. Deuterium exchanges with hydrogen on proteins and other non-water molecules, so raw heavy-water space overestimates TBW, by about 4 percent in the usual correction. Albumin markers leak slowly out of plasma, so samples are extrapolated back to mixing time.

**A worked example, with invented numbers.** An adult man receives 1.50 g of inulin; 0.10 g is in his urine by the time of sampling, and equilibrated plasma inulin is 0.100 g per L (baseline zero). ECF = (1.50 - 0.10) / 0.100 = 14.0 L. He receives 14 mg of Evans blue, and plasma concentration extrapolated to mixing time is 4.0 mg per L: plasma = 14 / 4.0 = 3.5 L. His raw heavy-water space is 43.7 L; dividing by an exchange factor of 1.04 gives TBW = 42.0 L. So ICF = 42.0 - 14.0 = 28.0 L and ISF = 14.0 - 3.5 = 10.5 L. Forget the inulin in urine and ECF comes out 15.0 L.

## Osmolality counts particles; tonicity counts the ones that stay on one side

[Osmoregulation](/lessons/osmoregulation/) defined **osmolality**: osmoles of dissolved particles per kilogram of water. **Osmolarity** is osmoles per litre of solution; plasma is about 93 percent water, so the two differ slightly. Laboratories measure osmolality by **freezing-point depression**. Each osmole per kilogram lowers the freezing point about 1.86 degrees C, so plasma at 290 mOsm per kg freezes near -0.54 degrees C. Adult plasma osmolality is typically about 275 to 295 mOsm per kg.

A common estimate is

> **Estimated osmolality (mOsm per kg) = 2 x [Na+] + glucose / 18 + BUN / 2.8**

with sodium in mmol per L and glucose and **blood urea nitrogen (BUN)** in mg/dL. Doubling sodium counts its accompanying anions. Glucose / 18 converts mg/dL to mmol per L (molar mass 180). BUN counts only urea's nitrogen, 28 g per mol, hence 2.8; for whole urea in mg/dL divide by 6. In mmol per L the estimate is 2[Na+] + glucose + urea. For sodium 140 mmol per L, glucose 90 mg/dL and BUN 14 mg/dL: 280 + 5 + 5 = 290 mOsm per kg.

**Tonicity**, or effective osmolality, counts only solutes that cannot cross the membrane in question over the time considered, because only those hold water on one side. Urea crosses most cell membranes and equilibrates, so it raises osmolality without moving water out of cells. Estimated tonicity is 2[Na+] + glucose / 18, here 285 mOsm per kg. Glucose is counted because, when insulin action is low, it enters cells such as muscle slowly.

The **osmolal gap** is measured osmolality minus estimated osmolality; a wide gap points to an unmeasured solute, such as an alcohol (not the anion gap of acid-base balance).

## Water follows effective solute: what holds fluid in each compartment

Water crosses most cell membranes quickly ([membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/)), so at steady state ICF and ECF share one osmolality and each holds water in proportion to its effective osmoles. Picture two rooms joined by a door only water can pass: the guests stuck in each room decide how much water it holds.

Inside cells, the stuck guests are anions that cannot leave (proteins, nucleotides, organic phosphates) and the potassium that balances their charge. Outside, they are sodium with chloride and bicarbonate. Sodium leaks in, but the sodium-potassium pump moves it back out, so it behaves as if it could not enter: sodium salts are the ECF's effective solute.

Small solutes cross capillary walls freely and do not divide plasma from ISF. Plasma proteins, mainly albumin, cross slowly and exert a **colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure** that holds fluid in the vessels against capillary blood pressure. Starling's 1896 balance of these forces is in [blood and vessels](/lessons/blood-and-vessels/); the revised view, with the glycocalyx and slight net filtration in most tissues, is in capillary exchange and lymph.

If water follows effective solute, plasma sodium should report solute per water, not the body's sodium stock. Isidore Edelman and colleagues tested this in San Francisco (1958) in 98 chronically ill adults chosen for variety (heart, liver, kidney, lung, gut, neurological and endocrine disease; 66 had edema), on an analyzed low-sodium diet. In most they measured exchangeable sodium (sodium-24 dilution), exchangeable potassium (potassium-42), TBW (heavy water, six-hour equilibration) and sodium per litre of serum water.

The common reading predicted that low serum sodium means too little body sodium. Instead, serum sodium correlated poorly with exchangeable sodium, potassium or water per kilogram, or with sodium/water or potassium/water alone, and closely with the sum:

> **Serum-water [Na+] (mEq per L) = 1.11 x (Na_e + K_e) / TBW - 25.6, with r = 0.83**

That fit passive water distribution in proportion to osmotically active solute, with cell potassium counting about as much as extracellular sodium. It ruled out serum sodium as a measure of body sodium: edematous patients with excess body sodium could be hyponatremic. Limits: it is a correlation in sick patients, not an intervention; exchangeable cation is not total cation (much bone sodium does not exchange); and the line is for serum-water sodium.

## Predicting the shifts: infusions, losses and hyponatremia

### Worked problem: where does the litre go?

These numbers are made up. A 70-kg reference man has ICF 28 L and ECF 14 L, both at an effective osmolality of 300 mOsm per kg (rounded; measured adult values are about 275 to 295). Assume a closed system (no urine, sweat or breath losses), free water movement across cell membranes, no solute gain or loss by cells, added sodium chloride staying outside cells, ideal behaviour, and 1 kg of water = 1 L. In separate trials he retains 1 L of (A) isotonic saline, 300 mOsm; (B) solute-free water, the model for 5% dextrose after its glucose is metabolized; or (C) sodium chloride at 900 mOsm per L (a model value).

1. *Count osmoles.* ICF = 28 x 300 = 8,400 mOsm; ECF = 14 x 300 = 4,200 mOsm. Total water after each trial is 43 L.
2. *Final osmolality.* C = (12,600 + added osmoles) / 43; then ICF = 8,400 / C and ECF = 43 - ICF.
3. *Trial A.* C = 12,900 / 43 = 300 mOsm per kg. ICF stays 28.0 L; ECF becomes 15.0 L. With a 3:1 ISF-to-plasma split, plasma gains 0.25 L and ISF 0.75 L.
4. *Trial B.* C = 12,600 / 43 = 293.0 mOsm per kg. ICF = 28.67 L (+0.67 L, two-thirds of the litre); ECF = 14.33 L (+0.33 L, of which plasma gains only about 0.08 L). Plasma sodium falls about in proportion: 140 x 42/43 = 136.7 mmol per L.
5. *Trial C.* C = 13,500 / 43 = 313.95 mOsm per kg. ICF = 8,400 / 313.95 = 26.76 L (-1.24 L); ECF = 5,100 / 313.95 = 16.24 L (+2.24 L, more than the litre given). Plasma sodium follows sodium balance, (140 x 14 + 450 added) / 16.24, to about 148.4 mmol per L; scaling by osmolality would give 146.5, but the added salt raises sodium's share of the particles. Splitting this gain between plasma and ISF needs an added assumption.
6. *Check.* ICF + ECF = 43.00 L each time, and volume x C returns the osmoles (26.756 x 313.95 = 8,400).

Equilibrium arithmetic predicts where a retained load goes, not how long it stays. Dileep Lobo and colleagues in Nottingham (2001) gave ten healthy men 2 L of 0.9% saline or 2 L of 5% dextrose over one hour on separate occasions, in a double-blind randomized crossover. The static model expects dextrose water to spread mostly into cells and saline to stay outside them. The kidneys added a time course: all the dextrose water had been excreted by two hours after the infusion ended, but only about one-third of the saline's sodium and water by six hours. Serum albumin fell by about 20 percent after saline and 16 percent after dextrose, in both cases more than dilution alone explained, and bioimpedance seemed to track the infusate's electrolyte content more than its volume. It did not measure compartments, and ten healthy men say little about women, older adults or illness.

In **hypotonic hyponatremia from water retention** (water, sodium and potassium covers the causes), let the reference man retain 3 L of solute-free water. C = 12,600 / 45 = 280 mOsm per kg; ICF = 8,400 / 280 = 30.0 L (+2.0 L, cells swell about 7 percent); ECF = 4,200 / 280 = 15.0 L (+1.0 L); plasma sodium falls to about 140 x 42/45 = 130.7 mmol per L. Body sodium did not fall; the ratio of cation to water did, as Edelman's line predicts. Brain cells swell inside a rigid skull, so acute severe hyponatremia can cause dangerous cerebral edema. Over hours to days they lose potassium and chloride, then organic osmolytes more slowly, which limits swelling; that is why a fast rise in plasma sodium can then shrink the adapted cells.

Low sodium does not always mean swollen cells. With sodium 130 mmol per L, glucose 630 mg/dL and BUN 14 mg/dL, osmolality is 260 + 35 + 5 = 300 and tonicity 295 mOsm per kg: glucose has drawn water out of cells. Establish tonicity first.

**Acute hemorrhage** removes isotonic whole blood, so plasma sodium does not change at first and there is no net water shift across cell membranes. Over hours, as capillary pressure falls, ISF moves into plasma, so hematocrit falls later. **Pure water loss** shrinks both compartments, about two-thirds of the deficit coming from cells.

### Design problem

You want to test, in healthy adult volunteers, whether 1 L of isotonic saline infused over 30 minutes ends up one-quarter in plasma and three-quarters in ISF, as the equilibrium model predicts, and how fast the kidney removes it. Design the measurements and say what each possible result would mean.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use a crossover: each volunteer has an infusion session and a control session (no infusion or a tiny volume) in random order. Measure plasma volume with a plasma marker, such as labelled albumin or indocyanine green, before and at several times after the infusion, and ECF with bromide. Hemoglobin dilution also works if red-cell mass is assumed constant. Collect all urine for volume and sodium, so that retained = infused - excreted.

Predict first. At equilibrium with no excretion, plasma rises by about 0.25 L, from 3.5 to 3.75 L (a 7 percent albumin dilution if albumin stays in plasma), and ISF by about 0.75 L. A plasma gain near one-quarter of the retained volume supports the model; a larger gain that falls slowly means distribution is incomplete.

Do not rely on albumin alone. For Lobo's 2 L, with nothing excreted, the model predicts a 12.5 percent fall (3.5/4.0); he saw about 20 percent, which could mean the load had not yet left the plasma or that albumin itself left.

Limits: marker spaces are operational, markers leak, excretion overlaps distribution, and young healthy adults are not patients.
