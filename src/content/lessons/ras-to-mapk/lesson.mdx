techniques:
  - name: "DNA transfection and the focus-formation assay"
    blurb: >-
      DNA from tumour cells is precipitated with calcium phosphate and added to a flat, single
      layer of mouse NIH 3T3 fibroblasts. Cells that take up a transforming gene stop respecting
      their neighbours and pile into foci that can be counted. The assay shows that a piece of
      DNA is enough to change how a cell grows, with the caution that NIH 3T3 cells are already
      part of the way to transformed.
  - name: "Activity-guided protein purification"
    blurb: >-
      A cell extract is passed over a series of chromatography columns, and every fraction is
      tested for one activity, here the ability to phosphorylate a test protein or to switch on
      ERK. Following the activity until a single band remains ties a behaviour to one protein
      that can then be sequenced and cloned.
  - name: "Always-on and interfering mutants"
    blurb: >-
      A kinase is mutated so that it stays active with no signal from above, or so that it blocks
      the normal enzyme. For MEK the active form was made by replacing the serines that Raf
      phosphorylates with acidic residues that imitate phosphate. The active form tests whether a
      step is sufficient for an outcome, and the interfering form tests whether it is necessary.
  - name: "Time course of ERK activity"
    blurb: >-
      Cells are stimulated and sampled at intervals, and each sample is tested for ERK activity
      or for ERK carrying both activating phosphates. The result is a curve of activity against
      time, which is how a brief response is told apart from a sustained one.

    rationale: >-
      SOS is a guanine nucleotide exchange factor. It loosens the grip of Ras on GDP, and GTP,
      which is more plentiful in the cell, then binds. Speeding the cutting of GTP to GDP is the
      job of a GAP and turns Ras off. No enzyme adds a phosphate to the GDP sitting on Ras; the
      nucleotide is swapped whole. The lipid tail is attached by other enzymes when Ras is made
      and has nothing to do with SOS.

    choices:

      - "It binds GTP and is switched off by hydrolysis"
      - "It phosphorylates a threonine and a tyrosine on ERK"
      - "It removes a threonine and a tyrosine phosphate from ERK"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      MEK is a dual-specificity kinase. It puts a phosphate on a threonine and on a tyrosine, one
      residue apart, in the activation loop of ERK. Phosphorylating two serines in one loop
      describes what Raf does to MEK, with the direction reversed. Binding GTP and switching off
      by hydrolysis describes Ras. Removing the two phosphates from ERK is the work of a
      dual-specificity phosphatase, which ends the signal.
  - stem: "What does Ras-GTP do to Raf?"
    choices:

      - "It brings Raf to the membrane, where Raf opens and pairs"
      - "It passes its GTP to Raf, which uses it as a phosphate donor"

    rationale: >-

**In PC12 cells, a line grown from a rat adrenal tumour, epidermal growth factor (EGF) makes the cell divide, while nerve growth factor (NGF) makes it stop dividing and grow long, nerve-like fibres—both through Ras, Raf, MEK and ERK.** Stuck on, this same relay drives many cancers, and mildly over-active it causes the RASopathy syndromes. The first film traced the relay from surface to nucleus; below are the physical mechanisms, the proof behind them, and the worked cases.

<Support>**The one idea.** A three-kinase relay can amplify, sharpen, and control a membrane signal; duration, cell context, and pathway state shape what the cascade does. **Scope:** This rule describes the classical RTK–Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK cascade. How steep the two-phosphate switch is inside crowded living cells, and how widely cells other than PC12 read ERK by duration, remain open questions; Ras-GTP also recruits other effectors such as PI 3-kinase.</Support>

## Ras-GTP catches Raf at the membrane

The opening steps review three earlier lessons. An active receptor tyrosine kinase carries phosphotyrosines on its tail. The adaptor **Grb2** binds one with its SH2 domain and holds **SOS** with its SH3 domains, bringing SOS to the membrane where **Ras** sits on its lipid anchors (see [adaptors and scaffolds](/lessons/adaptors-and-scaffolds/) and [lipid anchors and addresses](/lessons/lipid-anchors-and-addresses/)). SOS is a **guanine nucleotide exchange factor** (GEF): it makes Ras release GDP so plentiful GTP takes its place. Ras stays on until it cuts that GTP to GDP ([GTPase switches](/lessons/gtpase-switches/)).

With GTP in its pocket, two surface loops on Ras—**switch I** and **switch II**—snap taut into a shape effectors can grip; with GDP they go slack. Here Ras-GTP catches **Raf**, a serine/threonine kinase.

In a resting cell, Raf floats in the cytosol folded shut, its front half (carrying a **Ras-binding domain**) held against its kinase domain. Ras is not a kinase; it acts by contact. Moving Raf to the membrane is well established, but binding alone does not open Raf: membrane lipids and further steps help release the closed shape. Two Raf molecules then pair side to side, kinase domain beside kinase domain, which normal activation requires. Phosphates are added to and removed from Raf along the way, and their exact order is still being worked out.

**Predict.** A mutant Ras lacks its lipid anchor and floats in the cytosol, yet still binds GTP and catches Raf. Will it activate Raf and MEK?

## Three kinases in series, and the two-phosphate lock

Paired Raf phosphorylates **MEK** on two serines in its **activation loop**, the flexible stretch beside the active site ([the phosphorylation switch](/lessons/phosphorylation-switch/)). Unlike most kinases (which target Ser/Thr or Tyr alone), MEK is a **dual-specificity kinase**: it phosphorylates **ERK** (extracellular signal-regulated kinase) on both a threonine and a tyrosine, one residue apart, in ERK's activation loop. ERK needs both phosphates; strip either one off with a phosphatase and ERK goes quiet.

**Predict.** When Natalie Ahn fractionated EGF-treated cell extracts on chromatography columns, ERK stayed inactive with ATP alone until a different fraction was added back. Why did that rule out ERK activating itself?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

An activator that parts from ERK on a column is a separate protein. Purifying that activator (MEK) with Rony Seger showed it phosphorylates ERK on both threonine and tyrosine (see Going deeper).

</details>

What do three kinases in a row give the cell? [Signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/) covered two answers—each tier can amplify, and the cascade can turn a graded input into an all-or-none output—and a third is **control**, giving phosphatases, feedback loops, and scaffolds separate points to act.

ERK's two-phosphate lock is one ingredient of that sharpness. Suppose MEK fills ERK's threonine and tyrosine sites independently in a test tube, and only doubly phosphorylated ERK is active. With illustrative round numbers, compare 10% occupancy per site at low MEK with 50% per site at higher MEK (a 5-fold rise per site):

- At 10% per site, doubly phosphorylated ERK is 0.10 × 0.10 = 0.01 (1%).
- At 50% per site, doubly phosphorylated ERK is 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25 (25%).

**Predict.** Suppose you want half of all ERK doubly phosphorylated. With the two sites filled independently, how full must each site be?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

About 71% per site, because 0.71 × 0.71 ≈ 0.50. The square cuts both ways: it keeps doubly phosphorylated ERK very low while occupancy is low, and it means each site must be well filled before much ERK switches on. That is what makes the output steeper than the input—though crowding in living cells may change it (see Going deeper).

</details>

MEK has almost no known substrates besides ERK, making the middle of the chain a **narrow pipe**. ERK is a **wide fan**: it phosphorylates serines and threonines followed by a proline on many cytoplasmic proteins, and part of active ERK enters the nucleus to phosphorylate transcription factors such as **Elk-1**. Elk-1 helps switch on **c-fos**, the best-known **immediate early gene**—a gene turned on within minutes with no new protein made first, because the proteins it needs are already present; its product is often a transcription factor driving later genes.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/ras-to-mapk/narrow-pipe-wide-fan.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a schematic Raf–MEK–ERK relay narrows at MEK, whose known substrates are almost exclusively ERK. Active ERK branches to many cytoplasmic targets and into the nucleus, where it phosphorylates Elk-1, which helps switch on the immediate early gene c-fos." caption="The cascade narrows to a single pipe at MEK and fans out at ERK to cytoplasmic proteins and nuclear targets such as Elk-1 and c-fos." />

Do not memorize every downstream target; learn the logic of a narrow relay ending in a wide fan:

> **growth factor → receptor → Grb2 and SOS → Ras-GTP → Raf → MEK → ERK → transcription factors → immediate early genes**

## One three-tier plan, kept apart by scaffolds

Raf, MEK and ERK are one example of a general **MAP kinase (MAPK)** plan: a MAP kinase kinase kinase activates a MAP kinase kinase, which phosphorylates a MAP kinase on a threonine and a tyrosine in its activation loop. Animal cells run at least two more modules built this way, ending in **JNK** and **p38**; they use their own kinases (some shared between JNK and p38) and respond mainly to stress such as ultraviolet light or inflammatory signals.

Look-alike cascades in one cytoplasm create a wiring problem, solved in part by **scaffolds**. A scaffold holds one module's kinases side by side so they signal mainly to each other—as **Ste5** does in yeast and **KSR** does for Raf, MEK and ERK ([adaptors and scaffolds](/lessons/adaptors-and-scaffolds/)). Scaffolds also localize the cascade: **paxillin** gathers Raf, MEK and ERK at focal adhesions (where a cell grips its surroundings), and **MP1** holds MEK and ERK on endosomes.

Wiring one module cleanly still leaves the bigger puzzle: how that same chain drives opposite cell fates and opposite drug responses. The second film explores how timing, cell identity, and pathway state alter what the cascade instructs.

## How long the signal stays on

The second film contrasted brief EGF signalling with sustained NGF signalling in PC12 cells; here is how ERK's two feedback arms split in speed, and where the evidence's limits lie.

Negative feedback works alongside receptor persistence to set duration, and ERK supplies two arms:
- **Fast feedback:** active ERK phosphorylates upstream proteins, including SOS and Raf, in ways that weaken them.
- **Slow feedback:** active ERK turns on genes for **dual-specificity phosphatases** that strip both phosphates back off ERK, taking longer because new protein must be made.

**Predict.** Treat PC12 cells with a drug that blocks new protein synthesis, then add EGF. Which feedback arm still acts right away, and which fails?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Phosphorylation of SOS and Raf still acts right away because those proteins are already present; induction of dual-specificity phosphatases fails because it requires new protein synthesis.

</details>

How long the window stays open depends on stimulus, cell type, dose, and readout. In PC12 cells, ERK activity after EGF rises and falls within about an hour, whereas after NGF it climbs to a plateau lasting hours. In 1992 Christopher Marshall and Philip Cohen's group in Dundee proposed that duration itself is the instruction—brief ERK for division, sustained ERK for differentiation—an idea Marshall expanded in a 1995 review. The rule comes from this one cell line, and how widely cells read kinases by the clock remains open.

## When the cascade is stuck on: context, cancer, and drugs

Remember the second half of the guiding rule: **duration, cell context, and pathway state shape what the cascade does.** In 1994 Sally Cowley, Christopher Marshall and colleagues put permanently active MEK1 into two cell types: PC12 cells grew neurites with no NGF, while mouse NIH 3T3 fibroblasts piled into **foci**, disordered heaps marking transformation toward cancer. Unending ERK means "differentiate" to a PC12 cell and "keep dividing" to an NIH 3T3 fibroblast.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/ras-to-mapk/same-mek-two-answers.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two panels compare the effects of the same always-on MEK1. A PC12 cell grows neurites without NGF on the left. NIH 3T3 mouse fibroblasts form a piled-up focus on the right." caption="The same permanently active MEK1 instructs a PC12 cell to grow neurites without NGF and mouse fibroblasts to pile into transformed foci." />

Human cancers reach that state from higher up. In 1982 Robert Weinberg, Mariano Barbacid and Michael Wigler each compared a *ras* gene from one bladder-tumour cell line with the normal gene and found a single base change turning glycine 12 to valine—**G12V**, which blunts the GTP timer so Ras stays on ([GTPase switches](/lessons/gtpase-switches/)). Mutant *RAS* genes are common in cancers and vary widely by tissue: a 1989 survey found them in roughly nine of ten pancreatic tumours and about half of colorectal cancers (see Going deeper for updated overall figures).

**Predict.** A tumour carries Ras G12V. Will an EGF-receptor inhibitor shut down ERK in these cells? What about a MEK inhibitor?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

An EGF-receptor inhibitor fails because Ras G12V stays stuck on independently of the receptor and SOS above it. A MEK inhibitor acts below Ras and Raf, shutting down ERK.

</details>

One tier down, roughly half of cutaneous melanomas (the common skin form) carry a mutation in **B-Raf**, one of three human Raf kinases, most often **V600E**. Glutamate in the activation loop makes B-Raf active without Ras-GTP; it is often described as signalling without a partner, though mutant B-Raf can pair and whether it truly works alone is debated.

**Vemurafenib** fills the ATP pocket of B-Raf V600E, while **trametinib** binds MEK in an allosteric pocket beside its ATP site to hold MEK inactive. As the second film showed, membrane Raf pairing explains why pathway state flips vemurafenib's effect:

| Pathway state | Raf ATP-pocket drug (vemurafenib) | MEK allosteric drug (trametinib) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **B-Raf V600E + normal Ras** | Blocks mutant B-Raf → ERK **falls** | Holds MEK inactive → ERK **falls** |
| **Active mutant Ras + normal B-Raf** | Drug-bound Raf activates drug-free partner in pairs → ERK **rises** | Holds MEK inactive below Raf → ERK **falls** |

Blocking MEK cuts off signalling below Raf pairs, one reason Raf and MEK inhibitors are commonly combined.

The **RASopathies** are developmental syndromes from mutations, inherited or new at conception, that mildly over-activate this pathway; affected children share features of the face, heart and growth. Carried in every cell, the mutation still has tissue-specific effects, and rarer mosaic forms arise later in only some cells. They include Noonan syndrome, Costello syndrome, cardio-facio-cutaneous syndrome and neurofibromatosis type 1—where losing the Ras GAP **neurofibromin** removes Ras's off-timer (see Going deeper for genes).

Raf is not Ras-GTP's only target; PI 3-kinase is another, and it is the next lesson.

### Design problem

In PC12 cells, the same Ras-to-ERK cascade answers EGF with division and NGF with an end to division and neurite growth. Design a two-part circuit that both **sets** signal duration and **reads** it so only sustained ERK triggers differentiation genes, then explain how Cowley and Marshall's matched pair of MEK1 mutants (one always-on, one interfering) tests sufficiency and necessity.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Build two parts:

1. **Duration setter.** Let ERK's negative feedback shut EGF off within an hour while NGF resists shutoff—for example via longer receptor activity or positive feedback on Raf.
2. **Delayed persistence reader.** Use a gene product made after a delay that survives only if ERK is still active. In fibroblasts, the immediate early protein **c-Fos** is short-lived unless ERK and RSK (a kinase ERK activates) phosphorylate it, so only sustained ERK stabilizes c-Fos long enough to turn on later genes.

**Sufficiency and necessity:** Always-on MEK1 mimics a sustained NGF signal without NGF or its other pathways, and drove neurite growth alone (showing MEK is **sufficient**). An interfering MEK1 that could not be activated blocked growth-factor-driven differentiation (showing MEK is **necessary**—nothing else stood in for it).

One caution: the duration rule comes from this one cell line, and how widely cells read kinases by the clock remains open.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How the activator of ERK was found

Melanie Cobb's laboratory followed an insulin-stimulated kinase activity from rat cells through eight chromatography columns—more than 6,300-fold purified as one 43-kilodalton band—cloned its gene, and named it ERK. Its sequence resembled two yeast mating-pheromone kinases, KSS1 and FUS3. Natalie Ahn, in Edwin Krebs's laboratory, then isolated two activities from EGF-treated fibroblasts in 1991 that switched on inactive ERK with ATP; purifying the activator with Rony Seger showed its dual specificity for threonine and tyrosine. Several laboratories reached MEK at about the same time.

### Does the two-phosphate lock stay steep in living cells?

The worked calculation assumes MEK phosphorylates ERK in two separate encounters, releasing singly phosphorylated ERK in between, as in dilute test tubes. Inside cells, scaffolds and crowding can change that: in HeLa cells, crowding appears to make phosphorylation nearly one step, lowering steepness, and how widely that holds is open.

### How common are RAS mutations?

Beyond the 1989 tissue figures above, reviews have long quoted up to about 30% for all human tumours. A 2020 analysis cross-checking large public cancer-mutation databases put it nearer 19% of patients, varying widely by tissue.

### Genes behind the RASopathies

Most of the genes are this lesson's cast: *PTPN11* (the phosphatase SHP2) in about half of Noonan syndrome cases and *SOS1* and *RAF1* in some others, *HRAS* in Costello syndrome, *BRAF* and the *MEK* genes in cardio-facio-cutaneous syndrome, and loss of the Ras GAP neurofibromin in neurofibromatosis type 1.
