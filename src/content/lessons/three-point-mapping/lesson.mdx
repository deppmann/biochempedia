**Two crossovers on one chromatid between the same pair of genes cancel out. If you score only those two genes, an offspring that inherits that chromatid looks exactly like one with no crossover.**

In [linkage and mapping](/lessons/linkage-and-mapping/) you measured the distance between two genes by counting recombinant offspring. That count has a blind spot. A **crossover** is an exchange of segments between two non-sister chromatids in meiosis (see [chromosomes and meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/)). One crossover between two genes recombines them. A second crossover between the same two genes, on the same chromatid, puts them back together. The gamete has been rearranged twice and looks parental. This lesson shows how a third gene, placed between the other two, makes those hidden events visible, and what the counts then tell you about order, distance and the spacing of crossovers.

## Why add a third gene: the middle marker catches the doubles

A **locus** is the place a gene occupies on a chromosome, and the versions of a gene are its **alleles**. A **test cross** mates an individual of unknown genotype with one homozygous recessive at the loci being followed. The tester's gametes carry only recessive alleles, so every offspring's phenotype shows directly which alleles came in the gamete from the other parent. A test cross is a way of reading gametes.

Follow three linked loci in one test cross and each gamete can carry one of eight allele combinations. Those combinations fall into four pairs:

- **Parentals**: no crossover detected between the three loci. Two classes, the largest.
- **Single crossovers in the first interval**: two classes.
- **Single crossovers in the second interval**: two classes.
- **Double crossovers**: one crossover in each interval. Two classes, usually the smallest.

The analogy is a sentence copied from two drafts, switching drafts at random. Check only the first and last words and you see a switch only when there was an odd number of them. Check a word in the middle as well and you catch the pair. The middle marker does that for chromosomes.

Why are doubles the rarest? If crossovers in the two intervals happened independently, the chance of both would be the product of the two chances, by the product rule from [probability and crosses](/lessons/probability-and-crosses/). The product of two small fractions is a smaller fraction.

## Reading the eight classes: find the middle gene first

**Worked problem.** A female heterozygous for three recessive mutations, p, q and r, carries p q r on one chromosome and + + + on the other (+ is the normal allele). She is test-crossed to a p q r / p q r male. Assume that every class survives equally well, that phenotypes are scored without error, and that her phase (which alleles sit together on each chromosome) is known. Here are the 1,000 progeny, written with the genes in alphabetical order, not map order:

- p q r: 350
- + + +: 342
- + q +: 53
- p + r: 55
- p q +: 96
- + + r: 92
- + q r: 7
- p + +: 5

Check the total first: 350 + 342 + 53 + 55 + 96 + 92 + 7 + 5 = 1,000.

**Step 1: the parentals.** The two largest classes are p q r and + + +. They match the mother's two chromosomes, as they should.

**Step 2: the doubles and the middle gene.** The two smallest classes are + q r and p + +. Match each with the parental it most resembles. + q r against p q r: only p differs. p + + against + + +: only p differs. A double crossover swaps only the gene that lies between the two crossovers, so p is the middle gene. The order is q, p, r.

Notice what did not decide it. The middle of the written name is q, and q is an outer gene. The order on the page is a listing convention; the doubles tell you the order on the chromosome.

**Step 3: rewrite in map order.** Put every class in the order q p r:

- Parentals: q p r (350) and + + + (342)
- Singles between q and p: q + + (53) and + p r (55)
- Singles between p and r: q p + (96) and + + r (92)
- Doubles: q + r (7) and + p + (5)

In q + +, the chromosome starts as the q p r parental and switches to the + + + one between q and p. In q p +, the switch comes between p and r. Every class now has one clear reading.

A caution. Rarest usually means double, but a class can also be small because those offspring survive poorly. Sturtevant reported reduced viability for one of his wing mutations in 1913. Check the doubles against the parentals, as in Step 2, before you trust rarity alone.

## Distances, coincidence and interference

**Step 4: distances.** A double crossover is a crossover in each interval, so it counts in both:

> distance (cM) = 100 x (single crossovers in the interval + double crossovers) / total

- q to p: (53 + 55 + 7 + 5) / 1,000 = 120 / 1,000 = 12.0 cM
- p to r: (96 + 92 + 7 + 5) / 1,000 = 200 / 1,000 = 20.0 cM
- q to r: 12.0 + 20.0 = 32.0 cM

Now score only q and r, as a two-point cross would. The doubles q + r and + p + carry the parental q-r combinations, so they drop out: (53 + 55 + 96 + 92) / 1,000 = 296 / 1,000 = 29.6%. The two-point estimate is short by 2.4 points, which is the 12 doubles counted twice (24 / 1,000). Do not leave the doubles out of the interval counts.

Even 32.0 cM is a lower bound. Two crossovers inside the q-p interval, with no marker between them, still escape detection. That is why Sturtevant preferred to build maps from short adjacent intervals.

**Step 5: are doubles as common as chance predicts?** If the intervals behaved independently:

> expected doubles = (interval 1 fraction) x (interval 2 fraction) x total = 0.12 x 0.20 x 1,000 = 24

Twelve were observed. The **coefficient of coincidence** is observed doubles over expected doubles, and **interference** is one minus that:

> coincidence = 12 / 24 = 0.50
> interference = 1 - 0.50 = 0.50

An interference of 0.5 means half the expected doubles are missing. This is **positive interference**: a crossover in one interval makes a crossover in the neighbouring interval less likely. Sturtevant showed in 1913 that doubles were scarcer than chance predicts, and in 1915 he named the effect interference, crediting Hermann Muller with suggesting the name. Interference tends to be strongest for short neighbouring intervals; for intervals far apart, coincidence approaches 1. Interference of 1 means no doubles at all. Interference can also be negative, with more doubles than expected, and that has been reported in some fungal and phage crosses, so do not call a coincidence above 1 impossible.

Interference is a property of a pair of intervals in a given organism, not a constant. In the plant Arabidopsis, one 2018 study measured an interference of 0.64 for one pair of intervals, which fell to 0.34 when the dosage of a meiotic protein called HEI10 was raised. And in a 2016 sequencing study of Drosophila female meioses, noncrossover gene conversions, which exchange a short patch without exchanging the flanking DNA, did not show interference at all.

### Design problem

In another three-point test cross of 500 progeny, the two intervals are 6 cM and 10 cM, and no double-crossover progeny appear. A classmate concludes that interference here is complete. Is that justified? What would you do to decide?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with the expectation if the intervals were independent: 0.06 x 0.10 x 500 = 3 doubles.

Three is a small number, and small counts scatter. Treat the number of doubles as a Poisson count with mean 3, a good approximation for a rare event in many trials. The chance of seeing none is

> P(0) = e^-3 = about 0.05

So even with no interference at all, about one cross in twenty this size would show zero doubles. (The exact binomial, (1 - 0.006)^500, gives 0.049.) Zero is suggestive but is not strong evidence of complete interference, and it cannot distinguish complete interference from, say, an interference of 0.5, which predicts 1.5 doubles.

To decide, score more progeny. With 5,000 progeny you expect 30 doubles under independence, and the chance of zero becomes e^-30, about 10^-13. If you still see none, interference is close to complete over these intervals. If you see about 15, interference is about 0.5.

Before blaming interference, check that the double-crossover classes survive and can be scored, since a missing class can be a viability problem. And remember why interference is plausible here: in organisms whose crossovers show interference, it is strongest over short adjacent intervals like these. Over widely separated intervals the coincidence would be near 1.

</details>

## Maps versus sequence: where crossovers really fall

A centimorgan is a frequency: 1 cM means that, for a short interval, about 1 gamete in 100 is recombinant. It is not a length. Sturtevant said in 1913 that map spacing need not reflect physical spacing, and molecular maps have shown how far apart the two can be.

In humans the genome-wide average is close to 1 cM per million base pairs. That is a rough average. In mice and humans most crossovers fall in **hotspots**, short stretches of DNA where recombination is far more frequent than in the DNA around them. In 2010 three groups linked hotspot position to the gene *PRDM9*. Its protein binds particular DNA sequences through a row of zinc fingers and places a methyl mark on histone H3 nearby; breaks made by the enzyme Spo11 near such sites start recombination (see [DNA repair and recombination](/lessons/dna-repair-and-recombination/)). Change the zinc fingers and the hotspots move. This holds for humans and mice. It does not hold for all animals: dogs lack a working PRDM9, and birds appear to lack the gene, and their hotspots tend to lie near promoter-like features such as transcription start sites and CpG islands, as they do in budding yeast, which has far more recombination per kilobase than humans.

The map also depends on who makes the gametes. The human female map is longer than the male map, by about 1.6 to 1.7 times over the genome as a whole. Males of *Drosophila melanogaster* go further: they normally have no meiotic crossing over, although males of some other species, such as *D. ananassae*, do. That is why *D. melanogaster* mapping crosses put the triple heterozygote in the female, as in the fly question at the end of this lesson. Near centromeres, crossovers are suppressed in many organisms, including Drosophila and Arabidopsis. And in *D. melanogaster* females, crossover rates varied strongly along the chromosomes and between strains.

So a genetic map and a DNA sequence agree on order and disagree on spacing. Use the map to predict how often genes travel together. Use the sequence to say how much DNA lies between them.
