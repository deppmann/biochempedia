techniques:
  - name: "Three-point test cross"
    blurb: >-
      An individual heterozygous at three linked loci is crossed to a tester homozygous recessive
      at all three. The tester adds only recessive alleles, so each offspring's phenotype reads
      out one gamete of the heterozygote, and the eight classes can be counted directly.
  - name: "Cytological markers on chromosomes"
    blurb: >-
      A chromosome is made visibly different from its partner, for example by a knob at one end
      or a piece exchanged with another chromosome. Scoring both the visible chromosome and
      linked genes in the progeny tests whether genetic crossing over goes with a physical
      exchange of chromosome parts.
  - name: "Dense SNP genotyping of progeny"
    blurb: >-
      Single-nucleotide differences between the two parental chromosomes are read in many
      offspring, or in the genomes of single offspring. A lasting switch from one parent's sequence to the other's marks a crossover to within the spacing of the markers, which places crossovers on the DNA sequence itself. A short switch that returns to the first parent marks a gene conversion instead.

    choices:

      - "The two smallest classes, which differ from a parental only at the middle gene"
      - "The two classes in which the gene written first has switched"
      - "The two classes that together make up about half the progeny"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A double crossover needs a crossover in both intervals at once, so it is the rarest event,
      and compared with its matching parental, only the middle gene differs. The largest classes are the parentals, made mostly with no crossover. The gene written first is only first in the listing,
      which need not be map order. Two classes making up about half the progeny are large classes, not the rare products of two crossovers; with unlinked genes, any two of the eight classes would make up about a quarter. Rarity is a clue, not proof: check that
      the rare pair is reciprocal and that every class survives and is scored.
  - stem: "A test cross gives parental classes A B C and a b c. The two rarest classes are a B C and A b c. Which gene lies in the middle?"
    choices:
      - "A, the only gene that differs between doubles and parentals"
      - "B, the gene written in the middle of every class name"
      - "C, the gene whose alleles never switch in any class"
      - "None, since three genes cannot be ordered from one cross"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Match a B C with the parental a b c: B and C differ, A does not; so match it instead with A
      B C, and only A differs. The same holds for A b c against a b c. The gene that switches
      between doubles and parentals is the middle one, so the order is B A C. B is in the middle
      only of the written name, which is not map order. C does switch, in the single crossovers
      of its interval. One three-point cross is enough to give the order.
  - stem: "Two adjacent intervals are 10 cM and 15 cM long. If crossovers in the two intervals are independent, how many double crossovers do you expect among 2,000 test-cross progeny?"
    choices:
      - "15 progeny"
      - "300 progeny"
      - "30 progeny"
      - "500 progeny"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Independent events multiply: 0.10 x 0.15 = 0.015, and 0.015 x 2,000 = 30. Fifteen is the
      same product applied to 1,000 progeny instead of 2,000. Three hundred uses only the 15 cM

  - stem: "A three-point cross predicts 30 double crossovers if the intervals are independent, and 6 are observed. What are the coefficient of coincidence and the interference?"
    choices:
      - "Coincidence 0.8 and interference 0.2; doubles are in excess"
      - "Coincidence 5.0 and interference -4.0; doubles are in excess"
      - "Coincidence 0.2 and interference 0.8; doubles are in excess"
      - "Coincidence 0.2 and interference 0.8; doubles are too few"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Coincidence is observed over expected, 6/30 = 0.2, and interference is 1 - 0.2 = 0.8.
      Fewer doubles than expected is positive interference. Swapping the two values gives
      coincidence 0.8, which confuses the two terms. Coincidence 5.0 divides expected by
      observed. Coincidence 0.2 with doubles in excess gets the numbers right and the meaning
      backwards: a coincidence below 1 means too few doubles.
  - stem: "In a human genetic map, two pairs of markers each lie about 1 million base pairs apart. One pair is 0.2 cM apart and the other 3 cM. What best explains the difference?"
    choices:
      - "The 3 cM pair must lie on a longer chromosome than the other pair"
      - "The 3 cM pair likely spans a region with more crossover hotspots"
      - "The two distances must have been measured in opposite sexes"
      - "The 0.2 cM pair holds less DNA than its 1 Mb length suggests"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Crossovers in humans cluster in hotspots, so equal lengths of DNA can have very different
      genetic lengths, and the interval with more active hotspots has the longer map distance.
      Chromosome length does not set the local rate per base pair. Male and female maps do
      differ, but a 15-fold gap needs no difference in sex and none is given. A million base
      pairs is a physical length; it does not shrink when few crossovers fall in it.

      the two interval frequencies times the total; the coefficient of coincidence is observed
      over expected, and interference is 1 minus coincidence. Genetic distance is a crossover
      frequency, and in humans and mice crossovers concentrate in hotspots placed largely by
      PRDM9.

The first film showed why a two-gene count, as in [linkage and mapping](/lessons/linkage-and-mapping/), misses two crossovers on one chromatid, and how a middle marker exposes them. A **crossover** exchanges segments between two non-sister chromatids in meiosis (see [chromosomes and meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/)).

A **locus** is a gene's place on a chromosome; its versions are **alleles**. A **test cross** mates the individual being studied with one homozygous recessive at every locus followed, so each offspring's phenotype reads one gamete from the other parent.

> **The one idea.** A middle marker makes double crossovers visible; comparing observed and expected doubles reveals crossover spacing.
>
> *Scope:* a three-point test cross with scorable classes and short adjacent intervals. Unequal survival can upset the usual size ranking, and even three markers miss an even number of crossovers within one interval.

## Read the eight classes in a fixed order

The first film sorted the eight classes into reciprocal pairs: **parentals** (no detected crossover), **single crossovers** in each interval, and **double crossovers** (one in each interval, usually smallest). Under independence, a double's probability is the product of the two interval probabilities ([probability and crosses](/lessons/probability-and-crosses/)), and the product of two small fractions is smaller still.

**Worked problem.** A female heterozygous for recessive mutations p, q and r carries p q r on one chromosome and + + + (normal alleles) on the other. She is test-crossed to a p q r / p q r male. Assume every class survives equally well, phenotypes are scored without error, and her **phase**, the alleles together on each chromosome, is known. The 1,000 progeny are listed alphabetically, not in map order:

- p q r: 350
- + + +: 342
- + q +: 53
- p + r: 55
- p q +: 96
- + + r: 92
- + q r: 7
- p + +: 5

Total: 350 + 342 + 53 + 55 + 96 + 92 + 7 + 5 = 1,000.

**Step 1: find the parentals.** The two largest classes, p q r and + + +, match the female's two chromosomes.

**Predict.** The provisional doubles are + q r and p + +. Match each to its closest parental. Which gene is in the middle?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

+ q r differs from p q r only at p, and p + + differs from + + + only at p. So p is in the middle, giving q–p–r. The alphabetical middle, q, is an outer gene.

</details>

<ThreePointOrderFinder />

**Step 2: rewrite every class in map order,** q to p to r, and note where each switches from one parental pattern to the other.

- Parentals: q p r (350) and + + + (342)
- Singles between q and p: q + + (53) and + p r (55)
- Singles between p and r: q p + (96) and + + r (92)
- Doubles: q + r (7) and + p + (5)

Rarity is a clue, not proof: a class can be small because its offspring survive poorly, as Sturtevant reported for one wing mutation in 1913. With short intervals and every class surviving and scored equally well, take the reciprocal pair with the smallest combined count as the doubles. The map-order sort then labels the singles but cannot confirm that choice: any reciprocal pair taken as the doubles sorts just as neatly. If survival or scoring may differ, seek independent evidence.

**Step 3: calculate adjacent distances.** Map distance is measured in **centimorgans** (cM), a frequency, not a length: for a short interval, 1 cM means about 1 gamete in 100 is recombinant. Count every double in both intervals:

> distance (cM) = 100 × (single crossovers in the interval + double crossovers) / total

- q to p: (53 + 55 + 7 + 5) / 1,000 = 120 / 1,000 = 12.0 cM
- p to r: (96 + 92 + 7 + 5) / 1,000 = 200 / 1,000 = 20.0 cM
- q to r on the map: 12.0 + 20.0 = 32.0 cM

**Predict.** If you now ignore p and score only q and r, what recombinant percentage will you obtain? Will it equal 32.0?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The doubles q + r and + p + carry parental q–r combinations, so only the 296 single crossovers count: 296 / 1,000 = 29.6%. The shortfall of 2.4 points is the 12 doubles counted once in each interval: 24 / 1,000.

</details>

Even 32.0 cM is a lower bound: two crossovers inside q–p or inside p–r, with no marker between them, still escape detection. That is why Sturtevant built maps from short adjacent intervals.

Twelve doubles turned up. Is that as many as chance predicts?

## Compare observed doubles with chance

The second film named the **coefficient of coincidence**, observed doubles over the number expected under independence, and **interference**, one minus coincidence.

**Predict.** The interval fractions are 0.12 and 0.20. Among 1,000 offspring, how many doubles does independence predict, and is that above or below the 12 observed?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

> expected doubles = 0.12 × 0.20 × 1,000 = 24
>
> coincidence = 12 / 24 = 0.50
>
> interference = 1 − 0.50 = 0.50

Half the expected doubles appeared. This is **positive interference**: a crossover in one interval is associated with fewer crossovers in the neighbouring interval.

</details>

Interference of 1 means no doubles at all; a coincidence above 1 is negative interference, an excess of doubles, not an impossible result. These values describe one pair of intervals in one organism, not a species constant: interference tends to be strongest across short neighbouring intervals, and for intervals far apart, coincidence approaches 1. This is how visible doubles become a measure of crossover spacing.

### Design problem

**Predict.** Another three-point test cross has 500 progeny, intervals of 6 cM and 10 cM, and no double-crossover progeny. A classmate concludes that interference here is complete. Using the independent expectation as a Poisson mean, calculate P(0), then repeat for 5,000 progeny. Is the classmate justified?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Independence predicts 0.06 × 0.10 × 500 = 3 doubles. A Poisson count with mean 3 is a good approximation for a rare event in many trials:

> P(0) = e^-3 = about 0.05

The exact binomial, (1 − 0.006)^500, gives 0.049. So even without interference, about one cross in twenty this size shows zero doubles. Zero is suggestive, not strong evidence, and cannot distinguish complete interference from interference of 0.5, which predicts 1.5 doubles.

Score more progeny. With 5,000, independence predicts 30 doubles and P(0) = e^-30, about 10^-13. If none appear, interference is close to complete over these intervals; about 15 would indicate interference near 0.5. Also check that double-crossover classes survive and can be scored. Interference is plausible here, since it is strongest over short adjacent intervals like these.

</details>

## Genetic distance is not DNA length

The second film's two rulers disagree on spacing, as Sturtevant warned they might in 1913. In humans the genome-wide average is close to 1 cM per million base pairs, only a rough average. In humans and mice, most crossovers fall in **hotspots**, short DNA stretches with much more recombination than the DNA around them.

In 2010, three groups linked hotspot position to *PRDM9*, whose protein binds particular DNA sequences through zinc fingers and marks nearby sites where recombination starts (see [DNA repair and recombination](/lessons/dna-repair-and-recombination/)).

**Predict.** In humans or mice, if the zinc-finger array of PRDM9 changes, should the same DNA sites remain hotspots?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not necessarily: the bound sequences change, so hotspots move. People carrying different zinc-finger variants differed in which hotspots they used across the genome.

</details>

Not every animal works this way. Dogs lack working PRDM9, birds appear to lack the gene, and their hotspots tend to lie near promoter-like features such as transcription start sites and CpG islands. Budding yeast shows a similar placement and far more recombination per kilobase than humans.

Maps also differ by sex and region. Across the genome, the human female map is about 1.6 to 1.7 times the male map. *Drosophila melanogaster* males normally have no meiotic crossing over, although males of some species, including *D. ananassae*, do. That is why *D. melanogaster* mapping crosses use a triple-heterozygous female, as in the final fly question. Crossovers are suppressed near centromeres in many organisms, including Drosophila and Arabidopsis; in *D. melanogaster* females, rates also varied strongly along chromosomes and between strains.

Maps and sequence agree on order, not always on spacing. Use a genetic map to predict how often genes travel together; use sequence to measure how much DNA separates them.

## Going deeper: when the simple comparison changes

Sturtevant showed in 1913 that doubles were scarcer than chance predicted. In 1915 he named the effect interference, crediting Hermann Muller with suggesting the name.

Positive interference is common, but not universal. Negative interference has been reported in some fungal and phage crosses. In Arabidopsis, one 2018 study measured interference of 0.64 for one interval pair; raising the dosage of the meiotic protein HEI10 lowered it to 0.34. In a 2016 sequencing study of Drosophila female meioses, noncrossover gene conversions, which exchange a short patch without exchanging the flanking DNA, did not show interference.
