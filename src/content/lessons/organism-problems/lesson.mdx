techniques:
  - name: "Gene overexpression with an empty-vector control"
    blurb: >-
      Insert a gene into a plant behind a promoter that keeps it turned on, so that its product is made in larger amounts than usual, and compare
      those plants with controls that went through the same procedure with the same vector minus
      the gene being tested. The control accounts for the vector and the transformation process.
      Two or more independent lines, and a check that the gene really is overexpressed, make it
      more likely that a difference comes from the gene and not from where it landed in the genome.
  - name: "Epidermal impressions and light microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Take an impression of a leaf surface (Hughes and colleagues pressed dental resin on the leaf, then painted clear nail varnish on the resin), peel off the varnish copy, and count the
      imprinted stomata in a known area under a light microscope. It gives stomatal density per
      square millimetre without destroying the plant.
  - name: "Infrared gas analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Clamp an attached leaf in a small chamber and measure how much carbon dioxide and water
      vapour the air gains or loses as it flows past. From these you calculate the leaf's carbon
      assimilation, its transpiration and its stomatal conductance.
  - name: "Common-environment rearing"
    blurb: >-
      Raise the descendants of two wild populations side by side under the same conditions,
      ideally for more than one generation. A difference that persists is likely to be inherited,
      because the environments that might have caused it directly are now the same.

  - stem: "In a made-up example, a leaf assimilates 12 umol CO2 per m2 per s and transpires 4 mmol H2O per m2 per s. What is its water-use efficiency?"
    choices:
      - "8.0 umol CO2 per mmol H2O"
      - "48 umol CO2 per mmol H2O"
      - "3.0 umol CO2 per mmol H2O"
      - "0.33 umol CO2 per mmol H2O"

    rationale: >-
      Water-use efficiency is carbon gained divided by water lost: 12 / 4 = 3.0 umol CO2 per mmol

  - stem: "A leaf narrows its stomata while the light and air stay the same. Its water loss falls by 40%, but its carbon gain falls by only 20%. What best explains the smaller fall in carbon gain?"
    choices:
      - "Narrow pores hold back water more than carbon dioxide"
      - "Carbon dioxide inside the leaf falls, widening its gap"
      - "The leaf's photosynthesis speeds up to make up the loss"
      - "Water vapour diffuses faster than carbon dioxide does"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      With fewer open pores, carbon dioxide arrives more slowly while the cells go on fixing it, so
      its concentration inside the leaf falls and the carbon dioxide gap widens; that props up carbon
      gain while water loss falls with conductance. Narrow pores do not favour one gas: both flows
      pass through the same conductance. Water vapour does diffuse about 1.6 times faster, but that
      factor is the same before and after the pores narrow, so it cannot explain a change. Nothing
      here makes photosynthesis speed up; carbon gain still falls, only by less.
  - stem: "Worker honeybees rarely reproduce while a queen is present, yet their colony goes on producing new queens and males. What does this show about the reproduction problem in this course?"
    choices:
      - "Each worker must reproduce for its colony to last"
      - "Non-breeding workers show that bees skip reproduction"
      - "Reproduction is solved by lineages, not every individual"
      - "Most workers lay as many eggs as the queen does"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The problem is the continuation of a lineage. In eusocial insects such as honeybees most
      workers are non-breeding helpers while a queen is present, but the colony's queen and males reproduce, so the lineage
      continues. A worker still passes on copies of its genes indirectly, by helping the queen, its
      mother, raise its brothers and sisters. Saying bees skip reproduction ignores the queens and
      males. Saying most workers lay as many eggs as the queen is false: some workers can lay
      unfertilised eggs that develop into males, mainly when the queen is gone, but in a colony with
      a queen most eggs are hers. Saying each worker must reproduce
      applies an individual-level rule that the non-breeding workers themselves contradict.

To grow, a barley leaf must take in carbon dioxide, and the main way in is through tiny pores that also let water vapour out. How much water does each bite of carbon cost, and can a plant pay less?

> **The one idea.** A leaf's carbon dioxide comes in, and its water vapour goes out, through the same pores. The price, water lost per carbon dioxide gained, is set by the two concentration gaps across the pores; each gap is the difference between the air inside the leaf and the air outside. The pores set how fast gas moves; fewer or narrower pores change the price only by changing a gap, and cannot make it zero.
>
> *Scope:* a first approximation for a leaf in air not saturated with water vapour, with the pores as the main path (Going deeper lists what it leaves out). A lung also pays water for oxygen, but breathing sets its price. This is the course's first example of a solution paid for somewhere else; for any other solution, find and test the cost rather than assume it.

## Five problems, one checklist for the course

A multicellular organism, a body of many cells that depend on one another, faces five problems. The first film named them; this course uses them as a checklist for every organ system. The list is a teaching frame; other books divide the ground differently.

1. **Energy and materials.** All organisms use an energy source to run their chemistry; animals get theirs from food they ingest or absorb. Plants capture light energy in their leaves and use it to fix carbon from the air; splitting water along the way gives off oxygen as a by-product ([the light reactions](/lessons/light-reactions/), [the Calvin cycle](/lessons/calvin-cycle/)). The roots of seed plants absorb water and minerals from the soil.
2. **Exchange and transport, including wastes.** Materials must cross a surface and reach every cell, and wastes must leave. **Diffusion**, the net movement of molecules from higher to lower concentration, can do much of this in a small organism. In a large one, transport systems carry the load: blood vessels in animals; in plants the **xylem**, which carries water and minerals up from the roots, and the **phloem**, which carries sugars from where they are made to where they are used ([size, scale and surface](/lessons/size-and-scaling/), [gas exchange](/lessons/gas-exchange/), [moving water and sugar in plants](/lessons/plant-transport/)).
3. **Control and relative internal stability.** **Homeostasis** is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment. Adult humans hold their core temperature near 37 degrees C; a plant closes its leaf pores when its leaves run short of water ([tissues, organs and homeostasis](/lessons/tissues-and-homeostasis/)).
4. **Defense.** Animals have skin, mucus and immune cells ([innate immunity](/lessons/innate-immunity/)); in the small plant Arabidopsis, closing the stomata can be part of the defense against bacteria that enter through those pores.
5. **Reproduction, as continuation of a lineage.** Reproduction passes DNA, and the genes it carries, to offspring. It is a problem for the **lineage**, a line of ancestors and descendants, not for every individual: honeybee workers rarely reproduce while a queen is present, yet the colony goes on producing queens and males (more in Going deeper).

## Where the price comes from: one leaf, one lung

The first film showed a barley (Hordeum vulgare) leaf passing carbon dioxide in and water vapour out through its **stomata** (singular stoma), the pores between pairs of **guard cells**; that water loss is **transpiration**, water evaporating from the wet cell walls inside. Here is the arithmetic the film left out.

**The leaf.** Both gases take the same path, so one **stomatal conductance**, how freely gas passes through the stomata (measured for carbon dioxide), sets both flows:

> **carbon in = conductance × carbon dioxide gap**
>
> **water out = 1.6 × conductance × water vapour gap**

Water vapour, a lighter molecule, diffuses about 1.6 times faster than carbon dioxide. Divide the second line by the first and the conductance cancels:

> **water lost per carbon gained = 1.6 × water vapour gap / carbon dioxide gap**

In moderately dry air, with carbon dioxide scarce and the air inside the leaf close to saturated, the water gap is about a hundred times the carbon dioxide gap, so the leaf pays about 1.6 × 100 = 160 water molecules per carbon dioxide, roughly 150 to 300 as the carbon dioxide inside the leaf runs a little higher or lower. Humid air shrinks the water gap and the price; hot, dry air raises both.

**The lung.** Breathing moves air in bulk, and the airways wet incoming air and recover some water on the way out, so the two-gap rule does not apply; work from one breath. Air breathed in at about 21% oxygen leaves at about 16%: about 5% of its volume is taken up as oxygen. Room air at 20 degrees C and half humidity holds about 1% water vapour; the breath out, nearly saturated near body temperature, holds about 4 to 5% more. So the lung pays about one water molecule per oxygen, a rough size that breathing rate, oxygen extraction and the air's temperature and humidity move (cold, dry air raises it).

## Worked problem: what a leaf pays

> **water-use efficiency = carbon gained / water lost**, the price turned upside down

These numbers are illustrative, not barley data. Leaf A **assimilates** (takes up and fixes) 20 umol CO2 per m2 per s and transpires 5 mmol H2O per m2 per s. Leaf B, with fewer stomata under the same light and temperature, assimilates 16 umol CO2 per m2 per s while transpiring 3 mmol H2O per m2 per s. Assume both rates stay constant.

1. *Efficiency.* Leaf A: 20 / 5 = 4.0 umol CO2 per mmol H2O. Leaf B: 16 / 3 = 5.3 umol CO2 per mmol H2O.
2. *Read it as molecules.* A mmol is 1,000 umol, so 4.0 umol per mmol means 4 carbon dioxide for every 1,000 water molecules: one for every 1,000 / 4 = 250. Leaf B pays 1,000 / 5.33, about 190.
3. *Compare.* Leaf B gains 16 / 20 = 0.8 of A's carbon, 20% less. It loses 3 / 5 = 0.6 of A's water, 40% less. Its efficiency is 5.33 / 4.0 = 1.33 times A's.

**Predict.** Leaf B's water loss fell twice as far as its carbon gain. The pores set how fast gas moves; the gaps set the price. So how can fewer pores lower the price?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Through a gap. With fewer open pores, carbon dioxide arrives more slowly while the cells go on fixing it, so its concentration inside the leaf falls and the carbon dioxide gap widens, propping up carbon gain while water loss falls with conductance. One caution for a real leaf: with less water evaporating it can run warmer, depending on sun and wind, which would widen the water gap and give back part of the saving.

</details>

Neither leaf is better everywhere: when water is scarce, leaf B keeps more of it per unit of carbon gained; when water is plentiful and carbon limits growth, leaf A gains more.

## Why structure fits function, and why never perfectly

Structures usually fit what they do because of history, not intention. Individuals differ, some differences are inherited, and some inherited differences change how many offspring an individual leaves; over generations, those variants can become more common. That process is **natural selection**, and a heritable trait it shaped because it helped survival and reproduction is an **adaptation**. A trait can also be useful now without having been selected for that use; such a co-opted trait is sometimes called an **exaptation**.

So an evolutionary claim about stomata has this shape: "in dry places, barley lineages with fewer stomata lost less water and left more descendants." The shorthand "barley has fewer stomata to save water" skips that history and can read as if the plant had a goal.

**Predict.** Suppose a single barley plant whose soil dries out makes fewer stomata on its new leaves. Is that plant evolving?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. One plant changing its leaves within its own lifetime is a developmental response, a proximate question for the last section; populations evolve across generations. Testing an evolutionary claim is the subject of [testing evolutionary explanations](/lessons/testing-evolutionary-explanations/) and [selection in ecological context](/lessons/selection-in-ecological-context/).

</details>

The fit is never perfect, for at least three reasons.

**Trade-offs.** A **trade-off** is a link between two traits such that improving one worsens the other. More stomata raise a leaf's capacity to take in carbon and also to lose water, though actual rates depend on stomatal size, how wide the pores open and the conditions. Water loss is not only a cost: the flow of water through the plant helps carry minerals up from the roots and cools the leaf. Lifetime trade-offs, such as reproducing early against growing larger, appear in [life histories](/lessons/life-histories/).

**History.** The human arm, the bat wing and the whale flipper share one pattern: an upper arm bone, two forearm bones, wrist bones and digits. They are **homologous**, inherited from a common ancestor and modified in each lineage ([reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/)). Selection on a whale's ancestors could reshape those bones, but it began from them. Evolution does not move a population toward a preconceived ideal.

**Chance and by-products.** Not all evolutionary change is adaptive. Some traits spread by chance, especially in small populations, and some are by-products of other features, not selected for themselves. Gould and Lewontin called such by-products spandrels, after the leftover spaces between a Venetian cathedral's arches, later decorated but not built for decoration.

So "perfectly adapted" is never the right phrase. No living organism is "more evolved" than another, either: a honeybee and a human have both had the same length of time since their common ancestor, and each lineage has its own solutions to the same five problems.

## How questions and why questions

"Why does a barley leaf have the stomatal density it has?" can mean two different things. In 1961 Ernst Mayr distinguished **proximate** causes, which act within an organism's lifetime, from **ultimate** causes, which lie in the lineage's evolutionary history. "Ultimate" does not mean purpose: an ultimate answer is a history of what happened to a lineage, such as which variants left more descendants. In 1963 Niko Tinbergen set out four questions, and biologists now usually group them two to each kind:

| Kind | Question | Barley stomata |
|---|---|---|
| Proximate | Mechanism: how does it work now? | How guard cells open and close the pore |
| Proximate | Development: how does it form? | How genes such as HvEPF1 set how many stomata a leaf makes |
| Ultimate | Adaptive significance: what effect on survival and reproduction? | Whether density changes reproductive success in a given field environment |
| Ultimate | History: how did it change over the lineage? | How stomatal density changed across grasses on a tree |

The barley experiment answers proximate questions in engineered lines: the development row, and what fewer stomata do to gas exchange, water use and grain. It does not test how guard cells open and close. A full account needs both kinds of answer.

### Design problem

Guppies (Poecilia reticulata) in Trinidad streams live with many predators below some waterfalls and few above them. Reznick, Shaw, Rodd and Shaw (Science, 1997) reported on fish moved from high-predation sites into low-predation streams above waterfalls. At one site, El Cedro, males from the introduced population matured later than males from the source population within about four years. How would you show that this difference is inherited rather than a direct effect of food or water, and is that a proximate or an ultimate question?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Collect fish from the introduced population and, at the same time, from the source population as a control, and rear their descendants side by side in one laboratory environment (a common-environment, or common-garden, design), with the same food, temperature and density, for at least two generations; the study used second-generation laboratory fish. Two generations dilute effects passed on through the mother's condition. In that assay the adjusted mean male maturation ages were 60.6 days for source fish and 72.7 days for introduced fish; measured female traits had not changed significantly at four years (by 7.5 years females were larger at first birth and tended to be older, a difference that fell just short of significance).

<LessonFigure src="/figures/organism-problems/guppy-common-environment-design.webp" width={1200} height={580} alt="AI-generated illustration: guppies from a stream pool below a waterfall with predators are moved to a stream above the waterfall with fewer predators. In the laboratory, descendants of the source fish and the introduced fish are kept in two identical tanks under the same conditions for a second generation." caption="If introduced-population males still mature later after two generations side by side in one laboratory environment, the difference is likely inherited." />

An inherited, probably genetic, basis still does not show what caused the change. Moving fish above a waterfall may change density and food as well as predation, so to link the change to predation, compare several high- and low-predation streams and measure density and food at each.

Why later maturation spread where predators are few is an ultimate question, about adaptive significance and history; how a guppy's body times its maturation is a separate, proximate one.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Reproduction without every individual reproducing

In many **eusocial** species (animals that live in colonies with a division of reproductive labour) the workers reproduce little or not at all. A worker that does not reproduce still passes on copies of its genes indirectly, by helping raise close relatives.

### The barley test: lines, watering levels and efficiency measures

In the mature sixth leaf, the two lines kept about 24% and 12% of the control stomatal density. In making them, the team inserted the barley gene HvEPF1 behind a promoter, a DNA switch, that keeps it turned on.

In a greenhouse, plants were grown at 60% or 25% on the authors' calibrated soil-water scale. Under water restriction, carbon assimilation and stomatal conductance did not differ significantly from controls. Water-use efficiency integrated over the life of the flag leaf (the uppermost leaf, just below the grain head), estimated from carbon isotopes in the leaf, rose in both lines under water restriction and in one line when well watered. The intrinsic efficiency measure from gas exchange, assimilation divided by stomatal conductance, rose in one line when well watered but in neither line under water restriction; the measures are not interchangeable.

### What the gap rule leaves out

The two flow equations hold at steady state when the pores are the main resistance: the still layer of air over the leaf (its boundary layer) is thin, water lost through the waxy layer is small, and the air inside the leaf is treated as saturated. Otherwise the boundary layer, the waxy layer, leaf temperature and photosynthesis itself also shift the ratio; in saturated air the water gap, and the price, fall to about zero, and with the stomata shut both flows fall toward zero.

### Worked problem: a 10-hour day

Over a 10-hour day (36,000 s), Leaf A loses 5 mmol x 36,000 = 180,000 mmol = 180 mol H2O per m2, or 180 x 18 g per mol = 3,240 g, about 3.24 kg. Leaf B loses 3 x 36,000 = 108 mol, or 1,944 g, about 1.94 kg.
