techniques:
  - name: "Gene overexpression with an empty-vector control"
    blurb: >-
      Insert a gene into a plant behind a promoter that keeps it turned on, so that its product is made in larger amounts than usual, and compare
      those plants with controls that went through the same procedure with the same vector minus
      the gene being tested. The control accounts for the vector and the transformation process.
      Two or more independent lines, and a check that the gene really is overexpressed, make it
      more likely that a difference comes from the gene and not from where it landed in the genome.
  - name: "Epidermal impressions and light microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Take an impression of a leaf surface (Hughes and colleagues pressed dental resin on the leaf, then painted clear nail varnish on the resin), peel off the varnish copy, and count the
      imprinted stomata in a known area under a light microscope. It gives stomatal density per
      square millimetre without destroying the plant.
  - name: "Infrared gas analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Clamp an attached leaf in a small chamber and measure how much carbon dioxide and water
      vapour the air gains or loses as it flows past. From these you calculate the leaf's carbon
      assimilation, its transpiration and its stomatal conductance.
  - name: "Common-environment rearing"
    blurb: >-
      Raise the descendants of two wild populations side by side under the same conditions,
      ideally for more than one generation. A difference that persists is likely to be inherited,
      because the environments that might have caused it directly are now the same.

**A barley leaf cannot take in carbon dioxide without letting water out.** The gas enters through tiny pores, and water vapour leaves through the same pores. Your lungs face a version of the same bargain. This first lesson asks what every multicellular organism has to manage, and why its solutions always carry a cost.

## Five problems, one checklist for the course

A multicellular organism is a body built of many cells that depend on one another. Barley, honeybee or human, it faces the same few recurring problems, and this course uses five as a checklist. The list is a teaching frame, not an official definition; other books divide the ground differently.

1. **Energy and materials.** All organisms use an energy source to run their chemistry. Animals get theirs from food they ingest or absorb. Plants capture light energy in their leaves (the chemistry is in [the light reactions](/lessons/light-reactions/)) and use it to fix carbon from the air (in [the Calvin cycle](/lessons/calvin-cycle/)), but the roots of seed plants also absorb water and minerals from the soil. The oxygen a plant gives off is a by-product of splitting water during photosynthesis.
2. **Exchange and transport, including wastes.** Materials must cross a surface and reach every cell, and wastes must leave. In a small organism, diffusion, the net movement of molecules from higher to lower concentration, can do much of this. In a large one it cannot reach far enough, and transport systems carry the load: blood vessels in animals, and in plants the **xylem** (tissue that carries water and minerals up from the roots) and the **phloem** (tissue that carries sugars from where they are made to where they are used). [Size, scale and surface](/lessons/size-and-scaling/) shows why size forces this, and [gas exchange](/lessons/gas-exchange/) and [moving water and sugar in plants](/lessons/plant-transport/) show the solutions.
3. **Control and relative internal stability.** **Homeostasis** is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment. Adult humans hold their core temperature near 37 degrees C; a plant closes its stomata when its leaves run short of water. [Tissues, organs and homeostasis](/lessons/tissues-and-homeostasis/) builds this out.
4. **Defense.** Bodies are damaged and invaded. In the small plant Arabidopsis, closing the stomata can be part of the defense against bacteria that enter through those pores. Animals have skin, mucus and immune cells, the subject of [innate immunity](/lessons/innate-immunity/).
5. **Reproduction, as continuation of a lineage.** Reproduction passes DNA, and the genes it carries, to offspring. It is a problem for the lineage, not for every individual. In honeybees, workers rarely reproduce while a queen is present, and in many other **eusocial** species (animals that live in colonies with a division of reproductive labour) the workers reproduce little or not at all, yet their colonies go on producing queens and males. A worker that does not reproduce still passes on copies of its genes indirectly, by helping raise close relatives.

## One leaf, one lung: the gas-for-water trade-off in two lineages

A surface that passes gas from air to living cells, as in a leaf or a human lung, has to be thin and wet, because the gas has to dissolve in a film of water before it can diffuse into cells. A wet surface exposed to air loses water by evaporation. Two lineages show the coupling.

In barley (Hordeum vulgare), the leaf surface is covered by a waxy layer that water crosses poorly. Gas moves mainly through **stomata** (singular stoma), pores each flanked by two guard cells that open and close the gap. The thin, wet exchange surface itself lies inside the leaf: the walls of the cells that line its internal air spaces. When stomata are open, carbon dioxide diffuses in through the pores toward those cells, and water vapour that evaporates from their wet walls diffuses out. The loss of water vapour from leaves is called **transpiration**.

In an adult human, the exchange surface is the lining of the lung's tiny air sacs, and it must stay moist. The airways leading to it warm the air you breathe in and saturate it with water vapour before it arrives. On the way out the airways take back some of that water, but the air you breathe out is still nearly saturated, so in ordinary, drier air each breath carries water away. The lung sits deep inside the chest, which shelters it, but it cannot close the way a stoma can. Neither solution escapes the cost.

**A test in barley.** Hughes, Gray and colleagues asked what happens to barley with fewer stomata. They overexpressed a barley gene, HvEPF1 (overexpression means making a gene's product in larger amounts than usual; here the team inserted HvEPF1 behind a promoter, a DNA switch, that keeps it turned on). This limited the entry of leaf cells into stomatal development and their progress through it. They compared two such lines with **empty-vector controls**, plants put through the same genetic procedure without the added gene. In the mature sixth leaf, the two lines kept about 24% and 12% of the control stomatal density. In a greenhouse, plants were grown at 60% or 25% on the authors' calibrated soil-water scale.

You might expect less water lost and, with less carbon taken in, less grain. The first part held in well-watered plants: leaf gas exchange fell substantially (under water restriction, carbon assimilation and stomatal conductance (how freely gas passes through the stomata) did not differ significantly from controls). Water-use efficiency integrated over the life of the flag leaf (the uppermost leaf, just below the grain head), estimated from carbon isotopes in the leaf, rose in both lines under water restriction and in one line when well watered. The second part was not seen. With five plants per group, neither line showed a statistically significant drop in grain yield under either watering treatment. Two cautions. The intrinsic efficiency measure from gas exchange, assimilation divided by stomatal conductance, rose in one line when well watered but in neither line under water restriction. And five plants per group cannot prove the yields equal; it means no loss was detected. In a separate test where water was withheld, the engineered lines kept photosystem II, part of the light-capturing machinery, working about four days longer than controls.

**Worked problem.** These numbers are illustrative, not barley data. Leaf A assimilates 20 umol CO2 per m2 per s and transpires 5 mmol H2O per m2 per s. Leaf B has fewer stomata, under the same light and temperature, and assimilates 16 umol CO2 per m2 per s while transpiring 3 mmol H2O per m2 per s. Assume both rates stay constant.

> **water-use efficiency = carbon gained / water lost**

1. *Efficiency.* Leaf A: 20 / 5 = 4.0 umol CO2 per mmol H2O. Leaf B: 16 / 3 = 5.3 umol CO2 per mmol H2O.
2. *Compare.* Leaf B gains 16 / 20 = 0.8 of A's carbon, 20% less. It loses 3 / 5 = 0.6 of A's water, 40% less. Its efficiency is 5.33 / 4.0 = 1.33 times A's.
3. *Over a 10-hour day* (36,000 s). Leaf A loses 5 mmol x 36,000 = 180,000 mmol = 180 mol H2O per m2, or 180 x 18 g per mol = 3,240 g, about 3.24 kg. Leaf B loses 3 x 36,000 = 108 mol, or 1,944 g, about 1.94 kg.

Neither leaf is better everywhere. When water is scarce, leaf B keeps more of it for each unit of carbon gained. When water is plentiful and carbon limits growth, leaf A gains more.

## Why structure fits function, and why never perfectly

Structures usually fit what they do, and the explanation is historical. Natural selection acts on existing, inherited variation in a population. Over generations, it can increase the share of inherited traits associated with greater reproductive success. An **adaptation** is a heritable trait that natural selection shaped because it helped survival and reproduction. A trait can be useful now without having evolved for that use; such a co-opted trait is sometimes called an exaptation. So "lineages with fewer stomata lost less water and left more descendants in dry places" is the right form for an evolutionary claim. "Barley has fewer stomata to save water" is common shorthand, but it hides that history and can read as if the plant had a goal. A single plant that makes fewer stomata when its soil is dry is a separate case: a developmental response, which is a proximate question. Such claims are tested in [testing evolutionary explanations](/lessons/testing-evolutionary-explanations/) and [selection in ecological context](/lessons/selection-in-ecological-context/).

The fit is never perfect, for at least three reasons.

**Trade-offs.** A **trade-off** is a link between two traits such that improving one worsens the other. More stomata raise a leaf's capacity to take in carbon and also to lose water, though actual rates depend on stomatal size, how wide the pores open and the conditions. Water loss is not only a cost: the flow of water through the plant carries minerals up from the roots and cools the leaf. Lifetime trade-offs, such as reproducing early against growing larger, appear in [life histories](/lessons/life-histories/).

**History.** The human arm, the bat wing and the whale flipper share one pattern: an upper arm bone, two forearm bones, wrist bones and digits. They are **homologous**, inherited from a common ancestor and modified in each lineage, which you can read off a tree in [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/). Selection on a whale's ancestors could reshape those bones, but it began from them. Evolution does not move a population toward a preconceived ideal.

**Chance and by-products.** Not all evolutionary change is adaptive. Some traits spread by chance in small populations, and some are side effects of other features. Gould and Lewontin named the second kind after the spandrels of a Venetian cathedral: spaces left over between arches, later decorated, not built for the decoration.

So "perfectly adapted" is never the right phrase. No living organism is "more evolved" than another, either: a honeybee and a human have both had the same length of time since their common ancestor, and each lineage has its own solutions to the same five problems.

## How questions and why questions

Ask "why does a barley leaf have the stomatal density it has?" and you can mean two different things. In 1961 Ernst Mayr distinguished **proximate** causes, which act within an organism's lifetime, from **ultimate** causes, which lie in the lineage's evolutionary history. In 1963 Niko Tinbergen set out four questions, and biologists now usually group them two to each kind:

| Kind | Question | Barley stomata |
|---|---|---|
| Proximate | Mechanism: how does it work now? | How guard cells open and close the pore |
| Proximate | Development: how does it form? | How genes such as HvEPF1 set how many stomata a leaf makes |
| Ultimate | Adaptive significance: what effect on survival and reproduction? | Whether density changes reproductive success in a given field environment |
| Ultimate | History: how did it change over the lineage? | How stomatal density changed across grasses on a tree |

The barley experiment answers proximate questions in engineered lines: how HvEPF1 limits stomatal development, and how fewer stomata change gas exchange, water use and yield. It does not show how stomatal density evolved in wild barley, or field performance. A full account needs both kinds of answer, and neither replaces the other.

"Ultimate" does not mean purpose, although the word "why" can make a question sound as if it asks for an intention. An ultimate answer is a history of what happened to a lineage, such as which variants left more descendants.

### Design problem

Guppies (Poecilia reticulata) in Trinidad streams live with many predators below some waterfalls and few above them. Reznick, Shaw, Rodd and Shaw (Science, 1997) reported on fish moved from high-predation sites into low-predation streams above waterfalls. At one site, El Cedro, males from the introduced population matured later than males from the source population within about four years. How would you show that this difference is inherited rather than a direct effect of food or water, and is that a proximate or an ultimate question?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Collect fish from the introduced population and, at the same time, from the source population, which serves as the control. Rear their descendants side by side in one laboratory environment, with the same food, temperature and density, for at least two generations; the study used second-generation laboratory fish. Two generations dilute effects passed on through the mother's condition. If introduced-population males still mature later, the difference is likely inherited. In that assay the adjusted mean male maturation ages were 60.6 days for source fish and 72.7 days for introduced fish; measured female traits had not changed significantly at four years (by 7.5 years females were larger at first birth and tended to be older, a difference that fell just short of significance).

That points to an inherited, probably genetic, basis for the difference, not its cause. Moving fish above a waterfall changes predation, and it may also change population density and food supply. To link the change to predation, compare several high- and low-predation streams, and measure density and food at each site.

The overall question, why later maturation spread where predators are few, is ultimate: it asks about adaptive significance and history. How a guppy's body times its maturation would be a separate, proximate question.
