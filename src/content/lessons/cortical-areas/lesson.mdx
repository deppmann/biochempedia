summary: >-
  Position draws the cortex's area sketch and input finishes it: in the mouse, graded signals such as
  FGF8 and transcription factors such as Emx2 help set where the primary-area borders fall. The adult
  cortex is divided into areas for movement, touch, hearing and vision, but the embryonic sheet that
  makes them shows no clear borders. You lay out the mouse map and its timeline, weigh the two classic answers (a map
  carried by the progenitors and a map imposed by thalamic input), learn how a map is read in a newborn,
  and then test the sketch: a signaling centre, FGF8, that can move a border, opposing gradients of
  transcription factors, and cortex that rebuilds itself to suit new input. Afterwards you can predict
  how a mouse area map shifts when Emx2 or FGF8 is removed or added, and when Pax6 is removed.
hook: "Add one secreted protein to the back of a mouse embryo's cortex and a second, partial whisker map can appear there."

scientists:
  - name: "Pasko Rakic"
    contribution: >-
      Showed in monkeys that newborn cortical neurons climb along radial glial fibres, and in 1988
      proposed the radial unit hypothesis and the protomap: that cortical progenitors already
      carry a mosaic of area identities.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Rakic, J. Comp. Neurol. 145:61 (1972); Rakic, Science 241:170 (1988)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Rakic built his ideas about cortical areas from the primate brain. Working with rhesus
      monkeys, he reported in 1972 that newborn neurons travel from the ventricular zone to the
      cortex along radial glial fibres, the long scaffold cells that span the wall of the
      developing brain. That suggested a simple geometry: neurons born from one patch of
      progenitors stack up in one column above it. In a 1988 paper in Science he set out the
      radial unit hypothesis and, with it, the protomap: the proliferative layer is a mosaic of
      units that already differ by position, so the outline of the areas is laid down where the
      cells are made. He did not treat input as irrelevant. His group also reduced the thalamic fibres that reach primary visual cortex in
      fetal monkeys and reported that part of the area became a hybrid region, which they called area X; another group later found that this tissue looked like normal neighbouring visual cortex. The protomap gave later geneticists a clear prediction to test: molecular
      differences should exist across the progenitor sheet before any thalamic axon arrives.
  - name: "Dennis O'Leary"
    contribution: >-
      Proposed in 1989 that areas emerge from an initially equivalent protocortex under thalamic
      input, showed with Bradley Schlaggar that transplanted visual cortex can form barrels, and
      later showed that Emx2 and Pax6 shift the area map in opposite directions.
    profileSourceAnchor: "O'Leary, Trends Neurosci. 12:400 (1989); Schlaggar & O'Leary, Science 252:1556 (1991); Bishop, Goudreau & O'Leary, Science 288:344 (2000)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      O'Leary, then at Washington University in St. Louis and later at the Salk Institute, started from the opposite side. In a 1989 review in Trends
      in Neurosciences he proposed that the cortex begins as a protocortex, a sheet with broadly
      similar potential, and that areas emerge largely from the axons it receives from the
      thalamus. He tested the idea by moving tissue. In 1991, with Bradley Schlaggar, he grafted
      visual cortex from fetal rats into the somatosensory cortex of newborn rats. Thalamic axons
      that carry whisker signals grew into the graft, and it formed barrels, the clusters of
      cells that represent single whiskers. His laboratory then turned to genes. In 2000, with
      Kathie Bishop and Guy Goudreau, he reported that mice lacking Emx2 or Pax6 shift their area
      markers in opposite directions, and in 2004, with Tadashi Hamasaki and colleagues, that
      raising Emx2 in progenitors moves the map opposite to the shift seen without Emx2. His own work therefore supplied
      strong evidence on both sides, and it made area size something that could be measured and
      changed.
  - name: "Elizabeth Grove"
    contribution: >-
      With Tomomi Fukuchi-Shimogori, showed in mouse embryos that FGF8 from the front of the
      cortical primordium positions the area map: more FGF8 moves the barrel field back, less moves
      it forward, and an ectopic posterior source induces a partial duplicate barrel field.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Fukuchi-Shimogori & Grove, Science 294:1071 (2001); Fukuchi-Shimogori & Grove, Nat. Neurosci. 6:825 (2003)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Grove, at the University of Chicago, asked what tells cortical progenitors where they are.
      Her laboratory studied signaling centres at the edges of the cortical sheet: the cortical
      hem at the medial edge, a source of Wnt and BMP proteins, and a source of FGF8 at the front.
      To test such a source directly she needed to add a signal to one chosen spot in a living
      embryo. In 2001, with Tomomi Fukuchi-Shimogori, she used electroporation to deliver DNA
      encoding FGF8 into the mouse cortical primordium. Extra FGF8 at the front moved the barrel
      field toward the back. Blocking FGF8 at the front with a soluble receptor fragment moved it
      forward. A new source of FGF8 at the back produced a second, partial barrel field. In 2003 the pair reported that Emx2 helps set the cortex's map by regulating FGF
      signaling. The work made it possible to treat an area border as a position that a secreted
      signal can move.
  - name: "Mriganka Sur"
    contribution: >-
      Rerouted retinal axons to the auditory thalamus of newborn ferrets and showed with Jitendra
      Sharma and Alessandra Angelucci that the auditory cortex that then receives visual input
      builds a map of orientation-selective neurons.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Sur, Garraghty & Roe, Science 242:1437 (1988); Sharma, Angelucci & Sur, Nature 404:841 (2000)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Sur, at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, tested the role of input in ferrets,
      which are born with their visual pathways still immature. In 1988, with Preston Garraghty
      and Anna Roe, he reported that when the normal targets of retinal axons were reduced and the normal
      inputs to the auditory thalamus removed in newborn ferrets, retinal axons grew into the
      auditory thalamus, which then relayed visual activity to auditory cortex. In 2000, with
      Jitendra Sharma and Alessandra Angelucci, he mapped the rewired auditory cortex of adult
      ferrets. Neurons there preferred particular edge
      orientations and were grouped into orientation modules, as in visual cortex, though the
      map was less orderly. A companion paper by Laurie von Melchner, Sarah Pallas and Sur reported that the
      animals responded to light seen through the rewired pathway as a visual stimulus. The
      experiments showed that much of an area's internal organization depends on the input it
      receives during development.

techniques:
  - name: "In situ hybridization for area markers"
    blurb: >-
      A labelled strand of RNA or DNA binds the messenger RNA of one gene in a tissue section,
      showing where that gene is on. Genes whose expression borders line up with future areas let
      you read an area map at birth, before the areas can be recognized by their anatomy or
      function.
  - name: "In utero electroporation"
    blurb: >-
      DNA is injected into the fluid-filled ventricle of an embryo's brain and brief electric
      pulses drive it into the progenitor cells next to the positive electrode. It adds a gene, or
      a blocker, to one chosen spot of the cortical sheet in a living mouse embryo.
  - name: "Cytochrome oxidase staining of flattened cortex"
    blurb: >-
      The cortex is removed, flattened and cut parallel to its surface, and stained for a
      mitochondrial enzyme that is rich where thalamic axons end. In rodent somatosensory cortex
      the stain shows the barrels as dark patches, a sharp landmark for where an area lies.
  - name: "Cortical transplantation"
    blurb: >-
      A piece of young cortex from one area is grafted into another area of a host animal. What
      the graft becomes, under its new inputs, tests whether its identity was already fixed or
      could still be set from outside.

      - "Area borders appear only once sensory experience begins after birth"
      - "Progenitors already carry a bias toward particular area identities"

    rationale: >-
      Rakic's protomap places the first outline of the areas in the progenitors themselves, so a
      bias toward a given identity exists where the cells are made. Thalamic axons dividing an
      equal sheet is the protocortex hypothesis, the rival view. Neurons migrating in from the
      ventral brain describes interneurons, not the origin of area identity. Borders that appear
      only with experience after birth ignores that marker borders are already present at birth.
  - stem: "In the mouse cortical primordium, where is Emx2 expressed most highly?"
    choices:
      - "Only in the thalamic nuclei that project to cortex"
      - "Highest at the front and lateral side, like Pax6"
      - "Highest at the back and medial side, unlike Pax6"
      - "Evenly across the sheet, with no gradient at all"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Emx2 runs high at the back and medial edge and low at the front and lateral edge, the
      reverse of the Pax6 gradient. Highest at the front and lateral side describes Pax6, not
      Emx2. An even level with no gradient would give the protein no information about position.
      Expression only in the thalamus is wrong because Emx2 is made in the cortical progenitors.
  - stem: "In mice lacking Emx2, marker genes at birth show which change in the cortical area map?"
    choices:
      - "Areas vanish and the cortex has no regional markers"
      - "Front areas grow and the visual area shrinks backward"
      - "All areas keep their size but the cortex is thinner"
      - "Front areas shrink and the visual area grows forward"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Emx2 is highest at the back, so without it the back identity is weakened: the motor and
      somatosensory areas, at the front and middle, expand toward the back and the visual area shrinks. Front
      areas shrinking with a larger visual area is the shift reported for Pax6 mutants, the
      reverse case. Areas keeping their size misses the reported shift. Areas vanishing overstates
      the effect, since marker borders were still present, only moved.
  - stem: "An experiment adds a gene where it is normally absent and finds a structure enlarged or a new copy induced. What kind of evidence is this?"
    choices:
      - "Evidence that the gene is necessary for the structure"
      - "Evidence that the gene acts only through thalamic input"
      - "Evidence that the gene is expressed within the structure"
      - "Evidence that the gene is sufficient for the structure"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Adding a gene and getting more of a structure, or a new one, is a test of sufficiency.
      Necessity needs the opposite experiment, removing the gene and finding the structure lost or
      shrunk. Where a gene is expressed is shown by staining, not by adding it in a new place. The
      result says nothing about whether the gene acts through thalamic input or within the cortex.
  - stem: "Visual cortex from a fetal rat is grafted into the somatosensory cortex of a newborn rat, where thalamic axons that carry whisker signals grow into it. The graft forms barrels. What does this result show?"
    choices:
      - "Young cortex can take on a feature set by the input it receives"
      - "Barrels are built by the thalamic axons without help from cortex"
      - "Area features are fixed in progenitors and cannot be changed by input"
      - "Barrels can form in any tissue that whisker axons happen to reach"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Tissue that would have become visual cortex built a feature of somatosensory cortex once it
      received whisker input, so at that age the feature was still open to being set from
      outside. Features fixed in progenitors is the reading this result argues against. Any
      tissue at all goes beyond the experiment, which used cortex as the graft. Barrels built by
      the axons alone ignores that barrels are clusters of cortical cells arranged around the
      axon endings.

**In the adult mouse, a patch of cortex near the middle of each hemisphere maps the whiskers and a patch near the back maps what the eyes see. In the embryo, the sheet that will hold both looks the same from front to back.**

The cerebral cortex is the sheet of neurons on the surface of the forebrain. In 1909 Korbinian Brodmann divided the human cortex into numbered areas by how its cells were packed and layered, and many of his borders match borders of function: his area 17 is primary visual cortex, the first cortical stop for signals from the eyes. Each area has its own inputs and outputs, and many sensory areas hold an internal map of the body or the world. Yet the cortex grows from a smooth sheet of progenitors, cells that divide to make neurons, with no clear borders. Where do the areas come from? The process is called **arealization**.

## The layout

Most arguments in this lesson happen on one map, which the first film draws. Picture one hemisphere of a mouse brain seen from above.

- **Two axes.** Front to back runs from the nose end to the back of the head. **Medial** is the edge along the midline, where the two hemispheres meet; **lateral** is the outer edge.
- **Motor cortex** sits at the front.
- **Somatosensory cortex**, the touch area, sits in the middle. Inside it lies the **barrel field**, the whisker map.
- **Auditory cortex** sits on the lateral side, toward the back.
- **Visual cortex** sits at the back.

In this lesson, "the front-and-middle areas" means motor and somatosensory cortex, and "the back area" means visual cortex.

**When things happen** (mouse, roughly):

- Signalling centres at the edges of the sheet are active by about embryonic day 9.5 (E9.5) and keep signalling as the first neurons are made.
- Progenitors make the cortex's neurons from about E11 to E17.
- Axons from the thalamus, the relay station that passes signals from the eyes, ears and skin to the cortex, reach the developing cortex around E14 to E15 and wait in the subplate, a layer just beneath it, while neurons are still being made. They grow into the cortical plate itself later, around birth.
- Barrels can be seen in the first postnatal week, around postnatal day 4.

So the sheet is already patterned and making neurons before the thalamic axons arrive.

The map raises three puzzles.

1. **Where do the borders come from**, in a sheet that shows none?
2. **What tells a progenitor where it sits**, and can it be changed so that a border moves?
3. **What does input from the thalamus add**, if the sheet has already been marked?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Position draws the sketch; input finishes it. Each progenitor's position is recorded as levels of graded signals (FGF8 from the front) and of transcription factors, proteins that switch genes on or off (such as Emx2 and COUP-TFI). In this lesson's working model, a border falls where a level crosses a threshold, so a second source of the signal can repeat part of the pattern; real borders come from several interacting signals and factors. Thalamic input then sharpens the sketch, tells primary areas from higher-order ones, and helps shape the maps inside each area.

**The strongest evidence for a factor.** If losing a factor and adding more of it move borders in *opposite* directions, the factor is helping to allocate territory. A missing converse result does not rule a factor out, since redundancy or buffering can hide it.

**What the rule covers.** Where the primary-area borders sit front to back in the mouse cortex, as read from marker genes and thalamic connections at birth and from the barrel field in the first postnatal week. Its limits come at the end.

## Two answers, and what each predicts

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Two answers were proposed a year apart. In 1988 Pasko Rakic proposed the **protomap**. Cortical progenitors lie in the ventricular zone, the layer of the brain wall next to the fluid-filled ventricle, and newborn neurons climb outward along radial glia, long scaffold cells that span the wall. Neurons born from one patch of the ventricular zone therefore stack up in a column above it. If the patches already differ by position, the outline of the areas is laid down where the cells are made, like a city zoned into districts before a single house is built.

In 1989 Dennis O'Leary proposed the **protocortex**. Here the young cortex is a sheet with broadly similar potential, and areas emerge mainly from the axons that arrive from the thalamus. Different thalamic nuclei send axons to different parts of the sheet, and the input tells each part what to become.

On the left, three differently tinted patches of progenitors each send up a radial glial fibre with a column of same-tinted neurons. On the right, a uniform sheet where thalamic axon bundles arrive and the spots above them take on different tints." caption="Two historical hypotheses for where cortical areas come from. Protomap: progenitors at each patch of the ventricular zone already differ, and their neurons stack in a column above them along radial glia. Protocortex: the young sheet is much alike and thalamic axons shape each part. Both were partly right; colours are schematic." />

| | Protomap (Rakic, 1988) | Protocortex (O'Leary, 1989) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where the first outline lies | in the progenitors | in the thalamic axons that arrive |
| What it predicts | molecular differences across the progenitors, and some map that forms without thalamic input | young cortex given new input takes on features that match the input |

Both predictions came true in part: the map is drawn twice, sketched by the sheet and refined by its inputs.

### Reading a map in a newborn

First, how the maps were read. Mice lacking Emx2 or Pax6 die around birth, before the areas mature, so "an area" in those experiments means a proxy.

- **Marker genes.** In situ hybridization shows where a gene is switched on in a tissue section (see the techniques for this lesson). Some genes have expression borders at birth that line up with future areas. If a marker border moves in a mutant, the marker map has moved.
- **Connections with the thalamus.** A thalamic nucleus is a cluster of neurons, and each nucleus projects mainly to its own part of cortex. Dye placed in one spot of cortex labels the thalamic neurons that project there, sometimes in more than one nucleus, so you can ask whether a spot is wired like visual or like somatosensory cortex.
- **The barrel field.** In layer 4 of rodent somatosensory cortex, clusters of cells called barrels, one for each large whisker, sit in rows that copy the whisker rows on the snout. In mice that survive past birth, a stain for cytochrome oxidase on flattened cortex shows them in the first postnatal week as dark patches, a sharp landmark for where somatosensory cortex sits front to back.

Animals that survive, such as the grafted rats and rewired ferrets below, also allow later anatomy and adult function to be measured. Read each result at the age it was measured.

## A lamp at the front: FGF8

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

A gradient needs a source. In the mouse, part of it comes from signalling centres at the edges of the cortical sheet: the cortical hem at the medial edge, which releases Wnt and BMP proteins, and a source of the growth factor FGF8 at the front. FGF8 acts through FGF receptors, which are receptor tyrosine kinases (see [that lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)).

Two kinds of test ask whether a signal places a border.

- A test of whether it is **necessary**: remove or block it and look for the loss or shift of what it controls.
- A test of whether it is **sufficient**: add it where it is normally absent, or add more of it, and look for a structure induced, enlarged or moved.

In 2001 Tomomi Fukuchi-Shimogori and Elizabeth Grove ran both kinds of test on FGF signalling in mouse embryos, adding FGF8 itself in the gain experiments and a receptor-fragment blocker in the loss experiment. They used electroporation, injecting DNA into the brain and driving it into progenitors at a chosen spot with brief electric pulses. Their readout was the barrel field.

Think of FGF8 as a lamp at the front of a room: cells judge how far forward they sit by how bright it is.

**Predict.** Add extra FGF8 at the front, so the lamp burns brighter. Which way should the barrel field move?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Toward the back. Under a brighter lamp, cells read themselves as farther forward than they are, so each border falls farther back. That is what Fukuchi-Shimogori and Grove found. Later work (Toyoda and colleagues, 2010) showed that FGF8 can act directly on cells at a distance from its source; cells also respond through the transcription factor gradients it helps set, the subject of the next section.

</details>

### Design problem

You can place an extra source of FGF8 anywhere in the mouse cortical primordium. Put one at the back, far from the normal source, and predict what happens to the barrel field. Then predict the map if you reduce FGF8 at the front instead. Say which result tests sufficiency and which tests necessity.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Near the new source, progenitors read a high FGF8 level, as if they sat near the front. Between the two sources the level rises toward each end, so, for a suitable source strength and distance, the profile dips in the middle and climbs again at the back. If FGF8 level sets identity, part of the pattern should repeat there, giving a second barrel field. That is what Fukuchi-Shimogori and Grove found: a partial second barrel field, posterior to the normal one. An extra source inducing an extra area is a test of sufficiency.

To reduce FGF8 at the front, add a blocker there. They used a soluble fragment of an FGF receptor, which binds FGF8, and probably related FGFs, before they reach the cells. With less of the signal, cells at the front read themselves as farther back, so the front areas should shrink and the posterior areas expand forward; the barrel field moved toward the front. That is a test of necessity.

More FGF8 and less FGF signalling move the field in opposite directions, the strongest kind of evidence.

</details>

### How far does a border move?

A simple model makes the lamp quantitative. In 2010 Grove's laboratory (Toyoda and colleagues) measured FGF8 protein in the early mouse cortex and found that it falls off roughly exponentially beyond a plateau near the source. The model borrows that shape; its decay length and thresholds are illustrative. Let the level fall exponentially from the front, C = C₀e^(−x/λ), where x is the distance from the front and λ, the **decay length**, is the distance over which the level falls to about 37 percent (1/e). A border sits where the level equals that border's threshold T, at x = λ ln(C₀/T).

**Predict.** Double the source, from C₀ to 2C₀, and leave the thresholds alone. Does a border near the lamp move farther than a border far from it?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. With the decay length and thresholds fixed, every border that stays on the sheet moves back by the same distance, λ ln 2, about 0.7 decay lengths, because ln(2C₀/T) = ln(C₀/T) + ln 2 whatever the threshold. Halving the source moves each border forward by the same amount, and a border pushed past an edge is lost. With λ set at a fifth of the sheet and three borders, A, B and C, placed a quarter, a half and three quarters of the way back:

| Border | Normal source | Doubled | Halved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A (front) | 0.25 | 0.39 | 0.11 |
| B | 0.50 | 0.64 | 0.36 |
| C (back) | 0.75 | 0.89 | 0.61 |

Positions are fractions of the sheet's length from the front. The four bands are abstract; the real auditory area lies off this axis. The bands between A and C keep their width; the front band grows and the back band shrinks, or the reverse.

</details>

Put sizes on it. If λ were a fifth of a 5 mm cortex, 1 mm (again illustrative), a doubling would move a border about 0.7 mm, comparable in size to the hypothetical 0.6 mm shift in the MCAT practice question. The measured decay is much steeper relative to the sheet; this λ is chosen for easy arithmetic.

The equal shifts come from the exponential shape. If the level instead fell along a straight line, a doubling would move borders by different distances at different positions (worked in Going deeper). What survives either shape is the direction of the shift and its graded response to dose.

## The cells' own record of position

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

FGF8 is a signal outside the cells. Inside them, position is recorded as levels of transcription factors that bind DNA. Two of them run in opposite directions along the front-to-back axis of the mouse sheet. **Emx2** is highest at the back. **Pax6** is highest at the front. Each also varies from the midline outward, and two more factors join them:

| Factor | High along front-to-back | High along medial-to-lateral |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emx2 | back | medial |
| Pax6 | front | lateral |
| COUP-TFI (also called NR2F1) | back | lateral |
| Sp8 | front | medial |

The record and the lamp are linked: reducing Fgf8 shifted early gradients of gene expression toward the front (Garel, Huffman and Rubenstein, 2003). That link helps explain how a single source at one edge can shift gene expression across the whole sheet, though not every readout of the map moves with it. Whether Emx2 also acts back on FGF signalling is still argued.

A gradient is only a correlation. To show that a factor helps place the borders, run both tests from the last section: remove it, and add more of it.

**Predict.** Emx2 is highest at the back. Delete it, and the cells at the back lose part of their record of being at the back. Which way should the borders move, and which areas should grow?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Back: the front-and-middle areas, motor and somatosensory, grow toward the back and the visual area shrinks. In 2000 Kathie Bishop, Guy Goudreau and O'Leary found this in mice lacking Emx2. The same year Antonello Mallamaci, Edoardo Boncinelli and colleagues independently reported shifts of area identity in the early cortex of mice lacking Emx2. In mice without working Pax6 (the Small eye mutant), the shift went the other way: the front areas shrank and the visual area expanded forward.

</details>

**Predict.** Now raise Emx2 across the progenitors while keeping its normal slope. What result would show that Emx2 *allocates* territory, rather than simply being needed for cortex to grow?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The map should move opposite to the knockout, with the total cortex about the same size. In 2004 Tadashi Hamasaki and colleagues in O'Leary's laboratory found this: the visual area grew and the front areas shrank, with no significant change in the total area of cortex. Emx2 shows both halves of the test, so it allocates territory rather than simply permitting cortex to exist.

</details>

| Factor | Where it is high | Lose it | Raise it | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Emx2 | back | front-and-middle areas grow, visual shrinks (2000) | visual grows, front shrinks, total size unchanged (2004) | allocates territory |
| COUP-TFI | back (and lateral) | frontal motor areas spread across most of the sheet (cortex-only knockout, 2007) | not covered here | needed for sensory territory |
| Pax6 | front | front areas shrink, visual grows (markers, 2000) | map barely moves (2007) | loss and gain disagree; role in placing borders unsettled |

COUP-TFI tells a similar story for the loss half. In 2007 Michèle Studer's laboratory removed it from cortex alone, in a conditional knockout (a gene deleted only in chosen cells), and the sensory areas were squeezed toward the back as motor areas spread.

Pax6 is the cautionary case: its gain does not mirror its loss. In 2007 David Price's group raised Pax6 up to three times its normal level and the area map barely moved, and in 2008 a knockout limited to cortex shifted the marker genes while the area-specific connections with the thalamus stayed normal. Mice without Pax6 also have other forebrain defects. So Pax6 may bias how markers are expressed more than it decides where an area sits. A later cortex-only knockout from O'Leary's laboratory (Zembrzycki and colleagues, 2013) did report a smaller somatosensory area, so Pax6's part in placing borders is still unsettled.

## Input finishes the drawing

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

The sheet sketches a map before thalamic axons arrive. Does the sketch need them at all?

**Predict.** In mice lacking the transcription factor Gbx2, thalamic axons never reach the cortex. If areas emerged only from thalamic input, as a strict protocortex would have it, what should the marker borders look like at birth?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

They should be missing or misplaced, and they were not. In 1999 Emily Miyashita-Lin, John Rubenstein and colleagues found that several area marker genes still had their normal borders at birth in Gbx2 mutants. The marker sketch forms without thalamic input. The limit is the one from the readout: markers at birth are a sketch, and adult borders could still need input.

</details>

Input then shapes much of what the sketch becomes. In 1991 Bradley Schlaggar and O'Leary grafted visual cortex from fetal rats into the somatosensory cortex of newborn rats. Thalamic axons that carry whisker signals grew into the graft, and the graft formed barrels. Tissue bound for visual cortex could still build a feature of another area when given that area's input.

The ferret experiments from Mriganka Sur's laboratory went further. Ferrets are born with immature visual pathways, so in newborns the retina's normal targets could be reduced and the normal inputs to the auditory thalamus removed; retinal axons then grew into the auditory thalamus, and auditory cortex received visual activity. In 2000 Jitendra Sharma, Alessandra Angelucci and Sur reported that in adult ferrets this rewired auditory cortex held neurons tuned to edge orientation (each responding best to an edge at one angle), arranged in a map like that of visual cortex, though less regular. A companion paper reported that the animals responded to light seen through the rewired pathway as a visual stimulus.

Input also sharpens the sketch. In 2013 Shen-Ju Chou and colleagues in O'Leary's laboratory removed the axons from the visual thalamus by gene deletion in mice, and the genetic distinction between primary visual cortex and the higher-order visual areas around it, which further process what primary visual cortex passes on, failed to develop. Spontaneous and sensory activity then refine the maps inside each area, the subject of later lessons in this course.

## Putting it together

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

The manipulations in this lesson test different things: a border's position, a marker pattern, connections, or a map inside an area.

| Manipulation | What the map did | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More FGF8 at the front | barrel field moved back | more FGF8 is enough to move a border |
| FGF8 blocked at the front | barrel field moved forward | FGF signalling at the front, by FGF8 and probably related FGFs, is needed for its position |
| New FGF8 source at the back | partial second barrel field | the starkest sufficiency test: part of the pattern repeats |
| No Emx2 | front-and-middle areas grew back, visual shrank | the loss half of the test |
| More Emx2, same slope | visual grew, front shrank, total size unchanged | the gain half: opposite shifts, so Emx2 allocates territory |
| No Pax6, or more Pax6 | without it, front areas shrank and visual grew (markers); excess barely moved the map | loss and gain do not mirror each other |
| No thalamic axons (Gbx2) | marker borders in place at birth | the marker sketch does not need input |
| New input (graft, rewired ferret) | barrels in visual tissue; an orientation map in auditory cortex | input shapes what an area builds |

Back to the three puzzles. Borders come from graded levels in the progenitors crossing thresholds, in the working model (1). Edge signals such as FGF8 set those levels and factors such as Emx2 record them, so changing either moves borders (2). Input sharpens borders, separates primary from higher-order areas, and helps build the maps inside them (3).

**Where the rule stops.** It does not cover total cortical growth, which some of the same factors, Pax6 among them, also change. Its threshold model does not explain the maps inside an area, such as barrels and orientation modules; those depend on input and activity working with the cortex's own cells. And it is drawn from mice, so it does not claim to explain area sizes in primates or humans.

One question stays open. Gradients are smooth, yet mature borders are sharp. How the conversion happens, and how much of it in progenitors and how much in neurons under thalamic input, is not yet fully understood.

## Going deeper

### Pax6, the eye gene

Pax6 has an older fame: it is needed to build the eye. Mice with one broken copy have small eyes, which named the Small eye mutant; mice with two broken copies have no eyes. People with one broken copy of *PAX6* have aniridia, an eye lacking most of its iris. When the fly version, *eyeless*, was switched on in places where it is normally off, flies grew eyes on their legs and wings. That is sufficiency in its starkest form. In the cortex its role is less clear: it may bias position within a tissue that already exists. The 2007 overexpression study also reported that the added Pax6 turned down the cell's own Pax6 gene.

### Does Emx2 act through FGF signalling?

In 2003 Fukuchi-Shimogori and Grove reported that Emx2 shapes the map by regulating FGF signaling. O'Leary's group disagreed: when they raised Emx2 in progenitors, Fgf8 did not change, and they argued that Emx2 acts on the progenitors directly. Both routes may contribute.

### A shifted map, or a smaller sheet?

Later work, including a 2002 study from Luca Muzio, Mallamaci and colleagues, suggested that the late area changes in both kinds of mutant, Emx2 and Pax6, may partly reflect a poorly growing cortical sheet and not only a shifted map, since several of these factors also change progenitor proliferation. This is one reason the Emx2 gain result, with total cortical area unchanged, carries so much weight.

### The other two points of the compass

COUP-TFI belongs to the nuclear receptor family but has no known ligand, so it is called an orphan receptor (see [that lesson](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/)). This lesson's argument runs along the front-to-back axis, where Emx2 and Pax6 form the clearest pair of opposing gradients, though their experiments differ in how cleanly they can be read.

### What the threshold model does and does not say

In the exponential model, multiplying the source by any factor adds λ ln(factor) to every border's position; a tripled source moves every border that stays on the sheet back by λ ln 3, about 1.1 decay lengths. A straight-line profile behaves differently. If the level falls as C = C₀(1 − x/L) across a sheet of length L, doubling C₀ moves the three borders of the earlier table back by 0.375, 0.25 and 0.125 of the sheet. Halving it, compared with the normal source, moves them forward twice as far, by 0.75, 0.5 and 0.25. That would push border A off the sheet (to −0.5) and park border B exactly at the front edge. (Halving a doubled source simply restores the original.)

A second source in the same model adds to the level everywhere between the two sources, so the lowest-level bands between them can shrink or vanish. Near the new source the level climbs again, and any threshold it reaches is crossed a second time, in reverse order. Which bands repeat depends on the new source's strength and distance. With the same decay length and thresholds, and a source a tenth as strong at the back edge, the model loses its lowest band (no level falls below the lowest threshold) and gains a narrow band at the back edge with the same level range as the second band from the front. That would resemble a partial repeat, but it is a prediction of the toy model, not a reported result; the reported result is a partial second barrel field behind the first.
