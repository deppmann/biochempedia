techniques:
  - name: "In situ hybridization for area markers"
    blurb: >-
      A labelled strand of RNA or DNA binds the messenger RNA of one gene in a tissue section,
      showing where that gene is on. Genes whose expression borders line up with future areas let
      you read an area map at birth, before the areas can be recognized by their anatomy or
      function.
  - name: "In utero electroporation"
    blurb: >-
      DNA is injected into the fluid-filled ventricle of an embryo's brain and brief electric
      pulses drive it into the progenitor cells next to the positive electrode. It adds a gene, or
      a blocker, to one chosen spot of the cortical sheet in a living mouse embryo.
  - name: "Cytochrome oxidase staining of flattened cortex"
    blurb: >-
      The cortex is removed, flattened and cut parallel to its surface, and stained for a
      mitochondrial enzyme that is rich where thalamic axons end. In rodent somatosensory cortex
      the stain shows the barrels as dark patches, a sharp landmark for where an area lies.
  - name: "Cortical transplantation"
    blurb: >-
      A piece of young cortex from one area is grafted into another area of a host animal. What
      the graft becomes, under its new inputs, tests whether its identity was already fixed or
      could still be set from outside.

**In the adult mouse, a patch of cortex near the middle of each hemisphere maps the whiskers and a patch near the back maps what the eyes see. In the embryo, the sheet that will hold both looks the same from front to back.**

The cerebral cortex is the sheet of neurons on the surface of the forebrain. In 1909 Korbinian Brodmann divided the human cortex into numbered areas by how its cells were packed and layered, and many of his borders match borders of function: his area 17 is primary visual cortex, the first cortical stop for signals from the eyes. Each area has its own inputs and outputs, and many sensory areas hold an internal map of the body or the world. Yet the cortex grows from a smooth sheet of progenitors, cells that divide to make neurons, with no clear borders. Where do the areas come from? The process is called arealization.

## The idea: a map drawn twice, by the sheet and by its inputs

Two answers were proposed a year apart. In 1988 Pasko Rakic proposed the protomap. Cortical progenitors lie in the ventricular zone, the layer of the brain wall next to the fluid-filled ventricle, and newborn neurons climb outward along radial glia, long scaffold cells that span the wall. Neurons born from one patch of the ventricular zone therefore stack up in a column above it. If the patches already differ by position, the outline of the areas is laid down where the cells are made, like a city zoned into districts before a single house is built.

In 1989 Dennis O'Leary proposed the protocortex. Here the young cortex is a sheet with broadly similar potential, and areas emerge mainly from the axons that arrive from the thalamus, the relay station that passes signals from the eyes, ears and skin to the cortex. Different thalamic nuclei send axons to different parts of the sheet, and the input tells each part what to become.

Each hypothesis predicts a result you can look for. The protomap predicts molecular differences across the progenitors, and some map that forms without thalamic input. The protocortex predicts that young cortex given new input will take on features that match the input. Both predictions came true in part, which is why the field now treats the map as drawn twice: a sketch from the sheet, then a refined copy shaped by its inputs.

## Opposing gradients: Emx2 and Pax6

Cortical progenitors do differ by position. Two transcription factors, proteins that bind DNA and switch genes on or off, are made in opposing gradients across the mouse sheet. Emx2 is highest at the back and along the medial edge, near the midline. Pax6 is highest at the front and along the lateral edge. Two more factors join them. COUP-TFI (also called NR2F1) is highest at the back and lateral side; it belongs to the nuclear receptor family but has no known ligand, so it is called an orphan receptor (see [that lesson](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/)). Sp8 is highest at the front and medial side. In effect, each progenitor reads its position from the mix of these proteins it holds.

A gradient is only a correlation. To show that a factor matters, you remove it and look for a change, a test of whether it is "Necessary". In 2000 Kathie Bishop, Guy Goudreau and O'Leary reported that test for Emx2 and Pax6. In mice without Emx2, the front areas, motor and somatosensory, expanded toward the back, and the visual area shrank. In mice without working Pax6 (the Small eye mutant), the shift went the other way: front areas shrank and the visual area expanded forward. The same year Antonello Mallamaci, Edoardo Boncinelli and colleagues independently reported shifts of area identity in the early cortex of mice lacking Emx2.

Read these results with their limits. Both kinds of mutant mice die around birth, before the areas mature, so the "areas" were read from marker genes whose expression borders at birth line up with future areas, and from the early connections between cortex and thalamus. Mice without Pax6 have other forebrain defects as well. Pax6 also failed the test that Emx2 later passed: in 2007 David Price's group raised Pax6 up to three times its normal level and the area map barely moved, and in 2008 a knockout limited to cortex shifted the marker genes while the area-specific connections with the thalamus stayed normal. Later work, including a 2002 study from Luca Muzio, Mallamaci and colleagues, suggested that the late area changes in both kinds of mutant may partly reflect a poorly growing cortical sheet and not only a shifted map.

The Emx2 result passed a sharper test. In 2004 Tadashi Hamasaki and colleagues in O'Leary's laboratory raised the level of Emx2 in progenitors while keeping its normal slope. The map moved the opposite way from the knockout: the visual area grew and the front areas shrank, with no significant change in the total area of cortex. A factor whose loss and whose excess move a border in opposite directions is allocating territory, not simply permitting cortex to exist. COUP-TFI tells a similar story. In 2007 Michèle Studer's laboratory removed it from cortex alone, in a conditional knockout (a mouse in which a gene is deleted only in chosen cells), and frontal motor areas spread across most of the sheet while the sensory areas were squeezed toward the back.

Pax6 has an older fame. It is needed to build the eye. Mice with one broken copy have small eyes, which named the Small eye mutant; mice with two broken copies have no eyes. People with one broken copy of *PAX6* have aniridia, an eye lacking most of its iris. When the fly version, *eyeless*, was switched on in places where it is normally off, flies grew eyes on their legs and wings. That is the other kind of test, "Sufficient": adding a gene where it is normally absent induces or enlarges a structure. In the cortex the same protein does a quieter job, biasing position within a tissue that already exists.

## Moving a border with FGF8

A gradient of transcription factors needs a source of position. In the mouse, part of it comes from signaling centres at the edges of the cortical sheet: the cortical hem at the medial edge, which releases Wnt and BMP proteins, and a source of the growth factor FGF8 at the front. FGF8 acts through FGF receptors, which are receptor tyrosine kinases (see [that lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)).

In 2001 Tomomi Fukuchi-Shimogori and Elizabeth Grove tested FGF8 in mouse embryos by electroporation: they injected DNA encoding FGF8 into the brain and used brief electric pulses to drive it into progenitors at a chosen spot. Their readout was the barrel field. In the somatosensory cortex of rodents, layer 4 holds clusters of cells called barrels, one for each large whisker, laid out in rows that copy the whisker rows on the snout. The field can be seen in the first postnatal week and makes a sharp landmark.

Extra FGF8 added at the front shifted the barrel field toward the back. Think of FGF8 as a lamp at the front of a room: cells read how bright it is to judge how far forward they sit, so a brighter lamp makes every cell think it sits farther forward than it does.

### Design problem

You can place an extra source of FGF8 anywhere in the mouse cortical primordium. Put one at the back, far from the normal source, and predict what happens to the barrel field. Then predict the map if you reduce FGF8 at the front instead. Say which result tests sufficiency and which tests necessity.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Near the new source at the back, progenitors now read a high FGF8 level, as if they sat near the front. Cells between the two sources read a level that rises toward each end. If FGF8 level sets identity, the sequence of areas should repeat outward from the new source, so a second barrel field should form toward the back, repeating part of the first. That is what Fukuchi-Shimogori and Grove found: a partial second barrel field, posterior to the normal one. An extra source inducing an extra area is a test of sufficiency.

To reduce FGF8 at the front, add a blocker there. They used a soluble fragment of an FGF receptor, which binds FGF8, and probably related FGFs, before they reach the cells. With less of the signal, cells at the front read themselves as farther back, so the front areas should shrink and the posterior areas expand forward. The barrel field moved toward the front. Removing part of a signal and losing what it controls is a test of necessity.

</details>

The signal and the gradients are linked, though how is still argued. In 2003 Fukuchi-Shimogori and Grove reported that Emx2 shapes the map by regulating FGF signaling. O'Leary's group disagreed: when they raised Emx2 in progenitors, Fgf8 did not change, and they argued that Emx2 acts on the progenitors directly. Both routes may contribute. In a separate study, reducing Fgf8 shifted early gradients of gene expression toward the front (Garel, Huffman and Rubenstein, 2003). That cross-regulation helps explain how a single source at one edge can shift gene expression across the whole sheet, though not every readout of the map moves with it.

## Input matters too: transplanted and rewired cortex

Genes sketch the map, but input shapes much of it. In 1991 Bradley Schlaggar and O'Leary grafted visual cortex from fetal rats into the somatosensory cortex of newborn rats. Thalamic axons that carry whisker signals grew into the graft, and the graft formed barrels. Tissue that would have become visual cortex could still build a feature of a different area when it received that area's input.

The ferret experiments from Mriganka Sur's laboratory went further. Ferrets are born with immature visual pathways, so in newborns the retina's normal targets could be reduced and the normal inputs to the auditory thalamus removed, and retinal axons then grew into the auditory thalamus. Auditory cortex received visual activity. In 2000 Jitendra Sharma, Alessandra Angelucci and Sur reported that in adult ferrets this rewired auditory cortex held neurons tuned to edge orientation, arranged in a map like that of visual cortex, though less regular. A companion paper reported that the animals responded to light seen through the rewired pathway as a visual stimulus.

The protomap is not undone by these results. In mice lacking the transcription factor Gbx2, thalamic axons never reach the cortex, yet in 1999 Emily Miyashita-Lin, John Rubenstein and colleagues found that several area marker genes still had their normal borders at birth. The sketch forms without input. Later experiments in mice suggest that thalamic input then sharpens it: when thalamic axons from the visual thalamus were removed by gene deletion, the genetic distinction between primary visual cortex and the higher-order visual areas around it failed to develop. Spontaneous and sensory activity then refine the maps inside each area, the subject of later lessons in this course.
