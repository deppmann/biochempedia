    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Hubel was born in Windsor, Ontario, and took his medical degree at McGill University in
      1951. After clinical training and a period of research on cortical recording, he joined
      Stephen Kuffler's group at Johns Hopkins, where his collaboration with Torsten Wiesel
      began. Kuffler had shown that retinal ganglion cells have centre-surround
      receptive fields. The obvious next question was what the cortex does with them. Hubel and
      Wiesel recorded extracellularly from single cells in the visual cortex of anaesthetized,

      orientation drove cells hard. Their 1959 and 1962 papers in the Journal of Physiology

      from the other. In 1968 they extended the work to macaque and spider monkeys. Hubel and
      Wiesel shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Roger Sperry. Their
      method, a bar of light and an electrode, made receptive-field mapping the standard way to
      ask what a cortical cell encodes.

    story: >-
      Wiesel was born in Uppsala, Sweden, and took his medical degree at the Karolinska
      Institute in 1954. He moved to Johns Hopkins to work in Stephen Kuffler's group, where he
      and David Hubel began recording together. In the 1962 paper on cat visual cortex
      they did more than classify cells. They tested each cell through each eye separately and
      reported that most of the cells in their sample were influenced by both eyes,
      often unequally. Their electrode tracks showed that cells met along a penetration made at

      preference along the same penetrations and found that the two kinds of column had
      boundaries that seemed to be independent. Wiesel and Hubel also used eye closure in
      kittens to study how visual experience shapes these inputs, work covered in the
      developmental neuroscience course. Their binocular measurements gave later work on the
      development of vision a quantity to track.

      vision and a dorsal occipitoparietal pathway for spatial vision.

    story: >-
      Ungerleider worked in Mortimer Mishkin's laboratory at the National Institute of Mental
      Health, where monkeys were trained on two kinds of visual task. In an object
      discrimination, the monkey learned which of two objects covered a food well. In a
      landmark task, it learned to choose the food well closer to a landmark, whatever the
      objects looked like. Removing inferior temporal cortex impaired the first kind of task;
      removing posterior parietal cortex impaired the second. Tracing the connections from
      striate cortex outward gave two routes, one running down into the temporal lobe and one
      running up into the parietal lobe. In a 1982 book chapter, "Two cortical visual systems",
      Ungerleider and Mishkin proposed that the ventral route serves object vision and the
      dorsal route serves spatial vision, a scheme soon shortened to "what" and "where". It was
      a synthesis and a working hypothesis. Ungerleider later turned to functional imaging in
      humans with colleagues. The framework gave the dozens of extrastriate areas a first
      organizing principle that experiments could test.
  - name: "Melvyn Goodale"
    contribution: >-
      With David Milner, Lorna Jakobson and David Carey, showed that patient D.F. could scale her
      grasp to objects whose width she could not report, and with Milner proposed that the two
      streams differ in what vision is used for: perception versus action.

    story: >-
      D.F. is a patient with visual form agnosia after brain damage: her colour vision was largely preserved, but she had great difficulty reporting the shape, size or orientation of objects.
      Goodale and his colleagues noticed that her actions seemed better than her reports. To
      test it, they gave her flat plaques of equal area that differed in width. Asked to show
      the width of a plaque by opening her finger and thumb, she did poorly. Asked to reach out
      and pick the same plaque up, she opened her hand in proportion to its width as she
      reached, as control participants did. The objects and the viewing were the
      same; only the task, and so the use of the information, differed. Their 1991 letter in Nature interpreted
      the result as separate processing for different uses of visual information, not for
      different subsets of it. In 1992 Goodale and Milner proposed that the ventral stream
      serves perception and the dorsal stream serves the visual control of action, a revision
      of "what versus where". The work made the response task itself a variable in visual
      neuroscience.

techniques:
  - name: "Extracellular single-unit recording"
    blurb: >-
      A fine electrode placed near a neuron records its action potentials without entering the
      cell. Moving and rotating a stimulus while counting spikes maps the cell's receptive field
      and tuning. Each isolated unit is one neuron, though one electrode can pick up a few units at once, and the method favours large, active cells.
  - name: "2-deoxyglucose metabolic mapping"
    blurb: >-
      A radioactive glucose analogue is taken up by active neurons and trapped. If an animal
      views a chosen pattern during the infusion, autoradiographs of the cortex show where
      activity was high, giving a map of the whole area for that stimulus. It measures
      metabolism summed over the session, not spikes or connections.
  - name: "Two-photon calcium imaging"
    blurb: >-
      A calcium-sensitive indicator in cortical neurons is imaged through a cranial window with
      a focused near-infrared laser that excites the indicator mainly in a tiny focal volume, scanned across the tissue. Hundreds to thousands of cells can
      be followed at once, so the preferences of neighbouring cells can be compared directly.
  - name: "Lesions and matched perception and action tasks"
    blurb: >-
      In monkeys, a cortical area is removed and performance on tasks that depend on object
      identity or spatial position is compared. In patients, the same object is used for a
      perceptual report and for an action, so a dissociation points to different uses of the
      same visual input.

      Nasal-retina axons cross at the chiasm, and because the lens inverts the image they carry

      - "A complex cell that keeps orientation tuning"
      - "An unselective cell driven by any bright edge"
      - "A geniculate relay cell with a large surround"
      - "A simple cell with one large ON subregion"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Position tolerance with retained orientation selectivity, and no mappable ON and OFF
      subregions, fits Hubel and Wiesel's 1962 description of a complex cell. A simple cell has
      separate ON and OFF subregions, and moving a bar across them changes its response. A

**Cut one optic nerve and one eye goes blind. Cut one optic tract, a few centimetres further back, and both eyes lose the same half of the world.**

The difference comes from one crossing, and it is the first of two puzzles. The second: [the retina](/lessons/retina/) sends the brain a mosaic of centre-surround signals that small spots drive well, yet in cat visual cortex spots often did little, while a bar of light at the right angle drove some cells hard. One rule answers both.

## Two puzzles and one rule

The signals end in **primary visual cortex**, called **V1** or **striate cortex**. Striate means striped: a pale band of myelinated fibres runs through it, visible to the naked eye. V1 turns the retina's spots into oriented edges, lays them out as a map, and hands them on to dozens of further areas.

1. **Where.** Why does a cut nerve blind one eye, while a cut tract blinds the same half of the world in both eyes?
2. **What.** Why does a bar beat a spot in cortex?

The rule needs two terms. A neuron's **receptive field** is the patch of the visual field where a stimulus changes its firing. In an **ON region** of the field light excites the cell, and in an **OFF region** light inhibits it.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** For many cells early on the path (retinal ganglion cells, LGN cells and V1 simple cells), a good first approximation is that the cell adds up the light falling on its receptive field: plus on ON regions, minus on OFF regions.
>
> - A bigger sum means stronger drive, and the cell turns drive into firing.
> - A sum near zero leaves the spontaneous rate; a positive sum raises firing once it clears the cell's threshold, and a negative sum lowers whatever firing there is, down to zero.
> - *Where* the patch sits tells you which part of the world the cell reports. *How the plus and minus regions are laid out* tells you which stimulus it reports best.

The rule has a scope, and the lesson marks where it stops.

- It is an approximation for those cells, for steady light measured against a fixed background; some retinal and LGN cells already add light nonlinearly.
- It does not explain **complex cells** (below).
- It leaves out surround suppression, contrast saturation, attention, movement signals and the two cortical streams.

Phototransduction belongs to [senses and cGMP](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/), and receptive-field logic across the senses to [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/).

## Where: every fibre reports one patch of the world

The "where" half of the rule is the tool for the pathway: each ganglion-cell fibre carries the signal from one patch of the world, so following the fibres tells you what a cut removes.

The **visual field** is the part of the world one eye sees while it holds still. It has a nasal half (toward the nose) and a temporal half (toward the temple). The lens inverts the image, so the temporal field falls on the nasal retina and the upper field on the lower retina. Most mistakes here come from mixing field and retina.

Ganglion-cell axons gather at the optic disc and leave the eye as the **optic nerve**. At the **optic chiasm** of humans and other primates, only the fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross to the other side. Temporal-retina fibres stay **ipsilateral** (on the same side). In animals with sideways-facing eyes, such as mice, most fibres cross.

| Eye | Field half | Falls on | At the chiasm | Joins |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Left | left (temporal) field | nasal retina | crosses | right optic tract |
| Left | right (nasal) field | temporal retina | stays | left optic tract |
| Right | right (temporal) field | nasal retina | crosses | left optic tract |
| Right | left (nasal) field | temporal retina | stays | right optic tract |

Read down the last column. Behind the chiasm, each **optic tract** carries the **contralateral** (opposite) visual hemifield from both eyes: the left tract carries everything to the right of the point of gaze. The left hemisphere does not serve the right eye; it serves the right half of the world, seen by both eyes.

**Predict.** Which half of the world does the right optic tract carry, and from which eyes? What would cutting it do?

<details>

The right tract carries the left half of the world from both eyes. The left eye's nasal-retina fibres, which see its temporal (left) field, crossed to join it. The right eye's temporal-retina fibres, which see its nasal (left) field, stayed on their own side and joined it too. Cutting the right tract therefore removes the left half of the field in both eyes.

</details>

The tract ends mainly in the **lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN)** of the thalamus, a relay that keeps the two eyes' inputs in separate layers. LGN axons fan out as the **optic radiations** to V1, along the **calcarine sulcus**, a deep fold on the inner face of the occipital lobe. The radiations split. Fibres for the upper field sweep forward through the temporal lobe in **Meyer's loop** and end below the calcarine sulcus; fibres for the lower field run through the parietal lobe and end above it. The upper field lands on the lower retina, and that lower-for-upper order holds all the way to cortex.

### Four defects

Lesion logic from [the nervous system map](/lessons/nervous-system-map/) applies: **along the pathway to V1, a lesion removes the signals of the fibres it cuts, so a complete cut blinds the patches of world those fibres covered.** In human anatomy that gives four defects.

- **Optic nerve:** loss of the whole field of the eye on the same side.
- **Chiasm, centre:** the crossing nasal fibres of both eyes are cut, so both temporal fields are lost. This is a **bitemporal hemianopia**; a hemianopia is the loss of half the field.
- **Optic tract, radiation or V1 on one side:** the opposite half of the field in both eyes, a **homonymous hemianopia** (homonymous means the same side of the field in each eye).
- **One branch of the radiation:** a quarter field, a **quadrantanopia**. Meyer's loop damage gives an upper quarter defect on the opposite side, reported after temporal-lobe surgery; parietal radiation damage gives a lower quarter.

A partial lesion gives a partial defect, and real defects are less tidy than this list: close matching between the eyes, or sparing of central vision, are tendencies only.

## What: adding up the light

Now the second puzzle. Its first case is one you met in [the retina](/lessons/retina/). An ON-centre ganglion cell has a small round centre where light excites it and a ring around it, the surround, where light inhibits it.

**Predict.** Rank three stimuli for an ON-centre ganglion cell: a small spot that just fills the centre, diffuse light over the whole field, and a spot covering only the surround.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Light on the surround alone lands only on minus, so firing drops below the spontaneous rate. LGN cells have fields of the same centre-surround kind, with little preference for orientation. So the obvious next question in the late 1950s was what cortex does with them.

## Why a bar beats a spot

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel recorded extracellularly from single cells in the visual cortex of anaesthetized, light-adapted cats: an electrode just outside one cell picked up its spikes, and they counted them while they projected spots, slits and bars of light onto a screen in front of the animal. Spots often did little; a slit or bar at the right angle drove some cells hard. Their 1962 paper in the Journal of Physiology described two main classes, and showed that for the class they called **simple cells**, the layout of the field predicts which stimulus drives the cell.

In simple cells that answered small, stationary spots, they mapped the field spot by spot (they also used moving slits and edges). The field is split into **ON subregions**, where light excites, and **OFF subregions**, where light inhibits and its removal excites. The subregions are long and narrow and lie side by side. A common layout is a narrow ON strip with an OFF strip on each side. Light covering an ON and an OFF region together tended to cancel.

Now apply the rule. Picture the field as a grid of squares, six squares tall: a centre column of six ON squares, each worth +1, with a column of six OFF squares on each side, each worth −0.5. The numbers are made up for teaching, but they are balanced the way many real simple cells roughly are: the OFF total equals the ON total. To turn a sum into firing, give this made-up cell no spontaneous firing and a threshold of 2: its rate is the sum minus 2 when the sum is above 2, and zero otherwise (arbitrary units).

**Predict.** Work out the sum for each stimulus, and decide which drives the cell: (a) a small spot covering one ON square; (b) a bar lying along the ON strip; (c) the same bar turned to lie across the field; (d) diffuse light over everything; (e) a bar lying along one OFF strip.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

| Stimulus | Squares covered | Sum | Rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (a) small spot on ON | one ON | +1 | 0 |
| (b) bar along the ON strip | six ON | +6 | 4 |
| (c) bar across the field | one ON, one OFF each side | +1 − 0.5 − 0.5 = 0 | 0 |
| (d) diffuse light | six ON, twelve OFF | +6 − 6 = 0 | 0 |
| (e) bar along one OFF strip | six OFF | −3 | 0 |

The aligned bar wins by a wide margin. The small spot's sum stays below this cell's threshold, one reason spots often did little in cortex; real simple cells could still give weak responses to spots, enough to map their subregions. The crossing bar and diffuse light cancel. The OFF-strip bar gives a negative sum: this toy cell is already silent, but a cell with spontaneous firing would slow down. (Real OFF regions also fire a burst when their light goes off, a response to change that the steady sum does not capture.)

</details>

So orientation selectivity is not a separate property added on top of the field. **To a first approximation, it comes from the shape of the field.** Turning the bar takes it off the ON strip and onto the OFF flanks, and the sum falls. Make the subregions longer while keeping their width, and the sum falls faster as the bar turns, so the cell is more sharply tuned; what counts is length relative to width. The threshold sharpens tuning further. Only the part of the sum above it makes spikes, like the tip of an iceberg above the water, so measured spike tuning is narrower than the field's sum alone predicts (Carandini and Ferster, 2000); applied without the threshold, the rule predicts tuning that is too broad. Circuits within cortex contribute too.

### How might such a field be built?

Hubel and Wiesel suggested an answer that follows from the rule. If several LGN cells whose ON centres lie in a row all excite one cortical cell, that cell gets an elongated ON strip. The surrounds of those LGN cells could add OFF flanks, and OFF-centre LGN cells lined up alongside could reinforce them. Their recordings measured response properties; the wiring was a proposal. Recordings in the 1990s supported the aligned-input step: ON-centre LGN cells line up with a simple cell's ON subregions and OFF-centre cells with its OFF subregions. Exactly how the flanks are built is less settled.

| 60 | 24 |

## Beyond one sum: complex cells

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Hubel and Wiesel's second class, **complex cells**, also preferred one orientation. Their behaviour shows where a single sum stops working.

**Predict.** Take the simple cell from the grid and slide its best bar sideways, off the ON strip and onto an OFF strip. What happens to the sum? Could a cell that does one sum keep firing wherever the bar lands?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The sum falls from +6 to −3 as the bar crosses onto an OFF strip, so this cell goes from firing hard to falling silent. A cell with this field cannot keep firing wherever the bar lands. And no single sum can be excited by both a bright and a dark bar at the same place, because reversing the contrast flips the sign of the sum.

</details>

Complex cells did. A correctly oriented bar drove them over much of the field. A bar at the wrong angle still did little, so they do not ignore orientation; they tolerate position. In many complex cells that answered stationary spots, turning a spot on and turning it off excited the same places, unlike the separate ON and OFF subregions of a simple cell. Small spots did little for other complex cells, and in some, ON and OFF responses could be told apart but did not add and cancel the way a simple cell's do.

Hubel and Wiesel's proposal applies the rule twice. Several simple cells share one orientation but have fields at slightly different positions, and all of them excite one complex cell. Where their excitatory responses overlap enough, at least one of them responds strongly as the bar shifts, so the complex cell keeps orientation and gains tolerance of position, with responses weakening at gaps and at the edges of the covered span. They offered this, too, as a proposal, and how far every complex cell follows it is still debated. Some complex cells receive direct LGN input, and intracortical circuits contribute.

## The map: retinotopy, magnification and columns

V1 is **retinotopic**: neighbouring points in the visual field excite neighbouring points in cortex, so the field is laid out as a map. This is the "where" half of the rule written across the cortical sheet. In humans the fovea, the centre of gaze, is represented at the occipital pole, posterior to the periphery. How that map is wired up during development is covered in [topographic maps](/lessons/topographic-maps/).

The map is distorted. **Cortical magnification** is the length of cortex, in millimetres, given to one degree of visual field. It is largest at the fovea, largely because more ganglion cells serve each degree there, so more cortex is needed to map them. It falls steeply with **eccentricity**, the distance from the centre of gaze. In macaque, Tootell and colleagues (1988) mapped V1 with 2-deoxyglucose, a glucose analogue that marks active cells, while the animals viewed patterns of known position, and reported a maximum near the foveal representation of about 15 mm per degree. Estimates depend on the animal, the location, the direction measured and the method, so 15 is a reported macaque value, not a universal constant.

On top of the map sit **columns**: groups of cells, running through the thickness of the cortex, that share a property. Picture an electrode going down at right angles to the surface. In cat, Hubel and Wiesel (1962) found that cells along such a penetration tended to share an orientation preference, and that most of the cells they sampled were influenced by both eyes. In lightly anaesthetized macaque and spider monkeys (1968) they mapped orientation columns and **ocular-dominance columns**, groups of cells preferring one eye, along the same penetrations. They reported boundaries that seemed to be independent of each other; later work found the two maps related (see Going deeper). How eye closure in young animals reshapes ocular dominance belongs to [ocular dominance](/lessons/ocular-dominance/) and [critical periods](/lessons/critical-period-control/), and prenatal activity to [spontaneous activity](/lessons/spontaneous-activity/).

**Predict.** In the simple-cell account, a cell's preference comes from the layout of its own inputs. Does a cell then need neighbours that share its preference in order to be tuned? Predict what imaging every cell in a patch of rat V1 might show.

Ohki, Reid and colleagues (2005) imaged single cells with two-photon calcium microscopy. In rat V1, cells were orientation tuned but had no discernible local map: neighbours preferred unrelated orientations, a layout called salt-and-pepper. The same method was not blind to maps: in cat area 18, preferences were so orderly that direction-column borders were one or two cells wide. **Tuning does not need a map**, and columns are not universal. What generates each rat cell's tuning is a separate question this experiment did not answer.

</details>

## Beyond V1: two streams

Beyond V1, the questions change scale. In 1982 Leslie Ungerleider and Mortimer Mishkin summarized monkey evidence into two systems. Inferior temporal lesions impaired object discrimination; posterior parietal lesions impaired a landmark task that depends on spatial position. They proposed a **ventral stream**, occipitotemporal, for object vision, and a **dorsal stream**, occipitoparietal, for spatial vision: "what" and "where". It was a working hypothesis.

Patient D.F. changed the question. She has visual form agnosia and reports the width and orientation of objects poorly. Goodale, Milner, Jakobson and Carey (1991) gave her plaques of equal area but different widths. Her width matching was poor, yet when she reached to pick the same plaques up, her grip opened in proportion to width. The object was the same; the task changed, from a report to an action (the tasks also differ in other ways; see Going deeper). Brain imaging later placed her damage mainly in lateral occipital cortex of the ventral stream on both sides, while dorsal-stream areas still responded during grasping (James and colleagues, 2003). Goodale and Milner proposed in 1992 that the ventral stream serves perception and the dorsal stream the visual control of action.

Keep its limits in view. D.F. is one patient, and one dissociation does not localize a circuit. The streams are densely interconnected, no area has a single function, and the perception-action account is widely taught but still debated. Attention returns in [attention and decisions](/lessons/attention-and-decisions/), and recording thousands of cells in [large-scale neuroscience](/lessons/large-scale-neuroscience/).

**Carry the rule forward.** Where a cell's patch sits explains the map and the pathway defects; how its plus and minus regions are laid out explains why a bar beats a spot. Complex-cell tolerance needs a nonlinearity beyond one sum, with pooling one influential account, and areas beyond V1 need richer computations still.

## Going deeper

### Other targets of the optic tract

The LGN is the main target, but other branches of the optic tract reach the superior colliculus and pretectum, and a small set of retinal axons leaves at the chiasm for the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus. A nucleus is a cluster of neuron cell bodies in the central nervous system.

### Where the defect descriptions come from

The four defects are described for human anatomy by Patel and colleagues (2021). The upper quarter defect after temporal-lobe surgery was reported by Powell and colleagues (2005). Tract lesions often give incongruous defects, meaning the losses in the two eyes do not match closely, and V1 lesions often spare central vision, called macular sparing.

### Complex cells as pooled pairs

One modern form of the pooling idea is the energy model. Two linear filters share an orientation, a spatial frequency and an envelope, but their stripes are offset by a quarter of a cycle (in quadrature). Square each filter's signed output and add: E = u² + v². For a matched grating at phase φ ("phi", where the stripes fall), u = A cos φ and v = A sin φ. Then E = A² cos² φ + A² sin² φ = A² (cos² φ + sin² φ) = A², because cos² φ + sin² φ = 1 for any angle. So E = A² wherever the stripes fall. Reversing the contrast flips the sign of u and v and leaves E unchanged. Adding the two outputs without squaring would give just another single sum, still sensitive to position. The energy model is not a demonstrated circuit for every complex cell, and responses to finite bars depend on the filters chosen.

### Columns in cat and monkey

Cat V1 has ocular-dominance columns too: Hubel and Wiesel's cat work noted grouping by eye preference, and Shatz, Lindström and Wiesel showed the columns anatomically in 1977.

### The two column maps are related after all

Optical imaging later showed that orientation and ocular-dominance maps in monkey and cat are systematically related. Orientation pinwheel centres, the points where all orientations meet, tend to lie near the middle of ocular-dominance columns, and lines of equal orientation tend to cross ocular-dominance borders at steep angles (Bartfeld and Grinvald, 1992; Obermayer and Blasdel, 1993; Hübener and colleagues, 1997, in cat).

### What a human fMRI map measures

In human fMRI maps, a population receptive field describes the pooled activity of many neurons (Benson and colleagues, 2018, 181 participants), not one cell's field.

### What the rule leaves out

Even V1 is not a pure camera. In mouse, part of its ongoing activity is related to the animal's movements (Stringer and colleagues, 2019), and feedback from the higher area LM depends on which part of the visual field each region represents (Fişek and colleagues, 2023).

### The perception-versus-action debate

D.F.'s two tasks also differ in their movements and in the feedback a grasp gives, and whether that difference explains her grasping is debated (Schenk, 2012). Critics of the perception-action account argue that the dorsal stream also supports spatial perception and memory, not only action (Kravitz and colleagues, 2011).
