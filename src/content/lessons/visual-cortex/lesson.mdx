techniques:
  - name: "Extracellular single-unit recording"
    blurb: >-
      A fine electrode placed near a neuron records its action potentials without entering the
      cell. Moving and rotating a stimulus while counting spikes maps the cell's receptive field
      and tuning. It samples one cell at a time and favours large, active cells.
  - name: "2-deoxyglucose metabolic mapping"
    blurb: >-
      A radioactive glucose analogue is taken up by active neurons and trapped. If an animal
      views a chosen pattern during the infusion, autoradiographs of the cortex show where
      activity was high, giving a map of the whole area for that stimulus. It measures
      metabolism summed over the session, not spikes or connections.
  - name: "Two-photon calcium imaging"
    blurb: >-
      A calcium-sensitive indicator in cortical neurons is imaged through a cranial window with
      infrared laser light that excites only the focal plane. Hundreds to thousands of cells can
      be followed at once, so the preferences of neighbouring cells can be compared directly.
  - name: "Lesions and matched perception and action tasks"
    blurb: >-
      In monkeys, a cortical area is removed and performance on tasks that depend on object
      identity or spatial position is compared. In patients, the same object is used for a
      perceptual report and for an action, so a dissociation points to different uses of the
      same visual input.

**Cut one optic nerve and one eye goes blind. Cut one optic tract, a few centimetres further back, and both eyes lose the same half of the world.**

The difference comes from one crossing. [The retina](/lessons/retina/) sends the brain a mosaic of centre-surround signals. Primary visual cortex, called V1 or striate cortex, turns those spots into oriented edges, lays them out as a map, and hands them on to dozens of further areas. Here you follow the pathway, read V1 recordings, compute a tuning width and a cortical distance, and weigh the evidence for two streams. Phototransduction belongs to [senses and cGMP](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/); receptive-field logic to [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/).

## From eye to cortex: one crossing, four defects

The **visual field** is the part of the world one eye sees while it holds still. It has a nasal half (toward the nose) and a temporal half (toward the temple). The lens inverts the image, so the temporal field falls on the nasal retina and the upper field on the lower retina. Most mistakes here come from mixing field and retina.

Ganglion-cell axons gather at the optic disc and leave the eye as the **optic nerve**. At the **optic chiasm** only the fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross to the other side. Temporal-retina fibres stay **ipsilateral** (on the same side). Behind the chiasm, each **optic tract** therefore carries the **contralateral** (opposite) visual hemifield from both eyes: the left tract carries everything to the right of the point of gaze. The left hemisphere does not serve the right eye; it serves the right half of the world, seen by both eyes.

The main target of the tract is the **lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN)** of the thalamus; other branches reach the superior colliculus, pretectum and hypothalamus. A nucleus is a cluster of neuron cell bodies in the central nervous system; the LGN keeps the two eyes' inputs in separate layers. LGN axons fan out as the **optic radiations** to V1, along the calcarine sulcus at the back of the occipital lobe. The radiations split. Fibres for the upper field sweep forward through the temporal lobe in **Meyer's loop** and end below the calcarine sulcus; fibres for the lower field run through the parietal lobe and end above it.

Lesion logic from [the nervous system map](/lessons/nervous-system-map/) gives four defects, described for human anatomy (Patel and colleagues, 2021). Each "can" occur; a partial lesion gives a partial defect.

- **Optic nerve:** loss of the whole field of the eye on the same side.
- **Chiasm, centre:** the crossing nasal fibres of both eyes are cut, so both temporal fields are lost, a **bitemporal hemianopia**.
- **Optic tract, radiation or V1 on one side:** the opposite half of the field in both eyes, a **homonymous hemianopia** (homonymous means the same side of the field in each eye).
- **One branch of the radiation:** a quarter field, a **quadrantanopia**. Meyer's loop damage gives an upper quarter defect on the opposite side, reported after temporal-lobe surgery (Powell and colleagues, 2005); parietal radiation damage gives a lower quarter.

## Simple and complex receptive fields

A **receptive field** is the region of the visual field where a stimulus changes a neuron's firing. David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel recorded extracellularly from single cortical cells in anaesthetized, light-adapted cats while they projected spots, slits and bars onto a screen. Their 1962 paper in the Journal of Physiology described two main classes.

**Simple cells** have separate ON subregions (where light excites) and OFF subregions (where light inhibits and its removal excites), lying side by side. Light that covered an ON and an OFF region together tended to cancel. Because the subregions are elongated, the orientation of a bar was critical: a bar aligned with the ON strip drove the cell, a bar across it barely did.

**Complex cells** kept the orientation preference but tolerated position. A correctly oriented bar drove them over much of the field, and no separate ON and OFF regions could be mapped. They do not ignore orientation.

Hubel and Wiesel suggested how these fields might be built: several LGN cells with centres in a row converge on a simple cell, and simple cells of one orientation but different positions converge on a complex cell. They offered it as a suggestion. The recordings measured response properties; the wiring was a proposal, and how far every complex cell follows it has been debated since.

**Worked problem: reading a tuning curve.** This is a made-up V1 recording. A bar at the cell's best position is shown at six orientations, many trials each. Mean rates: 0 degrees, 2 spikes/s; 30 degrees, 6; 60 degrees, 24; 90 degrees, 40; 120 degrees, 22; 150 degrees, 5. The spontaneous rate, with no bar, is 2 spikes/s. Assume the response varies linearly between sampled orientations.

*Step 1: preferred orientation.* 90 degrees (40 spikes/s).

*Step 2: half height.* Measure from the spontaneous rate: 2 + (40 − 2)/2 = 2 + 19 = 21 spikes/s.

*Step 3: where the curve crosses 21.* On the rising side, between 30 degrees (6) and 60 degrees (24), 21 is 3 below the 60 degree value, out of a rate difference of 18 across the interval: 60 − 30 × 3/18 = 60 − 5 = 55 degrees. On the falling side, between 120 degrees (22) and 150 degrees (5), 21 is 1 below the 120 degree value, out of a rate difference of 17: 120 + 30 × 1/17 = 121.8 degrees.

*Step 4: width.* 121.8 − 55 = 66.8, about 67 degrees full width at half height.

*Step 5: orientation index.* Take 0 degrees, 90 degrees from the preferred, as orthogonal:

> **orientation index = (R_pref − R_orth) / (R_pref + R_orth) = (40 − 2)/(40 + 2) = 38/42 = 0.90**

An index near 1 means the cell strongly prefers its best orientation over the orthogonal one; it does not by itself give the tuning width. With samples every 30 degrees, the width is only an interpolated estimate.

## Maps, magnification and columns, and where columns are absent

V1 is **retinotopic**: neighbouring points in the visual field excite neighbouring points in cortex, so the field is laid out as a map. In humans the fovea, the centre of gaze, is represented at the occipital pole, posterior to the periphery; the upper field lies below the calcarine sulcus and the lower field above it. How that map is wired up during development is covered in [topographic maps](/lessons/topographic-maps/).

The map is distorted. **Cortical magnification** is the length of cortex, in millimetres, given to one degree of visual field. It is largest at the fovea and falls steeply with eccentricity. Tootell and colleagues (1988) mapped macaque V1 with 2-deoxyglucose while the animals viewed patterns of known position, and reported a maximum near the foveal representation of about 15 mm per degree.

**Worked problem: a cortical distance.** Assume a locally constant magnification of 15 mm per degree near the macaque fovea. Two points 0.20 degrees apart lie

> **distance on cortex = M × separation = 15 mm/degree × 0.20 degrees = 3.0 mm**

apart. Check: 3.0 mm ÷ 15 mm/degree = 0.20 degrees. This holds only near the fovea; never apply it across the field.

On top of the map sit **columns**: groups of cells, running through the thickness of the cortex, that share a property. In cat, Hubel and Wiesel (1962) found that cells along a penetration at right angles to the surface tended to share an orientation preference, and that most of the cells they sampled were influenced by both eyes. In lightly anaesthetized macaque and spider monkeys (1968) they found orientation columns and **ocular-dominance columns**, groups of cells preferring one eye, with boundaries that seemed to be independent of each other. The 1962 results are cat; the 1968 columns are monkey. How eye closure in young animals reshapes ocular dominance belongs to [ocular dominance](/lessons/ocular-dominance/) and [critical periods](/lessons/critical-period-control/), and prenatal activity to [spontaneous activity](/lessons/spontaneous-activity/).

Columns are not universal. Ohki, Reid and colleagues (2005) imaged single cells with two-photon calcium microscopy. In cat area 18, preferences were so orderly that direction-column borders were one or two cells wide. In rat V1, cells were orientation tuned but had no discernible local map: neighbours preferred unrelated orientations, a layout called salt-and-pepper. Tuning does not need a map. In human fMRI maps, a population receptive field describes pooled activity of many neurons (Benson and colleagues, 2018, 181 participants), not one cell's field.

## Two streams: what the evidence shows

In 1982 Leslie Ungerleider and Mortimer Mishkin summarized monkey evidence into two systems. Inferior temporal lesions impaired object discrimination; posterior parietal lesions impaired a landmark task that depends on spatial position. They proposed a **ventral stream**, occipitotemporal, for object vision, and a **dorsal stream**, occipitoparietal, for spatial vision: "what" and "where". It was a working hypothesis.

Patient D.F. changed the question. She has visual form agnosia and reports the width and orientation of objects poorly. Goodale, Milner, Jakobson and Carey (1991) gave her plaques of equal area but different widths. Her width matching was poor, yet when she reached to pick the same plaques up, her grip opened in proportion to width. The object was the same; only the use of vision differed. Goodale and Milner proposed in 1992 that the ventral stream serves perception and the dorsal stream the visual control of action.

Keep its limits in view. D.F. is one patient, and one dissociation does not localize a circuit. The streams are densely interconnected, and no area has a single function. Even V1 is not a pure camera: in mouse, part of its ongoing activity is related to the animal's movements (Stringer and colleagues, 2019), and feedback from the higher area LM depends on which part of the visual field each region represents (Fişek and colleagues, 2023). Attention returns in [attention and decisions](/lessons/attention-and-decisions/), and recording thousands of cells in [large-scale neuroscience](/lessons/large-scale-neuroscience/).

Four patients each have one lesion of the visual pathway. (i) The whole field of the left eye is lost. (ii) The temporal half of the field is lost in both eyes. Draw both retinal images first (inverted both ways), then the fibres.

(i) The **left optic nerve**, before the chiasm: all of the left retina is cut off.

(ii) Both temporal fields: the temporal field falls on the nasal retina, and the nasal fibres of both eyes meet at the **chiasm**. A lesion at its centre cuts the crossing nasal fibres of both eyes.

(iii) The right half in both eyes: the right field falls on the left half of each retina, the temporal half of the left eye and the nasal half of the right eye. Those fibres join in the **left optic tract**, so the lesion is in the left tract, left LGN, left optic radiation or left V1.

(iv) The upper right quarter in both eyes: the upper right field falls on the lower left quarter of each retina (lower temporal in the left eye, lower nasal in the right eye). Upper-field fibres run in **left Meyer's loop** to the cortex **below the left calcarine sulcus**, so either site fits.

Partial lesions give partial defects, and close matching between the eyes or sparing of central vision are tendencies only. This is anatomical reasoning, not clinical advice.
