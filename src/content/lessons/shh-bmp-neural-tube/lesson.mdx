techniques:
  - name: "Tissue grafts and ablations in chick embryos"
    blurb: >-
      A piece of tissue, such as a notochord, is moved to a new position in a living embryo, or
      removed. Gain at the new site tests sufficiency; loss at the normal site tests necessity.
      Chick embryos suit this because they develop in an egg that can be opened and resealed.
  - name: "Neural plate explants with purified signal"
    blurb: >-
      A small piece of neural plate is cut out and grown in a gel with a known concentration of a
      protein such as the Sonic hedgehog signaling fragment, for a set time. Antibodies to
      transcription factors then show which identity the cells took, so dose and duration can be
      varied one at a time.
  - name: "In ovo electroporation"
    blurb: >-
      DNA is injected into the chick neural tube and pushed into cells on one side with brief
      electric pulses. The other side serves as a control in the same embryo, which is how
      cross-repression between pairs of transcription factors was tested.
  - name: "Mouse knockouts and reporter lines"
    blurb: >-
      A knockout mouse carries a gene deliberately disrupted, so its embryos show what the gene is
      needed for. A reporter line carries a fluorescent protein driven by the target sites of a
      pathway, here Gli-binding sites, so signaling can be watched in the embryo.

**Graft a second notochord against the side of a chick embryo's neural tube, and the neural cells beside it start to behave like floor plate. The tube gains a second bottom.**

The neural tube is the hollow rod that rolls up from the neural plate, a sheet of ectoderm (the embryo's outer cell layer), and becomes the brain and spinal cord. Its bottom strip is the floor plate, a narrow line of specialized cells. Its top strip is the roof plate. Under the tube runs the notochord, a rod of mesoderm. In the wall between floor and roof, dividing cells called progenitors will make motor neurons near the bottom and relay neurons for body sensation near the top.

So each progenitor needs to know how far it sits from the bottom. The answer starts with a sentence Chris uses for this part of the course: "Differentiation in the neural tube depends on factors from adjacent, non-neural, tissues."

## The idea: the tube learns its bottom and top from its neighbours

Notochord and floor plate lie against each other and share features. That likeness could mean a common history, or it could mean the notochord tells the neural cells what to become. A static embryo cannot tell the two apart. Moving tissue can.

An earlier group had seen an extra floor plate after implanting a notochord in chick embryos in 1985. In 1990 Marysia Placzek, Marc Tessier-Lavigne, Toshiya Yamada, Thomas Jessell and Jane Dodd ran the test in both directions in chick. A notochord grafted beside the lateral wall of the tube induced floor-plate properties in the neural cells it touched. Removing the normal notochord early prevented floor-plate properties at the midline. The first result shows sufficiency, the second necessity.

The floor plate then passes the message on. In a 1993 culture assay, Placzek, Jessell and Dodd found that floor plate induced floor-plate markers in neural plate explants as well as notochord did, and that the signals acted by contact. They called this homeogenetic induction: an induced tissue recruits its neighbours to the same identity.

The top of the tube has its own neighbours. The epidermis, the non-neural ectoderm on either side of the closing neural plate, and later the roof plate send dorsalizing signals. Chris's summary of the axis is short. BMPs (bone morphogenetic proteins) act in neural induction and in dorsal identity, and dorsal BMP and ventral Sonic hedgehog work against each other.

## Hedgehog arrives: "Shh protein is sufficient to turn an explant ventral"

The signal got a name in the December 1993 issue of *Cell*. Three papers described vertebrate relatives of *hedgehog*, a fly gene for a secreted signal: Yann Echelard, Andrew McMahon and colleagues in mouse; Stefan Krauss, Jean-Paul Concordet and Philip Ingham in zebrafish; and Robert Riddle, Randy Johnson, Ed Laufer and Cliff Tabin in chick. The gene they called *Sonic hedgehog* (*Shh*) was expressed in notochord and floor plate, and in the mouse paper, forcing it on where it is normally absent in the central nervous system switched on floor-plate genes.

Jessell's laboratory then put the protein back into the assay. An explant is a small piece of tissue cut out and grown in a dish. In 1994 Henk Roelink and colleagues showed that cultured cells engineered to make a vertebrate Hedgehog induced floor-plate and motor-neuron markers in neural plate explants. In 1995 they used the purified amino-terminal fragment, the part of the protein that signals, and found that higher concentrations induced floor plate and lower ones induced motor neurons. One protein, at different doses, gave different ventral fates.

Necessity came from a knockout, a mouse with a gene deliberately disrupted. In 1996 Chin Chiang, Philip Beachy and colleagues reported that mice lacking *Shh* have no floor plate and lack most ventral cell types of the neural tube. Later, Chiang's group found that also removing *Gli3* restores motor neurons and several ventral interneuron classes, so much of what Shh does in the spinal cord is to block Gli3 repressor (Litingtung and Chiang, 2000).

Inside the cell, Shh binds its receptor Patched, which then stops inhibiting Smoothened, and less Gli2 and Gli3 is cut into repressors, a proteasome step that depends on the primary cilium (see [that lesson](/lessons/hedgehog-and-cilium/)). What a progenitor reads, in this model, is the balance of Gli activator against Gli repressor. Two links to people make the pathway's weight clear. Heterozygous mutations in human *SHH* can cause holoprosencephaly, in which the forebrain fails to divide into two halves. And cyclopamine, an alkaloid from the plant *Veratrum* that causes cyclopic lambs when pregnant ewes graze on it, blocks Smoothened.

## Reading a gradient by level and by time

A morphogen is a signal that spreads from a source and gives different instructions at different concentrations. Shh from the floor plate falls off with distance, so progenitors at different heights receive different amounts. Their answer is written in homeodomain transcription factors.

In 2000 James Briscoe, Alessandra Pierani, Jessell and Johan Ericson grouped these factors in chick into two classes. Class I proteins, including Pax7, Dbx1, Dbx2, Irx3 and Pax6, are repressed by Shh, so each is expressed above a border and lost below it. Class II proteins, Nkx6.1 and Nkx2.2, need Shh, so each is expressed below a border. Pax7 is switched off by low levels and so marks the dorsal half; Nkx2.2 needs high levels and sits next to the floor plate. Their staggered borders define five ventral progenitor domains. From dorsal to ventral these are p0, p1, p2, pMN and p3, which make V0, V1 and V2 interneurons, motor neurons and V3 interneurons. Olig2, a basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) repressor rather than a homeodomain protein, marks pMN and acts with these factors (Novitch et al. 2001). The markers Chris shows, Pax7, Olig2 and Nkx2.2, read the axis from top to bottom.

Concentration turned out to be only half of the reading. In 2007 Eric Dessaud, Briscoe and colleagues reported that chick neural cells adapt: Shh switches on its own receptor Patched1, which makes cells less sensitive, so signaling inside the cell fades. Higher concentrations keep signaling going longer. A 2010 study from the same laboratory held the dose fixed in chick explants and varied exposure time, and longer exposure gave progressively more ventral identities. Duration carries information in many pathways (see [that lesson](/lessons/shape-of-a-signal/)). Here, in this model, a cell's identity tracks something like the total signaling it has received over time.

## Turning a slope into stripes: cross-repression

A smooth gradient should give a blur, yet the domains have sharp borders. The reason is that factors on either side of a border repress each other. Briscoe and colleagues showed in chick by electroporation that Pax6 and Nkx2.2 repress each other at the border between pMN and p3, and that Dbx2 and Nkx6.1 do the same at the border between p1 and p2. Novitch, Chen and Jessell showed in 2001 that Irx3 and Olig2 repress each other at the dorsal edge of pMN, and a 2011 study found a microRNA that tunes this loop by lowering Olig2. Forcing Nkx2.2 into pMN suppresses motor neurons and gives V3 neurons.

Two factors that repress each other can form a switch with two stable states, the bistable circuit from [that lesson](/lessons/signaling-circuits/). A cell near a border cannot hold both for long. It falls to one side, and the loop helps hold it there, though a lasting change in signal can still move it.

The network also shapes where the border falls. In 2012 Nikolaos Balaskas, Vanessa Ribes, Briscoe and colleagues studied the mouse neural tube with a fluorescent reporter of Gli activity. In mice lacking both Pax6 and Olig2, Nkx2.2 spread dorsally although the reporter range did not. When embryos in culture received a short pulse of a drug that activates Smoothened, the borders stayed close to their normal positions; continuous treatment shifted them. The network buffers a passing change and remembers enough of its past to keep a border. Whether the gradient is read directly or mainly through such feedback is still argued.

## The dorsal half: BMPs from the roof plate

The top of the tube runs a parallel system with different signals. In 1995 Karel Liem, Gabi Tremml, Henk Roelink and Jessell found that BMP4 and BMP7 could mimic the dorsalizing effect of epidermis on chick neural plate explants. In 1997 Liem, Tremml and Jessell proposed that BMPs from the epidermis induce the roof plate, which becomes a second source of related signals. BMPs are members of the TGF-beta family and act through receptor kinases and Smad proteins (see [that lesson](/lessons/tgf-beta-and-smads/)). The roof plate also makes Wnts, including Wnt1 and Wnt3a.

The dorsal tube makes six early classes of interneurons, named dI1 to dI6 from the top down. In 2000 Kevin Lee, Paula Dietrich and Jessell removed the roof plate genetically in mice, and the three dorsal-most classes later failed to form. The two systems also oppose each other: BMP signaling alters the response to Shh, and BMP inhibitors such as follistatin, made by the notochord and somites, help shift cells toward ventral fates (Liem, Jessell and Briscoe, 2000).

### Design problem

You have intermediate neural plate explants from chick embryos, which on their own express Pax7. You also have purified Shh signaling fragment, cyclopamine and antibodies to Pax7, Olig2 and Nkx2.2. Design an experiment that tells whether progenitors read the Shh concentration, the duration of exposure, or both.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Run two series and score every explant at the same endpoint, say 48 hours, so age is the same for all.

In the dose series, give each explant a different concentration for the whole time. If concentration matters, rising dose should first switch off Pax7, then switch on Olig2, then at the highest doses switch on Nkx2.2 and lose Olig2.

In the duration series, hold one intermediate dose fixed and add cyclopamine at different times to end signaling. If duration matters, short exposures should leave cells Pax7-negative but without Olig2, longer ones should give Olig2, and with a higher fixed dose the longest exposures should also give Nkx2.2. Because progenitors can lose these markers once signaling stops, also score them at the time cyclopamine goes on, or score the neurons they made.

The expected answer from chick explant studies is that both matter. At a fixed time, higher doses push cells ventrally; at a fixed dose, longer exposure does too. Add one more test: measure a Gli reporter over time at each dose. Signaling should fade as cells adapt, faster at low dose, which is how one variable becomes two.

</details>
