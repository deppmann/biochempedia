summary: >-
  How does a dividing cell in the neural tube know how far it sits from the bottom, and so whether
  its daughters become motor neurons or relay neurons for body sensation? One idea carries the
  lesson: the neighbours supply a slope; the progenitors draw the lines. Sonic hedgehog from the
  notochord and floor plate below, and BMPs from the epidermis and roof plate above, set how much
  signal each cell receives. Progenitors read the level and the duration of the ventral signal,
  answer with a combination of transcription factors, and sharpen a smooth slope into sharp
  domains. Afterwards you can predict the result of grafting, removing or blocking each source,
  and of breaking one of the transcription factors that read it.
hook: "Graft a second notochord beside a chick neural tube, and the tube grows a second floor."

scientists:
  - name: "Marysia Placzek"
    contribution: >-
      With Marc Tessier-Lavigne, Toshiya Yamada, Thomas Jessell and Jane Dodd, showed in chick that
      a grafted notochord induces floor plate in the side of the neural tube and that removing the
      notochord early prevents the normal floor plate, then showed that induced floor plate can
      itself induce floor plate.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Placzek, Tessier-Lavigne, Yamada, Jessell & Dodd, Science 250:985 (1990); Placzek, Jessell & Dodd, Development 117:205 (1993)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Notochord and floor plate lie against each other and share features, so their likeness
      could mean a shared history or an instruction passed from one to the other. In 1990,
      working with Marc Tessier-Lavigne, Toshiya Yamada, Thomas Jessell and Jane Dodd, Placzek
      tested both directions in chick embryos. A notochord grafted beside the lateral wall of the
      neural tube gave the nearby neural cells floor-plate properties, and removing the normal
      notochord early prevented floor-plate properties at the midline. An earlier group had
      already seen an extra floor plate after a notochord implant; the 1990 paper added the test
      of necessity. In 1993, with Jessell and Dodd, she placed neural plate explants against
      notochord or floor plate in culture. The signals acted by contact, and floor plate worked as
      an inducer as well as notochord. They called this homeogenetic induction: an induced tissue recruits its
      neighbours to the same identity. That gave a cellular account of how a narrow contact could
      build a wider floor plate, and it set up the search for the signal.
  - name: "Andrew McMahon"
    contribution: >-
      With Yann Echelard and colleagues, cloned mouse Sonic hedgehog, found it in notochord, floor
      plate and the limb's polarizing region, and showed that expressing it in the wrong part of
      the mouse central nervous system switches on floor-plate genes.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Echelard et al., Cell 75:1417 (1993); Krauss, Concordet & Ingham, Cell 75:1431 (1993); Riddle, Johnson, Laufer & Tabin, Cell 75:1401 (1993)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By 1993 the fly gene hedgehog was known to encode a secreted signal that patterns segments,
      and several laboratories searched vertebrate genomes for relatives. Andrew McMahon's
      laboratory, with Yann Echelard as first author, found three mouse genes and named one
      Sonic hedgehog. Its RNA lay in exactly the tissues the graft experiments had pointed to:
      the notochord, the floor plate and the zone of polarizing activity, the small patch at the
      back of the limb bud that sets the order of the digits. When the team expressed Sonic
      hedgehog in transgenic mice in parts of the central nervous system where it is normally
      absent, genes typical of floor plate turned on there. The paper appeared in the same issue of Cell as two others:
      Stefan Krauss, Jean-Paul Concordet and Philip Ingham with the zebrafish gene, and Robert
      Riddle, Randy Johnson, Ed Laufer and Cliff Tabin with the chick gene, which reproduced the
      limb's polarizing activity. Three species and one expression pattern made Sonic hedgehog
      the leading candidate for the notochord's signal, ready to be tested as a protein.
  - name: "Thomas Jessell"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory showed that the amino-terminal fragment of Sonic hedgehog induces floor plate
      at high concentration and motor neurons at lower concentration, defined the class I and
      class II homeodomain code of ventral progenitors, and identified BMPs from the epidermis and
      roof plate as dorsal signals.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Roelink et al., Cell 81:445 (1995); Briscoe, Pierani, Jessell & Ericson, Cell 101:435 (2000); Liem, Tremml & Jessell, Cell 91:127 (1997)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The laboratory of Thomas Jessell at Columbia University took the floor plate from an
      anatomical seam to a coordinate system. After the notochord grafts, the group, with Henk
      Roelink, showed in 1994 that cells engineered to make a vertebrate Hedgehog induce
      floor-plate and motor-neuron markers in neural plate. In 1995 Roelink and colleagues used
      the purified signaling fragment and found that the higher concentrations they tested
      induced floor plate and lower ones induced motor neurons. With Johan Ericson and James
      Briscoe the laboratory then mapped the transcription factors that progenitors switch on or
      off in response, and in 2000 Briscoe, Alessandra Pierani, Jessell and Ericson proposed a
      code of class I and class II proteins that defines five ventral progenitor domains. In the
      same years Karel Liem, Gabi Tremml and Jessell showed that BMPs can mimic the dorsalizing
      effect of epidermis on neural plate and that the roof plate becomes a second source of
      related signals. The work gave the spinal cord a map that later studies of motor neuron
      identity and circuits could build on.
  - name: "James Briscoe"
    contribution: >-
      Showed with Eric Dessaud and colleagues that chick neural cells adapt to Sonic hedgehog, so
      that both its concentration and the duration of signaling set identity, and with Nikolaos
      Balaskas, Vanessa Ribes and colleagues that a small gene network in the mouse neural tube
      turns the gradient into stable domains.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Dessaud et al., Nature 450:717 (2007); Balaskas et al., Cell 148:273 (2012)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Briscoe worked on the ventral code in Jessell's laboratory, as an author of the 1997 and 2000
      papers that tied graded Sonic hedgehog to Pax6, Nkx2.2 and their relatives. A threshold
      diagram of that code invited a question: does a cell simply measure how much ligand it
      sees? In 2007, with Eric Dessaud and colleagues, his own laboratory in London reported that
      chick neural cells convert different concentrations into periods of intracellular
      signaling of different length, because the pathway desensitizes them over time, partly
      through rising Patched1. Higher concentrations kept signaling going longer. A 2010 study,
      with Dessaud and Noriaki Sasai, separated level from exposure time in chick explants and
      found that longer exposure gave progressively more ventral identities. In 2012, with
      Nikolaos Balaskas, Vanessa Ribes and colleagues, the laboratory turned to mouse embryos and
      a Gli reporter, and showed that the interactions among Pax6, Olig2 and Nkx2.2 help decide
      where boundaries sit and keep them in place after a brief change in signal. The work made
      the gradient a problem in dynamics.

techniques:
  - name: "Tissue grafts and ablations in chick embryos"
    blurb: >-
      A piece of tissue, such as a notochord, is moved to a new position in a living embryo, or
      removed. Gain at the new site tests sufficiency; loss at the normal site tests necessity.
      Chick embryos suit this because they develop in an egg that can be opened and resealed.
  - name: "Neural plate explants with purified signal"
    blurb: >-
      A small piece of neural plate is cut out and grown in a gel with a known concentration of a
      protein such as the Sonic hedgehog signaling fragment, for a set time. Antibodies to
      transcription factors then show which identity the cells took, so dose and duration can be
      varied one at a time.
  - name: "In ovo electroporation"
    blurb: >-
      DNA is injected into the chick neural tube and pushed into cells on one side with brief
      electric pulses. The other side serves as a control in the same embryo, which is how
      cross-repression between pairs of transcription factors was tested.
  - name: "Mouse knockouts and reporter lines"
    blurb: >-
      A knockout mouse carries a gene deliberately disrupted, so its embryos show what the gene is
      needed for. A reporter line carries a fluorescent protein driven by the target sites of a
      pathway, here Gli-binding sites, so signaling can be watched in the embryo.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "Which tissue first supplies Sonic hedgehog to the ventral neural tube?"
    choices:
      - "The somites lying on each side of the tube"
      - "The roof plate along the top of the tube"
      - "The epidermis flanking the neural plate"
      - "The notochord lying beneath the tube"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The notochord, a rod of mesoderm under the neural tube, makes Sonic hedgehog first and
      induces the floor plate, which then makes it too. The roof plate is the dorsal signaling
      center and makes BMPs and Wnts, not the ventral signal. The epidermis beside the neural plate
      supplies BMPs that promote dorsal identity. The somites flank the tube but are not its
      source of Sonic hedgehog.
  - stem: "Pax6 is expressed in ventral progenitors but is switched off where Sonic hedgehog signaling is strongest. How is it classified in the ventral code?"
    choices:
      - "Class I, a factor that Shh represses"
      - "Class I, a factor that Shh induces"
      - "Class II, a factor that Shh induces"
      - "Class II, a factor that BMP represses"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Class I proteins, such as Pax7, Irx3 and Pax6, are repressed by Sonic hedgehog, so they are
      expressed where the signal is low and lost where it is high. Class II proteins, such as
      Nkx6.1 and Nkx2.2, need the signal to be expressed, so a factor lost where signaling is
      strongest cannot be class II. Class I is not defined by induction; that is the class II
      definition. The two classes are defined by their response to Sonic hedgehog, not to BMP.
  - stem: "In a progenitor cell of the ventral neural tube, what change in the Gli transcription factors reports that Sonic hedgehog has arrived?"
    choices:
      - "More Gli3 is cut into its short repressor form"
      - "Less Gli repressor forms and Gli activator rises"
      - "Gli proteins leave the nucleus and stay in the cilium"
      - "Gli proteins are destroyed and Patched levels fall"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Without the signal, Gli2 and Gli3, Gli3 above all, are trimmed by the proteasome, in a step that needs the primary cilium, into
      repressors. When Sonic hedgehog binds Patched, Smoothened is freed, less repressor is made
      and full-length Gli acts as an activator, so the balance of activator to repressor shifts.
      Making more Gli3 repressor is what happens without the signal. Gli proteins carry the
      message into the nucleus rather than staying in the cilium. Patched is a target gene of the
      pathway, so its level rises with signaling rather than falling.
  - stem: "In a chick embryo, Nkx2.2 is forced on by electroporation in progenitors of the motor-neuron domain (pMN), which normally express Olig2 and Pax6 at a low level. What is the most likely result?"
    choices:
      - "Pax6 rises, and the cells make extra motor neurons"
      - "Olig2 rises, and the cells make extra V3 neurons"
      - "Olig2 and Pax6 fall, and the cells make V3 neurons"
      - "Shh output falls, and the cells take dorsal fates"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Nkx2.2 represses both Pax6 and Olig2, so forcing it on in pMN removes the motor-neuron
      program and pushes the cells toward the V3 fate of the p3 domain just below. Pax6 does not
      rise, because Nkx2.2 represses it, and extra motor neurons would need more Olig2, not less.
      Olig2 does not rise; Nkx2.2 represses it. The factor acts inside the cell that receives it
      and does not change how much Sonic hedgehog the floor plate makes.
  - stem: "In a mouse embryo, roof plate cells are killed by a toxin early in spinal cord development, while the floor plate and notochord are untouched. Which neurons are most likely missing?"
    choices:
      - "The ventral V0 to V3 classes, lost as Shh spreads"
      - "The dI4 to dI6 classes, nearest the tube's middle"
      - "The motor neurons, which need BMP from above"
      - "The dorsal-most dI1 to dI3 classes"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The roof plate supplies BMPs and Wnts that the dorsal-most progenitors need, and when it was
      removed genetically in mice the three dorsal-most interneuron classes, dI1 to dI3, failed to
      form. The ventral classes depend on Sonic hedgehog, which still comes from the notochord and
      floor plate. Motor neurons are a ventral fate that BMP opposes rather than supports. The dI4
      to dI6 classes lie further from the roof plate and in that experiment did not depend on it.

    text: >-
      Picture a tall tube with a lamp at the bottom and a different lamp at the top. Each cell in
      the wall looks at how much light it gets from each lamp, and for how long. Cells near the

  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      The spinal cord starts as a hollow tube in the embryo. A rod of tissue under the tube, the
      notochord, releases a protein called Sonic hedgehog, and it turns the bottom strip of the
      tube into the floor plate, which then releases the same protein. The amount of Sonic
      hedgehog falls with distance from the bottom, so cells at different heights receive
      different amounts. Each cell switches certain genes on or off depending on how much signal
      it gets and for how long, and that sets which kind of neuron it will make, such as motor
      neurons near the bottom. Meanwhile, the skin above and the top strip of the tube release
      different signals, BMPs, that make the top half produce other kinds of neurons.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      The notochord induces the floor plate, and both secrete Sonic hedgehog (Shh), which forms a
      ventral-to-dorsal gradient. The epidermis and later the roof plate secrete BMPs and Wnts,
      which promote dorsal identity, and the two systems oppose each other. Ventral progenitors
      read Shh through the balance of Gli activator and Gli repressor, set at the primary cilium.
      Their response is a set of homeodomain factors: class I proteins (Pax7, Irx3, Pax6, Dbx1,
      Dbx2) are repressed by Shh, and class II proteins (Nkx6.1, Nkx2.2)
      are induced. Pairs with adjacent borders repress each other, such as Pax6 and Nkx2.2, or
      Irx3 and Olig2, so the graded input resolves into the p0, p1, p2, pMN and p3 domains that
      make V0, V1 and V2 interneurons, motor neurons and V3 interneurons. Dorsally, BMP and Wnt
      signaling from the roof plate is needed for the dorsal-most interneuron classes among dI1
      to dI6.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      In chick explants, Shh-N concentration thresholds in the low nanomolar range distinguish the
      induction of Nkx6.1, the repression of Irx3 and the induction of Nkx2.2. Concentration is
      not read as a static value. Shh induces Ptch1, which desensitizes cells, so in chick neural
      tissue a higher concentration produces a longer period of Gli activity, and at a fixed dose
      longer exposure yields more ventral identities; Dessaud and colleagues summarized this as
      positional identity tracking something like the time integral of signaling. In the neural
      tube, a network in which Pax6 and Olig2 each repress Nkx2.2, Olig2 represses Pax6 and Nkx2.2 represses
      both Pax6 and Olig2 shows hysteresis: in chick explants less signaling maintains Nkx2.2
      than is needed to induce it, and in cultured mouse embryos the pattern is little changed by
      a transient pulse of a Smoothened agonist.


      An open question is how much of the final pattern reflects the shape of the extracellular
      gradient and how much the downstream network. Studies disagree on whether a single measured gradient is precise enough to place the
      domain boundaries, Gli reporters report signaling with a delay, and opposing BMP signaling
      and tissue growth change what each cell sees over time. Whether the gradient is read
      directly, or mainly through feedback and cross-repression, is still argued.

**Graft a second notochord against the side of a chick embryo's neural tube, and the neural cells beside it start to behave like floor plate. The tube gains a second bottom.**

So how does a dividing cell in the wall of the tube know how far it sits from the bottom, and so whether its daughters become motor neurons or relay neurons for body sensation? Its neighbours tell it. The notochord below and the skin above both send signals into the tube, and a cell's position is written in how much of each it receives. This lesson follows those signals, Sonic hedgehog from below and BMPs from above, and asks how the cells turn a smooth slope of signal into sharp stripes of different neurons.

## The layout

Most of this lesson happens on one picture, which the first film draws. Picture the embryonic spinal cord cut crosswise, like a slice through a hollow tube, with dorsal (the back) at the top.

- **The neural tube** is the hollow rod that rolls up from the neural plate, a sheet of ectoderm (the embryo's outer cell layer), and becomes the brain and spinal cord.
- **The notochord** is a rod of mesoderm (the embryo's middle cell layer) running under the tube.
- **The floor plate** is the tube's bottom strip, a narrow line of specialized cells just above the notochord.
- **The roof plate** is the tube's top strip.
- **The epidermis**, the non-neural ectoderm on either side of the closing neural plate, ends up over the top of the tube.
- **The ventricular zone** lines the central canal, in the wall between floor and roof. Its cells are **progenitors**, dividing cells that will make motor neurons near the bottom and relay neurons for body sensation near the top.

The ventral half of the wall will hold five stripes of progenitors, called **domains**, each making one class of neuron. From dorsal to ventral they are named p0, p1, p2, pMN and p3. For now treat them as five empty slots; the code section fills them in.

The map raises four puzzles.

1. **Who marks the bottom?** Is the floor plate born different from its neighbours, or is it told?
2. **What is the signal**, and how does a cell receive it?
3. **How is a slope read?** A cell can sense only the signal that reaches it. How does it turn that into "which neuron"?
4. **How does a slope become lines?** A smooth slope should give a blur, yet the domains have sharp borders.

The top of the tube, with its own signals, BMPs and Wnts, comes at the end.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** The neighbours supply a slope; the progenitors draw the lines. The notochord and epidermis, and the floor plate and roof plate they induce, are the sources of the slope (Sonic hedgehog below, BMPs and Wnts above); they set, to a first approximation, *how much* signal each cell receives. The cells decide *how sharp the lines are* and help decide *where they fall*: they read the level and how long it lasts, answer with a combination of transcription factors, and pairs of neighbouring factors repress each other, which pushes each cell toward one identity and helps hold it there.

**What the rule covers.** Dorsal-ventral patterning of spinal cord progenitors during early neurogenesis in chick and mouse, where the evidence is strongest: the ventral Sonic hedgehog system in full, and the dorsal BMP and Wnt system in outline. It has the same shape as the rule in [the netrin lesson](/lessons/netrin-and-dcc/) (netrin marks a place; the growth cone decides what to do there): a signal supplies position, and the receiving cell supplies the interpretation. It has three limits, each met where it arises: **sharp is not the same as accurate**, **the signal shapes its own slope**, and **not every fate is a line on this slope**.

## Who marks the bottom?

Notochord and floor plate lie against each other and share features. That likeness could mean a common history, or it could mean the notochord tells the neural cells what to become. A static embryo cannot tell the two apart. Moving tissue can.

In 1990 Marysia Placzek, Marc Tessier-Lavigne, Toshiya Yamada, Thomas Jessell and Jane Dodd ran the test in both directions in chick. In one direction, a notochord grafted beside the lateral wall of the tube induced floor-plate properties in the neural cells it touched: the second floor of the opening.

**Predict.** Now the other direction. Remove the normal notochord early, before the floor plate forms. What happens at the midline? And what does each experiment, the graft and the removal, prove on its own?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Removing the normal notochord early prevented floor-plate properties at the midline. The graft shows **sufficiency**: the notochord is enough to give floor-plate properties in a new place. The removal shows **necessity**: without it, the normal floor plate does not form. Together they point to an instruction passed from notochord to neural cells, at least in these assays (Going deeper qualifies this).

</details>

The floor plate then passes the message on. An **explant** is a small piece of tissue cut out and grown in a dish. In a 1993 culture assay, Placzek, Jessell and Dodd found that floor plate induced floor-plate markers in neural plate explants as well as notochord did, and that the signals acted by contact. They called this **homeogenetic induction**: an induced tissue recruits its neighbours to the same identity. Needing contact in this assay did not settle how far the signal itself travels; the next sections treat it as a graded protein whose level falls with distance from the source.

That answers the first puzzle. The bottom is told, and once told, it recruits. Something passes from the notochord, and then from the floor plate. What is it?

## Naming the signal

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The signal got a name in the December 1993 issue of *Cell*. Three papers described vertebrate relatives of *hedgehog*, a fly gene for a secreted signal: Yann Echelard, Andrew McMahon and colleagues in mouse; Stefan Krauss, Jean-Paul Concordet and Philip Ingham in zebrafish; and Robert Riddle, Randy Johnson, Ed Laufer and Cliff Tabin in chick. The gene they called *Sonic hedgehog* (*Shh*) was expressed in notochord and floor plate, the tissues the grafts had pointed to, and in the mouse paper, forcing it on where it is normally absent in the central nervous system switched on floor-plate genes.

Jessell's laboratory then put the protein back into the assay. In 1994 Henk Roelink and colleagues showed that cultured cells engineered to make a vertebrate Hedgehog induced floor-plate and motor-neuron markers in neural plate explants. In 1995 they used the purified amino-terminal fragment, the part of the protein that signals, and found that higher concentrations induced floor plate and lower ones induced motor neurons. One protein, at different doses, gave different ventral fates.

### How a cell receives it: lifting a brake

Shh's receptor, **Patched**, is a brake: on its own it holds a second membrane protein, **Smoothened**, off. Shh binds Patched, Patched stops inhibiting Smoothened, and the pathway turns on.

What the pathway changes is a family of transcription factors, the **Gli** proteins. Without the signal, Gli2 and Gli3 are cut into short repressors, a proteasome step that depends on the primary cilium (see [that lesson](/lessons/hedgehog-and-cilium/)). Gli3 is the main repressor; Gli2 is processed poorly and acts mainly as the activator. With the signal, less repressor is made and full-length Gli acts as an activator. What a progenitor reads, in this model, is the balance of Gli activator against Gli repressor.

**Cyclopamine**, an alkaloid from the plant *Veratrum* that causes cyclopic lambs when pregnant ewes graze on it, blocks Smoothened and so switches the pathway off.

### Is Shh needed, and what does it do?

Necessity came from a **knockout**, a mouse with a gene deliberately disrupted. In 1996 Chin Chiang, Philip Beachy and colleagues reported that mice lacking *Shh* have no floor plate and lack most ventral cell types of the neural tube.

A second knockout can ask what the missing signal normally does. If removing a second gene restores what the first loss took away, the second mutation **suppresses** the first defect. That is a **double-mutant rescue**. It shows the two genes interact, not how; the mechanism needs other evidence.

**Predict.** Suppose Shh's main job in the spinal cord is to stop Gli3 repressor forming. Then a mouse lacking *Shh* has Gli3 repressor where the signal should have stopped it. What should happen to its ventral cells if you remove *Gli3* as well?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Some ventral cells should come back. Chiang's group found that also removing *Gli3* restores motor neurons and several ventral interneuron classes (Litingtung and Chiang, 2000). Read with the known pathway, from Patched to Gli, this says that much of what Shh does in the spinal cord is to block Gli3 repressor.

</details>

That answers the second puzzle: the signal is Sonic hedgehog, received by lifting a brake.

## Reading the slope: a code written in two classes

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

A **morphogen** is a signal that spreads from a source and gives different instructions at different concentrations. Shh from the floor plate falls off with distance, so a progenitor near the bottom receives a lot and one halfway up receives little. The question is how a cell turns "how much" into "which neuron".

The answer is written in transcription factors that Shh switches on or off through the Gli balance, mostly **homeodomain** proteins (a family of DNA-binding proteins that set cell identity across the body). In 2000 James Briscoe, Alessandra Pierani, Thomas Jessell and Johan Ericson sorted them in chick by one question: does Shh switch the factor off, or does it switch it on?

- **Class I: Shh switches them off.** Examples are Pax7, Dbx1, Dbx2, Irx3 and Pax6. Each is expressed *above* a certain height and lost below it, where Shh is stronger. The more sensitive the factor, the higher its border sits. Pax7 is switched off by low levels, so it is confined to the dorsal half.
- **Class II: Shh switches them on.** Nkx6.1 and Nkx2.2 need Shh and are expressed only *below* a certain height. Nkx2.2 needs the most signal, so it sits right next to the floor plate.
Because the borders are staggered, every stretch of the wall between two borders carries a different combination of factors. That combination is the cell's address. One more factor completes it: **Olig2**, a basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) repressor rather than a homeodomain protein, which Shh also induces, marks the motor-neuron progenitors and acts with these factors (Novitch et al. 2001).

So the five slots are filled (the table's last column). You do not need every name. Three markers serve as landmarks, though they do not tell every domain apart: **Pax7** for the dorsal half, **Olig2** for motor-neuron progenitors and **Nkx2.2** for the domain beside the floor plate.

## Level and time

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

Concentration turned out to be only half of the reading, and part of the reason lies in the brake. *Patched1* is also one of the genes Shh turns on. That sounds backwards, since more receptor ought to mean more sensitivity, but Patched is a brake. In chick neural cells, that extra brake contributes to adaptation: it then takes more Shh to keep signaling.

**Predict.** Two cells start signaling at once, one at a low dose of Shh and one at a high dose. Both cells make Patched as they go, the high-dose cell somewhat more. Which cell's signaling falls below its working level first?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The low-dose cell, because less Shh is left to overcome the rising brake. In 2007 Eric Dessaud, Briscoe and colleagues reported this adaptation in chick neural cells: signaling inside the cell fades, and higher concentrations keep it going longer. A cell can turn a level into a length of time.

</details>

If time is the variable, it should matter even at one dose. A 2010 study from the same laboratory held the dose fixed in chick explants and varied exposure time, and longer exposure gave progressively more ventral identities. Duration carries information in many pathways (see [that lesson](/lessons/shape-of-a-signal/)). Here, roughly, a cell's identity tracks how much signaling it has had so far, and it needs the signal to continue for a while to keep that identity: in chick explants, ending Shh signaling with cyclopamine after a period of exposure let cells fall back to more dorsal identities than continued exposure gave (Dessaud and colleagues, 2010). ## Drawing the lines: cross-repression

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

Why should a smooth slope give a blur? Picture a cell whose output simply rises with Shh, the gentle curve of simple binding (a **Hill coefficient** of 1; the Hill coefficient measures how steeply a response rises with concentration). Such a cell goes from 10 percent to 90 percent of its maximum over an 81-fold change in concentration: 9-fold to reach half, and another 9-fold to reach 90 percent. Now take an illustrative slope on which Shh falls by a factor of e (about 2.7) every 20 micrometres, the order measured for Shh protein in mouse (Cohen and colleagues, 2015), and assume progenitors 5 micrometres wide. An 81-fold change is 4.4 such steps (the natural log of 81), so it spans about 90 micrometres, roughly 18 cells of in-between answers. That is a blur.

| How steeply the cell responds | Cells spanned from 10% to 90% of the response |
| --- | --- |
| Hill coefficient 1 (graded) | about 18 |
| Hill coefficient 2 | about 9 |
| Hill coefficient 4 | about 4 |
| Hill coefficient 8 (switch-like) | about 2 |

(Illustrative values on that slope, not measurements for the neural tube.) In this model, where cells stay put, a border one or two cells wide needs a much steeper, switch-like response. A steep response does not remove cells; it leaves fewer of them giving in-between answers. Steepness alone does not prove a switch, and cells need not stay put: in zebrafish, progenitors are at first specified in a mixed, salt-and-pepper way and then sort into sharp domains (Xiong and colleagues, 2013). In the chick and mouse neural tube, the domains get their switch from the factors themselves: factors on either side of a border repress each other.

Briscoe and colleagues showed this in chick by **electroporation**, in which DNA is pushed into cells on one side of the tube with brief electric pulses and the other side serves as a control in the same embryo. Pax6 and Nkx2.2 repress each other at the border between pMN and p3. Novitch, Chen and Jessell showed in 2001 that Irx3 and Olig2 repress each other at the dorsal edge of pMN. These are the pairs you saw trading places in the table.

**Predict.** Force Nkx2.2 on by electroporation in the pMN domain, where cells normally carry Olig2 and a low level of Pax6. What happens to those cells, and which neurons do they make?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Nkx2.2 represses Pax6 and Olig2, so the motor-neuron program goes and the cells take the p3 identity just below: motor neurons are suppressed and V3 neurons made. One factor in the wrong place moved the line for those cells, without any change to the Shh source.

</details>

Two factors that repress each other can form a switch with two stable states, the bistable circuit from [that lesson](/lessons/signaling-circuits/). In simple models they do so only when the repression is strong and cooperative; a weak loop has a single steady state and no switch. In a strongly cross-repressive circuit a cell near a border is pushed toward one state or the other and cannot hold both for long. The loop then helps hold it there, though a lasting change in signal can still move it. That gives the network some memory.

In 2012 Nikolaos Balaskas, Vanessa Ribes, Briscoe and colleagues found in mouse embryos in culture that a short pulse of a drug that activates Smoothened left the borders close to their normal positions; continuous treatment shifted them.

**Sharp is not the same as accurate.** Cross-repression makes a border sharp, not necessarily *accurately placed*. A large or lasting misreading of the Shh level can tip a cell to the wrong side, and the switch may then hold the error; a brief fluctuation may be filtered out, as the pulse experiment suggests. Accurate placement is a separate problem; in models, reading the signal over time and reading the two opposing slopes together can help, by amounts still debated (Going deeper). This is the first limit of the rule.

## The top of the tube: BMPs from the epidermis and roof plate

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

Earlier, in [neural induction](/lessons/neural-induction/), blocking BMP signaling let frog animal-cap cells become neural, though in chick BMP inhibition alone does not. Now, inside the tube, BMP comes back from the skin and the roof plate and tells the top of the tube it is the top. BMPs (bone morphogenetic proteins) are members of the TGF-beta family and act through receptor kinases and Smad proteins (see [that lesson](/lessons/tgf-beta-and-smads/)).

In 1995 Karel Liem, Gabi Tremml, Henk Roelink and Jessell found that BMP4 and BMP7 could mimic the dorsalizing effect of epidermis on chick neural plate explants. In 1997 Liem, Tremml and Jessell proposed that BMPs from the epidermis induce the roof plate, which becomes a second source of related signals. The roof plate also makes Wnts, including Wnt1 and Wnt3a.

The dorsal tube makes six early classes of interneurons, named dI1 to dI6 from the top down.

**Predict.** Remove the roof plate early in a mouse embryo, leaving the floor plate and notochord untouched. Which interneuron classes should be lost?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In 2000 Kevin Lee, Paula Dietrich and Jessell removed the roof plate genetically in mice, and the three dorsal-most classes later failed to form. The classes nearest the roof plate needed it; dI4 to dI6, further away, did not depend on it in that experiment, and the ventral classes still had Shh from below.

</details>

The two systems also oppose each other: BMP signaling alters the response to Shh (Liem, Jessell and Briscoe, 2000), so cells in the middle of the wall can be shaped by both.

### Design problem

You have intermediate neural plate explants from chick embryos, which on their own express Pax7. You also have purified Shh signaling fragment, cyclopamine and antibodies to Pax7, Olig2 and Nkx2.2. Design an experiment that tells whether progenitors read the Shh concentration, the duration of exposure, or both.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Run two series and score every explant at the same endpoint, say 48 hours, so age is the same for all.

In the dose series, give each explant a different concentration for the whole time. If concentration matters, rising dose should first switch off Pax7, then switch on Olig2, then at the highest doses switch on Nkx2.2 and lose Olig2.

In the duration series, hold one intermediate dose fixed and add cyclopamine at different times to end signaling. If duration matters, short exposures should leave cells Pax7-negative but without Olig2, longer ones should give Olig2, and with a higher fixed dose the longest exposures should also give Nkx2.2. Because progenitors can lose these markers once signaling stops, also score them at the time cyclopamine goes on, or score the neurons they made.

The expected answer from chick explant studies is that both matter. At a fixed time, higher doses push cells ventrally; at a fixed dose, longer exposure does too. Add one more test: measure a Gli reporter over time at each dose. Signaling should fade as cells adapt, faster at low dose, which is how one variable becomes two.

</details>

## Putting it together

The four puzzles share one answer: the neighbours supply a slope; the progenitors draw the lines. Grafts showed that, in these chick assays, the notochord instructs the floor plate, and the floor plate recruits its neighbours. Sonic hedgehog is the signal, received by lifting a brake. Progenitors read its level and how long it lasts, and answer in class I and class II factors. Pairs of those factors repress each other, which helps make each line sharp and lets it resist brief changes. From above, BMPs and Wnts run a matching system for the dorsal half.

The floor plate has a second job. Later the same strip of cells becomes a target for growing commissural axons, the subject of [netrin and DCC](/lessons/netrin-and-dcc/).

## Going deeper

### Where the floor plate comes from

An earlier group had seen an extra floor plate after implanting a notochord in chick embryos in 1985; the 1990 paper added the test of necessity. Even so, the grafts did not close the "shared history or instruction?" question entirely. Later work showed that in chick part of the medial floor plate comes from cells that arise from Hensen's node (the chick's organizer) alongside notochord precursors, and that its induction involves early signals besides Shh (reviewed by Placzek and Briscoe, 2005). So both answers are partly true.

Nor is the floor plate simply the top step of the same dose ladder that the 1995 explants suggest. In the mouse, floor plate needs Gli2 and an early period of high signaling, while motor neurons form without Gli2 (Ribes and colleagues, 2010). That is the third limit of the rule: not every fate is a line on the slope.

### Holoprosencephaly

Heterozygous mutations in human *SHH* can cause holoprosencephaly, in which the forebrain fails to divide into two halves. Together with the cyclopic lambs of cyclopamine, the two make the pathway's weight clear, though both concern the head rather than the spinal cord this lesson follows.

### A third pair, and a microRNA

Briscoe and colleagues also showed by electroporation in chick that Dbx2 and Nkx6.1 repress each other at the border between p1 and p2. A 2011 study found a microRNA that tunes the Irx3 and Olig2 loop by lowering Olig2.

### The network moves the border

The 2012 Balaskas study also used a fluorescent reporter of Gli activity. In mice lacking both Pax6 and Olig2, Nkx2.2 spread dorsally although the measured Gli-reporter domain did not: losing the network moved the border, without a matching shift in the reporter. Whether the gradient is read directly or mainly through such feedback is still argued.

### The cells shape the slope they read

Patched1, which Shh induces, also binds Shh and limits how far it spreads. So the signal partly shapes its own slope, and "the neighbours supply the slope" is a first approximation. That is the second limit of the rule.

### How precise is a border?

On a steady exponential slope with fixed source level and decay length, each threshold falls at a height set by the logarithm of its concentration. On the illustrative slope used above, thresholds 2-fold apart give a domain about 3 cells wide and thresholds 10-fold apart one about 9 cells wide, so domains several cells wide need thresholds 2-fold or more apart.

A switch does not remove noise. Take an illustrative model in which each cell's reading of Shh has a random error, independent of its neighbours, with a standard deviation of 20 percent of the concentration. The border's position is then uncertain by about 0.8 cell (one standard deviation), so even with a sharp switch about 3 cells near it each have more than a 5 percent chance of landing on either side (a 90 percent interval 2.6 cells wide). Averaging about 10 independent readings over time cuts that to about 1; averaging does not remove an error shared by every reading. The noise level is an assumption, not a measurement. Models also suggest that reading the two opposing slopes together improves precision, though how much, and how precise one slope is alone, are debated.

### BMP inhibitors from below

BMP inhibitors such as follistatin, made by the notochord and somites, help shift cells toward ventral fates (Liem, Jessell and Briscoe, 2000).
