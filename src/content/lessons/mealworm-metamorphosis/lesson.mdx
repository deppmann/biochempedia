## The big idea

A kitten looks like a small cat from the day it is born. A mealworm looks nothing like the beetle it will become. Watching that change over a few weeks lets your child see a whole insect life cycle up close.

Mealworms are the young of a darkling beetle. They go through four stages: egg, **larva** (the mealworm, which eats and grows), **pupa** (a still stage that does not eat while the body is rebuilt) and adult beetle. This kind of life cycle, with a pupa in the middle, is called **complete metamorphosis**. Butterflies, ladybugs and bees grow up the same way.

Virginia’s grade 2 standard asks children to compare a life cycle like this with one like a kitten’s, where the young look like the parents from birth. The grade 4 standard adds that what an animal does and needs can change from one life stage to the next. The usual example there is the tadpole and frog, and the mealworm shows the same idea in a tub.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child whenever the tub is open. Younger children watch, and nobody puts a mealworm or beetle in their mouth. Keep the tub, with its lid on, out of reach of children under 4 and pets between visits.
- Mealworms, their droppings and their dust can cause allergy and asthma symptoms. People allergic to shellfish or dust mites can react to them too, so if your child has asthma or one of those allergies, ask their doctor first, and skip the activity if in doubt. If anyone gets itchy skin or eyes, a runny nose, sneezing or wheezing, stop handling them and keep the tub closed. If breathing is hard, call 911. Open the tub away from faces, and you empty old bedding outdoors into a trash bag, without shaking it.
- Move the insects with a spoon or paintbrush, gently and for short times. Wash hands with soap after every visit.
- Never release mealworms or beetles outdoors. Tell your child at the start what will happen at the end. When the project ends, offer them to someone who feeds reptiles, birds or chickens. If no one wants them, an adult can chill them in a sealed container in the fridge for a few hours, then freeze them in a sealed bag for at least 3 days before putting them in the trash.
- Keep the bedding dry. Replace the carrot or potato every 2 or 3 days. An adult throws away anything mouldy outdoors, without sniffing it; children with asthma or a mould allergy stay away from it.
- Wheat bran is wheat, and ordinary oats can carry traces of wheat. If a child has a wheat allergy, ask their allergist before using either, and if they agree, use oats labelled gluten-free and have an adult handle the bedding.
- You poke the air holes in the lid with the lid flat on a table, and you cut the carrot or potato slices. Use a plastic tub, not cardboard or foam, which mealworms can chew through.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Set up the tub.** You poke 10 to 20 small holes in the lid. Spread oats or bran 3 to 5 cm deep. You cut two thin slices of carrot or potato and lay them on top. The slices give the mealworms water, so no water dish is needed.
2. **Meet the mealworms.** Tip the mealworms onto a paper plate. With the hand lens, your child counts legs and body parts. Ask, "Is this a worm? What do you think it will turn into?" Write down their guess.
3. **Measure and settle them.** Measure two or three mealworms in centimetres, then move all the mealworms into the tub with the spoon. Put the lid on and keep the tub at room temperature, out of direct sun, away from heaters and out of the fridge.
4. **Visit two or three times a week.** Look for shed skins, which are thin and see-through. Count larvae, pupae and beetles, and write the numbers in the table. Swap in fresh carrot every few days.
5. **Spot the pupae.** Most store mealworms are already large, so the first pupae often appear within a few weeks. A pupa is pale cream, curled, and has no legs for walking. Move each one gently into the small dish with a little bran. Before you look each time, ask, "What do you think the pupa will look like next?"
6. **Meet the beetles.** After about 1 to 3 weeks as a pupa, a beetle comes out. It starts pale cream or tan and darkens to dark brown or black over a few days. Move new beetles back to the main tub. Measure one and compare it with the mealworms you measured.
7. **Look for the next round.** Beetles lay tiny eggs in the bedding that are very hard to see. After a few weeks, look for very small mealworms. If you see them, the circle is complete.
8. **Compare with a mammal.** Draw the four stages in a circle. Next to it, draw a kitten growing into a cat. Ask, "Which one looks like its parent from the start?"

Cool rooms slow everything down. Below about 20 °C (68 °F), the whole project can take two or three months.

With a class, use one tub per table and a shared chart of how many larvae, pupae and beetles there are each week.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder what this thin, empty skin is. Where did it come from?"
- "How many legs do you think a mealworm has? Let’s count."
- "Is the pupa alive? What could we watch for to find out?"
- "What do you think the beetle will look like when it first comes out?"
- "Do you think the beetle will be bigger or smaller than the mealworm?"
- "What do you think will happen if the tub gets too wet?"

## Why it works

An insect has a hard outer skeleton that cannot stretch. To grow, a mealworm splits its old skin and crawls out in a new, larger one. This is called **molting**, and a mealworm may molt 9 to 20 times, sometimes more, before it is fully grown.

When the larva is big enough, it molts into a pupa. Inside, much of the larval body is broken down and rebuilt as an adult with legs, wings and new mouthparts. The adult beetle does not grow any more. Its job is to mate and lay eggs.

Temperature controls the pace. Mealworms take the temperature of their surroundings, so, up to a point, warmth speeds them up and cold slows them down. In warm rooms a pupa may become a beetle in about a week. In cool rooms it can take three weeks or more. Some "giant" mealworms are raised with a hormone that keeps them from turning into beetles, so ordinary mealworms work better.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Keep just five mealworms so counting stays easy. Use picture tallies: draw a worm, a curl and a beetle. At every visit, add a mark under the matching picture for each one you count. Focus on two questions: "Does it look like its parent?" and "What changed?" Let your child hold the hand lens while you move the insects.

### Ages 8 to 10
Keep a weekly table of how many larvae, pupae and beetles there are, then make a bar graph for each week. Measure in centimetres at each stage. Try a fair test: set up two tubs the same way, one in a warm spot and one in a cooler room. Change only the temperature and record which tub has pupae first.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If no pupae appear after five or six weeks, the room may be cool, or the mealworms may be a "giant" kind. Move the tub somewhere warmer, around 24 to 27 °C (75 to 80 °F). A smell or fuzzy mould means the bedding is too wet: an adult replaces it outdoors and uses thinner slices. Larvae and beetles sometimes chew on pupae, which is why the pupae get their own dish.
