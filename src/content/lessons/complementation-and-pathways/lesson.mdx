techniques:
  - name: "Complementation (cis-trans) test"
    blurb: >-
      Two recessive mutations are brought together in one cell, one on each chromosome (trans).
      A wild-type result means each copy supplies what the other lacks, so the mutations are in
      different functional units. The cis arrangement, both mutations on one copy opposite a wild-type copy, is the control.
  - name: "Growth rescue on minimal medium"
    blurb: >-
      A nutritional mutant is grown on minimal medium plus one candidate compound at a time. A
      compound that restores growth lies after the blocked step, and one that fails lies before it,
      so the pattern across mutants orders the steps of a pathway.
  - name: "Heterokaryon test"
    blurb: >-
      In Neurospora, hyphae of two strains can fuse so that two kinds of nucleus share one
      cytoplasm. If the heterokaryon grows without the supplement that each strain needs alone, the
      two mutations are in different genes, which is a complementation test for a haploid organism.
  - name: "Selective plating of phage recombinants"
    blurb: >-
      Progeny of a cross between two T4 rII mutants are plated on E. coli B, where all types grow,
      and on K12S(λ), where only wild-type recombinants grow. Twice the ratio of corrected counts
      estimates the recombination frequency between the two sites.

  - stem: "Two recessive mutations that each cause the same mutant phenotype are combined in trans in one diploid, and the diploid is wild type. Under the simple recessive model, where do the two mutations most likely lie?"
    choices:
      - "In the same gene, at two separate sites"
      - "In two different genes, each one supplied"
      - "In the same gene, at one identical site"
      - "In two genes, one allele being dominant"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A wild-type trans heterozygote means each chromosome carries a working copy of the gene the
      other has broken, so the mutations are in different genes. Two separate sites in the same
      gene would leave both copies of that gene broken, so the diploid would stay mutant (barring
      rare intragenic complementation). The same site in one gene likewise leaves no working
      copy. A dominant allele would give a mutant diploid, not a wild-type one.
  - stem: "Why can a complementation test not be used to assign a dominant mutation to a gene?"
    choices:
      - "A dominant allele recombines too often to be scored"
      - "A dominant allele is never found on an autosome"
      - "A dominant allele always lies in a regulatory region"
      - "A dominant allele shows its phenotype in any heterozygote"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The test reads a wild-type phenotype as rescue, and a dominant allele gives a mutant phenotype
      even opposite a wild-type copy, so the trans combination is mutant whatever the other mutation
      is. Recombination frequency has nothing to do with dominance. Dominant alleles occur on
      autosomes and on sex chromosomes. Dominant mutations can alter coding sequence, for example
      a dominant-negative subunit, so they are not confined to regulatory regions.
  - stem: "Why did Beadle and Tatum keep each X-rayed Neurospora strain on complete medium before testing it on minimal medium?"
    choices:
      - "A strain that cannot make a nutrient would die on minimal medium"
      - "Complete medium raises the mutation rate of the irradiated spores"
      - "Minimal medium is toxic to spores that have been exposed to X-rays"
      - "Complete medium lets the mold form heterokaryons with wild type"

    rationale: >-
      The mutants they wanted had lost a synthesis, so only a medium already holding the product

  - stem: "An arginine-requiring Neurospora mutant grows on minimal medium plus citrulline or plus arginine, but not plus ornithine. Assume the pathway is precursor, ornithine, citrulline, arginine, and the mutant blocks one step. Which step is blocked?"
    choices:
      - "The step that makes ornithine from its precursor"
      - "The step that makes arginine from citrulline"
      - "The step that makes citrulline from ornithine"
      - "The step that takes in arginine from the medium"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Citrulline rescues, so the block lies before citrulline, and ornithine does not, so the block
      lies after ornithine: the conversion of ornithine to citrulline. A block before ornithine would
      be rescued by ornithine as well. A block between citrulline and arginine would not be rescued by
      citrulline. A defect in taking up arginine would stop arginine itself from rescuing.
  - stem: "In the same arginine pathway, a double mutant carries a block before ornithine and a block between citrulline and arginine. On which supplement will it grow?"
    choices:
      - "Ornithine, since it bypasses the earlier block"
      - "Arginine only, since the later block still stands"
      - "Citrulline, since it lies after both of the blocks"
      - "No supplement, since two blocks cannot be bypassed"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Any supplement must enter after the last block, and only arginine lies beyond the step from
      citrulline to arginine, so the later block sets the requirement, as Srb and Horowitz found for
      their double mutants. Ornithine bypasses the earlier block but still has to pass the later one.
      Citrulline lies before the later block, not after both. Arginine bypasses both blocks at once,

**Cross two pure-breeding fruit fly lines that both look white-eyed, and the offspring can have ordinary red eyes if the whiteness has a different cause in each.**

If whiteness were one thing, that could not happen. It happens because a red eye needs pigment, pigment is made in several steps by the products of several genes, and losing any one step leaves the eye pale. Two white-eyed strains may have lost different steps. This lesson asks two questions that follow. When many mutants share one phenotype, how many genes have been hit? And when the genes act in a chain, in what order?

## The idea: one phenotype can hide many genes

Think of an assembly line with five stations. A broken station anywhere stops the product, so every breakdown looks the same from the end of the line. If one line has a broken station 2 and another a broken station 4, borrowing working stations from each gives a working line. If both broke station 2, borrowing helps nothing.

A few terms first. A **locus** is a gene's place on a chromosome; an **allele** is one version of a gene. A **recessive** allele shows its effect only when no working allele is present, so a diploid shows a recessive mutant phenotype only when both copies are defective: **homozygous** (the same mutant allele twice) or a **compound heterozygote** (two different mutant alleles of the gene). A mutation that inactivates a gene is usually recessive, because one working copy makes enough product.

Mapping, from [linkage and mapping](/lessons/linkage-and-mapping/) and [three-point mapping](/lessons/three-point-mapping/), tells you where mutations sit. It does not tell you whether two nearby mutations damage the same function. For that you need a test of function.

## The complementation test: put two mutations in one cell

Combine the two mutations in one diploid, one on each homologous chromosome. This arrangement is called **trans**. Then look at the phenotype.

If the mutations are in different genes, a and b, the diploid is a/+ ; b/+. Each chromosome set supplies the working allele the other lacks, and the phenotype is wild type. The mutations **complement**. If they are alleles of one gene, the diploid is a1/a2, with no working copy of that gene, and it stays mutant. The two white sweet peas in [extending Mendel](/lessons/extending-mendel/) that gave purple hybrids are complementation in action.

Mutations that fail to complement one another form a **complementation group**, which in the simple model is one gene.

The test has conditions. Both mutations must be recessive, which you check first by crossing each to wild type. A dominant allele gives a mutant phenotype opposite any partner, so the trans result says nothing; a dominant-negative allele, whose product spoils the wild-type product, will look like "same gene" falsely. There are also exceptions with recessive alleles. In proteins built of several identical subunits, two differently damaged subunits can sometimes rebuild a working enzyme, called **intragenic complementation**. In human argininosuccinate lyase each active site holds residues from three subunits, which explains how this can happen. And mutations in different genes sometimes fail to complement.

**Worked problem: how many genes?** Six recessive white-eyed mutants of an idealized fly (1 to 6) are crossed in all fifteen pairs, and the F1 is scored (+ is wild-type F1, − is white). Assume each mutation is recessive, autosomal and fully penetrant.

1x2 +, 1x3 −, 1x4 +, 1x5 −, 1x6 +, 2x3 +, 2x4 +, 2x5 +, 2x6 −, 3x4 +, 3x5 −, 3x6 +, 4x5 +, 4x6 +, 5x6 +.

1. List the failures only: 1x3, 1x5, 2x6, 3x5.
2. Start with mutant 1. It fails with 3 and 5, so \{1, 3, 5\} is a group. Check the inside pair: 3x5 fails, as it must if all three are in one gene.
3. Mutant 2 fails only with 6: group \{2, 6\}. Check that neither 2 nor 6 fails with 1, 3 or 5. Neither does.
4. Mutant 4 complements all five others: group \{4\}.

Three complementation groups, so at least three genes. "At least", because other genes needed for red pigment may simply not have been hit in these six mutants.

## Beadle and Tatum: one mutant, one blocked step

The idea that a gene acts by controlling a chemical step is older than the experiments that proved it. In 1902, in the Lancet, the physician Archibald Garrod described alkaptonuria, in which urine darkens on standing because it holds homogentisic acid. He noted that it often appeared in children of parents who were cousins, the pattern expected for a recessive Mendelian trait, and he described it as an alternative course of metabolism. The recessive Mendelian reading of the cousin data came from William Bateson. The missing enzyme, homogentisate dioxygenase, was identified later.

George Beadle and Edward Tatum, at Stanford, turned the question around. Rather than start with a trait and hunt its chemistry, they started with known chemistry. The bread mold *Neurospora* grows on a simple minimal medium and makes its amino acids and most vitamins itself. In its growing phase it is haploid, one copy of each chromosome, so a recessive mutation shows at once. The design had four steps.

1. Irradiate the mold with X-rays.
2. Grow single-spore cultures on a complete medium, rich in amino acids and vitamins, so that a mutant unable to make one of them stays alive.
3. Test each culture on minimal medium. Keep the ones that grow on complete medium but not on minimal.
4. Add supplements one at a time to minimal medium. The single compound that restores growth names the synthesis that was lost. Then cross the mutant to wild type and check that the defect segregates as one gene.

Their 1941 paper reported three mutants among about 2,000 strains, needing pyridoxine (vitamin B6), the thiazole half of thiamine or para-aminobenzoic acid. The pyridoxine and thiamine mutants were *Neurospora sitophila*, and the para-aminobenzoic acid mutant *N. crassa*. Crosses of the pyridoxine mutant fit a single-gene difference, on what the authors called limited data. Beadle and Tatum shared the 1958 Nobel Prize with Joshua Lederberg.

The summary "one gene, one enzyme" came later; it is not the wording of the 1941 paper. Today you would say that a gene encodes a polypeptide or a functional RNA. Some genes encode only RNA, alternative splicing gives one gene several proteins, and an enzyme of several different subunits needs several genes.

## Ordering a pathway from rescue data

In what order do the steps run? Adrian Srb and Norman Horowitz answered it for arginine in 1944. The compounds involved are the ones you met in the urea cycle in [amino acid catabolism](/lessons/protein-turnover-amino-acid-catabolism/): ornithine gains a carbamoyl group to become citrulline, and citrulline is converted to arginine.

The rule is simple. A supplement rescues a mutant only if it enters the pathway after the block, because the mutant can still carry out every later step (see [enzyme action](/lessons/enzyme-action-basics/) for why one missing enzyme stops a pathway).

**Worked problem: put the steps in order.** Three classes of arginine-requiring *Neurospora* mutants are tested on minimal medium plus one supplement. Assume each mutant blocks one step and that uptake of every supplement is normal.

- Class A grows with ornithine, citrulline or arginine.
- Class B grows with citrulline or arginine.
- Class C grows only with arginine.

1. Arginine rescues every class, so it is the end product.
2. Citrulline rescues A and B but not C, so C's block lies after citrulline. Ornithine rescues only A, so ornithine comes earlier than citrulline.
3. The order is precursor → ornithine → citrulline → arginine.
4. A is blocked before ornithine, B between ornithine and citrulline, and C between citrulline and arginine.
5. A blocked step leaves its substrate with nowhere to go, so class C is expected to accumulate citrulline.

Three brackets below show which blocks can rescue each blocked class: everything after the bar." caption="A supplement rescues a mutant only if it enters the pathway after the block. Class A is blocked before ornithine, B between ornithine and citrulline, and C between citrulline and arginine, giving the order precursor, ornithine, citrulline, arginine." />

The real data were less tidy. Srb and Horowitz had fifteen arginine-requiring strains. Heterokaryon tests and crosses showed at least seven were genetically different, yet the seven they described fell into these same three classes, so one rescue class need not be one gene. Under their one-gene, one-step model they inferred at least seven steps from sugar and ammonia to arginine. In double mutants, the block nearer arginine set the requirement, just as an assembly line broken at two stations needs parts that enter after the later one.

## Benzer: function versus fine structure

A complementation test asks whether two mutations damage the same function. A recombination test asks whether the two sites can be separated by crossing over. Seymour Benzer, working at Purdue in the 1950s, showed how differently the two can answer.

His system was the rII region of the phage T4. rII mutants form large plaques on *E. coli* strain B but cannot grow on K12S(λ), a K-12 strain that carries the phage lambda. That host difference gives two tests.

- **Function.** Coinfect K12S(λ) with two rII mutants. If they lie in different functional units, each phage supplies what the other lacks and the cells release progeny. Benzer's 1955 paper found that this test split the rII region into two independent segments. By 1959 he called such a unit, defined by the cis-trans test, a **cistron**.
- **Recombination.** Coinfect B, where all types grow, then plate the progeny on B to count everything and on K12S(λ) to count only wild-type recombinants. Each crossover between the two sites gives one wild-type and one double-mutant chromosome, and the double mutant cannot be seen on K12S(λ), so you double the wild-type fraction.

> **recombination frequency = 2 × (wild-type recombinants per mL) / (total progeny per mL)**

Because only recombinants grow on the selective host, Benzer could count very rare ones and map many sites inside one cistron. Two mutations can fail to complement, because they are in one gene, and still recombine, because they sit at different points within it. A zero count has limits, though: finding no recombinants in a finite sample sets only an upper limit on the distance, and Benzer noted that fine-scale frequencies were not always additive.

### Design problem

Two T4 rII mutants, rX and rY, each fail to grow on *E. coli* K12S(λ). Coinfecting K12S(λ) with rX and rY gives no growth. The cis control (an rX rY double mutant plus wild type) grows. Crossing rX and rY on *E. coli* B and plating the progeny gives 40 plaques per 0.1 mL on K12S(λ) at a 10^-3 dilution, and 200 plaques per 0.1 mL on B at a 10^-6 dilution. Each parent alone gives no plaques on K12S(λ). Interpret the results and give the recombination frequency.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Function.** rX and rY fail to complement in trans, so they lie in the same cistron. The cis control matters: the double mutant with wild type grows, so the double mutant does not interfere with the wild-type phage, and the trans failure reflects shared function, not dominance.

**Recombination.** Plaques on K12S(λ) appear only from the cross, not from either parent, so they are wild-type recombinants. The two sites can be separated.

1. Wild-type recombinants: 40 plaques in 0.1 mL is 400 per mL; times the dilution, 400 × 10^3 = 4 × 10^5 per mL.
2. Total progeny: 200 in 0.1 mL is 2,000 per mL; times 10^6 gives 2 × 10^9 per mL.
3. Wild-type fraction: 4 × 10^5 / 2 × 10^9 = 2 × 10^-4.
4. Double for the unseen double-mutant class: recombination frequency = 4 × 10^-4, or 0.04 percent.

Assumptions: reversion and leakage are negligible, and the two reciprocal recombinant classes are equally recovered. The two mutations are in one gene and at two different sites, close together.

</details>
