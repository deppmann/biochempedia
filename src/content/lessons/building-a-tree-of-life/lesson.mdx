**A whale swims in the sea and its body is shaped like a fish's. Yet it breathes air with lungs, it feeds its young milk, and its closest living relatives are hippos.**

At first glance, a whale and a tuna seem to belong together. A hippo spends its days in rivers and walks on four legs. Yet biologists group living things by shared ancestry, read from inherited traits, and not by shape or home. Lungs and milk are inherited mammal traits, so the whale is a mammal, and its fishlike shape is a later change that fits life in water. Fossil whales with legs and whale embryos show that whales came from four-legged land mammals, and whale DNA points to hippos as their closest living relatives. A branching diagram called a cladogram records this kind of reasoning: it is built from shared derived traits, inherited traits that first appeared in a common ancestor, and each branch point stands for a common ancestor.

## Explain: the evidence for shared ancestry

<WordBank terms={['classification', 'homologous structures', 'embryology', 'fossil record', 'common ancestor', 'genetic code', 'cladogram', 'shared derived characteristic', 'branch point']} />

**Classification** is the grouping of organisms by how they are related. Biologists use several kinds of evidence, and they trust a grouping most when the kinds agree.

**Homologous structures** are body parts built from the same set of parts because they were inherited from a common ancestor. A **common ancestor** is an earlier population that two or more groups descended from. Look at the forelimb of a human, a bat and a whale. A hand grasps, a wing flies and a flipper steers, yet all three have one upper arm bone, two forearm bones, wrist bones and finger bones.

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/building-a-tree-of-life/forelimb-homology.webp" width={1200} height={800} keyItems={["upper arm bone", "two forearm bones", "wrist bones", "finger bones"]} given={[0, 3]} alt="AI-generated illustration: the forelimb bones of a human arm, a bat wing and a whale flipper side by side, with matching bones shaded the same colour in all three. Four numbered circles point to the bone sets on the human arm." caption="Homologous structures: a hand, a wing and a flipper do different jobs but are built from the same bones. Name the four numbered bone sets, then find each one in the bat and the whale." />

**Embryology** is the study of how organisms develop before birth or hatching. Embryos can share parts that the adults lose. Early fish, chick and human embryos all have a tail and a row of folds in the neck called pharyngeal arches, with grooves between them. In fish, openings called gill slits form between the arches, and several arches go on to support gills. In humans the grooves never open into slits. The arches become parts of the jaw, ear and throat, and the tail shrinks away. Human embryos do not turn into fish first. They share early parts with other vertebrates, then follow their own path. Some old textbook drawings made embryos look more alike than they really are, so biologists now compare photographs of real embryos.

The **fossil record** is the collection of preserved remains and traces of past life, placed in time order by the rock layers they are found in. Fossils show what bodies looked like long ago, including groups that no longer exist.

DNA and proteins give a fourth line of evidence. The more two species' sequences match, the more recently they usually shared an ancestor. Deeper still, almost every organism reads its DNA with the same **genetic code**, the rule that matches each three-letter "word" in a gene's message to an amino acid or a stop signal. Some organisms and mitochondria read a few codons differently, but these are small changes to the same code. Most organisms also share the same basic chemical reactions for breaking down sugar. A shared code and shared chemistry are best explained by common ancestry. You can read how the code works in [that lesson](/lessons/protein-synthesis/).

Classification is also a nested list, from broad to specific: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. Each level holds fewer organisms that share more traits. [That lesson](/lessons/domains-and-kingdoms/) covers the top levels.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Similar body parts, embryos, fossils and DNA are compared together, and a shared genetic code points to one common origin for life.</Support>

## Explain: reading and building a cladogram

A **cladogram** is a branching diagram that shows the order in which groups split from common ancestors. It is built from a **shared derived characteristic**, a trait that first appeared in one ancestor and was passed to its descendants. Hair is one: mammals share it, and fish and frogs do not. A trait that every animal in your sample has, such as a backbone in a group of vertebrates, cannot split that group.

A **branch point**, or node, is where one line splits into two. Each branch point stands for the common ancestor of everything above it. To decide who is most closely related, trace the branches from two tips back to the branch point where they meet. The pair that meets at the most recent branch point is the closest.

{/* DIAGRAM: a cladogram of lancelet, lamprey, tuna, salamander, turtle and leopard, with numbered parts: 1 the root, 2 a branch point labelled common ancestor, 3 a trait mark for jaws on a branch, 4 two tips that are sister groups, 5 a spun branch showing the same tree */}

Three rules keep you from misreading a tree:

- **It branches; it is not a ladder.** No tip is the goal, and a tip at the right edge is not "more advanced".
- **Tips side by side are not always closest.** You can spin branches around a branch point and the meaning stays the same. Always trace back to the shared branch point.
- **Line length depends on the tree.** On a plain cladogram, lengths mean nothing. Some trees scale length to the amount of change, and some to millions of years. Read the key before you read lengths.

An analogy: a cladogram is like a family tree that shows only cousins and their shared grandparents. The analogy breaks because the "grandparents" at the branch points are whole populations that lived long ago, not single people, and none of them is alive today.

### Design problem

Plan a study to test whether whales are closer to hippos or to pigs, using inserted pieces of DNA. Say what you would measure and what result would support each answer.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **What you measure:** at many matching DNA spots, whether an inserted piece of DNA is present or absent in whales, hippos, pigs, cows and an outgroup such as a horse.
- **Prediction if whales and hippos are closest:** several insertions found in whales and hippos but not in pigs.
- **Prediction if hippos and pigs are closest:** several insertions shared by hippos and pigs but not by whales.
- **Constants:** look at the same DNA spots in every species, and use the same lab method for each sample.
- **Checks:** sequence each spot to confirm the insertion is really there. Test more than one individual of each species, and use many spots, not one, so that a single odd result cannot decide the answer.
- **Ethics:** use DNA from museum tissue, stranded animals or zoo samples, so no animal is harmed for the study.

</details>

### Dates for the whale evidence

### The 2005 chimpanzee genome comparison

In 2005 a team compared the genome of one chimpanzee, named Clint, with human DNA. Across about 2.4 billion bases that could be lined up, they found 1.23 mismatches per 100 bases, so about 98.8 percent matched. That figure covers only the aligned parts, not every difference between the two genomes.
