    story: >-
      Nucleoplasmin is a nuclear protein of frog oocytes, and it is built as a pentamer, five
      copies held together by a compact core, each with a tail. In 1982 Colin Dingwall, Stephen
      Sharnick and Ronald Laskey used partial proteolysis, a brief digestion that clips the
      exposed tails and leaves the resistant core, to make pentamers with no tails, one tail or
      several. They injected these into the cytoplasm of Xenopus laevis oocytes. Cores without
      tails stayed out of the nucleus. A pentamer that kept a single tail went in. The control
      that made the result was the second injection: cores placed directly into the nucleus
      stayed there. So the core could be kept inside but could not get inside, and the
      information for entry was in the tail. In 1995, with Dirk Görlich and colleagues, Laskey
      was an author on the paper reporting that importin α recognizes the signal and importin β
      docks the complex at the pore. The 1982 split between entry and retention made it
      reasonable to look for a signal and a receptor that reads it.

techniques:
  - name: "Compartment-specific microinjection"
    blurb: >-
      A protein is injected into either the cytoplasm or the nucleus of a large cell such as a
      Xenopus oocyte, and its location is read later. Comparing the two injection sites separates
      a failure to get in from a failure to stay in.
  - name: "Gold-particle electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Colloidal gold coated with a protein is injected, the cells are fixed and sectioned, and the
      dense grains are located by electron microscopy. Where the grains sit shows the route a
      cargo takes, and changing the coating while keeping particle size fixed shows what the
      route selects for.
  - name: "Signal-transfer reporter fusions"
    blurb: >-
      A candidate signal is attached to a protein that normally stays in one compartment, with an
      unfused reporter and a point-mutant signal as controls. If the fusion moves and the mutant
      does not, the sequence is sufficient in that reporter.
  - name: "FRAP-based nuclear exchange assay"
    blurb: >-
      In living cells expressing a fluorescent reporter, the reporter's fluorescence in one compartment is bleached with light and the
      return to equilibrium is followed over time. Fitting the time course gives an exchange time
      constant, which can be compared across reporter sizes, mutants or species.

  - stem: "A classical NLS has carried a protein into the nucleus. What happens to the signal?"
    choices:
      - "It is removed in the ER lumen before the protein reaches a pore"
      - "It stays in the protein, which can then be imported again later"
      - "It is removed by a nuclear protease once the protein is inside"
      - "It is phosphorylated, which fixes the protein to chromatin for good"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Classical NLSs are ordinarily not cleaved and remain part of the imported protein, so a
      protein that leaves the nucleus, or is released at mitosis in animal cells, can be imported

  - stem: "Which pair of cargo-binding rules fits the Ran cycle?"
    choices:
      - "Importins drop cargo on RanGTP binding; exportins hold cargo with RanGTP"
      - "Both bind cargo only when RanGTP is bound, in either compartment"
      - "Importins hold cargo with RanGTP; exportins drop cargo on RanGTP binding"
      - "Both bind cargo only when RanGDP is bound, then free it at the pore"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Importins load cargo where RanGTP is low (the cytoplasm) and release it when RanGTP binds in
      the nucleus; exportins such as CRM1 bind their cargo cooperatively with RanGTP in the nucleus
      and release it in the cytoplasm, where Ran-binding proteins and GTP hydrolysis take the complex apart. The reversed pairing would
      carry cargo the wrong way. If both receptor classes needed RanGTP, import cargo would be
      loaded in the nucleus, where it already is. Receptors do not grip cargo through RanGDP, and
      release in the nucleus needs RanGTP, helped by nucleoporins on the pore's nuclear face.
  - stem: "RanGAP1 is injected into the nuclei of Xenopus oocytes so that RanGTP is lost there as well. What do you predict for import of an NLS cargo by importin α/β?"
    choices:
      - "Import is inhibited, since cargo is not unloaded and receptors stall"
      - "Import is unchanged, since importins load their cargo in the cytoplasm"
      - "Import is faster, since more RanGDP is free to carry cargo inwards"
      - "Import is unchanged, since crossing the pore needs no GTP hydrolysis"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Without nuclear RanGTP, importin β cannot let go of importin α and its cargo inside and cannot be returned
      for another round, and importin α is not exported by CAS, so net import fails; injections of

**A protein sits mostly in the cytoplasm. Add one export-blocking drug, and within an hour it piles up in the nucleus.** The simplest explanation is that it has been crossing the border all along. In an ordinary eukaryotic cell, soluble nuclear proteins are synthesized on cytosolic ribosomes, while messenger RNA and ribosomal subunits made inside must leave. How can one border sort traffic moving both ways?

The first film gave a first picture of Ran-dependent soluble-protein traffic; it leaves out the border itself, how much it leaks, and the evidence. The page maps the border and measures its leak, the second film follows the experiments, and the page ends on the drug puzzle.

## The envelope, lamina and pore

To test a two-compartment transport model, first map the physical border and the spaces it separates.

The nuclear envelope's inner and outer lipid bilayers are described as about 20 to 40 nm apart in animal cells. The outer membrane is continuous with the endoplasmic reticulum, so the **perinuclear space** between them is ER lumen, the space enclosed by the ER membrane. The membranes meet around each pore's water-filled channel.

**Predict.** A soluble tracer is placed in the perinuclear space. Without crossing a membrane, which space can it reach: the cytosol, nucleoplasm or ER lumen?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The ER lumen. It and the perinuclear space are **topologically equivalent**: they are continuous without crossing a membrane. The pore instead joins cytosol to nucleoplasm. See [membrane organization and topology](/lessons/membrane-organization-topology/).

</details>

Under the inner membrane lies the **lamina**, a lamin meshwork that gives the nucleus mechanical stability and bridges envelope and chromatin. Each pore has an eightfold-symmetric scaffold built from multiple copies of roughly 30 kinds of **nucleoporin**. Nucleoporins with disordered phenylalanine-glycine, or **FG**, repeats line the channel and form its selective barrier.

## A soft barrier, measured

The first film used the simplest case; here is the fuller picture: passive passage slows continuously rather than stopping at one size. Textbooks once proposed a sharp 40 kDa limit. In 2016 Timney and colleagues bleached cytoplasmic fluorescence from different-sized reporters in live budding yeast and followed the excess nuclear signal relaxing, or returning, toward equilibrium. Model that excess as one exponential:

> **x(t) / x(0) = exp(-t / τ)**

Their normalized time constants were τ = 15 s for 27 kDa GFP and τ = 413 s for a 67 kDa reporter. Assume these values apply to the cell and ignore their uncertainty.

No. For GFP, 60/15 = 4, so exp(-4) = 0.018: 1.8% remains. For the 67 kDa reporter, 60/413 = 0.145, so exp(-0.145) = 0.865: 86.5% remains. The larger reporter is far from halfway to equilibrium.

The half-time is t½ = τ × ln 2. It is 15 × 0.693 = 10.4 s for GFP and 413 × 0.693 = 286 s for the 67 kDa reporter. At 826 s, two time constants for the larger reporter, exp(-2) = 0.135, so only 13.5% remains. A 2.5-fold rise in mass (67/27 = 2.48) increased the time constant about 27-fold (413/15 = 27.5), yet did not stop exchange. Passive permeability falls steeply but continuously with size: this is a soft barrier, not a cutoff.

These values describe relaxation of excess signal in a yeast nucleus, not the time one molecule spends in one pore. The pore's physical fine print comes in Going deeper.

## Signals: what an experiment can prove

The second film traced how the labels were found; here is what they are.

A **classical nuclear localization signal** (NLS) is a basic amino-acid sequence recognized by importin α. It can have one basic cluster, such as SV40 large T antigen's Pro-Lys-Lys-Lys-Arg-Lys-Val, or two clusters separated by a spacer, as in nucleoplasmin. Unlike many ER signal sequences in [protein sorting](/lessons/protein-sorting/), it is ordinarily not cut off. Its effect depends on protein context.

In the classical pathway, importin α binds the NLS, importin β binds importin α, and importin β engages FG repeats in the pore. A **nuclear export signal** (NES) recognized by CRM1, also called exportin 1, is a pattern of spaced hydrophobic residues, often leucines.

### Entry is not retention

In 1982 Dingwall, Sharnick and Laskey clipped the tails from pentameric nucleoplasmin while leaving its core. Tail-less cores injected into *Xenopus* oocyte cytoplasm stayed out; a larger one-tail pentamer entered. Tail-less cores injected into the nucleus stayed there. ### Route and signal

In 1984 Feldherr, Kallenbach and Schultz injected nucleoplasmin-coated gold particles, about 5 to 20 nm across including the coat. Electron microscopy placed transported gold at pores. Transport-deficient nucleoplasmin or inert-polymer coatings were largely excluded, showing that entry depended on a transport-competent coat, not gold itself.

That same year Kalderon, Roberts, Richardson and Smith transferred the SV40 large-T signal region to β-galactosidase and pyruvate kinase; both reporters became nuclear. Changing lysine 128 to threonine abolished accumulation. Earlier that year Lanford and Butel had found a cytoplasmic T-antigen mutant with lysine 128 changed to asparagine.

## Crossing is not direction

The second film showed that blocking Ran stops traffic; it did not separate passage through the pore from directional accumulation over many transport rounds. The next experiment makes that separation.

Ran is a small GTPase that works like the switches in [GTPase switches](/lessons/gtpase-switches/): on with GTP and off with GDP. In an in vitro experiment, transportin, another import receptor, carried cargo across pores without Ran or nucleotide. Nuclear RanGTP, but not its hydrolysis, was needed to release cargo and return the receptor; hydrolysis appeared necessary to restore import competence for further rounds. Receptor-assisted crossing is best described as **facilitated diffusion**: a receptor speeds diffusive passage through the pore rather than delivering a power stroke. Repeated directional accumulation still requires energy to maintain and reset the Ran system.

The receptors read the Ran gradient. Importins load cargo where RanGTP is low, in the cytoplasm, and release it when nuclear RanGTP binds. Exportins such as CRM1 bind cargo only together with RanGTP in the nucleus and release it in the cytoplasm, after Ran-binding proteins and GTP hydrolysis take the complex apart.

The evidence accumulated in steps. Adam and Gerace purified import-stimulating NLS binders in 1991; Moore and Blobel showed import needs Ran in 1993; and Görlich, Laskey and colleagues assigned NLS recognition to importin α and pore docking to importin β in 1995. In 1997 Izaurralde, Mattaj, Görlich and colleagues collapsed Ran asymmetry in oocyte nuclei with RanGAP1, RanBP1 or a Ran mutant unable to hold GTP. Major import and export pathways stopped, export more readily than import.

The same year Fornerod, Ohno, Yoshida and Mattaj found three converging results in oocytes and in vitro: extra CRM1 stimulated export of Rev and U snRNAs; leptomycin B inhibited that export; and CRM1 bound NES cargo cooperatively with RanGTP.

This rule is scoped to Ran-dependent transport. Most messenger RNA leaves through NXF1, also called TAP, largely independently of Ran; spliced mRNA export proceeded without Ran in a *Xenopus* oocyte assay. RNA preparation before export belongs to [RNA processing](/lessons/rna-processing/). Cells can also regulate import, for example by masking or exposing an NLS, to switch transcription factors on or off; see [signaling to the nucleus](/lessons/signaling-to-the-nucleus/).

## Predict and design: the drug puzzle

Both films now meet in one perturbation: the loading-and-unloading rule, and the habit of claiming only what a result shows.

Leptomycin B inhibits CRM1-dependent export. Protein Z is cytoplasmic at steady state in cultured cells, but within an hour of adding the drug it is nuclear.

**Predict.** What is the simplest interpretation, and what does nuclear accumulation fail to prove?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Z must enter the nucleus fast enough to accumulate within an hour once export is blocked. The simplest interpretation is that Z shuttles: it enters through its own NLS, rides on a partner with one or, if small enough, diffuses; it leaves through CRM1 using its own NES or a partner's. Export normally wins at steady state. The drug result alone proves neither that Z has its own NLS nor that it binds CRM1 directly.

Test the model in five ways:

1. **NES necessity.** Mutate key hydrophobic residues in a candidate leucine-rich signal. Z should become nuclear without drug. Check expression, folding and partner binding, because the mutation could disrupt more than export.
2. **NLS necessity.** Mutate a candidate basic cluster. If it is Z's main route in, Z should stay cytoplasmic even with leptomycin B. Remaining entry could reflect another signal, a partner or passive diffusion.
3. **Signal sufficiency.** Attach each candidate signal to a large inert reporter, such as a multi-GFP fusion that diffuses only slowly. The NLS fusion should become nuclear. Adding the NES should shift it toward the cytoplasm, and leptomycin B should reverse that shift.
4. **Direct shuttling.** Fuse cells so two nuclei share one cytoplasm, block new protein synthesis and ask whether Z moves from a labelled nucleus to an unlabelled one. Alternatively, photoactivate nuclear Z and watch it leave.
5. **Controls.** Check cell health, include a known CRM1 cargo, and, if available, rescue with a leptomycin-resistant CRM1 mutant.

</details>

## Going deeper

These details give the receptor-recycling machinery and the pore's structure.

### The receptor-recycling machinery

RCC1, Ran's exchange factor, sits on chromatin and loads GTP onto Ran, making RanGTP in the nucleus. Cytoplasmic RanGAP1, helped by RanBP1, stimulates hydrolysis and keeps cytoplasmic RanGTP low. NTF2 returns RanGDP to the nucleus. During classical import, nuclear RanGTP separates importin β from importin α; Nup50 helps α release cargo. Importin β returns with RanGTP, while CAS returns α with RanGTP. During export, RanBP1 helps dismantle cytoplasmic CRM1-cargo-RanGTP before RanGAP-stimulated hydrolysis frees the receptor.

### The pore is not one fixed mesh

Passive entry of inert probes into digitonin-permeabilized HeLa cells, whose plasma membranes had been selectively opened with detergent, supported a model with a dominant FG-domain mesh radius of about 2.6 nm and a small fraction of wider channels. That is a model-dependent description, not a literal universal hole size. Whether FG domains behave more like a cohesive gel or a brush of waving chains remains debated. Nor is the scaffold rigid: fission-yeast pores constricted reversibly during energy depletion and hypertonic stress. In permeabilized HeLa cells, single NLS-carrying molecules interacted with pores for about 10 ms on average.

### Lamina fine print

Lamins belong to the intermediate-filament family. In cryo-electron tomograms of extracted mouse fibroblasts lacking vimentin, a cytoplasmic intermediate-filament protein, A- and B-type lamins formed tetrameric filaments only about 3.5 nm thick. Mutations in *LMNA*, which encodes A-type lamins, cause laminopathies. A mechanically weaker nucleus is one proposed mechanism, and why one gene produces such varied diseases remains argued. Budding yeast has no lamins.
