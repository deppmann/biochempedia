  - name: "Short-circuit current with tracer fluxes (Ussing-type chamber)"
    blurb: >-
      Mount an epithelial sheet between two chambers and pass whatever current holds the voltage
      across it at zero. That current equals net active ion transport if one ion carries it, and
      radioactive tracer fluxes measured in both directions check which ion that is.
  - name: "Matched tumor and blood sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      Sequence the coding DNA of a tumor and of the same patient's blood. A variant found in the
      tumor but not the blood is somatic, acquired in the tumor's cell lineage, rather than
      inherited.
  - name: "Stable-isotope dilution with mass spectrometry"
    blurb: >-
      Infuse a hormone labelled with a heavy isotope at a known rate and measure how far the body's
      own unlabelled hormone dilutes it. At steady state the dilution gives the body's production
      rate.
  - name: "Block-and-replace infusion with metabolic tracers"
    blurb: >-
      Block the body's own cortisol synthesis with an enzyme inhibitor such as metyrapone, replace
      cortisol by infusion on a chosen schedule, and follow glucose, amino acid or fatty acid
      tracers to measure production and uptake.

**Mineralocorticoid receptors bind cortisol as tightly as they bind aldosterone, and adult human plasma carries far more cortisol (a hundredfold molar excess is a commonly cited figure). Yet sodium transport in the kidney follows aldosterone. How?**

The answer is an enzyme beside the receptor. On the way you will follow one gland's steroids and catecholamines, then use their controls to predict disease. The first look is in [the endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/).

## Two glands in one capsule: three cortical zones and a medulla

Each adrenal gland, on the upper pole of a kidney, is two organs in one capsule. The outer **cortex** makes steroids from cholesterol. The inner **medulla** is a modified sympathetic ganglion whose **chromaffin cells**, which arise from the [neural crest](/lessons/neural-crest/), release adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine) into the blood.

Zonation of the adult human cortex is a matter of which enzymes each zone expresses: one assembly line, with stations switched on or off in three workshops. In every zone, the mitochondrial side-chain cleavage enzyme (CYP11A1) cuts cholesterol to pregnenolone. This is the slow step, and ACTH speeds it, within minutes by raising cholesterol delivery to the enzyme (through the StAR protein) and over days by inducing the pathway's enzymes. Then the routes split.

- **Zona glomerulosa** (outer): makes aldosterone, the main **mineralocorticoid** (a steroid acting on sodium and potassium balance). Pregnenolone becomes progesterone, 21-hydroxylase makes 11-deoxycorticosterone (DOC), and aldosterone synthase (CYP11B2), expressed only here, converts DOC through corticosterone to aldosterone. No 17-alpha-hydroxylase, so no cortisol.
- **Zona fasciculata** (middle): makes cortisol, the main **glucocorticoid** (named for its effect on blood glucose). 17-alpha-hydroxylase (CYP17A1) gives 17-hydroxyprogesterone, 21-hydroxylase gives 11-deoxycortisol, and 11-beta-hydroxylase (CYP11B1) gives cortisol. No aldosterone synthase.
- **Zona reticularis** (inner): CYP17A1 acts strongly as a 17,20-lyase, giving DHEA, then DHEA sulfate and androstenedione, weak androgens.

So 21-hydroxylase lies on the routes to cortisol and aldosterone but not to the androgens. Species differ: rats and mice express little adrenal 17-alpha-hydroxylase and make corticosterone, with little adrenal DHEA, so a laboratory rat or mouse "glucocorticoid" result is a corticosterone result.

{/* DIAGRAM: adrenal gland on a kidney with capsule, three cortical zones and medulla labelled, blood flowing from capsule inward to medulla, a preganglionic fibre on chromaffin cells */}

Preganglionic sympathetic fibres release acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors of chromaffin cells, which depolarize and release catecholamines within seconds ([hypothalamus and autonomic control](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/)). In adult humans most of the output, often given as about four-fifths, is adrenaline. Blood drains from cortex to medulla, and work mainly in rats indicates that this glucocorticoid-rich blood supports **PNMT**, the enzyme that converts noradrenaline to adrenaline.

## ACTH drives cortisol; angiotensin II and potassium drive aldosterone

ACTH binds the **MC2 receptor** (melanocortin 2 receptor) on fasciculata and reticularis cells, a G-protein-coupled receptor that signals through Gs and cyclic AMP ([cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/)). The feedback loop and its tests belong to [endocrine axes and testing](/lessons/endocrine-axes-and-testing/) and [emotion and stress](/lessons/emotion-and-stress/).

Aldosterone answers to **angiotensin II**, the end product of the renin cascade ([blood pressure regulation](/lessons/blood-pressure-regulation/)), and to a rise in **plasma potassium**. ACTH stimulates it too, but less, and with continuous exposure that effect fades, which is why loss of pituitary ACTH leaves aldosterone largely intact.

The glomerulosa cell is a voltage sensor. Potassium channels dominate its resting conductance, so it rests near the potassium equilibrium potential, and a rise in plasma potassium depolarizes it ([membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/)). Angiotensin II, through its AT1 receptor, closes potassium channels with the same result. Depolarization opens voltage-gated calcium channels, and calcium drives aldosterone synthase. In mouse adrenal slices, Hu and colleagues (2012) reported spontaneous voltage oscillations in 73 of 85 glomerulosa cells, at a mean of 0.44 Hz at 22 degrees C: an electrical rhythm in mouse cells, not a human hormone pulse rate.

In adult human plasma about 90 percent of cortisol is protein-bound, mostly to **cortisol-binding globulin** (CBG). On the **free hormone hypothesis**, only the unbound fraction acts. Aldosterone binds CBG weakly, so more of it circulates free. By **stable-isotope dilution**, Esteban and colleagues (1991) measured a mean cortisol production of 5.7 mg per square metre of body surface per day in 12 normal volunteers, lower than earlier estimates by the authors' account.

## Cortisol shifts fuel, restrains inflammation and supports pressure

Dinneen and colleagues (1993) blocked cortisol synthesis in normal volunteers with metyrapone, an 11-beta-hydroxylase inhibitor, and replaced cortisol by infusion either at a constant basal rate or on a schedule mimicking the night-time rise. After a mixed meal, the variable schedule increased the liver's glucose release, reduced glucose uptake in a way that appeared to reflect insulin resistance, and raised palmitate appearance, suggesting lipolysis. Insulin and glucagon changed too, so not every effect is direct. Simmons and colleagues (1984) found that an eight-hour hydrocortisone infusion increased whole-body **proteolysis** in seven normal subjects. Those amino acids feed **gluconeogenesis** ([integration of metabolism](/lessons/integration-of-metabolism/)). Cortisol thus raises glucose supply and lowers its use by muscle and fat, opposing insulin ([insulin, glucagon and glucose](/lessons/insulin-glucagon-glucose/)).

On inflammation, cortisol acts through the **glucocorticoid receptor**, a ligand-activated transcription factor ([nuclear receptors](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/)). In mice and cultured cells, Auphan and colleagues (1995) found that glucocorticoids inhibited activation of NF-kappa-B, which switches on many inflammatory genes ([TLRs and NF-kappa-B](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb/)), and that inducing its inhibitor, I-kappa-B-alpha, could be a major part of that; Scheinman and colleagues reported a similar mechanism the same year.

On pressure, Whitworth and colleagues (1984) infused cortisol into seven subjects for five days at rates meant to resemble ACTH stimulation, and systolic pressure rose from 108 to 129 mmHg, by a mechanism the authors left unresolved. Part of cortisol's support of pressure is thought to be **permissive**: it keeps vascular smooth muscle responsive to noradrenaline and angiotensin II.

## Aldosterone acts on the epithelium itself, and an enzyme guards its receptor

By the late 1950s aldosterone was known to make mammals retain sodium. Did it act on sodium-transporting cells directly?

Jean Crabbé (1961) used the urinary bladder of the toad *Bufo marinus*, an epithelium that moves sodium from its urine side to its blood (serosal) side. He cut each bladder into paired halves and mounted each as a sheet between two chambers of aerated frog Ringer solution at room temperature. He measured the **short-circuit current**, the current needed to hold the voltage across the sheet at zero, which equals net active ion transport when one ion carries it. One half received d-aldosterone at 2.8 x 10^-7 mol per L on the serosal side; its partner was the control. If the effect required another hormone or blood flow, the sheet should not respond. After a latent period of about one hour, the treated half's current rose above its control's, and in eight bladders bidirectional sodium tracer fluxes confirmed that the extra current was net sodium transport.

That ruled in a direct, delayed action on the epithelium and ruled out, in this preparation, a systemic intermediary; pairing removed differences between animals. It did not show which mammalian nephron segment responds (the distal nephron and collecting duct, from later work: [tubular transport and diuretics](/lessons/tubular-transport-diuretics/)), and the delay suggested, without proving, new protein synthesis, which later toad-bladder work, including from Isidore Edelman's laboratory, supported.

**Worked problem, part 1.** These numbers are invented, modelled on Crabbé's design; he did not report them. Two paired halves each expose 1.00 cm2. Once the response has developed, the aldosterone half carries 100 microamperes and its control 40 microamperes. Assume sodium (charge +1) carries all the net current.

> **sodium flux (mol per s) = current (C per s) / F, with F = 96,485 C per mol**

1. Extra current = 100 - 40 = 60 microamperes = 60 x 10^-6 C per s.
2. Extra flux = (60 x 10^-6 C per s) / (96,485 C per mol) = 6.22 x 10^-10 mol per s. C per s divided by C per mol gives mol per s.
3. Per hour: 6.22 x 10^-10 x 3,600 s = 2.24 x 10^-6 mol, so 2.24 micromol per cm2 per h.
4. Check: the control alone carries 40 x 10^-6 / 96,485 x 3,600 = 1.49 micromol per cm2 per h and the treated half 3.73; the difference is 2.24, a 2.5-fold rise.

If another ion carried part of the current, step 2 would overstate sodium transport; hence the tracers.

**Part 2: compare the concentration with plasma.** 2.8 x 10^-7 mol per L = 280 nmol per L. Take an illustrative adult plasma aldosterone of 15 ng per dL (made up; real ranges differ by laboratory, posture and salt intake), which is 150 ng per L. With a molar mass of 360.4 g per mol, 150 x 10^-9 / 360.4 = 4.16 x 10^-10 mol per L, or 0.416 nmol per L. The ratio 280 / 0.416 is about 670. The classic shows that the epithelium can respond, not that it responds at physiological concentrations.

Now the opening puzzle. Funder, Pearce, Smith and Smith (1988) reported that mineralocorticoid receptors bind aldosterone and the physiological glucocorticoids with equal affinity, and that **11-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase** (11-beta-HSD) in targets such as kidney, colon and parotid protects them by converting cortisol to inactive cortisone (corticosterone to 11-dehydrocorticosterone in rats). Aldosterone escapes this conversion. Edwards and colleagues reached a similar conclusion that year; the epithelial type 2 isoform was characterized in the 1990s. What the receptor then does is taught in [water, sodium and potassium](/lessons/water-sodium-potassium/).

## Reading adrenal disease from the loops

**Primary aldosteronism** tests the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone loop: an adenoma or bilateral hyperplasia makes aldosterone without regard to renin. Sodium reabsorption rises and extracellular volume expands only modestly, because higher pressure raises sodium excretion (**pressure natriuresis**) until output matches intake, often called mineralocorticoid escape; edema is not typical. Pressure rises; potassium falls in some patients, but many are normokalemic; bicarbonate may rise ([acid-base balance](/lessons/acid-base-balance/)); renin falls. So aldosterone is read against renin. In a recruited cross-sectional cohort, Brown and colleagues (2020) found renin-independent aldosterone production forming a continuum across blood-pressure categories, and a 2025 Endocrine Society guideline suggests screening everyone with hypertension.

**The modern experiment.** Murim Choi, Ute Scholl and colleagues in Richard Lifton's laboratory (2011) exome-sequenced four human aldosterone-producing adenomas against the same patients' blood, then sequenced KCNJ5 in 18 more tumor and blood pairs. Recurrent mutations in **KCNJ5**, a potassium channel gene, appeared in 8 of 22 adenomas and were somatic: in the tumor, not the blood. Expressed with KCNJ3 in cultured 293T cells, the mutant channels conducted sodium and depolarized the cells. A depolarized glomerulosa cell opens calcium channels and makes aldosterone whatever angiotensin II is doing. Keep apart association, somatic status and function. It does not show that all primary aldosteronism has this cause (most of these adenomas lacked it), that a cell line behaves like a glomerulosa cell, or why the cells proliferate.

The other disorders follow from the same loops (directions relative to a healthy adult; cases vary).

| Disorder | Cortisol | ACTH | Aldosterone | Renin | Plasma K+ | Adrenal androgens | Blood pressure |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary insufficiency (Addison) | Down | Up | Down | Up | Up | Down | Down |
| Central insufficiency | Down | Low | Near normal | Normal | Normal | Down | Can fall |
| Cushing, adrenal adenoma | Up | Down | Normal | Normal | Normal | Down | Up |
| Cushing, pituitary or ectopic ACTH | Up | Up or normal | Normal or down | Normal or down | Normal or down | Up | Up |
| CAH, 21-hydroxylase, salt-wasting | Down | Up | Down | Up | Up | Up | Down |
| CAH, 11-beta-hydroxylase | Down | Up | Down | Down | Normal or down | Up | Up in many |

In **primary insufficiency** high ACTH and related peptides from its precursor (POMC) darken skin and mucosa; in **central insufficiency** angiotensin II and potassium still drive the glomerulosa, so neither hyperkalemia nor hyperpigmentation appears. **Cushing disease** is only the pituitary form of **Cushing syndrome**. In **congenital adrenal hyperplasia** (CAH) from 21-hydroxylase deficiency, 17-hydroxyprogesterone piles up and is diverted into androgens, virilizing affected children ([sexual differentiation](/lessons/sexual-differentiation-testis/)). In 11-beta-hydroxylase deficiency DOC acts as a mineralocorticoid; 38 of 70 patients with blood-pressure data in one international cohort were hypertensive (Khattab and colleagues, 2017).

### Design problem

Mineralocorticoid receptors extracted from rat kidney bind aldosterone and corticosterone with about equal affinity, and free corticosterone in rat plasma far exceeds free aldosterone. Yet aldosterone controls sodium transport in the kidney. Design experiments to find out how the kidney's receptors are kept for aldosterone, and say what each result would mean.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

1. **Confirm equal binding in vitro**, with labelled steroids and receptors from targets (kidney, colon, parotid) and from hippocampus, a non-epithelial comparison. Equal affinity means selectivity is not in the receptor.

2. **Ask what binds in vivo.** Give labelled corticosterone to adrenalectomized rats; measure what is bound to kidney receptors and what the steroid has become. Most label as 11-dehydrocorticosterone points to local inactivation.

3. **Block the enzyme** (11-beta-HSD) with carbenoxolone or glycyrrhetinic acid from liquorice. Predict that corticosterone now occupies kidney receptors and, in the intact animal, that sodium is retained and potassium excreted.

4. **Controls**: vehicle; the inhibitor alone without glucocorticoid, to exclude a direct action on the receptor; and a glucocorticoid the enzyme cannot inactivate.

Funder and colleagues (1988) concluded that specificity is enzyme-mediated, not receptor-mediated. Limits: rat and corticosterone; the type 2 isoform came later; and saturation of the enzyme by very high cortisol is an inference to test.

</details>
