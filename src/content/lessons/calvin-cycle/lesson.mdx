summary: "The Calvin cycle is the chemistry that builds you. Most of the carbon in the sugars, proteins, and DNA of living things was first fixed from CO2 by this one loop, which runs in the chloroplast stroma of plants and algae and in cyanobacteria. Counted three turns at a time, it spends ATP and NADPH from the light reactions to weld CO2 onto a five-carbon sugar, reduce the product to a three-carbon sugar (a triose), and regenerate the acceptor so the loop never stops. Derive one number set (3 ATP + 2 NADPH per CO2) from a carbon count, meet one enzyme (Rubisco), and the whole pathway falls into place."

  - name: "Carbon-14 radiotracer kinetics"
    blurb: "Feed a photosynthesizing system 14CO2, then kill the cells at shorter and shorter intervals. Whichever molecule is labeled first is the earliest product. This is how Calvin's team caught 3-phosphoglycerate as the first stable product of fixation — in fractions of a second it held 80 to 90 percent of the fixed label."
  - name: "Two-dimensional paper chromatography"
    blurb: "Spread a killed cell extract across paper, run solvent in one direction, rotate 90°, run a second solvent, then press the sheet against X-ray film. Each labeled compound lands at its own spot and exposes the film. Pairing this with the 14C pulse let the Berkeley group map the entire loop one intermediate at a time."
  - name: "Stable-isotope (13C/12C) signature analysis"
    blurb: "PEP carboxylase discriminates much less against heavy 13C than Rubisco does, so C4 plants leave a measurably different 13C/12C ratio than C3 plants. CAM plants fall across a wide range, depending on how much of their carbon they fix at night. Mass spectrometry of this ratio lets paleoecologists read whether ancient diets and ecosystems were C3- or C4-based from fossil teeth and soil carbon."
  - name: "Transition-state analog crystallography"
    blurb: "To trap Rubisco in its active, carbamylated form, crystallographers co-crystallize it with 2-carboxyarabinitol-1,5-bisphosphate (2-CABP), a stable mimic of the unstable 6-carbon reaction intermediate.     rationale: "Reduction uses ATP and NADPH 1:1 (PGA kinase + G3P dehydrogenase).     answer: 3
    rationale: "In the dark the light reactions stop making reduced ferredoxin, so thioredoxin is re-oxidized (via peroxiredoxin, which passes the electrons to hydrogen peroxide); its target enzymes (FBPase, SBPase, PRK, chloroplast GAPDH and, in some plants, Rubisco activase) reform their regulatory disulfides and switch off.   - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: "The Calvin cycle fixes CO2 in three stages: fixation (Rubisco carboxylates RuBP into two 3-PGA via an enediol and a transient C6 intermediate), reduction (PGA kinase + ATP, then G3P dehydrogenase + NADPH yield G3P), and regeneration (aldolase/transketolase plus FBPase, SBPase, and phosphoribulokinase rebuild RuBP). Per CO2: 3 ATP + 2 NADPH; per hexose: 18 ATP + 12 NADPH. The extra regeneration ATP explains the 3:2 ATP:NADPH ratio. Light activates the cycle via rising stromal pH and Mg2+ (promoting the Lys-201 carbamate) and via reduced thioredoxin switching on FBPase, SBPase, PRK, and chloroplast GAPDH (and, in some plants, the alpha form of Rubisco activase)."
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: "Carboxylation proceeds through a 2,3-enediolate of RuBP; CO2 (distinct from the activating carbamate CO2 on Lys-201, which chelates the catalytic Mg2+) adds at C2 to give a 6-carbon 2-carboxy-3-keto-arabinitol-1,5-bisphosphate, hydrated and cleaved to two 3-PGA — a mechanism frozen crystallographically by the transition-state analog 2-CABP. kcat is ~3 s−1 with imperfect CO2/O2 specificity, so oxygenation generates 2-phosphoglycolate, salvaged at a net CO2 and ATP/reductant cost. Three mechanisms gate the cycle. Rubisco activase, an AAA+ ATPase, strips tight-binding inhibitory sugar phosphates (such as CA1P) off the enzyme. The ferredoxin–thioredoxin system reductively switches on FBPase, SBPase, and PRK. Stromal pH and Mg2+ shifts tune activity. Together they match the cycle to the redox poise of linear electron flow — balancing ATP:NADPH supply (about 1.3:1 from linear electron flow, topped up by cyclic flow) against the ~3:2 demand, including the extra ATP spent at phosphoribulokinase."

**Pick a carbon atom in the CO₂ you just breathed out. Follow it. A leaf might fix it next week, or years from now (on average a CO₂ molecule spends several years in the air), and the machine that welds it into a sugar is the Calvin cycle.** Nearly every carbon in your body, in your last meal, in the wood of the table in front of you, entered the living world through this one loop of chemistry. Each year it builds roughly 100 billion tons of carbon into new plant and algal biomass, on land and in the sea.

The cycle runs in the **stroma**, the soup inside the chloroplast surrounding the thylakoid stacks. It is often called the "dark reactions," and that name is doubly wrong. It runs in broad daylight, and it cannot run without the light reactions, because it spends two products they make: **ATP for energy** and **NADPH for reducing power**, the electrons that actually build the sugar.

Its triose-phosphate product can supply starch synthesis inside the chloroplast or cross the envelope for sucrose synthesis in the cytosol; sucrose is then transported through the phloem." />

The film above animates the carbon count and energy bill: 3 ATP + 2 NADPH per CO₂, with one triose in six kept. That rule assumes the ordinary (C3) cycle in the light at steady state, with the RuBP pool neither growing nor shrinking; photorespiration, C4 and CAM plants, and start-up at dawn bend it (Going deeper, "Where the rule bends").

Below, derive the chemistry and see where that rule bends. The Calvin cycle supplies hexose phosphates; making sucrose or starch then costs a little more ATP or UTP.

Act 1 is the engine and its cost. Act 2 is the control panel that light switches on at dawn and off at dusk.

## Act 1, the first clue: a three-carbon acid

Calvin, Benson and Bassham fed green algae carbon-14-labeled CO₂, killed the cells after shorter and shorter exposures, and separated the labeled molecules on paper (Techniques). At thirty seconds the label had spread into a wide variety of compounds. At a few seconds it sat in only a handful. At the shortest exposures one spot held most of it: **3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA)**.

Whatever lights up first is the earliest product, so carbon from the air lands first in a three-carbon acid. What the CO₂ joins came from a second experiment, later in this act.

## Count the carbons, and the bill follows

Now the three stages.

**Stage 1, fixation.** **Rubisco** (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase), the most abundant protein on Earth, joins one CO₂ to one RuBP (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate). The six-carbon intermediate is so unstable it never accumulates; it is hydrated and split at once into two 3-PGA. It needs no ATP because the reaction is strongly downhill on its own, and no NADPH because no outside electrons enter; Rubisco shuffles electrons within the molecule. It is often the **rate-limiting step**, especially when CO₂ is scarce; under other conditions, rebuilding RuBP sets the pace.

**Stage 2, reduction.** Each 3-PGA is primed by ATP (phosphoglycerate kinase adds a phosphate to its acid end) to **1,3-bisphosphoglycerate**, then reduced by NADPH (G3P dehydrogenase) to **glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P)**, a triose. These are glycolysis's two steps run backward, using NADPH instead of NADH. Per three CO₂, reduction spends **6 ATP and 6 NADPH**, most of the cost.

**Stage 3, regeneration.** The job is to turn five trioses (15 C) into three five-carbon sugars (15 C), adding and losing no carbon. **Aldolase** joins two sugars into one; **transketolase** moves a two-carbon piece from one sugar to another; two phosphatases, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (FBPase) and sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphatase (SBPase), clip off phosphates.

| Step | Carbons | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 3 + 3 → 6 | Aldolase joins two trioses into fructose-1,6-bisphosphate; FBPase clips one phosphate |
| 2 | 6 + 3 → 4 + 5 | Transketolase moves two carbons from the six-carbon sugar onto a triose |
| 3 | 4 + 3 → 7 | Aldolase joins the four-carbon sugar to a triose; SBPase clips one phosphate |
| 4 | 7 + 3 → 5 + 5 | Transketolase moves two carbons again |

The table uses 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 5 trioses. Finally, **phosphoribulokinase (PRK)** spends one ATP on each five-carbon sugar to make RuBP, closing the loop: **3 ATP per three CO₂**, with no NADPH.

**Predict.** Why does the cycle need more ATP than NADPH?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Two kinds of step spend ATP and only one spends NADPH: **1 ATP + 1 NADPH per 3-PGA** to make a triose, and **1 ATP per RuBP** rebuilt. One CO₂ makes two 3-PGA and uses one RuBP: 2 × (1 ATP + 1 NADPH) + 1 ATP = **3 ATP + 2 NADPH**.

</details>

Chemistry checks the NADPH independently. Turning CO₂ carbon (oxidation state +4, as oxidized as carbon gets) into sugar carbon (0 on average) takes at least four electrons per carbon, the load of two NADPH, by *any* route, and the Calvin cycle spends exactly that.

Now follow the carbon from the opening. Welded onto RuBP, it becomes the acid (–COO⁻) end of one 3-PGA, and ATP and NADPH turn that end into the aldehyde (C=O) end of a G3P. Only one triose in six leaves each time, so it may go around the loop several times before it is exported as sugar.

Exported G3P doesn't stay G3P. Two trioses (one first isomerized to dihydroxyacetone phosphate) join into fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, then fructose-6-phosphate, a path that mirrors **gluconeogenesis** almost step for step. The plant then makes **sucrose**, exported through the phloem, or **starch**, stockpiled in the chloroplast for the night. Trees also bank carbon across seasons: the sweetness in a tapped maple's spring sap is sucrose the tree fixed in summers past and stored, now mobilized through the xylem.

## Switch off the light, or the CO₂

Calvin's group let algae fix labeled CO₂ steadily, then suddenly changed one input and watched two pools, 3-PGA and RuBP.

**Predict.** The light goes off. Which pool rises?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

3-PGA rises and RuBP falls. Without ATP and NADPH, 3-PGA cannot be reduced, and RuBP cannot be rebuilt while Rubisco keeps using it. Cutting off CO₂ in the light does the reverse: RuBP rises and 3-PGA falls, because regeneration keeps making RuBP and nothing uses it.

</details>

A molecule that piles up when CO₂ is withheld is a candidate acceptor: this is the experiment that pointed to RuBP, later confirmed with the purified enzyme. It also shows the loop's weak point: without regeneration, fixation stops when RuBP runs out.

## Act 2: the control panel runs on light

In the dark, a running cycle would spend ATP and NADPH that are no longer being made. Worse, at night the chloroplast makes NADPH by burning sugar (the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway), so the cycle would build sugar while that pathway burns it: a futile cycle. So light flips the switches, through three stromal signals.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/calvin-cycle/light-control-panel.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: in light, a thylakoid pumps protons into its lumen, so the stroma pH rises from about 7 to 8, and in the textbook account magnesium ions move into the stroma (drawn dashed). Photosystem I reduces ferredoxin, which through ferredoxin-thioredoxin reductase reduces thioredoxin, and thioredoxin opens the disulfide switches on FBPase, SBPase, PRK and GAPDH. Reduced thioredoxin breaks regulatory disulfides on four Calvin enzymes. At night thioredoxin is re-oxidized, the disulfides reform, and the cycle stalls." />

First, as the light reactions pump protons into the thylakoid lumen (the space inside the thylakoids), **stromal pH rises from about 7 to 8**. Second, in the textbook account, **Mg²⁺ flows out of the lumen into the stroma** to balance the charge.

Both act on Rubisco. A *separate* CO₂ first reacts with a lysine on the large subunit (**Lys-201**) to form a **carbamate**, which then holds a **Mg²⁺** ion; that pair builds the working active site. High pH helps the lysine react, and more Mg²⁺ completes the pair. So one CO₂ *switches the enzyme on*, and a different CO₂ gets fixed. A Rubisco that is not switched on clamps onto RuBP so tightly it shuts itself down, and an ATP-burning chaperone, **Rubisco activase**, pries the sugar loose.

Third, and most elegant: Photosystem I makes **reduced ferredoxin**, which feeds **ferredoxin-thioredoxin reductase** to make **reduced thioredoxin**. In four Calvin enzymes (FBPase, SBPase, PRK and the chloroplast G3P dehydrogenase), thioredoxin reduces a regulatory disulfide, breaking an S–S bridge between two cysteines into two –SH groups so the enzyme can take its active shape. In plants that make the redox-sensitive alpha form of Rubisco activase, it switches that on too. At nightfall thioredoxin is re-oxidized via peroxiredoxin, which passes the electrons to hydrogen peroxide; the disulfides reform, and the cycle quietly stalls. The plant has wired its sugar factory directly to the sun.

## Photorespiration: the ledger broken

Rubisco has a costly flaw written into its name: it is also an **oxygenase**. When O₂ wins the active site, RuBP is split into one 3-PGA plus one two-carbon **phosphoglycolate**: RuBP spent, no carbon fixed. This is **photorespiration**, and it worsens on hot, dry days.

**Predict.** On a hot afternoon the leaf closes its stomata to save water. Which reaction gains, carboxylation or oxygenation?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Oxygenation. With stomata closed, CO₂ inside the leaf falls while O₂ stays high. Heat also lowers Rubisco's preference for CO₂ over O₂ and makes CO₂ less soluble relative to O₂.

</details>

Salvaging phosphoglycolate costs ATP and reducing power, and it *releases* CO₂: two phosphoglycolates (4 carbons) become one 3-PGA (3 carbons) plus one CO₂. On a scorching afternoon a plant can lose a quarter of its fixed carbon this way.

**Predict.** Run six oxygenations, using six RuBP (30 carbons). After salvage, is there enough carbon to rebuild all six?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Oxygenation gives six 3-PGA (18 carbons) and six phosphoglycolates (12 carbons); salvage turns those 12 into three 3-PGA (9 carbons) and three CO₂. That is 27 carbons back and three lost as CO₂. Six RuBP need 30, so the loop ends three carbons short, and it takes three ordinary fixations just to break even. Real leaves mix both reactions, so net gain shrinks, and in extreme heat and low CO₂ can stop.

</details>

## C4 and CAM: paying for a CO₂ pump

<LessonFigure src="/figures/calvin-cycle/c4-space-cam-time.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: in a C4 leaf, a mesophyll cell turns CO2 into bicarbonate, and PEP carboxylase fixes it onto PEP to make malate. Malate crosses into a bundle-sheath cell, where a decarboxylase releases CO2 into a chloroplast holding Rubisco and the Calvin cycle. Pyruvate returns to the mesophyll, where rebuilding PEP spends ATP. Two lower panels show a CAM cell storing malate in its vacuole with stomata open at night, and releasing CO2 to Rubisco with stomata closed by day." caption="Two ways around Rubisco's flaw. C4 plants split the work in space: PEP carboxylase captures CO2 in mesophyll cells, malate carries it to bundle-sheath cells, where it is released around Rubisco, and pyruvate returns to rebuild PEP at an extra ATP cost. CAM plants split it in time, fixing CO2 into malate at night and releasing it to Rubisco by day behind closed stomata." />

Two fixes evolved, each many times over. **C4 plants** (corn, sugarcane) separate the chemistry in *space*:

1. **Capture.** In mesophyll cells, carbonic anhydrase turns CO₂ into bicarbonate, and **PEP carboxylase**, which binds bicarbonate tightly and has no oxygenase activity, fixes it onto PEP to make oxaloacetate, then malate.
2. **Carry.** Malate is shuttled into bundle-sheath cells, where Rubisco sits (some species carry aspartate instead).
3. **Release.** Malate is decarboxylated there, flooding Rubisco with CO₂.
4. **Pay.** Regenerating PEP costs two extra ATP per CO₂, so maize-type C4 runs at about **5 ATP + 2 NADPH** per CO₂, worth it in hot, bright, dry conditions.

**CAM plants** (cacti, agave, pineapple) separate it in *time*: they open stomata mainly at night and use the same PEP carboxylase to fix CO₂ into malate, then close up by day and release that CO₂ to the Calvin cycle, conserving water. CAM pays extra ATP too, usually somewhat more than C4 (Going deeper, "Where the rule bends").

The film's carbon count frames both detours: photorespiration spends RuBP without adding carbon, while C4 and CAM pay ATP to suppress it.

Next comes the **pentose phosphate pathway**. Your own cells run the same transketolase carbon shuffles you just watched regenerate RuBP, there to make NADPH and the ribose for nucleotides.

## Going deeper

### Where the rule bends

Photorespiration uses RuBP without fixing CO₂, so the cost per *net* CO₂ rises. CAM's surcharge over C4 varies with the subtype and the carbohydrate it stores, because it also stores malate overnight and rebuilds its PEP from that carbohydrate. At dawn the cycle keeps *more* than five of six trioses to build up its RuBP pool (it is autocatalytic). On the supply side, with 14 c subunits in the chloroplast ATP synthase (4.67 H⁺ per ATP) and about 6 H⁺ per NADPH, linear electron flow makes about 1.29 ATP per NADPH, short of the 1.5 the cycle needs; cyclic electron flow tops up the ATP.

### How Rubisco makes the cut

RuBP first forms a reactive 2,3-enediolate (an enediol across carbons 2 and 3). CO₂ adds to C2, producing a six-carbon intermediate, 2-carboxy-3-keto-arabinitol-1,5-bisphosphate, that is immediately hydrated and cleaved between C2 and C3 into two 3-PGA. Only one 3-PGA carries the new carbon, as its carboxyl; the other is old RuBP carbon that Rubisco's internal electron shuffle left as an acid. So the bill is counted per 3-PGA, not per new atom.

### The full regeneration list

Triose-phosphate isomerase turns some G3P into dihydroxyacetone phosphate, aldolase's partner in steps 1 and 3. Step 2 makes erythrose-4-phosphate and xylulose-5-phosphate; step 3 makes sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphate, which SBPase trims to sedoheptulose-7-phosphate; step 4 makes ribose-5-phosphate and a second xylulose-5-phosphate. Ribose-5-phosphate isomerase and ribulose-5-phosphate epimerase turn these into ribulose-5-phosphate for PRK.

### Rubisco activase

Lys-201 is spinach numbering. The RuBP-locked, uncarbamylated enzyme is structure 1RCX. Inhibitory sugar phosphates such as CA1P can jam the site too, and activase, an AAA+ ATPase, spends ATP to strip them off as well.

### C4 isotope signatures

A C4 plant makes its first capture with PEP carboxylase rather than Rubisco, and the two enzymes treat carbon isotopes differently. PEP carboxylase discriminates much less against heavy 13C than Rubisco does, which is why C4 and C3 plants carry distinct **isotopic signatures**, a fingerprint paleoecologists read straight out of fossils.

### Diffuse light and the Pinatubo canopy

When **Mount Pinatubo** erupted in 1991, its sulfate haze scattered sunlight into soft, diffuse light. Photosynthesis at a well-studied deciduous forest *rose* on clear days: the upper canopy was already light-saturated, so the diffuse light reached deeper into shaded lower leaves that had been starved for photons. A plant canopy's carbon fixation depends on how evenly light reaches its leaves as well as on how much light arrives.
