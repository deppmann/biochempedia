    story: >-
      Platt worked on embryos of the mudpuppy, a salamander with large, yolky embryos. Its ectodermal cells carry
      little yolk, unlike the deeper layers, and that difference helped her follow them. Following cells through embryos fixed at
      successive stages, she traced them from the neural crest and thickened head ectoderm into
      the branchial arches, where cartilage later formed. In 1893, writing from the University of
      Chicago, she concluded that ectoderm contributes to the cartilages of the head. She did not
      label or move a single living cell, so her case rested on careful reading of fixed
      sections. She later named the ectoderm-derived middle cells mesectoderm. The claim cut
      against the germ-layer scheme and was long doubted. Later grafting and lineage experiments
      in amphibians and birds supported her central point and revised its details, and the word
      mesectoderm is still used.

      population contain many kinds of committed cells? Publishing as Marianne Bronner-Fraser, she
      answered in the embryo with Scott Fraser. In 1988 they injected a fluorescent dextran, a

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Contact inhibition of locomotion was described in the 1950s by Michael Abercrombie in
      cultured fibroblasts: when two cells touch, both stop moving towards each other and set off in
      new directions. Mayor's laboratory at University College London asked whether migrating
      neural crest uses it. In 2008, with Carlos Carmona-Fontaine as first author, they reported
      that cranial crest cells of the frog Xenopus collapse their protrusions at the point of
      contact and move apart, that components of the planar cell polarity pathway gather at the
      contact and activate the small GTPase RhoA there, and that blocking this behaviour disrupts
      directional crest migration in frog and zebrafish embryos. Later work from the group added a
      chemical attraction between crest cells that keeps the group together, and showed in
      Xenopus that a cluster follows a gradient of the chemokine Sdf1 better than single cells.
      Together these made crest migration a model for how cells move as a group.

techniques:
  - name: "Quail-chick chimeras"
    blurb: >-
      A piece of quail embryo is grafted into the same place in a chick embryo. Quail nuclei carry
      a dense chromatin mass that chick nuclei lack, so every donor descendant can be identified
      later in sections. The method maps where a population goes, not what a single cell can do.
  - name: "Single-cell lineage tracing by dye injection"
    blurb: >-
      A large fluorescent tracer is injected into one cell; it is passed to that cell's
      descendants and not to neighbours, though it dilutes as the cells divide. Finding the labelled cells later shows what one cell
      actually made in the embryo.
  - name: "Clonal culture"
    blurb: >-
      Single cells are placed one per well and grown under fixed conditions, and the cell types in
      each colony are scored with markers. It measures what a cell can make in that dish, which
      may differ from what it would make in the embryo.
  - name: "Stripe assay"
    blurb: >-
      Alternating stripes of a test protein and a control protein are laid on a dish, and cells are
      plated over them. If cells pile up on the control stripes, the test protein repels them or
      fails to support them, and further controls tell which.

**A child with Hirschsprung disease has a stretch of colon with no nerve cells in its wall. A person with Waardenburg syndrome may have a white forelock, eyes of two colours and hearing loss. Some patients have both conditions, from a mutation in a single gene.**

Why would gut neurons, hair pigment and hearing fail together? Part of the answer is ancestry. The gut's neurons and the pigment cells, called melanocytes, of skin, hair and inner ear share an ancestor: the neural crest. But a shared ancestor is only half an answer. It does not say why one gene can break both lineages, or why it is almost always the far end of the gut that is left empty. Hold that puzzle; the lesson follows crest cells from the neural plate to the clinic and solves it at the end.

## A border population that leaves home

In [the lesson on neural induction](/lessons/neural-induction/) you saw the ectoderm, the embryo's outer cell layer, split into neural plate and future skin. The neural crest forms at the border between the two. BMP signaling helps define that border: in the standard gradient model, the middle of the plate gets little BMP, the future skin a lot, and the border, in between, an intermediate dose (see [the lesson on TGF-β and Smads](/lessons/tgf-beta-and-smads/)). Wnt signals are needed to make crest there (see [the lesson on Wnt signaling](/lessons/wnt-signaling/)). When the plate rolls up into the neural tube, described in [the lesson on neurulation and tube defects](/lessons/neurulation-and-tube-defects/), the border cells end up along the dorsal midline, the top of the tube.

Then they do what almost no other cells in the tube do. They loosen their grip on their neighbours, lose the top-to-bottom polarity of an epithelial cell, break through the basement membrane, a mat of extracellular matrix under the epithelium, and crawl away. That change, from a cell in an epithelium to a free mesenchymal cell, is an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, or EMT.

Where they end up, and what they become, differs along the body:

| Axial level | Main derivatives |
|---|---|
| Cranial | Cartilage and bone of the face and much of the skull, connective tissue, some cranial sensory and parasympathetic neurons, glia, melanocytes |
| Vagal and sacral | Neurons and glia of the gut wall (the enteric nervous system) |
| Cardiac (part of the vagal level) | Tissue that divides the heart's outflow into aorta and pulmonary trunk |
| Trunk | Dorsal root ganglion sensory neurons, sympathetic neurons, adrenal medulla, Schwann cells, melanocytes |

In birds and mammals, trunk crest does not normally make skeleton; crest bone and cartilage are a head speciality.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** One border, many destinations. Where along the body a crest population starts biases what it can become; the signals it meets on its route and where it stops help decide which of those fates appear. Because many tissues share that start, and many need the same genes, one failed gene can strike tissues that look unrelated.

## How do you follow a cell that leaves?

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

To know where a migrating cell came from, you have to mark it before it goes, and the mark has to last.

The question of what crest makes was opened by Julia Platt, working on the mudpuppy *Necturus*, a salamander with large, yolky embryos. Its ectoderm cells carry less yolk than the deeper layers, and that difference helped her tell them apart in a section, a natural marker of sorts. Reading serial sections of embryos fixed at successive stages, she followed cells from the neural crest and thickened head ectoderm into the branchial arches. In 1893 she concluded that ectoderm contributes to the cartilages of the head. That broke a rule, since cartilage was supposed to be mesoderm, and her evidence was indirect, from fixed sections with no living cell labelled or moved. The conclusion was long doubted.

### Classic experiment: quail-chick chimeras

The lasting mark came from Nicole Le Douarin. She noticed that quail nuclei, stained for DNA, show a large dense mass of condensed chromatin associated with the nucleolus, which chick nuclei lack. Graft a short piece of quail neural tube into a chick embryo at the same level, and the quail cells that emigrate can be recognised in sections long afterwards. The mark neither fades nor dilutes as cells divide.

With Marie-Aimée Teillet and others, Le Douarin used these same-level grafts, called isotopic, to map the crest origin of sensory, sympathetic and enteric ganglia, the adrenal medulla and the skeleton of the face. The neurons of the bird gut, for example, come from crest at the vagal level of the neck, with a contribution from the lumbosacral level to the hindgut.

A same-level graft shows where each level's cells go. It cannot show *why* they become what they do, because origin and route are confounded: the cells start and travel as they would have anyway. To separate the two, move the cells.

### Moving the cells: heterotopic grafts

A heterotopic graft puts cells at a different level from the one they came from.

**Predict.** Take quail neural tube from the adrenomedullary trunk region, opposite somites 18 to 24. At home its crest makes sympathetic neurons and adrenal chromaffin cells, which release noradrenaline and adrenaline. Graft it into a chick at the neck, the vagal level. Where will the quail cells go, and what transmitter will their neurons use?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

They go where vagal crest goes, into the gut wall, and form enteric ganglia. Some donor-derived neurons are cholinergic: they use acetylcholine, as many gut neurons do (Le Douarin and Teillet, 1974; Le Douarin, Renaud, Teillet and Le Douarin, 1975). The new site changed what the population made. The graft cannot say whether it instructed cells that would have become noradrenergic or favoured a subset already able to become cholinergic. Run the swap the other way, and vagal crest grafted to the adrenomedullary level makes adrenal medulla and sympathetic derivatives.

The skeleton behaves differently. In most early reports, trunk crest grafted to the head joins the arches but makes little or no cartilage. So the start biases what the cells can make, and the surroundings help choose.

</details>

| Graft (quail into chick) | Result | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Same level | cells reach that level's usual targets | where each level's crest goes |
| Adrenomedullary trunk crest to vagal level | enteric ganglia, some cholinergic neurons | the new site changes the population's outcome |
| Vagal crest to adrenomedullary level | adrenal medulla, sympathetic derivatives | the same, in reverse |
| Trunk crest to head | little or no cartilage in most early grafts | origin biases what the cells make |

Neither simple story survives the table. "Origin fixes fate" fails on the second and third rows. "The surroundings alone decide" fits the last row poorly.

### One cell, or a mixed population?

A graft carries many cells, so it shows what a population can do, not whether each cell could make everything the population makes. Two kinds of single-cell experiment asked that, and they ask different questions.

**Predict.** One crest cell, alone in a dish, grows into a colony containing both neurons and pigment cells. Does that show the same cell would have made both in the embryo?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not necessarily. Culture shows what a cell *can* make under one condition; in the embryo its route and surroundings might have chosen only one of the two. To learn what a cell *did* make, label it in place and find its descendants later.

</details>

In a dish, Anne Baroffio, Elisabeth Dupin and Le Douarin plated single migrating crest cells from the quail midbrain and anterior hindbrain region onto a feeder layer of 3T3 cells in 1988. They analysed 243 clones, colonies each grown from one cell, after 7 to 10 days. Some clones contained a single neuronal type. Others contained several neuron types, non-neuronal cells and melanocytes. Many fell in between.

In the embryo, Marianne Bronner-Fraser and Scott Fraser injected a fluorescent dextran into single premigratory cells in the chick trunk neural tube, also in 1988. Some single clones spread into more than one derivative, and some included cells that stayed in the neural tube.

So some crest cells can make several cell types. Whether the typical premigratory crest cell is broadly multipotent in the living embryo is still debated: later single-cell tracing in chick and mouse has produced both multipotent and restricted clones. The grafts speak for populations, not for every cell.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which cells.** Crest derivatives at each axial level. The graft tests are in birds; in mice, genetic lineage tracing gives the fate map and also shows differences between levels.
- **Biases rather than fixed menus.** Because some cells are already biased or restricted before they leave, a cell's start and its route are not fully separate causes.
- **Context.** The trunk-cartilage limit is not absolute. Cultured under the right conditions, trunk crest can make cartilage, and trunk crest placed directly into developing head tissue has contributed to head skeleton (McGonnell and Graham, 2002). So the limit is partly what the cell carries and partly what its surroundings and stage allow.

## Leaving home: an identity first, then a release

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

A crest cell has two jobs before it can travel. It has to become a crest cell, and it has to let go.

**Becoming crest.** The border signals, BMP and Wnt from earlier, along with FGF, switch on a first tier of genes, the border genes. These switch on a second tier, the crest genes, such as *Snail2*, *FoxD3* and *Sox10*. The crest genes in turn control the genes that change adhesion and movement and, later, influence fate. Daniel Meulemans and Marianne Bronner-Fraser drew this in 2004 as a proposed gene-regulatory network, a diagram of which genes switch on which. Read each arrow as a hypothesis until it has been tested; some since have been.

**Letting go.** In the neural tube, premigratory chick crest cells are held to their neighbours partly by a cadherin, Cadherin-6B. To leave, they must loosen that grip. In 1994 a group including Angela Nieto showed that blocking the transcription factor Slug, now called Snail2, reduced crest emigration in chick embryos. Snail2 was needed for departure. How it acted was still open.

**Predict.** Suppose Snail2 promotes departure by switching off *Cadherin-6B*. Lower Snail2 in premigratory crest with a morpholino, a synthetic molecule that blocks the making of one protein. What should happen to Cadherin-6B RNA soon afterwards, and to the number of cells that leave?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Cadherin-6B RNA should rise, and fewer cells should leave. In 2007 Lisa Taneyhill, Edward Coles and Bronner-Fraser did this experiment, and Cadherin-6B RNA rose within about half an hour. Snail2 bound clusters of E boxes, short DNA motifs, in the *Cadherin-6B* regulatory region, and the authors concluded that it represses the gene directly.

</details>

That makes Cadherin-6B a candidate brake. A brake needs its own test: release it without touching Snail2, and the cells should leave early. In a companion study, Coles, Taneyhill and Bronner-Fraser lowered Cadherin-6B, and crest cells did emigrate early.

So Snail2 probably promotes departure at least partly by lifting the Cadherin-6B brake, and EMT involves more than one adhesion gene. "Partly" matters. If lowering Cadherin-6B along with Snail2 restored only half of the lost departure, that would be consistent with Cadherin-6B explaining part of the defect; other Snail2 targets, or incomplete knockdown, could explain the rest. That is the reasoning in the third MCAT question below, a hypothetical experiment, not a published result.

## Steering: a forbidden half and cells that back away

In the chick trunk, crest cells take two main routes. The ventromedial route runs through the somites, the blocks of mesoderm beside the neural tube, and gives dorsal root ganglia, sympathetic ganglia and adrenal medulla. The dorsolateral route, taken later, runs under the skin and gives mostly melanocytes.

On the ventral route the cells obey a rule. They pass only through the rostral (front) half of each sclerotome, the part of the somite that will form vertebrae. The caudal (back) half keeps them out.

What keeps them out? In chick, crest cells carry EphB receptors, and caudal-half sclerotome cells carry ephrin-B, a membrane-bound ligand. Because both sit in membranes, the signal passes only where a crest cell touches a caudal-half cell. In 1997 two groups showed that this repels crest. Catherine Krull and colleagues, working with chick, found that trunk crest plated on alternating stripes avoids ephrin-B1 stripes. Hai Wang and David Anderson showed that clustered ephrin-B ligands repel crest cells and motor axons. Ephrin-B can also signal back into its own cell (see [the lesson on reverse signaling](/lessons/reverse-signaling/)), but here the forward signal into the crest cell repels.

In mouse the caudal half also makes semaphorin 3F, which repels crest through its receptor neuropilin-2 (Laura Gammill and colleagues, 2006). Mice lacking either protein lose segmental crest migration. So the caudal half uses more than one repellent: ephrin-B is the best-studied in chick, semaphorin 3F in mouse, and neither is known to be the only one.

### Design problem

Trunk crest cells in culture avoid stripes coated with ephrin-B. A sceptic says the stripe is simply a poor surface to stick to, and the cells drift onto the better one. How would you show that the avoidance is active repulsion through Eph receptors?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Add soluble ephrin-B to the medium, or a reagent that blocks Eph receptors. Soluble ligand (or a blocking reagent) occupies or saturates the receptors everywhere, so the cell can no longer read the stripe as different. If cells now cross the stripes and spread evenly, the avoidance needed Eph-ephrin signaling. That is what the 1997 chick study showed: soluble ephrin-B1 blocked avoidance of ephrin-B1 stripes. A passive adhesion difference would not be relieved by adding ligand, because the stripe surface itself is unchanged. Watching cells turn away at the stripe edge, rather than slip off it, adds further evidence. As a control, add a soluble protein that does not bind Eph receptors; avoidance should persist.

The same study took the logic into tissue: soluble ephrin-B1 added to trunk explants disrupted segmental migration, and crest cells entered caudal as well as rostral halves. One trap: clustered soluble ephrin can activate the receptors, while unclustered ligand can block them, so say which form and dose you used, and check that the cells still move.

</details>

**Crest cells also steer each other.** Contact inhibition of locomotion was first described by Michael Abercrombie in cultured fibroblasts in the 1950s: two cells that touch stop moving towards each other and head off in new directions. In 2008 Roberto Mayor's laboratory showed that frog and zebrafish crest cells do this. Collective migration of frog cranial crest is thought to depend on three ingredients.

1. **Contact inhibition pushes cells apart and gives each a front.** At the contact, planar cell polarity signaling (a Wnt branch that acts without β-catenin) activates RhoA, a small GTPase. RhoA collapses protrusions there. Another GTPase, Rac, drives new protrusions at the free edge (see [the lesson on integrins and Rho GTPases](/lessons/integrins-and-rho-gtpases/)). So a cell at the edge of the group tends to protrude from its free side and move outward.
2. **Mutual attraction keeps the group from scattering.** In *Xenopus*, crest cells attract one another chemically.
3. **A chemoattractant gives the group a direction.** The cells move up a gradient of the chemokine Sdf1.

The payoff: in *Xenopus* a cluster follows Sdf1 better than a single cell does. How far this recipe applies to trunk crest in birds and mammals is less clear.

## Stopping and gathering

Then the travellers have to stop and gather. This is the coalescence problem: no central planner tells cells where to form a ganglion, and how they manage it is still poorly understood. Three kinds of signal have some support: adhesion molecules such as N-cadherin, which help cells sort and hold together; chemoattractants such as SDF1, which draw cells to some sites; and boundaries such as Eph-ephrin and semaphorin signals, which divide the embryo into zones. Their roles and timing differ between ganglia. In chick sympathetic ganglia, crest cells first spread out along the aorta and only then sort into separate clusters, with N-cadherin contacts helping to shape them.

**Predict.** Remove N-cadherin from trunk crest cells. What happens to dorsal root ganglia if adhesion only holds clusters together once they have begun? What if it is needed to start them?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If N-cadherin only holds clusters together, the cells should still arrive and begin to gather, but the ganglia would be loose or poorly separated from their neighbours. If it is needed to start clusters, the cells would stay scattered along the route. The evidence above is from chick sympathetic ganglia; for dorsal root ganglia, treat both outcomes as predictions to test, not results.

</details>

Arriving is not the end of the test. Once these sensory and sympathetic neurons send axons to their targets, many die, as [the lesson on the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/) shows.

## Neurocristopathies: tissues that fail together often share a requirement

A disorder of neural crest cells is a neurocristopathy, a name Robert Bolande gave in 1974. Back to the opening patient. First, is it a coincidence? Using commonly cited rough rates, Hirschsprung disease affects about 1 in 5,000 births and Waardenburg syndrome about 1 in 42,000. If the two were independent, both would turn up together in about 1 in 210 million births. Yet the combination is a recognised syndrome that runs in families, with variants in the same few genes. That points to a shared cause, not chance.

That patient has Waardenburg syndrome type 4, also called Shah-Waardenburg syndrome. The genes are *SOX10*, *EDNRB* (the endothelin receptor B) and *EDN3*. Each is needed by both enteric precursors and melanocytes, including the melanocytes the inner ear needs for hearing. One broken gene, two lineages that need it.

Hirschsprung disease has a second feature to explain. In typical cases the segment without neurons begins at the far, anal end of the gut and extends back for a variable length. Rare skip segments break this pattern.

**Predict.** Take a toy model. Vagal crest colonises the gut as a single front, moving from the mouth end towards the anus, within a limited time window. Suppose the front moves more slowly than normal. Which part of the gut is left without neurons?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The end the front reaches last: a segment at the anal end. Put numbers on it. Give the gut a fixed length, and let the front move at constant speed and normally reach the anal end just as the window closes. Then the empty fraction equals the slowing: a front at 90 percent of normal speed leaves the last 10 percent empty, and one at 80 percent leaves the last 20 percent. If the gut grows unevenly, the numbers shift. This is an illustration, not a measured law, and it uses speed alone. Real front advance also depends on cell division and survival, which is why the table below says crest fails to *colonise* the distal gut rather than fails to *migrate*. Real colonisation is more complex still: in mice some vagal cells take a shortcut across the mesentery to the colon, sacral crest adds a small contribution, and the gut wall becomes less welcoming as it ages.

</details>

A shared crest start does not explain every pattern; a shared requirement explains several rows below. The step labels are entry points, not exclusive mechanisms: one disorder can touch several steps. Read the table by how widely each disorder spreads, and test a common guess against it: the earlier the failed step, the more tissues are hit.

| Disorder | Gene | Step that fails | Tissues hit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treacher Collins syndrome | *TCOF1*, a ribosome biogenesis gene | survival of early crest; in mice, cell death reduces crest | mainly the face: small jaw and cheekbones, ears, lower eyelids |
| Waardenburg syndrome type 1 | *PAX3*, needed later to switch on *MITF* in melanocyte precursors | melanocyte development | pigment changes and deafness |
| Waardenburg syndrome type 3 | *PAX3* | as type 1 | as type 1, plus limb muscle (not crest) |
| Waardenburg syndrome type 4 | *SOX10*, *EDNRB*, *EDN3* | development of enteric and melanocyte precursors | pigment, hearing, distal gut |
| PCWH | particular *SOX10* variants | as type 4, plus glial development and myelination | as type 4, plus Schwann cells (crest) and central oligodendrocytes (not crest) |
| Hirschsprung disease | *RET*, *EDNRB*, *EDN3*, *SOX10* | crest fails to colonise the distal gut | distal gut; *RET* is also used in the developing kidney (not crest) |
| Piebaldism | *KIT* | melanoblast survival or migration | patches that lack melanocytes altogether |

PCWH stands for peripheral demyelinating neuropathy, central dysmyelinating leukodystrophy, Waardenburg syndrome and Hirschsprung disease.

Two lessons sit in the table. First, Treacher Collins syndrome is a counter-example to the guess: it cuts crest numbers early, yet its effects stay mainly in the head and face. Which tissues are hit depends on where the failed gene is needed at vulnerable stages, and on the variant, its dose and each tissue's sensitivity: different *SOX10* variants give type 4 or the wider PCWH. Second, a shared requirement is not always a shared lineage: it reaches outside the crest, so a disorder can hit crest and non-crest tissues together. *PAX3* also works in limb muscle precursors, *SOX10* in central oligodendrocytes, and tyrosinase in the retinal pigment epithelium, which comes from the optic cup. Albinism sits at the very end of the chain for crest-derived pigment cells.

Two steps are missing from the table. Border genes such as *PAX3* act in specification at the border in animal embryos, but no disorder in this table traces to that step alone. Few human disorders trace to EMT alone either; the clearest evidence on EMT is from chick.

## Going deeper

### History of the crest idea

Grafting and removal experiments in amphibians gave Platt's conclusion experimental support in later decades, notably the work of Sven Hörstadius and Sven Sellman in the 1940s. Some authors call the crest a "fourth germ layer". That is a popular label, not a formal term: the crest arises from ectoderm.

### Genetic lineage tracing

The modern equivalent of the quail mark is genetic: a Cre line is a mouse strain that makes the enzyme Cre recombinase in a chosen cell type, and Cre switches on a reporter gene permanently in those cells and all their descendants.

### A second, later source of trunk derivatives

In mice, Schwann cell precursors, crest-derived cells that travel along growing nerves, give rise to many adrenal chromaffin cells and skin melanocytes. They reach their targets along nerves, a later route than the two main streams.

### The gene-regulatory network, arrow by arrow

Border genes include *Msx*, *Pax3/7* and *Zic*; *Sox9* is another crest gene. In chick cranial crest, for example, the Bronner laboratory later showed that Sox9, Ets1 and cMyb act directly on an enhancer of *Sox10*.

### Timing and a cadherin switch

Timing of EMT differs: chick trunk crest leaves after the tube has closed, while in the mouse head many cells leave while the folds are still open. A subset of migrating chick crest cells switches from Cadherin-6B to Cadherin-7.

### Segmented migration and segmented ganglia

Segmented migration is thought to help segment the dorsal root ganglia, but the two can be separated. In mice lacking one caudal-half repellent, crest enters both halves of the sclerotome and the ganglia still form in segments; the ganglia lose their segments only when two guidance pathways are lost together.

### Cleft palate

Cleft palate is harder to place in the table. The palatal shelves are built largely of crest-derived mesenchyme that must grow, lift and fuse, and many genes and environmental factors can make that fail.
