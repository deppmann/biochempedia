techniques:
  - name: "Pulse labeling of DNA synthesis"
    blurb: >-
      A labeled DNA building block, tritiated thymidine or the analogue BrdU, is given briefly and
      is taken up only by cells copying DNA. Fixing tissue at later times shows where the labeled
      nuclei go and when they divide, which gives the position of S phase, the movement of the
      nucleus and the length of the cell cycle.
  - name: "Retroviral lineage labeling with time-lapse imaging"
    blurb: >-
      A retrovirus carrying a marker gene such as GFP is injected into the ventricle, where it
      enters dividing progenitors and inserts into their DNA, so all their descendants are
      marked. Filming living slices then shows which cell divided and what each daughter became.
  - name: "Cell sorting and Cre-based fate mapping"
    blurb: >-
      Cells that make a fluorescent protein from a cell-type promoter can be sorted and grown
      alone to test what they can make. Cre recombinase made from the same promoter switches on a
      reporter permanently, so every descendant stays marked and lineage can be read in the
      animal.
  - name: "Whole-cell recording with dye filling"
    blurb: >-
      An electrode records a single cell's electrical properties and then fills it with a dye that
      does not pass easily to neighbours. The cell's full shape can then be matched to its
      physiology, which tied precursor-like recordings to radial glial shape.

**Look at a slice of embryonic mouse cortex and most cells caught in mitosis sit against the ventricle, the fluid-filled space at the centre of the brain. The cells copying their DNA sit farther out.**

Why would a cell copy its DNA in one place and divide in another? The answer leads to the cell that makes most cortical neurons, a cell long filed under a different job.

Some words first. The brain begins as the neural tube, an epithelium around a canal that becomes the ventricles (see [the neurulation lesson](/lessons/neurulation-and-tube-defects/)). The surface of the wall that faces the ventricle is apical. The outer surface, covered by a basement membrane and the pia, the thin membrane over the brain, is basal. A progenitor is a cell that still divides and whose daughters will become neurons or glia. The layer beside the ventricle where progenitors live is the ventricular zone (VZ).

## The idea: interkinetic nuclear migration

The early wall looks layered, with nuclei stacked at many heights, so nineteenth-century anatomists read it as several kinds of cells. In 1935 Frederick Sauer argued for a simpler picture. Each cell of the wall, a neuroepithelial cell, is long and thin and reaches from the apical surface to the basal one. Only the nucleus moves. It travels toward the ventricle to divide, and the daughters' nuclei travel back out. A single layer of cells whose nuclei sit at many heights is called a pseudostratified epithelium.

Picture a lift: the shaft, the cell, is fixed; the car, the nucleus, rides up and down on a timetable, the cell cycle. In G1 the nucleus moves basally. S phase, when DNA is copied, happens in the basal part of the VZ. In G2 the nucleus moves back to the apical surface, and mitosis happens there. Chris calls this "Interkinetic nuclear migration": movement between divisions.

Sauer's cycle was an inference from fixed sections. Tritiated thymidine, a radioactive DNA building block taken up only by cells in S phase, made it a timetable. Richard Sidman, Irene Miale and Ned Feder reported in 1959 that labeled nuclei in the embryonic neural wall moved between the outer wall and the ventricle in step with the cell cycle.

The movement has motors. In embryonic rat cortex, Jin-Wu Tsai, Richard Vallee, Arnold Kriegstein and colleagues (2010) knocked down motor proteins with RNA interference and found that the kinesin Kif1a carries the nucleus basally in G1 and cytoplasmic dynein pulls it apically in G2. That division of labour was measured in rat. In zebrafish retina the apical movement has been reported to depend on actomyosin instead, so it is not a rule for every vertebrate.

As neurogenesis begins (in mouse, around embryonic days 10 to 12), neuroepithelial cells become radial glia. They keep the shape, a foot on the ventricle and a long fibre to the pia, and they keep the lift. They also switch on proteins associated with glia, such as the glutamate transporter GLAST, the brain lipid-binding protein BLBP and, in primates, GFAP. Notch signalling between neighbours helps keep them as progenitors: in mouse forebrain, an activated form of Notch1 pushed cells toward radial glial identity (Gaiano, Nye and Fishell, 2000). How that receptor works is in [the Notch lesson](/lessons/notch-signaling/); how Notch picks which cell leaves is in [proneural genes and lateral inhibition](/lessons/proneural-and-lateral-inhibition/).

## One progenitor, several futures

Where cells divide does not tell you what each makes. For that you need lineage, the descendants of one cell. Chris names two ways in. In viral labeling, a virus inserts a marker gene into one progenitor's DNA, so all its daughters inherit the mark. In clonal analysis, you study the family of a single founder.

Sally Temple did clonal analysis in the most literal way (Nature, 1989). She dissociated the forebrain of rat embryos at embryonic days 13.5 to 14.5, isolated cells able to divide, and grew them one per tiny culture, with live conditioning cells present because the founders would not divide without them. Most of the clones held only neurons or only glia, while about a fifth (22 percent) were mixed: neurons together with non-neuronal cells. One founder had made both neurons and non-neuronal cells. Chris's terms for such cells are "Multipotent progenitors" and, where a cell can also renew itself, "Neural Stem Cells".

Keep the limit in view. A well shows a cell's potential, what it can make under set conditions, not its fate, what it makes in the embryo. And her cells came from the forebrain as a whole, so they need not have been cortical progenitors.

## The scaffold becomes the stem cell

Through the 1970s radial glia were known for another job. Pasko Rakic, using Golgi stains and serial electron microscopy in fetal rhesus monkey cortex, found young neurons pressed against radial fibres on their way to the cortical plate, the forming layer of neurons under the pia (Journal of Comparative Neurology, 1972). Radial glia were seen mainly as scaffolds, supports for neurons born from some other ventricular cell. Radial cells had been seen dividing, but few took them for the main source of neurons. How neurons climb the scaffold is in [radial migration](/lessons/radial-migration/).

Three lines of evidence, all in rodents and all around the year 2000, changed the picture.

1. **Sorting and fate mapping, in mouse.** Paolo Malatesta, Eva Hartfuss and Magdalena Götz (Development, 2000) used mice that make green fluorescent protein (GFP) from a human GFAP promoter, active in cortical radial glia, and sorted the green cells. In culture they made neurons. In 2003 the Götz group traced lineage in the living animal. Cre recombinase, an enzyme that recombines DNA at marked sites, was made from a GFAP promoter and switched on a permanent reporter in radial glia and all their descendants. Nearly all cortical projection neurons carried it, though the cortical interneurons, born in the ventral forebrain, did not.
2. **Filming lineage, in rat.** Stephen Noctor, Arnold Kriegstein and colleagues (Nature, 2001) injected a GFP retrovirus into the ventricles of embryonic rats and filmed living slices of cortex. Labeled radial glia divided at the ventricle, kept their fibre, and made daughters that became neurons and moved out along the parent's own fibre.
3. **Recording and filling, in rat.** The same group (Journal of Neuroscience, 2002) recorded from precursors with an electrode, then filled each recorded cell with dye. The filled cells had the shape of radial glia, and radial glial markers labeled nearly all cells caught in S phase or mitosis.

Each method has a blind spot: a dish shows potential, a recording electrode samples only the cells it can reach, and a marker is not a family tree. The three together persuaded. In embryonic rodent cortex, the cell that guides migrating neurons is also the cell that makes most of them.

## Two routes to a neuron: direct and indirect

A radial glial daughter can take one of two routes. In direct neurogenesis, a radial glial cell divides into another radial glial cell and a neuron. In indirect neurogenesis, the second daughter is an intermediate progenitor. It lets go of the ventricle, moves into the subventricular zone (SVZ), the band just outside the VZ, and divides there once more, usually into two neurons.

In direct neurogenesis a radial glial cell divides into another radial glial cell and a neuron; in indirect neurogenesis the second daughter is an intermediate progenitor that moves into the SVZ and usually divides into two neurons." />

Noctor and colleagues (Nature Neuroscience, 2004) tracked both routes in rat cortical slices labeled with a GFP retrovirus at embryonic day 16 and followed to day 19. In this study, about 79 percent of radial glial divisions were asymmetric, giving a radial glial cell plus a neuron or an intermediate progenitor, and about 90 percent of intermediate-progenitor divisions gave two neurons. The sample was 146 daughter cells, and only 19 divisions away from the ventricle, so read the percentages as rat, at those stages, in that study.

Transcription factors mark the steps. In mouse cortex, Chris Englund, Robert Hevner and colleagues (2005) found that Pax6 marks radial glia, Tbr2 (also called Eomes) marks intermediate progenitors and Tbr1 marks young neurons, in that order as cells move outward:

> radial glia (Pax6) → intermediate progenitor (Tbr2) → neuron (Tbr1)

Are the markers only labels? In 2008 two groups removed Tbr2 from the developing mouse brain (Arnold and colleagues; Sessa and colleagues). Division at the ventricle went on, division away from it fell, and fewer cortical neurons were made. Tbr2 is needed for the indirect route, not for all neurogenesis.

What makes two daughters unequal is the subject of [asymmetric division](/lessons/asymmetric-division/). The human cortex adds a third route, through outer radial glia far from the ventricle (Hansen and colleagues, 2010), taken up in [human brain expansion](/lessons/human-brain-expansion/).

## "CSF and basement membrane provide niche": counting what leaves

A niche is the local setting that keeps a stem cell a stem cell. A radial glial cell touches two. Its apical foot faces the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the ventricle; in mouse that fluid carries growth factors, among them IGF2, that support progenitor proliferation (Lehtinen and colleagues, 2011). Its basal foot rests on the basement membrane under the pia. In Chris's words, "CSF and basement membrane provide niche." In an asymmetric division, the daughter that leaves loses its contact with the apical surface.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/radial-glia-progenitors/radial-glia-niche.webp" width={1200} height={797} alt="AI-generated illustration: a radial glial cell stands on a band of cerebrospinal fluid with growth factor dots at its apical foot and its long fibre ends on the basement membrane under the pia, while a daughter cell moving up has lost its contact with the fluid band." caption="A radial glial cell touches two niches: cerebrospinal fluid at its apical foot and the basement membrane at its basal foot. The daughter that leaves in an asymmetric division loses its apical contact." />

Chris writes the cell population as P plus Q: P, progenitors; Q, cells that quit the cycle. Treat them as fractions of the daughters made each round, so P + Q = 1. A pool of N progenitors divides into 2N daughters:

> next pool = 2N × P;  neurons made this round = 2N × Q

Below Q = 0.5 the pool grows; at 0.5 it holds steady, as when every division is asymmetric; above it, it shrinks. In mouse cortex, Takahashi, Nowakowski and Caviness estimated that over the eleven cycles of neurogenesis Q passes 0.5 in the eighth cycle and reaches about 0.8 in the tenth.

The same group timed the clock (1995). In mouse dorsal cortex the cycle lengthens from about 8 hours at embryonic day 11 to about 18 hours near the end of neurogenesis, and nearly all the added time is in G1. One hypothesis links the two trends: a longer G1 gives fate determinants more time to act. In cultured mouse embryos, a drug that lengthened G1 without stopping the cycle led to early neurogenesis in the forebrain neuroepithelium (Calegari and Huttner, 2003). Layer order is in [birth order and layers](/lessons/birth-order-and-layers/); the later switch to glia, in [origins of glia](/lessons/origins-of-glia/).

### Design problem

One radial glial cell divides six times, each time keeping one radial glial daughter. How many neurons does it make if every division is direct (radial glia plus neuron)? How many if every division is indirect (radial glia plus an intermediate progenitor that divides once into two neurons)? Which change in division mode would most enlarge the cortex?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Direct: six divisions make 6 neurons, and one radial glial cell remains.

Indirect: each division adds one intermediate progenitor, which makes two neurons, so six divisions make 12 neurons, and one radial glial cell still remains. Routing output through intermediate progenitors doubles the neurons per radial glial division without spending the radial glial cell.

The bigger lever is earlier. A symmetric proliferative division, radial glia into two radial glia, makes no neurons now but doubles every later output. One such division before the six indirect ones gives two radial glial cells and 24 neurons; two such divisions give four and 48. In P plus Q terms, keeping Q low for a few extra early cycles multiplies the pool, and the pool multiplies everything after it.

</details>
