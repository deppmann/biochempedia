**Your brain is about 2 percent of your body weight and uses about 20 percent of the energy your body spends at rest.** Much of that energy is spent in axons and synapses, and the axon is the part of a neuron farthest from where most of its supplies are made. A motor axon running from your spinal cord to your foot is about a metre long. Along most of that length it is wrapped in myelin, a membrane coat made by glial cells that largely cuts it off from the fluid outside. So how is the far end of an axon fed?

This lesson's answer is that glial cells help. The evidence is firmest for the glia that wrap axons, the oligodendrocytes in the brain and spinal cord and, with fewer direct tests covered here, the Schwann cells in nerves, and weaker and more contested for the astrocytes around synapses. Astrocytes do take part in brain energy supply, for example by holding glycogen, a stored form of glucose that in the brain is held mostly in astrocytes. What is disputed is how much lactate they pass to neurons, and the lesson takes that up after the chemistry.

## The idea: an axon has a supply problem

Start with where the energy goes. Every action potential lets sodium in and potassium out, and excitatory synapses do much the same at a smaller scale. The Na+/K+-ATPase restores the gradients, spending one ATP to move three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in. Energy budgets worked out for rodent grey matter, beginning with Attwell and Laughlin in 2001 and revised since, put most of the energy used for signalling into this pumping, after action potentials and synaptic currents. The pumps never get a day off.

A quick refresher on cellular metabolism, since the rest of the lesson depends on it. [Glycolysis](/lessons/glycolysis/) splits one glucose into two pyruvate in the cytosol, with a net gain of two ATP, and turns NAD+ into NADH along the way. If the cell is to keep going, it must turn that NADH back into NAD+. Mitochondria can do it, taking in the electrons through shuttles such as the malate-aspartate shuttle while pyruvate goes into the citric acid cycle and [oxidative phosphorylation](/lessons/oxidative-phosphorylation/), which yields far more ATP. Or lactate dehydrogenase can reduce pyruvate to lactate, regenerating NAD+ in the cytosol. That reaction runs in both directions. A cell that takes up lactate can turn it back into pyruvate and burn it, so lactate works as a portable fuel. It crosses membranes on monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs), which also carry pyruvate.

Now place that chemistry in a neuron. Consider the problem of metabolic compartmentalization in neurons:

- **The soma** has dense mitochondria, makes most of the cell's proteins and has its biosynthetic machinery close at hand.
- **The axon** is long and thin. Its ATP has to be made locally, by axonal mitochondria and axonal glycolysis, because ATP cannot diffuse a metre. The enzymes and mitochondria themselves are mostly made in the soma and carried down by motor proteins that also spend ATP.
- **The synapse** has a high, fluctuating demand from recycling vesicles and pumping ions after each release.

Myelin adds a twist. In a myelinated axon, compact myelin covers the long stretches between nodes of Ranvier, and the axon membrane meets the extracellular fluid mainly at those narrow nodes. Insulation that speeds conduction also stands between the axon and its glucose. The glial cells that make the myelin, though, sit right against the axon along its whole length, each under its own segment. That position makes them candidate suppliers.

## Controversies surrounding lactate and astrocytes

Astrocytes are the other candidate. They wrap synapses, take glutamate back up after release, and put end-feet on blood vessels, so they sit between the blood's glucose and the neuron.

In 1994 Luc Pellerin and Pierre Magistretti gave glutamate to cultured mouse astrocytes. The cells took up more 2-deoxyglucose, a glucose analogue, and released more lactate. D-aspartate, an analogue taken up by the same transporters, had the same effect, and the authors concluded that receptor activation was not responsible. Blocking the sodium pump with ouabain blocked the response. Their explanation was that glutamate transporters bring sodium in with each glutamate, the pump has to remove it, and the pump's demand for ATP drives glycolysis. From this they proposed the astrocyte-neuron lactate shuttle: an active synapse makes its astrocyte burn glucose to lactate, and the lactate goes to the neuron as fuel. Astrocytes were reported to export lactate mainly through MCT1 and MCT4, and neurons to take it up through MCT2.

The model is attractive because it couples supply to use, and because it would explain why glucose uptake rises where synapses are active. Some later in vivo work supports it. In 2016 Philipp Mächler, Bruno Weber and colleagues used a fluorescent lactate sensor in mouse cortex and reported a lactate gradient running from astrocytes to neurons.

The objections are serious. Critics, including Gerald Dienel, have argued that culture results do not transfer cleanly to the brain and that lactate's near-equilibrium chemistry makes net flow hard to infer. In 2017 Gary Yellen's laboratory imaged sensors of NADH and other metabolites in single neurons of mouse brain slices and live mice. Stimulation made neurons raise their own glycolysis, and the group saw no sign of lactate import during the stimulus. Both sides now agree that neurons can take up and use glucose directly. They disagree about how much of a neuron's fuel comes through astrocytes, when, and in which brain region.

So treat the shuttle as a hypothesis with partial support. The answer may differ between resting and active states, between cortex and other regions, and between preparations.

## Myelinating glia feed the axons they wrap

The case is firmer for oligodendrocytes, the myelinating glia of the CNS. It began with a puzzle from [myelination](/lessons/myelination/) research. Mice lacking the myelin protein PLP (1998) or the enzyme CNP (2003) made myelin that looked close to normal, yet their axons swelled and degenerated as the mice aged. The insulation was there; something else was missing.

The Cox10 deletion experiment (the design problem below, reported by Fünfschilling, Nave and colleagues in 2012) showed that oligodendrocytes can live by glycolysis, and it fits with them feeding axons. It does not by itself show the feeding. Experiments on a transporter came closer, though they too infer the transfer rather than trace it. In 2012 Youngjin Lee, Jeffrey Rothstein and colleagues reported that MCT1 is concentrated in oligodendrocytes. Lowering MCT1 in cultured slices of spinal cord, or in the spinal cord of mice, killed motor neurons, and in mice it also caused axon swellings. In the cultures, adding lactate protected neurons stressed by an MCT1 inhibitor and a short period without glucose. The group also found less MCT1 in tissue from people with ALS and from an ALS mouse model. A later, cleaner test from the same group deleted MCT1 only in oligodendrocyte-lineage cells (Philips and colleagues, 2021). Young mice were normal; axons degenerated, and myelin thinned, only as the mice aged. Put together, the picture is of oligodendrocytes that take up glucose, run glycolysis, and export lactate or pyruvate through MCT1 to the axon, which takes it in and burns it in its mitochondria. The exact route from glial cytoplasm to axon is still being worked out, and so is how much of an axon's ATP depends on it in a living animal.

## After injury: when glial metabolism changes

In the peripheral nervous system, Schwann cells wrap axons the way oligodendrocytes do in the CNS. In 2011 the laboratory of Jeffrey Milbrandt reported that mice whose Schwann cells cannot maintain their mitochondrial DNA develop a neuropathy in which axons degenerate. Schwann cells also respond to injury with their own metabolic programs. After a nerve is cut, the pieces of axon cut off from the cell body degenerate, a process covered in [injury and regeneration](/lessons/injury-and-regeneration/). In 2020 Elisabetta Babetto, Keit Men Wong and Bogdan Beirowski reported that mouse Schwann cells shift toward glycolysis after injury, and that this shift slows the degeneration of the injured axons.

The signals that steer such shifts are ones you know from [mTOR and AMPK](/lessons/mtor-and-ampk/). AMPK senses a falling energy charge and turns on catabolism and autophagy. mTOR drives growth and lipid synthesis when nutrients and growth factors are plentiful. HIF1-alpha, a transcription factor stabilized at low oxygen, turns on glycolytic genes. In injured mouse nerve, mTORC1 and HIF1-alpha were reported to drive the glycolytic shift in Schwann cells; the role of AMPK there is less clear, and how they divide the work in a repairing nerve is not settled.

Satellite glia, which surround neuron cell bodies in sensory ganglia, change too. In 2020 the laboratory of Valeria Cavalli reported that after peripheral nerve injury in mice, satellite glia turn on genes for fatty acid synthesis, and that this program helps the sensory axons regrow.

Here is the big picture question: how might these metabolic changes in glia be beneficial or detrimental to neuronal survival and function? A glial cell that makes more lactate may tide a stressed axon over, and a glial cell that makes lipids may supply membrane for regrowth. Whether the net effect on the axon is a benefit or a harm probably depends on the cell, the injury and the timing, and that is still being measured.

### Design problem: do oligodendrocytes need to respire?

You want to test whether oligodendrocytes must respire to keep axons alive. You delete Cox10, a gene needed to assemble complex IV of the respiratory chain, only in myelinating cells, using a Cre line active in those cells. Once their existing complex IV wears out, the oligodendrocytes can make ATP only by glycolysis. What do you expect if oligodendrocytes must respire to support axons, and what result would instead support glycolytic oligodendrocytes feeding axons with lactate?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

If oligodendrocytes must respire, they lose energy, and either they fail or the myelin and axons they serve fail. Over months you would expect thinning or loss of myelin, swollen and degenerating axons, and perhaps death of neurons whose axons run through the white matter.

If instead oligodendrocytes can live by glycolysis and pass its products on, the picture is the reverse. The oligodendrocytes survive, the myelin stays, the axons stay intact, and, because the glia now make more lactate than they can burn, lactate may build up in the tissue unless the axons use it as fast as it is made.

This is roughly what Ursula Fünfschilling, Klaus-Armin Nave and colleagues reported in 2012. In the adult CNS, once myelination was complete, myelin and axons were largely preserved over the months the mice were followed. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy showed more lactate in mutant than in control brains, but only in mice under isoflurane anaesthesia; it fell below detection within an hour after the anaesthetic ended. The peripheral nerves told a different story. Schwann cells lacking Cox10 failed to myelinate properly, and the mice developed a severe neuropathy. The Schwann cells survived, but they lost respiration while still dividing and myelinating, and that stage is energy-costly. In these mice, then, oligodendrocytes that had already made myelin could do without respiration, while developing Schwann cells could not myelinate without it.

## Going deeper

### Background numbers

In rat liver the free NAD+-to-NADH ratio in the cytosol has been estimated at several hundred to one, which keeps glycolysis running in the forward direction; values in neurons depend on the cell and the measurement.

How many glial cells are there to do this work? Older textbooks said glia outnumber neurons by ten to one. Counts of whole human brains by the isotropic fractionator method (Azevedo and colleagues, 2009) found roughly as many non-neuronal cells, most of them glia, as neurons overall, with glia outnumbering neurons in the cerebral cortex and far outnumbered by neurons in the cerebellum.

### Possible costs of glial glycolysis (hypotheses)

Two possible costs of a large glial glycolytic response have been proposed, and neither is established in injured nerve. Lactate comes with protons, so a large glycolytic output can acidify the tissue, and a glial cell that diverts its resources to its own repair program may give less to the axon. Which outcome wins probably depends on the cell, the injury and the timing, and that is still being measured.
