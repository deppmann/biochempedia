    profileSourceAnchor: "Pellerin & Magistretti, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 91:10625 (1994)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In the early 1990s, brain imaging was built on a puzzle. Positron emission tomography
      tracked glucose use, and glucose use rose where neurons were active, but nobody could say
      which cells were burning the extra glucose or what linked the two. Pellerin, working in
      Pierre Magistretti's laboratory at the University of Lausanne, tested one candidate link in
      astrocytes grown in culture from mouse cortex. When the cells were given glutamate, the
      transmitter that excitatory synapses release, they took up more of a labelled glucose
      analogue and released more lactate. The effect followed glutamate transport, which carries
      sodium into the cell, and not glutamate receptors, and it needed the sodium pump. The pair
      proposed that every glutamate cleared from a synapse sets off glycolysis in the astrocyte,
      whose lactate then goes to the neuron as fuel. The proposal gave imaging a cellular
      mechanism to test, and it set off a debate about lactate that is still running.

techniques:
  - name: "Glucose analogue uptake in cultured cells"
    blurb: >-
      Cells are given 2-deoxyglucose, which is taken up and phosphorylated like glucose but goes
      no further and so stays trapped inside. The amount trapped measures glucose uptake. Pellerin
      and Magistretti used it to show that glutamate raises glucose uptake in cultured astrocytes.
  - name: "Conditional knockout with Cre and lox"
    blurb: >-
      A gene is flanked by short lox sequences, and the enzyme Cre, made only in chosen cells,
      cuts it out there. The rest of the animal keeps the gene. Deleting Cox10 only in myelinating
      cells asked whether those cells need respiration, while sparing nearly all neurons.
  - name: "Genetically encoded metabolite sensors"
    blurb: >-
      A fluorescent protein is engineered so that its brightness or colour changes when it binds
      a metabolite, such as lactate, or reports a ratio, such as NADH to NAD+. Expressed in one
      cell type, it reports that cell's metabolism in living tissue, which bulk chemistry cannot.
  - name: "Organotypic slice culture with gene knockdown"
    blurb: >-
      A thin slice of tissue, here spinal cord, is kept alive in a dish with its cells in place. An
      antisense oligonucleotide or an inhibitor is added to the medium to lower or block one gene product, and neurons and axons are counted
      afterwards. Adding a metabolite to the medium tests whether a missing supply caused the loss.

**Your brain is about 2 percent of your body weight and uses about 20 percent of the energy your body spends at rest.** Much of that energy is spent in axons and synapses, and the axon is the part of a neuron farthest from where most of its supplies are made. A motor axon running from your spinal cord to your foot is about a metre long. Along most of that length it is wrapped in myelin, a membrane coat made by glial cells that limits the axon's direct access to the fluid outside. So how is the far end of an axon fed?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Whoever wraps the axon helps feed it. Myelin covers almost all of a long, fully myelinated axon, and along each myelinated stretch the glial cell that made the myelin is the cell pressed against it. That cell can live on glycolysis (shown for oligodendrocytes that have already made their myelin, not for Schwann cells that are still myelinating), and the evidence fits it passing lactate or pyruvate across to the axon. When it loses the transporter thought to carry that fuel out, axons in ageing mice degenerate.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which cells.** Myelinating glia, most clearly oligodendrocytes, which myelinate axons in the brain and spinal cord. Schwann cells, which myelinate axons in peripheral nerves such as the one to your foot, have had fewer direct tests. Whether astrocytes feed neurons at synapses is a separate, contested question.
- **Which job.** Long-term upkeep of the axon, because that is what the experiments test. Glial fuel for each action potential as it fires has been proposed; the experiments do not measure it and do not rule it out.
- **Help, not sole supply.** Axons still take up glucose and still have their own mitochondria.

This is **metabolic support**: fuel from the cell alongside the axon, not the target-derived survival factor of [the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/).

## An axon has a supply problem

Three facts make the problem: what the axon spends, how far it is from the cell body, and how much of it is covered.

**Cost.** Every action potential lets sodium in and potassium out, and excitatory synapses do much the same at a smaller scale. The sodium-potassium pump (Na+/K+-ATPase) restores the gradients, spending one ATP to move three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in. Energy budgets for rodent grey matter, beginning with Attwell and Laughlin in 2001 and revised since, find that most of the energy used for signalling goes to these pumps. The pumps never get a day off.

**Distance.** A neuron's three parts have different supply problems.

- **The soma** has dense mitochondria, makes most of the cell's proteins and has its biosynthetic machinery close at hand.
- **The axon** is long and thin. The enzymes and mitochondria it uses are mostly made in the soma and carried down by motor proteins that also spend ATP.
- **The synapse** has a high, fluctuating demand from recycling vesicles and pumping ions after each release.

**Predict.** The soma is rich in mitochondria. Why can it not simply make ATP and let it diffuse down the axon? Guess how long ATP would take to diffuse one micrometre, one millimetre, one centimetre and one metre.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Diffusion time grows with the square of the distance: double the distance and the time goes up four times. These rough estimates use time ≈ L²/2D, with a diffusion coefficient D for ATP in cytoplasm of about 150 to 300 µm² per second. They ignore that ATP is used up along the way, which makes the far end harder still to reach.

| Distance | Time for ATP to diffuse that far |
| --- | --- |
| 1 micrometre | a few milliseconds |
| 1 millimetre | about half an hour to an hour |
| 1 centimetre | about 2 to 4 days |
| 1 metre | about 50 to 100 years |

So ATP spreads easily within one short stretch of axon; the problem is the distance from the soma to the far end, not that ATP diffuses poorly. An axon's ATP has to be made locally, by axonal mitochondria and axonal glycolysis.

</details>

Many proteins come from the soma by the slow form of axonal transport, a few millimetres a day, so they take months to more than a year to cover a metre.

**Cover.** In a myelinated axon, compact myelin covers the long stretches between the nodes of Ranvier, the short gaps where the axon membrane is bare. A node is about a micrometre long. The myelinated stretch between two nodes, the internode, is about 50 to 100 micrometres in many CNS axons and about a millimetre in a large peripheral axon. So along a continuously myelinated stretch, myelin covers about 98 to 99 percent of a CNS axon's length and about 99.9 percent of a large peripheral one. Real axons vary, and some CNS axons are myelinated only in part.

That cover is not a seal. A thin space remains between the axon and its myelin, but the axon membrane meets the extracellular fluid mainly at the nodes, so the insulation that speeds conduction also limits the axon's direct access to glucose. Whether the glucose that enters at the nodes and along the space under the myelin would be enough on its own is not known. So the geometry makes glial supply plausible; it does not prove the axon needs it. The genetic experiments below test that.

**Who is in contact.** Each segment of myelin is a sheet of one glial cell's membrane, wound around the axon, with the cell's cytoplasm still connected to it. In the CNS one oligodendrocyte makes many segments, on many different axons. In a peripheral nerve each myelinating Schwann cell makes one. So along each myelinated stretch, the cell pressed against the axon is the cell that made its myelin, which makes myelinating glia candidate suppliers.

## Just enough chemistry

[Glycolysis](/lessons/glycolysis/) splits one glucose into two pyruvate in the cytosol, with a net gain of two ATP, and turns NAD+ into NADH along the way. If the cell is to keep going, it must turn that NADH back into NAD+. Pyruvate then has two routes.

1. **Into the mitochondrion.** Pyruvate goes into the citric acid cycle and [oxidative phosphorylation](/lessons/oxidative-phosphorylation/), which yields far more ATP. The NADH made in glycolysis passes its electrons into the mitochondria through shuttles such as the malate-aspartate shuttle.
2. **To lactate.** Lactate dehydrogenase reduces pyruvate to lactate, regenerating NAD+ in the cytosol. Glycolysis can then keep running without mitochondria.

The lactate dehydrogenase reaction runs in both directions. A cell that takes up lactate can turn it back into pyruvate and burn it in its own mitochondria. Lactate and pyruvate cross membranes on monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs).

Now the arithmetic that makes lactate worth passing on. Burned all the way, one glucose can yield about 30 to 32 ATP, depending on which shuttle carries the NADH from glycolysis. A cell that stops at lactate keeps 2 ATP and passes on the rest. The two lactates still carry about 28 to 30 of those 30 to 32 ATP, more than nine-tenths. Lactate is a portable fuel.

## Myelin that looked fine, axons that did not

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The case for myelinating glia began with a puzzle from [myelination](/lessons/myelination/) research. Mice lacking the myelin protein PLP (Griffiths and colleagues, 1998), and later mice lacking the myelin enzyme CNP (Lappe-Siefke and colleagues, 2003), made myelin that looked close to normal under the electron microscope. Yet as the mice aged, their axons swelled and degenerated.

Normal-looking myelin does not prove the insulation worked normally, but it makes failed insulation an unlikely full explanation. The simplest reading is that the glial cell gives the axon something besides insulation. Fuel is one candidate, and two experiments test it.

### Switch off the glial cell's respiration

Ursula Fünfschilling, Klaus-Armin Nave and colleagues deleted *Cox10* only in myelinating cells with a conditional knockout: the gene is cut out only in cells that make the enzyme Cre, here a Cre line active in myelinating cells. Cox10 is needed to assemble complex IV, the last step of the respiratory chain. Once the cells' existing complex IV wears out, they can make ATP only by glycolysis.

**Predict.** Suppose oligodendrocytes must respire to keep their axons healthy. What would you expect to see in the adult brain over the following months? And what would you expect instead if they can live on glycolysis and pass its products to the axon?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If the glia had to respire, they or the axons would fail: over months, thinning or loss of myelin, swollen and degenerating axons, and perhaps the death of neurons whose axons run through white matter.

The 2012 result was the reverse. In the adult CNS, once myelination was complete, the oligodendrocytes survived, and myelin and axons were largely preserved over the months the mice were followed. Oligodendrocytes that have made their myelin can live on glycolysis alone, and the axons did not need their oligodendrocytes to respire. With complex IV gone, the glia cannot burn pyruvate in their own mitochondria, so any pyruvate and lactate they make by glycolysis would have to leave the cell or be left over. Leaving would fit glia feeding axons, but the result does not by itself show the feeding.

The peripheral nerves told a different story, and it sets a limit on the rule. Schwann cells lacking Cox10 failed to myelinate properly, and the mice developed a severe neuropathy. The Schwann cells survived, but they lost respiration while still dividing and myelinating, an energy-costly stage, and could not myelinate without it. Part of the difference may be the stage of myelination, not only the cell type. So the rule, as tested here, describes oligodendrocytes that have already made their myelin.

</details>

The mutant brains held one more clue. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy, which measures chemicals such as lactate in the living brain, found more lactate in mutant brains than in controls, but only while the mice were under isoflurane, an inhaled anaesthetic. Within an hour after the anaesthetic ended, the lactate fell below detection.

**Predict.** Why would extra lactate show up only under anaesthesia?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

One reading: glia that rely on glycolysis make more lactate than they can burn. In an awake animal, something uses that lactate as fast as it is made; under anaesthesia, that use slows and the excess builds up. The axons the glia wrap are an obvious candidate user, but the experiment never traced where the lactate went.

</details>

## Cut the export line

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

If glycolytic glia feed axons, the product has to leave the glial cell on a monocarboxylate transporter. In 2012 Youngjin Lee, Jeffrey Rothstein and colleagues used reporter mice, engineered so that cells making MCT1 carry a visible label, to show that one of them, MCT1, is concentrated in oligodendrocytes. They then lowered MCT1 and watched the neurons.

**Predict.** In cultured slices of spinal cord, the group withheld glucose for a short time, a stress the slices normally survive, so that any need for another fuel would show. In some slices they also blocked MCT1 with an inhibitor, and to some of those they added lactate. If oligodendrocytes feed neurons through MCT1, what should happen to the motor neurons in each condition?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

| Slice culture condition | Motor neurons |
| --- | --- |
| Glucose withheld briefly | survive |
| Glucose withheld, MCT1 blocked | die |
| Glucose withheld, MCT1 blocked, lactate added | survive |

Adding back the missing product while the transporter is still blocked is a **rescue**. In the terms of [the neurotrophic hypothesis lesson](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/), blocking MCT1 asks whether it is necessary, and adding lactate asks whether lactate is enough to replace what the block took away. Because lactate rescued the neurons, the best reading is that they were missing lactate, a monocarboxylate that MCT1 carries and that glia are thought to supply. That reading does not rule out every other effect of the drug. The drug also acts on every cell in the slice (and inhibitors of this kind can also reach MCT2 in neurons), so it does not show which cell's transporter mattered or the direction of transfer.

</details>

Lowering MCT1 itself in cultured slices of spinal cord also killed motor neurons. When the group lowered MCT1 in the spinal cord of living mice, axons swelled and degenerated, and motor neurons were lost. That knockdown, too, was not limited to oligodendrocytes. They also found less MCT1 in tissue from people with ALS and from an ALS mouse model.

A cleaner test came later from the same group. Philips and colleagues (2021) deleted MCT1 only in cells of the oligodendrocyte lineage. Young mice were normal. Only as the mice aged did axons degenerate, and myelin thinned.

### What the experiments show together

Switch off oligodendrocytes' respiration, and adult CNS axons are spared over the months followed. Remove the transporter they are thought to export fuel through, and axons degenerate as the mice age. One caution: the two sets of mice were not followed over matched ages, and MCT1 loss also thinned myelin late.

Put together, the picture that fits is this. Oligodendrocytes take up glucose, run glycolysis, and export lactate or pyruvate through MCT1. The axon takes it in, turns any lactate back into pyruvate, and burns the pyruvate in its own mitochondria.

Two things are still unknown: the exact route from glial cytoplasm to axon, and how much of an axon's ATP, in any tract of a living animal, depends on this supply.

## Astrocytes: a contested second candidate

Astrocytes wrap synapses, take glutamate back up after release, and put end-feet on blood vessels, so they sit between the blood's glucose and the neuron.

In 1994 Luc Pellerin and Pierre Magistretti gave glutamate to cultured mouse astrocytes. The cells took up more 2-deoxyglucose, a glucose analogue, and released more lactate. D-aspartate, an analogue taken up by the same transporters, had the same effect, and the authors concluded that receptor activation was not responsible. Blocking the sodium pump with ouabain blocked the response. Their explanation had three steps. Glutamate transporters bring sodium in with each glutamate. The sodium pump has to remove that sodium. The pump's demand for ATP drives glycolysis. From this they proposed the astrocyte-neuron lactate shuttle: an active synapse makes its astrocyte burn glucose to lactate, and the lactate goes to the neuron as fuel.

The model is attractive because it couples supply to use and would explain why glucose uptake rises where synapses are active. Some later in vivo work supports it. In 2016 Philipp Mächler, Bruno Weber and colleagues used a fluorescent lactate sensor in mouse cortex and reported a lactate gradient running from astrocytes to neurons. A gradient fits transfer but does not by itself measure net flow.

The objections are serious. Critics, including Gerald Dienel, have argued that culture results do not transfer cleanly to the brain, and that because lactate is in near-equilibrium with pyruvate through lactate dehydrogenase, net flow between cells is hard to infer from where the enzymes and transporters are. In 2017 Gary Yellen's laboratory imaged sensors of NADH and other metabolites in single neurons of mouse brain slices and live mice. Stimulation made neurons raise their own glycolysis, and the group saw no sign of lactate import during the stimulus.

Both sides now agree that neurons can take up and use glucose directly; they disagree about how much of a neuron's fuel comes through astrocytes, when, and in which brain region. So treat the shuttle as a hypothesis with partial support. The answer may differ between resting and active states, between cortex and other regions, and between preparations.

## After injury: when glial metabolism changes

After a peripheral nerve is cut, the pieces of axon cut off from the cell body degenerate, a process covered in [injury and regeneration](/lessons/injury-and-regeneration/). Schwann cells respond to the injury with metabolic programs of their own.

**Predict.** After injury, mouse Schwann cells shift toward glycolysis. This goes beyond the rule, which is about intact axons. Would you expect the shift to help or harm the cut-off piece of axon, and what would have to be true for it to help?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If the extra lactate or pyruvate leaves the Schwann cell and the injured axon uses it, the shift should help, at least for a while; more glycolysis alone does not guarantee that. In 2020 Elisabetta Babetto, Keit Men Wong and Bogdan Beirowski, who reported the shift, found that it slows the degeneration of the injured axons.

</details>

The big question is how these metabolic changes in glia might be beneficial or detrimental to neuronal survival and function. A glial cell that makes more lactate may tide a stressed axon over, and a glial cell that makes lipids may supply membrane for regrowth. Glial metabolism can also go wrong in ways that harm axons (see Going deeper). Whether the net effect on the axon is a benefit or a harm probably depends on the cell, the injury and the timing, and that is still being measured.

The guiding idea survives in its "helps" form only, with oligodendrocytes as the clearest case: the glia are not shown to be the axon's only supply, and the transfer has been inferred, not traced.

### Design problem: trace the transfer

You want to show that lactate made in oligodendrocytes is burned in the axon, not just that it is needed. Design the experiment.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The Cox10 experiment showed that glial respiration is not necessary for the axon, at least over the months studied. The MCT1 experiments showed that glial MCT1 is necessary as mice age. Neither follows the fuel. Two approaches, which can be combined:

- **Follow a labelled fuel.** Get the label into the glial cell first: for example, give labelled glucose to a compartment holding oligodendrocyte cell bodies whose processes myelinate axons in a second compartment. Measure labelled lactate released by the glia and labelled pyruvate and citric acid cycle products in the axon. Control for tracer leaking into the axon compartment, for the axon taking up glucose directly, and for loss of glial MCT1, which should lower the axon's label. Such a system is hard to build.
- **Watch the axon's lactate.** Express a lactate sensor in neurons and image an axon in white matter while glial MCT1 is deleted. A fall would fit a lost glial supply, but the axon could make up the loss with its own glycolysis, so a steady signal would not rule one out. An NADH sensor alone cannot tell the sources apart, since glycolysis and lactate conversion both make NADH.

Either way, the hard part is the axon's own glucose uptake, which would otherwise look like a glial supply.

</details>

## Going deeper

### What steers the glycolytic shift after injury

The signals that steer such shifts are ones you know from [mTOR and AMPK](/lessons/mtor-and-ampk/). AMPK senses a falling energy charge and turns on catabolism and autophagy. mTOR drives growth and lipid synthesis when nutrients and growth factors are plentiful. HIF1-alpha, a transcription factor stabilized at low oxygen, turns on glycolytic genes. In injured mouse nerve, mTORC1 and HIF1-alpha were reported to drive the glycolytic shift in Schwann cells; the role of AMPK there is less clear, and how they divide the work in a repairing nerve is not settled.

### Satellite glia and lipids for regrowth

Satellite glia, which surround neuron cell bodies in sensory ganglia, change too. In 2020 the laboratory of Valeria Cavalli reported that after peripheral nerve injury in mice, satellite glia turn on genes for fatty acid synthesis, and that this program helps the sensory axons regrow.

### When glial metabolism harms axons

In 2011 the laboratory of Jeffrey Milbrandt reported that mice whose Schwann cells cannot maintain their mitochondrial DNA develop a neuropathy in which axons degenerate. That looks at first like a missing fuel. A follow-up from the same laboratory (2013) reported that the metabolically abnormal Schwann cells release acylcarnitines, lipid metabolites that damage axons, and proposed this as one contributor to the neuropathy. If that account holds, glial metabolism here harms axons, not only fails to supply them.

### Astrocytes: glycogen and transporters

Astrocytes do take part in brain energy supply, for example by holding glycogen, a stored form of glucose that in the brain is held mostly in astrocytes. In the shuttle model, astrocytes were reported to export lactate mainly through MCT1 and MCT4, and neurons to take it up through MCT2.

### Energy budgets in white matter

The budgets in the main text are for grey matter. Harris and Attwell (2012) estimated that white matter uses less energy per volume than grey matter, with a larger share going to upkeep that is not signalling. A budget like this does not say which demands glial fuel meets.

### Background numbers

In rat liver the free NAD+-to-NADH ratio in the cytosol has been estimated at several hundred to one, which keeps glycolysis running in the forward direction; values in neurons depend on the cell and the measurement.

How many glial cells are there to do this work? Older textbooks said glia outnumber neurons by ten to one. Counts of whole human brains by the isotropic fractionator method (Azevedo and colleagues, 2009) found roughly as many non-neuronal cells, most of them glia, as neurons overall, with glia outnumbering neurons in the cerebral cortex and far outnumbered by neurons in the cerebellum.

### Possible costs of glial glycolysis (hypotheses)

Two possible costs of a large glial glycolytic response have been proposed, and neither is established in injured nerve. Lactate comes with protons, so a large glycolytic output can acidify the tissue, and a glial cell that diverts its resources to its own repair program may give less to the axon.
