**A seabird that takes in seawater or a salt load faces a problem: bird kidneys cannot make urine as salty as the sea. Where does the extra salt go?**

The answer is a pair of glands in its head, one solution to a problem every animal faces: holding its water and salt when the surroundings pull them the other way. This lesson compares how animals in the sea, in fresh water and on land solve it, and how they dispose of the nitrogen left over from protein.

## Three osmotic worlds

Water crosses membranes by **osmosis**, the net movement of water toward the side with more dissolved particles. How strongly a fluid draws water depends on its **osmolality**, the number of dissolved particles per kilogram of water, in milliosmoles per kilogram (mOsm per kg). Osmolality counts particles, not charges, so mOsm is not mEq. How water and ions cross membranes is in [membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/).

Some reference values, all approximate: seawater is near 1,000 mOsm per kg, fresh water is usually below 10, adult human plasma is about 290, and the blood of bony fish is roughly 270 to 400, lower in freshwater species than in marine ones.

An **osmoconformer** lets the osmolality of its extracellular fluid follow its surroundings. An **osmoregulator** holds its extracellular osmolality relatively steady when the surroundings change. Conformers still regulate. Many marine invertebrates conform outside their cells but keep their cells from swelling or shrinking by adjusting **organic osmolytes**, small organic molecules such as amino acids that cells accumulate to match the outside without disturbing their proteins ([tissues and homeostasis](/lessons/tissues-and-homeostasis/) has examples). A **stenohaline** animal tolerates only a narrow range of salinity; a **euryhaline** one tolerates a broad range.

Each habitat sets a different problem. For an osmoregulator in **seawater** whose blood is more dilute than the sea, such as a marine bony fish, the problem is losing water by osmosis and gaining salt by diffusion. In **fresh water** it is the reverse: water floods in and salts leak out. On **land** the threat is evaporation from skin and breathing surfaces, plus water in urine and feces, and in mammals sweat. Land animals gain water by drinking, in food, and as **metabolic water**, made when fuels are oxidized. The kangaroo rat Dipodomys merriami can live on dry seeds and metabolic water without drinking, though in the wild it also eats insects and succulent plants that supply water; the [kidney](/lessons/kidney/) lesson explains how.

## Fish in sea and river, and a shark full of urea

**Teleosts**, the large group of bony fish that includes salmon, cod and goldfish, are osmoregulators. A marine teleost's blood is roughly a third as concentrated as seawater, so it loses water across its gills. It drinks seawater and absorbs water, along with ions, through its intestine. That leaves a salt surplus. Excess monovalent ions such as sodium and chloride leave mostly across the gills, through ion-transporting cells. Most divalent ions, such as magnesium and sulfate, are never absorbed and leave with the gut fluid; the share that is absorbed leaves through the kidneys in a small volume of urine. The sodium chloride does not leave mainly in urine.

A freshwater teleost has the opposite budget. Water enters across its gills by osmosis and ions diffuse out. It makes a large volume of dilute urine, and its gills take up sodium and chloride from the water actively, using ATP. A review by Takvam and colleagues (2021) reports urine flows of roughly 1 to 6 mL per kg per hour across freshwater teleosts. Euryhaline fish such as salmon and eels move between the two worlds, and as they do, their gill ion-transporting cells switch between salt uptake and salt secretion. In tilapia moved into salt water, some existing cells start to change within hours, while others close and are lost; full acclimation takes longer.

**Worked problem.** These numbers are illustrative. Take a freshwater teleost of 1.0 kg at steady state, meaning its total body water and sodium do not change over the day. Assume urine is its only route of water loss and that it gains no sodium from food.

1. *Urine and water.* Urine flow is 4.0 mL per kg per hour, inside the reported range. Daily urine = 4.0 x 1.0 x 24 = 96 mL per day. At steady state, osmotic water gain must equal water loss, so the fish also gains 96 mL of water a day.
2. *Sodium.* Urine sodium is 10 mmol per L. Urinary loss = 0.096 L x 10 mmol per L = 0.96 mmol per day. If diffusive loss across the gills is 2.0 mmol per day, total loss is 2.96 mmol per day, so the gills must take up 2.96 mmol per day. Here most of the uptake replaces the gill leak. Measured gill sodium losses in real fish are usually larger than this, so in life the gill's share is bigger still.

Sharks, skates and rays, the **elasmobranchs** (fish with skeletons of cartilage), take a third route. In the spiny dogfish Squalus acanthias and its relatives, plasma holds less salt than seawater, but the blood also carries a large amount of **urea**, which the fish makes itself, plus **trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO)**. The kidney returns most filtered urea to the blood, and the gills let very little leak out. Homer Smith gathered the evidence for urea retention in a 1936 review. Urea at these levels tends to destabilize proteins. In laboratory tests Paul Yancey and George Somero (1979) found that methylamines such as TMAO counteract that effect.

3. *Shark osmolality.* Take illustrative plasma values of 600 mOsm per kg of inorganic ions, 350 of urea and 70 of TMAO: total 1,020 mOsm per kg, against seawater taken as 1,000. The plasma is slightly **hyperosmotic** (more concentrated), so water enters slowly by osmosis and the shark need not drink seawater. Remove the urea and the total is 670, well below seawater, and water would leave. Elasmobranchs still regulate ions: a **rectal gland** near the end of the gut secretes a concentrated sodium chloride solution.

## A bird with a second route for salt

Marine birds such as cormorants and gulls often feed at sea, where fresh water is scarce. Their kidneys cannot make urine as concentrated as seawater, so a bird clearing the salt of seawater through its kidneys alone would need more water than it drank. In 1958 Knut Schmidt-Nielsen, C. B. Jørgensen and H. Osaki tested whether another route existed. They gave double-crested cormorants a load of salt, orally or by infusion, and collected two fluids: the cloacal fluid (urine mixed with feces) and the clear fluid that dripped from the beak, made by the **nasal salt glands**, paired glands above the eyes.

If these birds handled salt the way mammals do, the cloacal fluid would carry the load. Instead, after the load, the nasal glands secreted a fluid of about 500 to 600 mmol per L of sodium chloride, similar to or somewhat above seawater (whose sodium is about 470 mmol per L), and the glands made it fast enough that continuous secretion could clear all the sodium chloride in the body in about 10 hours. In a later experiment on a gull, which Schmidt-Nielsen described in 1960, the salt gland removed about ten times as much salt as the cloaca. A gland that excretes salt more concentrated than seawater, as these glands can (gulls reach higher values still), lets a bird get rid of a gulp of seawater's salt in less water than the gulp held, and keep the difference.

## Ammonia, urea, uric acid

Breaking down protein strips the amino groups from amino acids, producing **ammonia**. Making it needs no ATP, and it is very soluble and diffuses fast, but it is toxic at low concentrations, so getting rid of it takes plenty of water. Most aquatic animals, including most bony fish, excrete mostly ammonia, much of it across the gills.

**Urea** is made from ammonia and carbon dioxide, in mammals in the liver, by a cycle of reactions that costs ATP ([protein turnover](/lessons/protein-turnover-amino-acid-catabolism/) covers the chemistry). It is much less toxic, so it can be held at higher concentration and excreted in less water. Urea is the main waste of mammals, many adult amphibians (fully aquatic ones often excrete more ammonia) and elasmobranchs. **Uric acid** is poorly soluble, so it can leave as a paste or solid with very little water. It is usually described as costlier still to make. It is the main waste of birds, many reptiles and many land insects. Humans also make some uric acid, from the breakdown of nucleotides ([nucleotide metabolism](/lessons/nucleotide-metabolism/)).

Development matters as much as habitat. A tadpole living in water excretes mostly ammonia; in frogs such as the bullfrog, urea production rises at metamorphosis. A bird's shelled egg cannot flush its wastes, and large amounts of soluble ammonia or urea would build up in its fluids. A bird embryo makes some ammonia and urea, but it turns more and more of its waste nitrogen into uric acid as it develops. Uric acid comes out of solution and collects in the allantois, the sac that holds the embryo's wastes. Not every shelled egg does this; snapping-turtle embryos, for example, accumulate mostly urea. Bradfield and Baggott (1993) reported that Japanese quail eggs that lost more water during incubation held more urate late in incubation, with little change in urea or ammonia.

So habitat gives tendencies, not fixed labels. Many teleosts excrete some urea, and African lungfish make more urea when they lie dormant out of water, though in one study fish dormant in mud mostly slowed their nitrogen production instead, and Patricia Wright's 1995 review treats water supply as a major factor in which waste an animal makes. No waste is a step up from another; each trades toxicity, water and energy.

## Four ways to make urine

Excretory organs use three steps in different mixes. **Filtration** pushes or pulls body fluid through a barrier that holds back large molecules and cells; the fluid that passes is the **filtrate**. **Reabsorption** returns useful substances to the body. **Secretion** moves substances from the body into the tubule.

**Protonephridia** in planarians are branched tubules ending in **flame cells**, whose beating cilia drive fluid through a fine filter into the tubule, where it is then modified. In Schmidtea mediterranea, Vu, Rink and colleagues (2015) showed that the filter sorts tracers by size and needs proteins related to those of the vertebrate kidney filter.

**Metanephridia** in earthworms work differently. Fluid filtered into the body cavity, the coelom, enters a funnel called the nephrostome, and the tubule modifies it before it leaves through a pore. Most segments carry a pair.

**Malpighian tubules** in insects do not filter at all. These blind-ended tubules open into the gut, and their cells secrete ions, often mostly potassium salts, into the tubule; water follows by osmosis. Wastes such as uric acid are also moved into the tubule. The rectum then reabsorbs salts and water. In fruit flies, Cabrero and colleagues (2020) found that most of the water likely crosses through one cell type, the stellate cells. How epithelia move ions is in [epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/).

**Vertebrate kidneys** usually filter by blood pressure in the **glomerulus**, a knot of capillaries, then reabsorb most of the filtrate and secrete some substances into it. A few marine bony fish have no glomeruli and make urine by tubular secretion. The nephron's segments are in [kidney](/lessons/kidney/).

### Design problem

The acoel Isodiametra pulchra, a small marine worm, has no excretory organ at all. Is its ammonia excretion purely passive diffusion, or does an active, energy-using step contribute? Design an experiment.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Andrikou, Thiel, Ruiz-Santiesteban and Hejnol (2019) did much of this. Load the worms by holding them in seawater with added ammonium chloride, then move them to clean seawater and measure ammonia appearing over time with an ammonia-sensitive electrode. In their study, 200 and 500 micromolar loads raised later excretion while 50 and 100 did not.

Next, add an inhibitor of the ATP-driven sodium-potassium pump or of microtubules, which support vesicle transport, and compare with worms given the solvent alone. Check that treated worms are alive, since a dying worm also excretes less. Both treatments reduced acoel excretion.
