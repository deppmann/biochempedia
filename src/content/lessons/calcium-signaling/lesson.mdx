  - name: "Sydney Ringer"
    dates: "1835–1910"
    contribution: >-
      Found that an isolated frog heart keeps beating only if its saline contains calcium, which
      made calcium a requirement for contraction long before anyone called it a signal.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Ringer, 'A further contribution regarding the influence of the different constituents of the blood on the contraction of the heart', J. Physiol. 4:29–42 (1883)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In 1883 Sydney Ringer, at University College London, was working through a long
      series of experiments on isolated frog hearts, asking which constituents of blood a heart
      needs to keep contracting. When he repeated the work after his first paper appeared, the hearts began to
      weaken and stop within about twenty minutes, although the recipe had not changed. He traced
      the difference to the water. The batches that worked had been made up with London pipe
      water from the New River Water Company, which was hard, and the failing batches with
      distilled water. The missing constituent was calcium. The purer water was the worse one,
      and the cause lay in the one part of his method he had never had reason to
      question. For decades the finding was used mainly as a recipe: calcium was known as the material of
      bone and as something blood needs to clot, and few physiologists thought of an ion
      as information. The recipe lasted. A version of Ringer's solution is still given to
      patients, and his result set the question that later workers answered: what is the calcium
      for?

    story: >-
      In the early 1950s Setsuro Ebashi was in the pharmacology department of the University of

      published with Hiroshi Kumagai and F. Takeda on a relaxing factor in muscle. By late 1961 Wilhelm Hasselbach and Madoka Makinose in Heidelberg had
      shown that vesicles of sarcoplasmic reticulum, given ATP, pull calcium out of the

  - name: "Robert Kretsinger"
    contribution: >-
      With Nockolds, solved the crystal structure of carp parvalbumin, described in full in 1973, and named the
      helix-loop-helix calcium site in it the EF hand.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Kretsinger & Nockolds, 'Carp muscle calcium-binding protein. II. Structure determination and general description', J. Biol. Chem. 248:3313–3326 (1973)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In 1973 Robert Kretsinger, at the University of Virginia, published with C. E. Nockolds
      the crystal structure of parvalbumin, a small calcium-binding protein from carp muscle.
      The chain folds into six helices, which they lettered A to F. Calcium sat in two places,
      and both had the same build: a helix, a loop that wraps the ion in oxygen atoms, and a
      second helix. For the site formed by helices E and F, Kretsinger offered a picture that
      stuck. Hold out your right hand with the forefinger pointing and the thumb raised. The
      forefinger is helix E, the thumb is helix F, and the curled middle finger is the loop that
      holds the calcium. He called the unit the EF hand. Because the loop has a recognisable
      pattern of residues, the motif could then be looked for in proteins whose structures were
      unknown, and it was found in troponin C, calmodulin, the myosin light chains and many
      others. One structure of a fish muscle protein gave biologists a way to read "this protein
      probably binds calcium" from an amino acid sequence, though a few EF hands have lost the ability.

    story: >-
      In 1986 James Putney proposed that an emptied calcium store is itself the signal for
      calcium to enter from outside. The idea gained a measurable current in 1992, called
      CRAC (calcium release-activated calcium), but for thirteen more years none of its proteins was known. The store sensor, STIM1, was found in 2005, and the pore a year later. Stefan Feske, working in Boston
      with Anjana Rao, had T cells, a class of immune cell, from a family with an

      stimulated, calcium did not come in through store-operated channels. The group searched for the gene by two independent
      routes. One was linkage analysis in the family with single-nucleotide polymorphism
      arrays. The other was a screen in fruit-fly cells that knocked genes down one at a time.
      Both landed on one gene, which the group named Orai1. The patients carried a single
      missense mutation in both copies. A working copy put back into their T cells restored
      calcium entry and the CRAC current. Orai1 crosses the membrane four times and belongs to no known channel family, so
      a search by resemblance would not have found it. With the gene named, store-operated entry

techniques:
  - name: "Aequorin luminescence"
    blurb: >-
      Aequorin is a jellyfish protein that gives off blue light when it binds calcium. Injected
      into a single cell, it reports a calcium rise as a flash that a light detector can count.
      It needs no excitation light, which keeps the background dark.
  - name: "Ratiometric calcium dyes (fura-2)"
    blurb: >-
      Fura-2 is a small fluorescent molecule that binds calcium, and binding shifts its
      excitation spectrum toward shorter wavelengths. The dye is excited in turn at 340 nm and
      at 380 nm. Dividing the two emission signals largely cancels out how much dye the cell holds, so
      the ratio can be calibrated to a calcium concentration.
  - name: "FRET protein sensors (cameleons)"
    blurb: >-
      A cameleon is one polypeptide: a cyan fluorescent protein, calmodulin, a calmodulin-binding
      peptide and a yellow fluorescent protein. Calcium makes calmodulin wrap around the peptide,
      which brings the two fluorescent proteins together and raises energy transfer from cyan to
      yellow. Because the cell makes the sensor from a gene, it can be sent to one cell type or
      one compartment.
  - name: "Calcium uptake by membrane vesicles"
    blurb: >-
      Break open muscle, collect the sealed vesicles that form from sarcoplasmic reticulum, add
      ATP, and measure how much calcium disappears from the surrounding fluid. Comparing calcium
      taken up with ATP split gives the stoichiometry of the pump, about two calcium ions per
      ATP.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "Why can a cell not end a calcium signal the way it ends a cAMP signal?"
    choices:
      - "Calcium binds its targets too tightly to come off again"
      - "Calcium channels stay open once a signal has begun"
      - "Calcium is made faster than any enzyme could degrade it"
      - "Calcium is an element, so no enzyme can break it down"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      cAMP is a molecule, and a phosphodiesterase destroys it. Calcium is an element: nothing
      can break it down, so the signal ends only when pumps, exchangers and buffers take the ion
      out of the free cytosolic pool. Calcium does not bind its targets too tightly to come off; sensors
      such as calmodulin let go once the concentration falls. Channels do not stay open; they
      close, and then the pumps win. And the cell never makes calcium at all, so the choice
      about how fast it is made starts from a false premise.
  - stem: "Which of these belongs to the 'off' machinery of a calcium signal?"
    choices:
      - "The ryanodine receptor in the membrane of the muscle store"
      - "The Orai1 channel in the plasma membrane of a T cell"
      - "The IP3 receptor in the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum"
      - "The SERCA pump in the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The SERCA pump spends ATP to move calcium out of the cytosol and into the store, which
      ends a signal. The IP3 receptor and the ryanodine receptor are channels that release
      stored calcium into the cytosol, so both are 'on' mechanisms. The Orai1 channel lets
      calcium in from outside the cell when the store is empty, so it is also an 'on' mechanism,
      even though its job is to help refill the store.
  - stem: "What does binding calcium do to calmodulin that lets it switch on a target enzyme?"
    choices:
      - "It attaches a phosphate group that the target enzyme recognises"
      - "It exposes hydrophobic patches that grip a helix on the target"
      - "It cuts the linker so that the two lobes can act separately"
      - "It replaces bound GDP with GTP and so changes the lobes' shape"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Calcium binding opens each lobe of calmodulin and uncovers a hydrophobic patch, and the
      two lobes then close around a helix on the target. No phosphate is attached: calmodulin is
      not a kinase and is not itself switched by phosphorylation here. The linker is not cut; it
      stays intact and simply bends. Calmodulin binds no guanine nucleotide; a GDP-for-GTP swap
      is how a G protein switches, not how a calcium sensor does.
  - stem: "Cells sit in a bath with no calcium while a drug that blocks the SERCA pump empties their stores. Calcium is then added back to the bath, and in normal cells cytosolic calcium rises sharply. What do you expect on add-back in cells that lack STIM1?"
    choices:
      - "The same rise, because Orai1 opens whenever stores empty"
      - "A delayed rise, because IP3 receptors must first reopen"
      - "Almost no rise, because the main store sensor is missing"
      - "A larger rise, because the stores stay empty for longer"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The rise on add-back is calcium entering through store-operated channels, and those open
      mainly when STIM1 reports that the store is empty. Without STIM1, most cells show only a small residual
      entry. A larger rise would need the channels to open more, and an empty store cannot open
      them without STIM1. The same rise assumes Orai1 detects the store by itself, but Orai1 is
      in the plasma membrane and cannot see inside the store. A delayed rise through IP3
      receptors confuses release from the store with entry from outside: IP3 receptors sit on
      the store, which is already empty.
  - stem: "An enzyme that needs about 10 micromolar calcium to work is active when it is anchored beside a calcium channel. The same enzyme left free in the cytosol stays silent, although the whole-cell calcium signal peaks at 1 micromolar in both cases. What best explains the difference?"
    choices:
      - "The anchored enzyme is phosphorylated by the channel next to it"
      - "The free enzyme is pumped into the store along with the calcium"
      - "The free enzyme never meets the high calcium near the channel"
      - "The anchored enzyme binds calcium more tightly than the free one"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Buffers keep calcium from spreading, so the concentration right beside an open channel is
      far above the whole-cell average. The anchored enzyme sits in that local signal and the
      free enzyme does not. A channel is not a kinase, so it does not phosphorylate its
      neighbour. Pumps move calcium ions, not enzymes, into the store. And anchoring changes
      where the enzyme is, not how tightly it binds calcium: it is the same protein in both
      cases.

      The buffer lost 50 minus 10, or 40 micromolar calcium. In 1.0 mL that is 40 nanomoles.

**In 1883 a frog heart kept beating in salt water mixed with London tap water, and stopped within about twenty minutes when the same recipe was mixed with distilled water.** Sydney Ringer traced the difference to calcium in the hard pipe water. Sixty-four years later Lewis Heilbrunn and Floyd Wiercinski injected a calcium salt into a frog muscle fibre. With no nerve impulse at all, the fibre shortened: calcium alone can trigger contraction. How can the same ion control other responses without switching on the whole cell at once?

<Support>**The one idea.** Cells give calcium meaning by controlling where and when free calcium rises. **Scope:** free cytosolic calcium as an intracellular messenger, not calcium's extracellular or structural roles. Different cells use different sources, sensors, spatial scales and time courses, and the size of a rise matters too; this lesson and its films concentrate on place and timing.</Support>

## Build the rise: gradient, channels and removal

As the first film showed, calcium is an element that no enzyme can make or destroy, so it signals only by being moved. **Free cytosolic calcium** means calcium ions dissolved in the cytosol and not bound to proteins or other molecules. A resting cell holds it near 100 nanomolar; outside, calcium is 1 to 2 millimolar, ten to twenty thousand times higher. In the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), the cell's folded internal calcium store, free calcium is several hundred micromolar; muscle's version is the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

A signal may first make another messenger, such as IP3 from the previous lesson, which opens its receptor channel on the ER. Ions begin crossing within milliseconds of a channel opening. Because the resting level is so low, a modest number of ions raises bulk cytosolic calcium about tenfold, to roughly 1 micromolar, and much higher right beside the pore.

The proof that a quiet cytosol is paid for came from muscle. By late 1961 Wilhelm Hasselbach and Madoka Makinose, in Heidelberg, had isolated sarcoplasmic-reticulum vesicles, added ATP and watched calcium leave the surrounding fluid. Setsuro Ebashi and Fritz Lipmann, in New York, independently reported ATP-driven uptake in 1962. Within two years the Heidelberg measurements put the cost at about two calcium ions per ATP split: relaxed muscle spends ATP to stay relaxed. Back in Tokyo, Ebashi and Ayako Kodama identified the separate protein through which calcium acts on the filaments of skeletal muscle in 1965, and named it troponin in 1966.

### An empty store asks for a refill

A store that never refills supports only a limited release. James Putney, reassessing earlier findings on calcium stores, proposed in 1986 that depletion is itself an instruction to open plasma-membrane channels: **store-operated entry**. STIM1 crosses the ER membrane once, with a calcium-binding site facing the lumen. When luminal calcium falls, STIM1 clusters beneath the plasma membrane and opens Orai1. Orai1 admits calcium to the cytosol; SERCA can then pump some into the ER.

**Predict 2.** A drug empties the ER while no calcium is outside. Calcium is then restored outside. What happens in a cell lacking STIM1?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The sharp store-operated rise should be largely missing. Orai1 cannot sense ER calcium by itself; without STIM1, the main store sensor, an empty store cannot efficiently open that route.

</details>

The parts took twenty years to name. Two groups, including laboratories led by Michael Cahalan and Tobias Meyer, independently reported STIM1 as the sensor in 2005. Stefan Feske and colleagues identified Orai1 in 2006 through a family whose immune cells lacked store-operated calcium entry.

## How a protein reads the rise

Calcium offers a double positive charge and a preference for oxygen atoms. A protein can therefore make an oxygen-lined pocket that is mostly empty at 100 nanomolar and fills around a micromolar: the Kd idea from the lesson on binding and dose-response.

A common calcium-binding motif is the **EF hand**. In the parvalbumin structure published by Robert Kretsinger and C. E. Nockolds in 1973, calcium sits in a twelve-residue loop between helices E and F. Aspartate and glutamate side chains supply oxygens around it. The same helix-loop-helix occurs in a large protein family, including troponin C and the myosin light chains, although a few EF hands have lost calcium binding.

**Predict 3.** Two proteins bind the same amount of calcium. Only one exposes a target-binding surface. Which is a sensor, and which could be only a buffer?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The one that exposes a surface is a sensor: binding changes what it can do. The other may still matter as a buffer, but binding alone sends no instruction.

</details>

Parvalbumin binds calcium and acts as a buffer. **Calmodulin** binds calcium and changes shape. It has 148 amino acids and four EF hands, two in each lobe, joined by a flexible linker. Calcium exposes a hydrophobic, water-avoiding patch on each lobe. With many targets, the lobes close around a target helix; other targets use one lobe or separate sites.

Calmodulin has no catalytic activity, so one molecule can serve many targets: each target carries its own calmodulin-binding site. In CaM kinase II (CaMKII), a segment blocks the active site at rest. Calcium-loaded calmodulin grips that segment and pulls it clear, letting the kinase phosphorylate substrates. A rise in an ion has become a phosphate on a protein, read as in the lesson on the phosphorylation switch.

## Where and how often: the evidence and its limits

In heart muscle, a local release through ryanodine receptors is a **spark**. Each electrical impulse sets off thousands of sparks at once, and together they make the whole-cell rise behind a beat. In eggs and liver cells, release travels instead: calcium from one cluster stimulates the release channels of the next, by positive feedback, and a wave crosses the cell. Not every global rise, however, is built only from sequential local events.

**Predict 4.** A fast calcium buffer is added, and the whole-cell calcium peak falls sharply. Should a sensor anchored beside an open channel lose its response just as sharply?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Probably not. It sits where calcium is highest, before the buffer can bind much of it; the whole-cell average reflects calcium that has spread, so a distant sensor loses more. The result depends on the buffer's concentration and binding kinetics.

</details>

In 1986 Woods, Cuthbertson and Cobbold used a light-emitting reporter in single rat liver cells. More hormone produced more frequent calcium spikes: the dose was written in frequency, not in a higher calcium level.

To respond to frequency, a reader must keep some activity from one spike to the next. Mitochondrial enzymes respond to spike rate in liver cells, and transcription factors such as NFAT respond best to frequent spikes in immune cells. For faster neuronal pulses, CaMKII is one counter. In its hub of twelve or fourteen subunits, when two neighbours hold calmodulin together, one can phosphorylate the other, and activity can persist after calcium falls. In 1998 De Koninck and Schulman showed with purified enzyme that lasting activity depends on pulse frequency. Which molecules decode slower cellular oscillations, and how much they read frequency rather than summed calcium, remain open questions.

## Seeing calcium without confusing the signal

None of this could be studied until calcium could be watched in a living cell. Aequorin, purified from the jellyfish *Aequorea* by Osamu Shimomura with Frank Johnson and Saiga and described in 1962, emits blue light when it binds calcium. Injected into one cell, as in the liver cells above, it reports rises as flashes. Fura-2, from Roger Tsien's laboratory in 1985, shifts its best excitation wavelength on binding calcium. The ratio at two wavelengths depends little on dye loading and can be calibrated to concentration.

### Design problem

How could calmodulin's shape change make a calcium sensor? Design a protein that reports calcium by light. State the control that shows the response depends on calcium.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Build one chain: cyan fluorescent protein, calmodulin, a calmodulin-binding peptide and yellow fluorescent protein. At low calcium the fluorescent proteins are apart. When calcium rises, calmodulin grips its peptide and brings the ends within a few nanometres. Energy can then pass from excited cyan protein to yellow protein by FRET, raising the yellow-to-cyan ratio. Miyawaki, Tsien and colleagues published this “cameleon” design in 1997.

Make a calcium-blind twin by changing the glutamate at the end of each EF-hand loop to glutamine, so calmodulin can no longer hold calcium at cellular concentrations. The ratio could also move with pH or bleaching; any change the twin shows is not calcium. The twin shows that the response requires calcium binding, but not where the calcium came from. Calibrate the working sensor inside cells: an ionophore, which carries calcium across membranes, gives the maximum ratio in high calcium, and a chelator, which grips calcium tightly, gives the minimum. The reporter is itself a calcium buffer, so use as little as can be read.

</details>

## Going deeper

Kretsinger and Nockolds lettered parvalbumin's six helices A to F, hence the name. Kretsinger's right-hand analogy (forefinger as helix E, thumb as F, curled middle finger as the loop) captures the topology, not a molecular structure.
