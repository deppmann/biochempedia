      a loss of one transmitter in the striatum.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Ehringer & Hornykiewicz, Klin. Wochenschr. 38:1236–1239 (1960); Hornykiewicz, 'Dopamine miracle: from brain homogenate to dopamine replacement', Mov. Disord. 17:501–508 (2002)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the late 1950s animal work had shown that dopamine was present in the brain and was
      concentrated in the striatum, and that drugs which depleted it left animals slow and still.
      The open question was whether anything like this happened in human disease. Herbert
      Ehringer and Oleh Hornykiewicz, working in pharmacology in Vienna, answered it the direct
      way: they measured dopamine in brain tissue taken at autopsy. In the striatum of people who had had Parkinson's disease, dopamine was greatly reduced compared
      with brains from people without neurological disease. They published the result in Klinische
      Wochenschrift in 1960. It turned a clinical syndrome into a chemical lesion. Later work traced the
      lost dopamine to the projection from the substantia nigra to the striatum. Soon afterwards Hornykiewicz and
      the neurologist Walther Birkmayer gave L-DOPA, the precursor of dopamine, to patients and
      reported short-lived improvement, and later work established L-DOPA as a treatment. The
      measurement made dopamine replacement a testable idea, and it made Parkinson's disease the
      model case for linking a transmitter to a movement disorder.

    quotes: []

      endings of their stores of monoamines. Animals given it became inactive. Carlsson's question
      was which missing amine caused the stillness. With Margit Lindqvist and Tor Magnusson he
      gave reserpine-treated animals DOPA, the precursor of dopamine and noradrenaline, and the

      of movement. Carlsson shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Paul
      Greengard and Eric Kandel. His animal work and the Vienna post-mortem measurements together
      set up the dopamine account of Parkinson's disease.

    contribution: >-
      With Anatol Kreitzer and colleagues, drove direct- or indirect-pathway striatal neurons with

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The two-pathway model was built from anatomy, gene expression and lesions, and driving one
      pathway alone in a living animal was hard, because the two kinds of striatal neuron are
      mixed together. Cre-driver mice changed that. Kravitz and colleagues in
      Anatol Kreitzer's laboratory used mice that express Cre recombinase under the D1 or the D2
      receptor promoter and injected a virus that makes channelrhodopsin-2 only in Cre-expressing
      cells, so blue light in the dorsomedial striatum excited one pathway. Exciting the indirect
      pathway on both sides increased freezing and reduced the starting of locomotion. Exciting
      the direct pathway reduced freezing and increased locomotion. In mice whose striatal dopamine
      had been depleted with 6-hydroxydopamine, direct-pathway stimulation rescued the motor
      deficits they measured. They published this in Nature in 2010. The work showed that each
      pathway is sufficient, when driven, to push movement in the direction the model predicts,
      and it opened pathway-specific tests of the model in behaving animals.

techniques:
  - name: "The neurological examination as lesion localization"
    blurb: >-
      Strength, tone, stretch reflexes, the plantar response, wasting and fasciculations sort a
      weakness into upper or lower motor neuron patterns. Combined with which side and which body
      parts are affected, and with sensory findings, they place a lesion along a pathway whose
      crossings you know.
  - name: "Single-unit recording in awake primates"
    blurb: >-
      A fine extracellular electrode records the spikes of one neuron while a monkey moves or
      rests. Comparing firing rates and patterns before and after MPTP treatment tested the rate
      model's predictions for the subthalamic nucleus and internal pallidum.
  - name: "Post-mortem neurochemistry and immunohistochemistry"
    blurb: >-
      Measuring a transmitter in autopsy tissue, or staining terminal fields for peptides that
      mark one projection, shows what is lost in a human disease. It measures the end state of
      tissue, not living firing, and cannot by itself separate cell death from reduced marker.
  - name: "Cre-dependent optogenetics"
    blurb: >-
      A light-gated channel is expressed only in neurons of one genetic type, here D1- or
      D2-expressing striatal neurons, and light then drives them. This tests whether driving
      those cells is sufficient to change a behaviour; it does not show how they act normally.

  - stem: "At which level do most fibres of the human corticospinal tract cross the midline?"
    choices:
      - "In the pons, where the fibres enter the cerebellum"
      - "In the internal capsule, just below the cortex"
      - "At the pyramidal decussation in the caudal medulla"
      - "In the spinal cord at the segment where they end"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Most corticospinal fibres cross at the pyramidal decussation in the caudal medulla and then
      run in the lateral corticospinal tract. The internal capsule is above the crossing, so a
      lesion there gives contralateral weakness. Crossing at the segment of ending describes the
      minority of fibres in the anterior tract, not most fibres. The corticospinal fibres pass
      through the basis pontis without crossing; the pontine fibres that cross to the cerebellum
      are a different projection.
  - stem: "Six weeks after an injury, a patient's weak left leg shows raised tone, brisk knee jerk, clonus at the ankle and an upgoing great toe. What does this pattern indicate?"
    choices:
      - "Damage to descending upper motor neuron axons"
      - "Damage to the left femoral nerve in the thigh"
      - "Damage to the left dorsal root ganglia only"
      - "Damage to lumbar ventral horn motor neurons"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Raised tone, brisk reflexes, clonus and an extensor plantar response in an adult are upper
      motor neuron signs, so the descending axons are damaged. A femoral nerve lesion and loss of
      ventral horn motor neurons are lower motor neuron lesions, which lower tone and reduce
      reflexes. Loss of dorsal root ganglia removes sensory input and would weaken reflexes, not
      make them brisk.
  - stem: "In the classic rate model, what is the net sign of the direct pathway from cortex back to cortex (cortex, striatum, GPi, thalamus, cortex)?"
    choices:
      - "Negative, because GPi output is inhibitory"
      - "Positive, because the two inhibitory links cancel"
      - "Negative, because the striatum is inhibitory"
      - "Zero, because the excitation and inhibition balance"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The links are excitatory, inhibitory, inhibitory, excitatory, so the product is
      (+1)(−1)(−1)(+1) = +1: two inhibitions in series, an even number, give disinhibition. Calling it negative
      because the striatum is inhibitory, or because GPi is inhibitory, counts only one of the two
      inhibitory links. A zero sign confuses multiplying signs with adding them.
  - stem: "In the classic model, how does dopamine from the substantia nigra pars compacta act on the two populations of striatal projection neurons?"
    choices:
      - "It inhibits both D1 direct and D2 indirect neurons equally"
      - "It inhibits D1 direct neurons and excites D2 indirect neurons"
      - "It excites D1 direct neurons and inhibits D2 indirect neurons"
      - "It excites both D1 direct and D2 indirect neurons equally"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      In the model dopamine excites D1-expressing direct neurons and inhibits D2-expressing
      indirect neurons, and both effects push the circuit toward releasing the thalamus. Inhibiting
      D1 neurons while exciting D2 neurons swaps the receptor actions. Exciting both populations
      equally, or inhibiting both equally, would make the two routes pull in opposite directions,
      so the signs alone cannot say which way the net effect goes.
  - stem: "In early Huntington's disease, striatal neurons projecting to GPe are more affected than those projecting to GPi. Using the signed indirect pathway, what does the model predict?"
    choices:
      - "GPi output rises and movement is reduced"
      - "Thalamus becomes more inhibited by GPe"
      - "STN activity rises and GPi output rises too"
      - "GPi output falls and extra movements appear"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Losing the inhibitory striatal input to GPe lets GPe fire more. GPe inhibits STN, so STN
      fires less, and STN excites GPi, so GPi fires less. Less GPi inhibition releases the
      thalamus, which the model links to chorea. Rising GPi output with less movement is the
      Parkinson's prediction. A rise in STN activity ignores the extra GPe inhibition. GPe does not
      project to the thalamus in this model, so it cannot inhibit it directly.

  - stem: "Monkeys are made parkinsonian with MPTP. Before treatment the mean firing rate of a sample of GPi neurons is 60 spikes per second; after MPTP it is 90 spikes per second. Thalamic relay neurons in the same animals, recorded in a separate session, fall from 20 to 12 spikes per second. (These numbers are invented for the problem.) Which statement best fits these data?"
    choices:
      - "Thalamic firing fell by 8%, too little to bear on the model"
      - "GPi rose by 150%, so thalamic firing should have risen as well"
      - "GPi rose by 30%, consistent with less inhibition of thalamus"
      - "GPi rose by 50% and thalamus fell by 40%, as the model predicts"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      GPi went from 60 to 90 spikes per second, a rise of 30/60 = 50%, and thalamus from 20 to 12,
      a fall of 8/20 = 40%. More inhibitory output with less thalamic firing is the rate model's
      prediction for dopamine loss. A 30% rise reads the change of 30 spikes per second as a percentage, and more GPi
      output means more inhibition of thalamus, not less. A 150% figure divides the final rate by
      the starting rate, and GPi inhibits thalamus, so thalamic firing should fall. A fall of 8
      spikes per second is 40% of the starting rate, not 8%. The data remain a correlation; only a
      manipulation would show the GPi change causes the thalamic one.

**A stroke deep in the left hemisphere weakens the right face, arm and leg. A cut through the right half of the spinal cord in the neck weakens the right arm and leg. Lesions on opposite sides, weakness on the same side.**

Something about where each lesion sits decides which side of the body pays for it. The same kind of reasoning turns out to explain how a set of deep nuclei that never reach the spinal cord can still decide whether a movement goes ahead.

## Three puzzles

Start with what has to be explained. Three observations about movement look unrelated.

1. **Side.** Why do the two lesions above, on opposite sides, weaken the same limbs?
2. **Control without contact.** The **basal ganglia** are a group of nuclei (a **nucleus** is a cluster of neuron cell bodies with shared connections) deep in the brain. They do not project to the spinal cord, yet in the classic model they decide whether a movement goes ahead. How?
3. **Opposite diseases.** Losing the dopamine neurons of the substantia nigra makes movement slow and hard to start, as in Parkinson's disease. Losing a different set of neurons early in Huntington's disease is thought to contribute to extra, involuntary movements. Why do two losses in one circuit push movement in opposite directions?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** To predict what a lesion or a drug does to movement, follow the motor chain from the place it acts toward the muscle and count two things. Each crossing of the midline flips the side: an odd number of crossings puts the effect on the opposite side of the body. Each inhibitory link flips the sign: an even number of inhibitions passes a change on with its direction kept, and an odd number reverses it. The counts give the side and the **direction** of a change, not its size.

Each count has limits, and each is stated where the count is first used.

## Counting crossings: the corticospinal tract

Picture a front view of the brain and spinal cord. Draw one line from the motor cortex of the left hemisphere down to the right hand, with one X low in the brainstem where the line crosses the midline. Now attach names to the picture.

- The line starts at an **upper motor neuron**: a neuron in the cortex or brainstem whose axon descends to control lower motor neurons, directly or through interneurons.
- It ends at a **lower motor neuron**: a motor neuron in the brainstem or the spinal cord's ventral horn whose axon leaves the central nervous system and ends on muscle. You met it in [motor units and reflexes](/lessons/motor-units-reflexes/).
- The descending axons travel together as a **tract**, a bundle of axons inside the central nervous system.
- The X is a **decussation**, a place where a tract crosses the midline.
- **Ipsilateral** means on the same side; **contralateral** means on the opposite side.

The corticospinal tract starts in layer V of primary motor cortex, with contributions from premotor, supplementary motor and parietal areas. On the way down its axons pass five stops:

1. the corona radiata;
2. the posterior limb of the internal capsule;
3. the cerebral peduncle of the midbrain;
4. the base of the pons;
5. the medullary pyramids.

In the caudal medulla most of the fibres cross at the **pyramidal decussation**. That is the X in the picture. The crossed fibres run in the lateral column of the cord as the **lateral corticospinal tract** and end mainly on interneurons of the intermediate grey matter and on ventral horn motor neurons.

The X splits the pathway in two. Damage above it, with one crossing still to come, shows up on the opposite side of the body. Damage in the cord, below it, shows up on the same side.

### Which neuron is damaged: the signs

The side tells you where along the tract to look. The signs tell you whether the damage is in the descending pathway or in the final motor neuron.

- A **lower motor neuron** lesion causes weakness with low tone and reduced or absent reflexes, because the motor limb of the reflex arc is cut. Later the muscle wastes and may show fasciculations, the visible twitches of single motor units firing on their own.
- An established **upper motor neuron** lesion causes weakness with raised tone (more resistance when the limb is moved passively), brisk stretch reflexes, clonus (rhythmic beats of contraction set off by a sudden stretch) and an extensor plantar response, in which stroking the sole makes the great toe extend. Independent finger movements are often lost.

"Established" matters. Right after a spinal cord injury the limbs below can be flaccid and areflexic (spinal shock), and spasticity appears later. And an upgoing toe is normal in infants, so in a young child it has to be read against age.

The face adds one more clue. Corticobulbar fibres to the motor neurons for the lower face come mainly from the opposite cortex, while the forehead receives input from both sides. An upper motor neuron lesion above the brainstem therefore weakens the contralateral lower face more than the forehead, which is relatively spared. This is the side count's limit: the forehead, fed from both sides, is weakened less rather than flipped.

### Two cases

Assume one focal lesion, examined weeks after onset so the upper motor neuron signs have developed.

**Worked problem.** *Case 1.* Weakness of the right lower face, right arm and right leg, with raised tone, brisk reflexes and an extensor plantar response on the right.

- Step 1: upper motor neuron signs put the lesion in the descending pathway, not the motor neurons or nerves.
- Step 2: the lower face is involved. Fibres for voluntary face movement end on facial motor neurons in the pons, so the lesion is above the facial nucleus: in the hemisphere, or occasionally in the pons above that level.
- Step 3: above the decussation the deficit is contralateral, so right-sided weakness means a left-sided lesion.
- Answer: most likely the left hemisphere, for example the posterior limb of the left internal capsule, where descending fibres for the face, arm and leg are packed into a small space.

For sensation, run the side count the other way: count the crossings between the body and the lesion. The dorsal column pathway for touch and vibration crosses in the medulla; the spinothalamic pathway for pain and temperature crosses within a segment or two of entering the cord ([touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/)).

**Predict.** *Case 2.* The same right arm and leg signs, but the face is normal. Vibration sense is lost on the right, and pain and temperature are lost on the left below about the C6 dermatome. Where is the lesion, and on which side?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

- Step 1: the face is spared, which fits a lesion below the facial nucleus, probably in the cord; the sensory findings in step 3 test this.
- Step 2: in the cord the corticospinal fibres have already crossed, so no crossing lies between the lesion and the muscle and the weakness is ipsilateral: a right-sided lesion.
- Step 3: check with sensation. The dorsal columns have not yet crossed in the cord, so right vibration loss points to the right cord. The spinothalamic fibres have already crossed, so right cord damage removes pain and temperature on the left, starting a little below the lesion.
- Answer: a right hemisection of the cord near C5, with lower motor neuron signs expected at the level itself, where ventral horn cells are destroyed.

</details>

## Counting inhibitions: the basal ganglia brake

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The corticospinal tract carries the command. In the classic **rate model**, which treats each nucleus as one firing rate that rises or falls, the basal ganglia decide whether that command goes ahead. Start with a picture and add the names after.

Picture one hemisphere's circuit as a column of boxes. At the top is the cortex. Below it is an **input stage**, and near the bottom an **output stage**, which feeds the thalamus. The thalamus sends an arrow back up to the cortex, closing a loop. Between input and output run two routes: a short one straight down, and a long one that detours through two more boxes. Off to one side sits one more box that sprays dopamine onto the input stage. Every arrow is either excitation or inhibition.

Now the names.

- The input stage is the **striatum** (caudate and putamen).
- The output stage is the internal segment of the globus pallidus, **GPi**, with a partner that works much the same way, the substantia nigra pars reticulata (**SNr**).
- The detour runs through the external segment of the globus pallidus, **GPe**, and the **subthalamic nucleus** (STN).
- The dopamine comes from the substantia nigra pars compacta, **SNc**.

Besides the thalamus, the output also reaches brainstem targets.

### The brake

The output neurons of GPi and SNr are GABAergic, releasing the inhibitory transmitter GABA, and fire tonically, that is, steadily, even at rest. So the motor thalamus is held under steady inhibition, like a foot resting on a brake. In the classic model, movement needs the brake lifted: GPi must fire less.

How can a circuit made largely of inhibitory neurons lift anything? Try the smallest case.

**Predict.** Neuron A inhibits neuron B, and B inhibits neuron C. B is firing, and C has steady excitatory drive of its own. A starts firing faster. What does C do?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A fires more, so B is inhibited more and fires less. B fires less, so C gets less inhibition and fires more. Inhibiting an inhibitor releases its target. This is **disinhibition**: two inhibitory links in a row pass the change on with its direction kept, as long as each cell can still change its firing (a B that is already silent cannot slow further). Removing inhibition only permits firing; C's own drive does the exciting.

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/basal-ganglia/gpi-brake.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two panels with a GPi output neuron inhibiting a motor thalamus neuron that projects to cortex. With the brake on, GPi fires steadily and the thalamus is silent; with the brake lifted, GPi fires less and the thalamus fires and drives cortex." caption="In the classic model, GPi fires steadily and holds the motor thalamus down; when GPi fires less, the thalamus is released and movement goes ahead." />

### Two routes to the brake

Striatal projection neurons are themselves GABAergic, so every arrow leaving the striatum is an inhibition.

- In the **direct pathway**, striatal neurons that express D1 dopamine receptors inhibit GPi and SNr directly.
- In the **indirect pathway**, striatal neurons that express D2 receptors inhibit GPe; GPe, also GABAergic, inhibits STN; and STN, which is glutamatergic (it releases the excitatory transmitter glutamate), excites GPi.

Follow more striatal firing down each route and count the inhibitions from the striatum to the thalamus. A released thalamus means a push toward movement; a thalamus held down harder means a push away.

| Route | Chain from striatum to thalamus | Inhibitory links | More striatal firing means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Direct | striatum ⊣ GPi ⊣ thalamus | 2, even | thalamus released: toward movement |
| Indirect | striatum ⊣ GPe ⊣ STN → GPi ⊣ thalamus | 3, odd | thalamus held down harder: away from movement |

Read the indirect row one step at a time. D2 neurons fire more, so GPe fires less. STN, freed from GPe, fires more. GPi, excited by STN, fires more. The thalamus is held down harder. The links into the striatum from cortex and out of the thalamus to cortex are excitatory, so they add no flips.

### Dopamine is one more link

In the classic model, SNc dopamine acts on the two kinds of striatal neuron in opposite ways: it **excites** D1 direct-pathway neurons and **inhibits** D2 indirect-pathway neurons. Dopamine is not a box in the loop; it acts on the first box of each route. So treat each action as one more link at the head of its route, and count again.

**Predict.** Acting through each receptor, does dopamine push toward movement or away from it? What should losing dopamine do?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Through D1: an excitatory link, then the direct route's two inhibitions. Two flips, an even number: more dopamine, more movement.

Through D2: dopamine's inhibitory action is one flip, then the indirect route's three. Four flips, an even number: more dopamine, more movement again.

Both actions agree, so the count can call the net effect. Losing dopamine pushes both routes toward less movement. The direct pathway weakens and the indirect pathway strengthens: GPe is more inhibited and STN is released. So GPi fires more for two reasons at once: less inhibition from the striatum, and more excitation from STN. The thalamus is held down harder and movement becomes hard to start, which is called **akinesia**. That is the classic model's prediction for dopamine loss.

</details>

**What the sign count can and cannot call.** It assumes each link has one fixed sign, and it counts a lesion as a fall in activity. When several routes carry a change and **agree** in sign, the count predicts the net effect. When they **disagree**, it says only that they pull against each other; which one wins depends on how strong each route is, and signs cannot tell you that. "Where the box model breaks" returns to this.

How D1 and D2 receptors couple to cAMP belongs to [G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/) and [cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/); where dopamine is released and cleared is in [transmitter systems](/lessons/transmitter-systems/).

This counting is the logic of the rate model set out by Albin, Young and Penney in 1989 and by DeLong in 1990. It is a way to reason about the direction of a change, not a measured law of how firing rates add.

Use the simulation to watch the count: switch dopamine, the indirect-pathway neurons and the STN on and off, predict each box first, then check.

## Both counts together: silencing the STN

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

The dopamine count says that after dopamine loss the STN is overactive and drives GPi harder. If so, silencing the STN should help. This is where both counts work at once: crossings set the side, inhibitions set the sign. For the side, follow the loop. In the classic model each hemisphere's basal ganglia loop returns to the cortex on its own side, and that cortex's commands cross once, at the pyramidal decussation.

The test animal is a monkey made parkinsonian with **MPTP**, a toxin that preferentially kills SNc dopamine neurons (its discovery is in the anecdote "Parkinsonism from a contaminated drug").

**Predict.** In a monkey treated with MPTP, you lesion the left STN. Which way should movement change, and on which side of the body?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

*Sign.* STN excites GPi, and GPi inhibits the thalamus: one inhibition, an odd number. So STN activity and thalamic activity move in opposite directions. A lesion makes STN activity fall, so the thalamus is released, toward movement.

*Side.* Left STN, left GPi, left thalamus, left cortex, then one crossing on the way to the cord: the right side of the body.

*Prediction:* the parkinsonian signs should ease on the right side.

</details>

Hagai Bergman, Thomas Wichmann and Mahlon DeLong did this experiment in MPTP-treated monkeys and reported in Science in 1990 that the parkinsonian signs improved on the side of the body opposite the lesion. The rate model had made a prediction a lesion could test, and the prediction held. The result made the STN a surgical target (DeLong's profile has the award that followed).

The same count, with the same sign, gives a different outcome when dopamine is normal. A lesion in or near one STN, classically a small stroke in a person without Parkinson's disease, can release the opposite limbs too far. The result is hemiballism, large flinging movements of one arm or leg.

## Parkinson's and Huntington's through the circuit

**Parkinson's disease.** The motor signs (slowness, small movements, rigidity, rest tremor) appear after substantial loss of SNc dopamine neurons; published threshold figures vary. The evidence that tied the disease to dopamine came from post-mortem tissue: Ehringer and Hornykiewicz found striatal dopamine greatly reduced in brains from patients (1960). The dopamine count above gives the model's account of the slowness: GPi pressed harder from two directions, the thalamus held down.

Treatments follow the same logic. A dopamine precursor such as L-DOPA raises the dopamine available in the striatum. In mice with dopamine depleted by 6-hydroxydopamine, driving direct-pathway neurons with light rescued the motor deficits Kravitz and colleagues measured (2010). Why the dopamine neurons die is taken up in [neurodegeneration](/lessons/neurodegeneration/).

**Huntington's disease** has a different cause: an expanded CAG repeat in the huntingtin gene (the mutation type is in [mutation](/lessons/mutation/)), not a primary loss of dopamine.

**Predict.** Suppose the striatal neurons of the indirect pathway, the ones projecting to GPe, are affected first, so their output to GPe falls. Predict what GPe, STN and GPi do, and what happens to movement.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Losing that output removes the inhibition of GPe, so GPe is released and fires more. STN is more inhibited and fires less. GPi gets less excitation and fires less, and the thalamus is released. In the model this pushes toward extra movement. That is thought to contribute to chorea, the brief, irregular involuntary movements of early disease.

</details>

What supports the premise? Enkephalin marks the striatal projection to GPe and substance P the one to GPi, and in post-mortem tissue the enkephalin terminals in GPe are the more affected in early and middle stages (Reiner and colleagues, 1988; the anecdote "Seventeen brains and two peptides" has the study). Staining alone cannot tell neuron death from terminal loss or reduced peptide. Later loss of direct-pathway output is associated with the akinesia of late disease.

## Where the box model breaks

The rate model predicts the sign of several clinical effects. Four sets of results show its limits, and each meets a limit of the count stated with dopamine.

**First, routes that disagree are active together.** The cartoon has the pathways alternating on and off. Cui and colleagues (2013) found calcium signals from both D1-expressing (direct-pathway) and A2A-expressing (indirect-pathway) striatal populations in mice rising around the start of an action. Two routes of opposite sign active at once is exactly the case the count cannot call.

**Second, there are more routes.** In awake monkeys, Nambu and colleagues (2000) found that cortical stimulation evoked an early pallidal excitation that depended on STN and arrived faster than effects through the striatum: a **hyperdirect** pathway. Count it: cortex excites STN, STN excites GPi, GPi inhibits the thalamus. One inhibition, so it pushes away from movement, against the direct route. And in mice, direct-pathway neurons branch into GPe as well (Labouesse and colleagues, 2023). Counted with the simple signs, a branch through GPe and STN has three inhibitions and would push against the direct route. Yet inhibiting the direct-pathway terminals in GPe impaired the mice's motor performance, the opposite of what that count predicts. The authors traced part of the effect to inhibition of Npas1-expressing GPe neurons, one of several GPe cell types with different targets. So the count cannot assign this branch a sign; the result shows only that the branch does real work.

**Third, rate may not be the main variable.** The count follows mean firing rates. In mice, dopamine depletion disrupted the clustered pattern of movement-related indirect-pathway activity (Parker and colleagues, 2018), a change in pattern rather than only in mean rate. Studies of parkinsonian animals and patients also report more bursting and synchronized oscillation in STN and GPi.

**Fourth, some symptoms and treatments do not fit.** Lesioning GPi relieves parkinsonism, as the model predicts. But the model also predicts that removing GPi output should cause extra movements, and pallidotomy usually does not cause them; it also eases dyskinesia and dystonia. The model gives no account of rest tremor, and deep brain stimulation of the STN relieves parkinsonian signs by a mechanism that is still unresolved. What the basal ganglia contribute to choosing actions by their value continues in [motivation and reward](/lessons/motivation-and-reward/).

### Design problem

You have seen the result. Now design the experiment as if it had not been done, with its controls. The rate model predicts that the STN is overactive in parkinsonism and that silencing it should help. Design a primate experiment to test this, and say what result would count against the model.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use monkeys made parkinsonian with MPTP. Record single units in STN and GPi before and after MPTP; the model predicts both rates rise. Then lesion or inactivate one STN and score akinesia and rigidity on the opposite side of the body, with the other side as a within-animal comparison. This is the lesion step Bergman, Wichmann and DeLong did.

Controls: sham surgery; histology for lesion position and SNc loss; scoring blind to side and condition; and GPi recording after the lesion to see whether its output fell.

Against the model: no motor improvement after an accurate STN lesion; or improvement with no fall in GPi activity, which would mean the benefit does not work by lowering GPi rate as the model says. Two cautions: pattern and synchrony change as well as rate, so record them too; and a lesion result does not explain deep brain stimulation.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How much of the tract crosses

Estimates of the fraction of corticospinal fibres that cross at the pyramidal decussation vary between sources, from roughly 75 to 90%. Most of the uncrossed minority forms the anterior corticospinal tract, whose fibres largely appear to cross segmentally, near the level where they end. How the fibres cross during development is in [crossing the midline](/lessons/crossing-the-midline/).

### Direct contacts on motor neurons

In macaques some cortical cells contact digit motor neurons directly (Rathelot and Strick, 2006).

### The count as a product of signs

The inhibition count can be written as arithmetic. Give each excitatory link +1 and each inhibitory link −1, and multiply along the route from cortex back to cortex. A positive product means activity entering the loop ends up releasing the thalamus and so favours movement; a negative product means it ends up holding the thalamus down.

> **Direct: cortex→striatum (+1) × striatum→GPi (−1) × GPi→thalamus (−1) × thalamus→cortex (+1) = +1**

> **Indirect: cortex→striatum (+1) × striatum→GPe (−1) × GPe→STN (−1) × STN→GPi (+1) × GPi→thalamus (−1) × thalamus→cortex (+1) = −1**

### What the SNr subgroups did

Freeze and colleagues (2013) found that stimulating either pathway produced both excitatory and inhibitory responses among SNr neurons in mice, so neither pathway simply pushed all of SNr one way. Even so, movement initiation during direct-pathway stimulation tracked the inhibited SNr subgroup, and movement suppression during indirect-pathway stimulation was most strongly associated with the excited subgroup. These pathway-specific relationships fit the model despite the mixed responses.

### Counting the branched cells

Of 148 direct-pathway neurons in the mouse dorsomedial striatum that carried the SNr tracer, 147 also carried the GPe tracer. That is 147/148 = 99.3% of the SNr-labelled cells counted, not of all striatal neurons. The anecdote "One cell, two targets" tells the story.
