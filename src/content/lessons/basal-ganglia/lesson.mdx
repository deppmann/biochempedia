techniques:
  - name: "The neurological examination as lesion localization"
    blurb: >-
      Strength, tone, stretch reflexes, the plantar response, wasting and fasciculations sort a
      weakness into upper or lower motor neuron patterns. Combined with which side and which body
      parts are affected, and with sensory findings, they place a lesion along a pathway whose
      crossings you know.
  - name: "Single-unit recording in awake primates"
    blurb: >-
      A fine extracellular electrode records the spikes of one neuron while a monkey moves or
      rests. Comparing firing rates and patterns before and after MPTP treatment tested the rate
      model's predictions for the subthalamic nucleus and internal pallidum.
  - name: "Post-mortem neurochemistry and immunohistochemistry"
    blurb: >-
      Measuring a transmitter in autopsy tissue, or staining terminal fields for peptides that
      mark one projection, shows what is lost in a human disease. It measures the end state of
      tissue, not living firing, and cannot by itself separate cell death from reduced marker.
  - name: "Cre-dependent optogenetics"
    blurb: >-
      A light-gated channel is expressed only in neurons of one genetic type, here D1- or
      D2-expressing striatal neurons, and light then drives them. This tests whether driving
      those cells is sufficient to change a behaviour; it does not show how they act normally.

  - stem: "In the classic rate model, what is the net sign of the direct pathway from cortex back to cortex (cortex, striatum, GPi, thalamus, cortex)?"
    choices:
      - "Negative, because GPi output is inhibitory"
      - "Positive, because the two inhibitory links cancel"
      - "Negative, because the striatum is inhibitory"
      - "Zero, because the excitation and inhibition balance"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The links are excitatory, inhibitory, inhibitory, excitatory, so the product is
      (+1)(−1)(−1)(+1) = +1: two inhibitions in series give disinhibition. Calling it negative
      because the striatum is inhibitory, or because GPi is inhibitory, counts only one of the two
      inhibitory links. A zero sign confuses multiplying signs with adding them.
  - stem: "In the classic model, how does dopamine from the substantia nigra pars compacta act on the two populations of striatal projection neurons?"
    choices:
      - "It inhibits both D1 direct and D2 indirect neurons equally"
      - "It inhibits D1 direct neurons and excites D2 indirect neurons"
      - "It excites D1 direct neurons and inhibits D2 indirect neurons"
      - "It excites both D1 direct and D2 indirect neurons equally"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      In the model dopamine excites D1-expressing direct neurons and inhibits D2-expressing
      indirect neurons, and both effects push the circuit toward releasing the thalamus. The
      reverse assignment swaps the receptor actions. Equal excitation of both would push the two
      pathways in opposite directions, so the signs give no net release, and equal inhibition of
      both has the same problem with the opposite sign.
  - stem: "In early Huntington's disease, striatal neurons projecting to GPe are more affected than those projecting to GPi. Using the signed indirect pathway, what does the model predict?"
    choices:
      - "GPi output rises and movement is reduced"
      - "Thalamus becomes more inhibited by GPe"
      - "STN activity rises and GPi output rises too"
      - "GPi output falls and extra movements appear"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Losing the inhibitory striatal input to GPe lets GPe fire more. GPe inhibits STN, so STN
      fires less, and STN excites GPi, so GPi fires less. Less GPi inhibition releases the
      thalamus, which the model links to chorea. Rising GPi output with less movement is the
      Parkinson's prediction. A rise in STN activity ignores the extra GPe inhibition. GPe does not
      project to the thalamus in this model, so it cannot inhibit it directly.

**A stroke deep in the left hemisphere weakens the right face, arm and leg. A cut through the right half of the spinal cord in the neck weakens the right arm and leg. Same tract, opposite sides.**

The difference is where the lesion sits relative to one crossing. This lesson uses that crossing to place lesions, then turns to the basal ganglia, deep nuclei that never reach the spinal cord yet decide, in the classic model, whether a movement goes ahead. You will treat that circuit as a chain of plus and minus signs, use it on two diseases, and see where it breaks.

## The upper motor neuron: its crossing and its signs

A **lower motor neuron** is a motor neuron in the brainstem or the spinal cord's ventral horn whose axon leaves the central nervous system and ends on muscle; you met it in [motor units and reflexes](/lessons/motor-units-reflexes/). An **upper motor neuron** is a neuron in the cortex or brainstem whose axon descends to control lower motor neurons, directly or through interneurons. A **tract** is a bundle of axons inside the central nervous system, and a **decussation** is a place where a tract crosses the midline. **Ipsilateral** means on the same side; **contralateral** means on the opposite side.

The corticospinal tract starts in layer V of primary motor cortex, with contributions from premotor, supplementary motor and parietal areas. Its axons pass through the corona radiata, the posterior limb of the internal capsule, the cerebral peduncle of the midbrain and the base of the pons, and form the medullary pyramids. In the caudal medulla most of them cross at the pyramidal decussation. Estimates vary between sources, from roughly 75 to 90%. The crossed fibres run in the lateral column of the cord as the **lateral corticospinal tract** and end mainly on interneurons of the intermediate grey matter and on ventral horn motor neurons. In macaques some cortical cells contact digit motor neurons directly (Rathelot and Strick, 2006). Most of the uncrossed minority forms the anterior tract, whose fibres largely appear to cross segmentally. How they cross during development is in [crossing the midline](/lessons/crossing-the-midline/).

A **lower motor neuron** lesion causes weakness with low tone and reduced or absent reflexes, because the motor limb of the reflex arc is cut; later the muscle wastes and may show fasciculations, the visible twitches of single motor units firing on their own. An established **upper motor neuron** lesion causes weakness with raised tone, brisk stretch reflexes, clonus (rhythmic beats of contraction set off by a sudden stretch) and an extensor plantar response, in which stroking the sole makes the great toe extend. Independent finger movements are often lost. Two timing traps: right after a spinal cord injury the limbs below can be flaccid and areflexic (spinal shock), and spasticity appears later; and an upgoing toe is normal in infants, so in a young child it has to be read against age.

The face adds one more clue. Corticobulbar fibres to the motor neurons for the lower face come mainly from the opposite cortex, while the forehead receives input from both sides. An upper motor neuron lesion above the brainstem therefore weakens the contralateral lower face more than the forehead, which is relatively spared.

**Worked problem: two cases.** Assume one focal lesion, examined weeks after onset so the upper motor neuron signs have developed.

*Case 1.* Weakness of the right lower face, right arm and right leg, with raised tone, brisk reflexes and an extensor plantar response on the right.
- Step 1: upper motor neuron signs put the lesion in the descending pathway, not the motor neurons or nerves.
- Step 2: the lower face is involved. Fibres for voluntary face movement end on facial motor neurons in the pons, so the lesion is above the facial nucleus: in the hemisphere, or occasionally in the pons above that level.
- Step 3: above the decussation the deficit is contralateral, so right-sided weakness means a left-sided lesion.
- Answer: most likely the left hemisphere, for example the posterior limb of the left internal capsule, where descending fibres for the face, arm and leg are packed into a small space.

*Case 2.* The same right arm and leg signs, but the face is normal. Vibration sense is lost on the right, and pain and temperature are lost on the left below about the C6 dermatome.
- Step 1: the face is spared, which fits a lesion below the facial nucleus, probably in the cord; the sensory findings in step 3 test this.
- Step 2: in the cord the corticospinal fibres have already crossed, so the weakness is ipsilateral: a right-sided lesion.
- Step 3: check with sensation ([touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/)). Dorsal column fibres ascend on the same side to nuclei in the medulla, and only the next neurons cross, so right vibration loss points to the right cord. Spinothalamic fibres cross within a segment or two of entry, so right cord damage removes pain and temperature on the left, starting a little below the lesion.
- Answer: a right hemisection of the cord near C5, with lower motor neuron signs expected at the level itself, where ventral horn cells are destroyed.

## The basal ganglia as a signed loop

A **nucleus** in the brain is a cluster of neuron cell bodies with shared connections. The basal ganglia include the **striatum** (caudate and putamen), the external and internal segments of the globus pallidus (**GPe** and **GPi**), the **subthalamic nucleus** (STN), and the substantia nigra, whose pars reticulata (**SNr**) works like GPi and whose pars compacta (**SNc**) holds dopamine neurons. The basal ganglia do not project to the spinal cord. Their output acts through the thalamus, which projects back to cortex, and through brainstem targets.

The output neurons of GPi and SNr are GABAergic and fire tonically, holding the motor thalamus under steady inhibition, like a foot resting on a brake. In the classic model, movement needs the brake lifted.

Striatal projection neurons are themselves GABAergic, and two routes from them reach the brake. In the **direct pathway**, striatal neurons that express D1 dopamine receptors inhibit GPi and SNr directly. In the **indirect pathway**, striatal neurons that express D2 receptors inhibit GPe; GPe inhibits STN; and STN, which is glutamatergic, excites GPi.

**Worked problem: count the signs.** Give each excitatory link +1 and each inhibitory link −1, and multiply along the route. A positive product means activity entering the loop ends up increasing cortical drive; a negative product means it ends up decreasing it.

> **Direct: cortex→striatum (+1) × striatum→GPi (−1) × GPi→thalamus (−1) × thalamus→cortex (+1) = +1**

> **Indirect: cortex→striatum (+1) × striatum→GPe (−1) × GPe→STN (−1) × STN→GPi (+1) × GPi→thalamus (−1) × thalamus→cortex (+1) = −1**

The direct route has two inhibitions in series, which give disinhibition. The indirect route has three negatives, so its product is −1.

Now add dopamine. In the classic model, SNc dopamine excites D1 direct neurons and inhibits D2 indirect neurons. Exciting a +1 route raises cortical drive. Inhibiting a −1 route also raises it. So both actions push toward movement, and losing dopamine pushes both routes toward less movement: the prediction of akinesia. How D1 and D2 receptors couple to cAMP belongs to [G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/) and [cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/); where dopamine is released and cleared is in [transmitter systems](/lessons/transmitter-systems/).

This arithmetic is the logic of the rate model set out by Albin, Young and Penney in 1989 and by DeLong in 1990: a way to reason about the direction of a change, not a measured law of how firing rates add.

## Parkinson's and Huntington's through the circuit

**Parkinson's disease.** The motor signs (slowness, small movements, rigidity, rest tremor) appear after substantial loss of SNc dopamine neurons; published threshold figures vary. Ehringer and Hornykiewicz found striatal dopamine greatly reduced in post-mortem brains from patients (1960). Run the signs: without dopamine the direct route weakens and the indirect route strengthens, so GPe is more inhibited, STN is released, and GPi fires more. The thalamus is held down harder and movement is hard to start.

A dopamine precursor such as L-DOPA raises the dopamine available in the striatum, and the predicted overactive STN became a surgical target. In mice with dopamine depleted by 6-hydroxydopamine, driving direct-pathway neurons with light rescued the motor deficits Kravitz and colleagues measured (2010). Why the dopamine neurons die is taken up in [neurodegeneration](/lessons/neurodegeneration/).

**Huntington's disease** has a different cause: an expanded CAG repeat in the huntingtin gene (the mutation type is in [mutation](/lessons/mutation/)), not a primary loss of dopamine. In post-mortem tissue from 17 patients, Reiner and colleagues (1988) found the enkephalin-containing projection to GPe more affected in early and middle stages than the substance P projection to GPi, and broad loss in the most advanced cases. Run the signs for losing indirect striatal neurons: GPe is released, STN is more inhibited, GPi gets less excitation and fires less, and the thalamus is released. That is thought to contribute to chorea, the brief, irregular involuntary movements of early disease. Later loss of direct-pathway output is associated with the akinesia of late disease.

## Where the box model breaks

The rate model predicts the sign of several clinical effects. Four sets of results show its limits.

First, the pathways do not alternate on and off. Cui and colleagues (2013) found calcium signals from both D1-expressing (direct-pathway) and A2A-expressing (indirect-pathway) striatal populations in mice rising around the start of an action. Freeze and colleagues (2013) found that stimulating either pathway produced both excitatory and inhibitory responses among SNr neurons in mice, although the inhibited and excited subgroups predicted the movement effects the model expects.

Second, there are more routes. In awake monkeys, Nambu and colleagues (2000) found that cortical stimulation evoked an early pallidal excitation that depended on STN and arrived faster than effects through the striatum: a **hyperdirect** pathway. And in mice, direct-pathway neurons branch. Labouesse and colleagues (2023) found that 147 of 148 SNr-labelled direct-pathway neurons in their sample also carried a GPe label. That is 147/148 = 99.3% of the SNr-labelled cells counted, not of all striatal neurons.

Third, rate may not be the main variable. Parker and colleagues (2018) found that dopamine depletion in mice disrupted the clustered activity of movement-related indirect-pathway neurons, a change in pattern rather than only in mean rate. Studies of parkinsonian animals and patients also report more bursting and synchronized oscillation in STN and GPi.

Fourth, some symptoms and treatments do not fit. Lesioning GPi should release the thalamus and cause extra movements, but pallidotomy instead relieves parkinsonism and also eases dyskinesia and dystonia. The model gives no account of rest tremor, and deep brain stimulation of the STN relieves parkinsonian signs by a mechanism that is still unresolved. What the basal ganglia contribute to choosing actions by their value continues in [motivation and reward](/lessons/motivation-and-reward/).

### Design problem

The rate model predicts that the STN is overactive in parkinsonism and that silencing it should help. Design a primate experiment to test this, and say what result would count against the model.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use monkeys made parkinsonian with MPTP, which preferentially kills SNc dopamine neurons. Record single units in STN and GPi before and after MPTP; the model predicts both rates rise. Then lesion or inactivate one STN and score akinesia and rigidity on the opposite side of the body, with the other side as a within-animal comparison. Bergman, Wichmann and DeLong (1990) did the lesion step and reported improvement in the limbs opposite the lesion.

Controls: sham surgery; histology for lesion position and SNc loss; scoring blind to side and condition; and GPi recording after the lesion to see whether its output fell.

Against the model: no motor improvement after an accurate STN lesion; or improvement with no fall in GPi activity, which would mean the benefit does not work by lowering GPi rate as the model says. Two cautions: pattern and synchrony change as well as rate, so record them too; and a lesion result does not explain deep brain stimulation.
