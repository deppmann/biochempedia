## The big idea

Put one cup of seedlings in a cupboard and leave one on a windowsill. Within a week they often look like different plants. The one in the dark grows tall, thin and pale yellow. The one in the light stays short, sturdy and green.

Plants need water, air, light, nutrients and a place with room to grow. This activity tests two of those needs, light and water, with a **fair test**: a test where only one thing changes and everything else stays the same. Same soil, same cups, same seeds, same amount of water.

Light matters because a green plant makes its own food. Its leaves use light energy to turn water and carbon dioxide, a gas from the air, into sugar, and they give off oxygen. This is **photosynthesis**. A seedling in the dark lives on food stored in its seed, and when that runs out it cannot make more. Plants do not get their food from soil. Soil holds the roots, water and small amounts of nutrients.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay nearby while your child plants and waters. An adult pokes the holes in the cups and opens the bag of potting mix.
- Potting mix can contain fertilizer and germs, including Legionella, which can cause a lung infection if the dust is breathed in. An adult opens the bag with scissors, outdoors or by an open window and with faces turned away, wears a dust mask if you have one, and dampens the mix before anyone scoops it so it does not make dust. Keep it out of mouths, and wash hands with soap and water after planting and after each check.
- Garden seeds are not food. Some are coated with a pesticide and dyed a bright colour, so read the packet and use untreated seed. Keep seeds away from children under 4, who can choke on them or push them into a nose or ear.
- For the dark place, use a cupboard with no cleaning products in it and that children don't climb into, or turn a cardboard box over the cups.
- Stand the cups on a tray and wipe up spilled water right away so no one slips. Keep the cups where pets cannot dig in them or eat the soil.
- If fuzzy mould grows on the soil, do not sniff or touch it. An adult carries that cup outside and throws it away. A child with asthma or a mould allergy should stay away from it.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Set up.** An adult opens the bag and dampens the mix first (see the safety box). Then fill four cups with damp potting mix to about 2 cm below the rim. Label them "light + water", "dark + water", "light, no water" and "dark, no water". Stand them on the tray.
2. **Plant.** Have your child press 4 or 5 radish seeds about 1 cm deep into each cup and cover them. Give each cup 2 tablespoons of water. With a class, give each group of four one tray of four cups.
3. **Grow them all the same.** Keep all four cups on a sunny windowsill. Water each the same amount whenever the top feels dry. Radish sprouts usually appear in 3 to 10 days, faster in a warm room. Wait until most seedlings are about 2 cm tall.
4. **Predict.** Before you change anything, ask your child what will happen to each cup. Write or draw their guesses on the sheet.
5. **Start the test.** Move the two "dark" cups, on a plate or a second tray, into the cupboard or under the box. Keep giving the two "water" cups 2 tablespoons when the top feels dry. Give the two "no water" cups nothing more. Change nothing else. If you have a fifth cup, plant fresh seeds in it, water it, and put it straight into the dark.
6. **Check every day or two.** Open the cupboard, look quickly, and put the cups back. Draw each cup. Ages 8 to 10 measure the tallest seedling in each cup in centimetres. Note the colour, the height, and whether the leaves droop.
7. **Notice the changes.** The dry cups usually droop within 2 to 5 days, sooner in small cups and warm rooms. Within about a week, the dark, watered seedlings are often taller, thinner and paler than the windowsill ones, and they lean or flop. Over 1 to 2 weeks they grow weaker. The "light + water" cup stays green and upright.
8. **Bring one back (optional).** Move a pale "dark + water" cup back to the windowsill. If it is not too weak, its leaves usually turn green over a few days.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "What do you think will happen to a plant that gets water but no light?"
- "I wonder which cup will droop first. Why that one?"
- "The dark plant is the tallest. Is it the healthiest? How could we tell?"
- "I wonder if a seed needs light to sprout. How could we find out?"
- "What do you think will happen if we put the pale plant back in the sun?"
- "Where do you think a plant gets its food?"

## Why it works

A seed carries stored food. It uses that food to sprout and push up its first leaves. Radish seeds do this in light or in dark, though a few kinds, such as some lettuce, need light to sprout. After that, a green plant makes nearly all of its own food by photosynthesis. **Chlorophyll**, the green colouring in leaves, captures light energy. The plant uses that energy to join carbon dioxide with water, making sugar and giving off oxygen.

In the dark, seedlings grow in a different way, called **etiolation**. The stem stretches fast, the leaves stay small, and little chlorophyll forms, so the plant is pale yellow. In nature this stretching can carry a buried or shaded seedling up to light. In a cupboard there is no light to reach, so the seedling uses up its stored food and then weakens and dies.

Water has several jobs too. It is one of the raw materials for sugar, it keeps the cells firm so stems stand up, and it carries nutrients up from the roots.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use only two cups, both watered: one on the windowsill and one in the dark. That gives one clear difference. Skip measuring. Have your child draw both cups every few days, using crayons that match the real leaf colours. At the end, hold the two plants side by side and ask which one looks stronger and why.

### Ages 8 to 10
Let your child plan the test: what will change, what stays the same, and what to measure. Measure the tallest seedling in each cup in centimetres every two days and keep a table. At the end, make a bar graph with one bar per cup. Ask whether one cup for each condition is enough. Two cups of each makes the result more trustworthy.

## If it doesn't go as planned

No sprouts after 10 days usually means a cold room or old seeds; try a fresh packet in a warmer spot. Fuzzy mould on the soil means it is staying too wet. An adult throws that cup away, as the safety box says, and you can start a new one with a little less water. If the "no water" cups do not droop, the mix may hold a lot of water; give it a few more days. If light leaks into the cupboard, use a box with no gaps.
