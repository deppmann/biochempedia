## Explain: what goes in and what comes out

<WordBank terms={['photosynthesis', 'chloroplast', 'chlorophyll', 'glucose', 'conservation of matter', 'chemical energy', 'starch', 'cellulose', 'cellular respiration', 'prokaryote', 'limiting factor']} />

**Photosynthesis** is the process in which plants, algae and cyanobacteria use light energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into sugar, releasing oxygen. The sugar is **glucose**, a small sugar molecule with 6 carbon atoms. In words:

> carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen

With chemical formulas:

> 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6O2

This equation is a summary. Inside the cell it takes many steps, but you only need the start and the end.

Count the atoms. On the left there are 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 18 oxygen atoms. On the right there are exactly the same. This is **conservation of matter**: in a chemical change, atoms are rearranged, never created or destroyed.

Now the willow makes sense. The atoms in its wood had to come from somewhere. The carbon and much of the oxygen in its sugar came from carbon dioxide in the air. The hydrogen came from water. Soil gave water and a small amount of minerals such as nitrogen, which is why the dried soil barely changed. Most of a living plant's fresh mass is water, often about 80 to 90 percent in textbook accounts, but most of its dry mass is built from carbon taken in as carbon dioxide.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/photosynthesis-energy-capture/willow-before-after.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a pot with a small willow stem on the left and the same pot years later with a tall willow tree on the right; arrows from the air and water lead to the tree." caption="A small willow in a pot gained more than 160 pounds while the soil lost only about 2 ounces. The tree's mass came mostly from carbon dioxide in the air, plus water." />

Energy is conserved too. Light energy is not used up or made. It is transformed into **chemical energy**, energy held in the arrangement of atoms in molecules. Glucose and oxygen together hold more chemical energy than the carbon dioxide and water they came from.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Photosynthesis rearranges the atoms of carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen, and stores light energy as chemical energy.</Support>

## Explain: where it happens and where the sugar goes

In plants and algae, photosynthesis happens in the **chloroplast**, a green organelle in leaf cells and other green parts. Inside it, stacks of membranes hold **chlorophyll**, a green pigment that absorbs light, mostly red and blue. That captured energy powers the reactions that split water and build glucose. The oxygen released comes from the split water. It is a by-product, and it leaves the leaf through tiny pores.

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/photosynthesis-energy-capture/leaf-cell-chloroplast.webp" width={1200} height={800} keyItems={["light", "carbon dioxide", "glucose", "oxygen", "water"]} given={[0, 4]} alt="AI-generated illustration: a cut-away leaf cell with one enlarged green chloroplast holding stacked bars and a few sand-coloured blocks; arrows bring light, gases and water in and a gas out through two pores. Five numbered circles mark parts to name." caption="Inside one leaf cell, the chloroplast captures light and uses materials from the air and roots to make sugar and release a gas." />

Not every photosynthetic cell has chloroplasts. A **prokaryote** is a cell with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles, such as a bacterium. Cyanobacteria are prokaryotes that photosynthesize and release oxygen. Their chlorophyll sits on folded membranes inside the cell instead of in chloroplasts.

Once a plant has glucose, it has three main uses for it:
- **Used right away.** Plant cells carry out **cellular respiration**, which breaks glucose down and releases its energy in a form cells can use. Plants respire all the time, in light and dark. [Respiration and ATP](/lessons/respiration-and-atp/) follows that path.
- **Stored.** Glucose molecules are linked into **starch**, a long chain the plant stores in seeds, roots and leaves.
- **Rebuilt.** Glucose molecules are linked a different way into **cellulose**, the tough fiber of plant cell walls. Wood is mostly cell walls. Plants also rebuild glucose, with minerals from soil, into proteins, fats and DNA. [What Am I Made Of?](/lessons/macromolecules/) covers those molecules.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/photosynthesis-energy-capture/glucose-three-uses.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a small plant with arrows from a glucose marker in a leaf to three places: a cell with a mitochondrion, a root packed with stored chains, and a plant cell wall made of fibres." caption="A plant uses its glucose three ways: it breaks some down right away in respiration, stores some as starch, and rebuilds some as cellulose for its cell walls." />

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Chlorophyll in chloroplasts (or on membranes in prokaryotes) captures light, and the glucose made is used in respiration, stored as starch, or rebuilt into cellulose and other molecules.</Support>

## Explain: what limits the rate

A **limiting factor** is the condition that holds a process back, such as the resource in shortest supply or a temperature that is too low or too high. Photosynthesis has three main limits:
- **Light intensity.** More light usually means faster photosynthesis, up to a point.
- **Carbon dioxide.** More carbon dioxide also speeds it up, up to a point.
- **Temperature.** The reactions are run by enzymes. Warmth speeds them up to a point, and too much heat slows them as the enzymes lose their shape. [Enzymes at Work](/lessons/enzymes-at-work/) explains why.

Think of a sandwich line with one worker making bread and one adding fillings. Speeding up the bread maker helps only until the fillings worker can't keep up. Then the line runs at the slower worker's pace. The analogy breaks because a plant's factors also affect each other: heat can change how much water a leaf loses, for example.

That is why a graph of rate against light usually rises and then levels off. At the flat part, more light does nothing, because carbon dioxide or temperature is now the limit.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Light, carbon dioxide and temperature can each limit photosynthesis, and the rate stops rising when a different factor holds it back.</Support>

## Elaborate: underwater grass in the Chesapeake

Underwater grasses, which scientists call submerged aquatic vegetation (SAV), live on the bottom of shallow parts of the Bay. Every year the Virginia Institute of Marine Science (VIMS) maps them from the air.

| Survey year | Mapped underwater grass, Bay and tidal rivers (acres) | Coverage note from VIMS |
|---|---|---|
| 2018 | 99,511 | Some tributary areas not fully mapped |
| 2019 | 66,684 | Much of the loss was widgeongrass |
| 2020 | 63,132 | Satellite images replaced aerial ones for the Anacostia and northernmost Potomac |
| 2021 | 68,025 | Weather kept parts of the Mattaponi and Pamunkey rivers from being imaged |
| 2022 | 77,425 | Satellite images filled in for murky rivers; part of the Potomac imaged by satellite only |
| 2023 | 79,716 | Upper Potomac imaged after the grass had peaked |

Source: VIMS annual SAV survey executive summaries, 2018 to 2023, for Chesapeake Bay and its tidal tributaries (all mapped beds, not a single station). For areas mapped in both 2018 and 2019, VIMS compared 98,828 acres with 62,334 acres, a 37% drop.

How much light do these plants need? A Chesapeake Bay Program technical synthesis (2000) set habitat targets for the growing season. In fresher upper reaches, more than 13% of the light at the surface should reach the grass through the water. In the saltier middle and lower Bay, the target is more than 22%. Most eelgrass lives in the lower Bay. Mud and floating algae block light, and so does material growing on the leaves themselves.

2. Why is it fairer to compare 2018 and 2019 using the matched areas than the two totals?
3. The table has no water-clarity numbers. What would you need to add before claiming that clearer water caused the increase after 2020?

### Design problem

Plan a real leaf-disk experiment to test how light intensity affects the rate of photosynthesis. Name your variables, what you keep the same, your control, and how many trials you would run.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Independent variable:** lamp distance, such as 10, 20, 30 and 40 cm.
- **Dependent variable:** the time for 5 of 10 disks to float, in minutes.
- **Constants:** same leaf type and disk size, same baking-soda amount, same volume of water, same starting water temperature, same lamp. An LED lamp or a clear water barrier keeps the lamp's heat from changing the temperature.
- **Control:** one cup of disks kept in the dark. If those float too, something other than photosynthesis is lifting them.
- **Trials:** three cups at each distance, and report the mean time.
- **Safety:** keep cords and lamps away from water, and let hot bulbs cool before you touch them.

If the time to float gets shorter as the lamp gets closer, then levels off, light was limiting at first and then something else became the limit.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The willow gained mass because its leaves took in carbon dioxide and, using light energy, combined it with water to build glucose and then wood. The soil gave water and a little mineral matter, which is why it barely changed. Bay grasses face the same rule underwater: when murky water cuts the light, light becomes the limiting factor.

**Science practices you used:** making predictions and testing them, planning a fair test with a control and repeated trials, reading a data table and its coverage notes, separating a pattern from a cause, and using a model and naming its limits.
