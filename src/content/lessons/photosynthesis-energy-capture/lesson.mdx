> **The one idea: a plant is built mostly from air and water, powered by light.** Photosynthesis rearranges the atoms of carbon dioxide and water into sugar and oxygen, so most of a plant's dry mass comes from the air. Light adds no atoms, only energy. And at any moment, photosynthesis runs only as fast as its **limiting factor** allows: the scarcest input or least favorable condition.
>
> *Scope:* dry mass is the mass left after drying; much of a living plant's fresh weight is water. Soil supplies a small but essential share of the dry mass as minerals such as nitrogen. The rate rule covers light, carbon dioxide and temperature over the range you can test here, with the other conditions held fixed. In real plants two factors can limit at once, and whole-plant growth also depends on how the plant spends its sugar.

## Explain: which atoms go where

<WordBank terms={['photosynthesis', 'chloroplast', 'chlorophyll', 'stomata', 'glucose', 'conservation of matter', 'chemical energy', 'starch', 'cellulose', 'cellular respiration', 'prokaryote', 'limiting factor']} />

**Photosynthesis** is the process in which plants, algae and cyanobacteria use light energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into **glucose**, a sugar with 6 carbon atoms, releasing oxygen:

> carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen
>
> 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6O2

The first film counted the atoms on each side and found them equal: **conservation of matter**, the rule that atoms are rearranged, never created or destroyed. The count does not say which oxygen atoms go where.

**Predict.** Suppose you could tag the oxygen atoms in the water a plant takes in. Would the oxygen gas the leaf gives off carry the tag?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes. In the standard account from atom-tagging experiments, the oxygen a leaf releases comes from split water. That looks impossible, since 6 water molecules hold only 6 oxygen atoms and 6 O2 need 12: the full process splits 12 water molecules and makes 6 new ones, which the summary equation cancels out. Some tagged oxygen can also turn up in sugar and in water, because oxygen atoms trade places between molecules, so the equation cannot assign every oxygen atom. The safe map: the sugar's carbon and much of its oxygen come from carbon dioxide, its hydrogen from water, and the released oxygen gas from split water.

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/photosynthesis-energy-capture/willow-before-after.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a pot with a small willow stem on the left and the same pot years later with a tall willow tree on the right; arrows from the air and water lead to the tree." caption="A soil loss of about 2 ounces cannot explain a gain of more than 160 pounds; the willow's dry matter was built mostly from carbon dioxide in the air." />

Energy is conserved too: light energy is not used up or made, only transformed. **Chemical energy**, energy held in the arrangement of atoms, belongs to glucose and oxygen together, not to glucose alone; cellular respiration releases it as they rejoin into carbon dioxide and water.

**Predict.** A leaf stores only part of the light energy it absorbs as chemical energy. Energy is never destroyed. So where does the rest go?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Most of it leaves the leaf as heat; no energy vanishes. Of all the sunlight that falls on a leaf, the share stored as chemical energy is smaller still, because some light is never absorbed.

</details>

<Support min={2}>**Key idea for this section:** The sugar's carbon comes from carbon dioxide and its hydrogen from water, the oxygen gas released comes from split water, and light supplies energy, not atoms.</Support>

## Explain: where it happens and where the sugar goes

The first film showed the **chloroplast**, the green organelle where plants and algae photosynthesize; its stacked membranes hold **chlorophyll**, a pigment that absorbs mostly red and blue light. Carbon dioxide enters and oxygen leaves through **stomata**, tiny leaf pores. The film left out cells with no chloroplasts, and what a plant does with its sugar.

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/photosynthesis-energy-capture/leaf-cell-chloroplast.webp" width={1200} height={800} keyItems={["light", "carbon dioxide", "glucose", "oxygen", "water"]} given={[0, 4]} alt="AI-generated illustration: a cut-away leaf cell with one enlarged green chloroplast holding stacked bars and a few sand-coloured blocks; arrows bring light, gases and water in and a gas out through two pores. Five numbered circles mark parts to name." caption="Inside one leaf cell, the chloroplast captures light and uses materials from the air and roots to make sugar and release a gas." />

A **prokaryote** is a cell with no nucleus and no chloroplasts or mitochondria, such as a bacterium. Cyanobacteria are prokaryotes that photosynthesize and release oxygen; in almost all of them, chlorophyll sits on folded membranes inside the cell instead of in chloroplasts.

A plant has three main uses for glucose:
- **Used right away.** Plant cells carry out **cellular respiration**, which breaks glucose down and releases its energy in a form cells can use, in light and dark. [Respiration and ATP](/lessons/respiration-and-atp/) follows that path.
- **Stored.** Glucose molecules are linked into **starch**, a long chain the plant stores in seeds, roots and leaves.
- **Rebuilt.** Linked a different way, glucose becomes **cellulose**, the fiber of cell walls and so of wood. Plants also rebuild glucose, with minerals from soil, into proteins, fats and DNA; the minerals supply elements sugar lacks, such as nitrogen. [What Am I Made Of?](/lessons/macromolecules/) covers those molecules.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/photosynthesis-energy-capture/glucose-three-uses.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a small plant with arrows from a glucose marker in a leaf to three places: a cell with a mitochondrion, a root packed with stored chains, and a plant cell wall made of fibres." caption="A plant uses its glucose three ways: it breaks some down right away in respiration, stores some as starch, and rebuilds some as cellulose for its cell walls." />

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Chlorophyll in chloroplasts (or, in almost all cyanobacteria, on membranes inside the cell) captures light, and the glucose made is used in respiration, stored as starch, or rebuilt into cellulose and other molecules.</Support>

## Explain: what limits the rate

Three things the second film did not show change how you read your race.

**What you timed was net oxygen.** The disks' own cells respire the whole time and use some of the oxygen. For otherwise matched disks, a shorter time to float means faster net oxygen build-up: what photosynthesis makes minus what respiration uses. It is evidence about the rate, not a direct measurement of it.

**Temperature is not an input.** Warmth adds no atoms to the sugar; it changes how fast **enzymes**, the proteins that speed up the reactions, can work.

**Predict.** Keep the lamp close and add plenty of baking soda. Now warm the cup step by step from cold to hot. Does the rate keep rising?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Warmth speeds the enzymes up at first. Too much heat slows photosynthesis, partly because key enzymes and the helper proteins that keep them working lose their shape. So the rate usually rises, peaks and then falls, unlike the light curve, which usually rises and levels off. [Enzymes at Work](/lessons/enzymes-at-work/) explains why.

</details>

**A real plant has limits the cup hides.** Water and mineral nutrients can limit photosynthesis too. A leaf short of water closes its stomata and takes in less carbon dioxide.

On the flat part of the light curve, the film's slow worker might be carbon dioxide supply, temperature, or how fast enzymes attach carbon from carbon dioxide to other molecules. Unlike workers in a line, a plant's factors affect each other and can limit together: heat, for example, changes how much water a leaf loses.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Light, carbon dioxide and temperature can each limit photosynthesis; when more of one stops raising the rate, a different factor is holding it back.</Support>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

A plant is built mostly from air and water, powered by light, and paced by its limiting factor.

### Design problem

Plan a real leaf-disk experiment to test how light intensity affects the rate of photosynthesis. Name your variables, what you keep the same, your control, and how many trials you would run.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Independent variable:** lamp distance, such as 10, 20, 30 and 40 cm.
- **Dependent variable:** the time for 5 of 10 disks to float, in minutes.
- **Constants:** same leaf type and disk size, same baking-soda amount, same volume of water, same starting water temperature, same lamp. An LED lamp or a clear water barrier cuts down the lamp's heat; check the water temperature in each trial.
- **Control:** one cup of disks kept in the dark. If those float too, something other than photosynthesis is lifting them.
- **Trials:** three cups at each distance, and report the mean time.
- **Safety:** keep cords and lamps away from water, and let hot bulbs cool before you touch them.

If the time to float gets shorter as the lamp gets closer, then levels off, light was limiting at first and then something else became the limit.

## Going deeper

### How much of a plant is water?

Much of a living plant's fresh mass is water, about 80 to 90 percent in soft leaves and stems. Drying a sample before weighing removes this water, so only solid material counts. Van Helmont weighed his willow alive, not dried, so part of its 164-pound gain was water.

### How good was van Helmont's soil weighing?

In the 1600s, van Helmont planted a 5-pound willow stem in a pot of 200 pounds of dried soil; five years later he reported a tree of about 169 pounds, and the soil was only about 2 ounces lighter. Too rough to measure the minerals the tree took up: rainwater carried minerals in, fallen leaves were never weighed, and dried soil is hard to weigh to the ounce. The conclusion stands, since even a missed pound or two is tiny next to 164 pounds.
