summary: "The light reactions use two photosystems in series to move electrons from water to NADP+, releasing oxygen, building a proton gradient that makes ATP, and making NADPH. The single organizing idea: one photon lifts one electron, each lift keeps only about 0.7 to 0.8 V, and the climb from water to NADP+ is 1.14 V, so every electron is lifted twice in series (the Z-scheme); between the two lifts cytochrome b6f pumps extra protons into the lumen, and together with the protons released from split water they make the ATP. One molecular innovation, the Mn4CaO5 cluster that pries electrons off water, became the source of the oxygen that slowly filled the planet's air."

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm 5"
    text: "Plants eat sunlight. Tiny green dots inside a leaf are packed with a green color called chlorophyll, which catches the light like a catcher's mitt. The light gives the leaf energy, and the leaf uses that energy to do something amazing: it pulls water apart. When it pulls water apart, it lets go of the oxygen — and that oxygen is what you breathe! The leaf keeps the leftover energy as two little battery packs (called ATP and NADPH) that it will use later to build sugar for food. So a leaf turns sunshine and water into the air you breathe and the food it eats."
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: "The light reactions happen in the thylakoid membranes inside a chloroplast. Pigments like chlorophyll absorb light; the energy from a photon excites an electron. Most pigments are antennas that funnel the energy to a special spot called a reaction center, where the electron is actually handed off. Photosystem II goes first: its chlorophyll (P680), once it has handed off its excited electron, is such a strong electron-grabber that it splits water, which releases the oxygen you breathe and dumps protons inside the thylakoid. The electron then flows downhill to cytochrome b6f (pumping more protons), then to Photosystem I (P700), where a second photon boosts it high enough to make NADPH. The protons trapped inside power ATP synthase. Picture the path as a 'Z': two upward kicks from light separated by a downhill slide. The products, ATP and NADPH, get spent later in the Calvin cycle to build sugar."
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: "In the thylakoid membrane, light drives linear (non-cyclic) electron flow described by the Z-scheme. A photon's energy migrates by resonance through ~200–300 antenna pigments to a reaction center, where charge separation occurs — only there does an electron actually transfer. PSII's P680 is excited to P680*, and charge separation moves an electron to pheophytin (the first step often starts at the accessory chlorophyll ChlD1); the P680+ left behind (~+1.2 V) is the strongest biological oxidant, ripping electrons from water at the Mn4CaO5 oxygen-evolving complex via the S-state (Kok) cycle: 2 H2O + 4 photons → O2 + 4 H+ (lumen) + 4 e-. Electrons pass P680 → pheophytin → QA → QB/plastoquinol → cytochrome b6f (Q-cycle pumps ~4 H+ per 2 e-) → plastocyanin → PSI. A second photon excites P700 → P700*, lifting electrons to A0 → A1 → Fe-S clusters → ferredoxin → FNR, which reduces NADP+ to NADPH on the stromal side (2 Fd_red + NADP+ + H+ → 2 Fd_ox + NADPH). The lumen proton gradient (water-splitting + Q-cycle) is the proton-motive force that drives ATP synthase. Cyclic photophosphorylation loops PSI → Fd → b6f to make ATP alone when NADPH demand is low."
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: "Oxygenic photosynthesis chains two photosystems in series. A 680 nm photon carries ~1.8 eV, more than the ~1.1 V gap from the H2O/O2 couple (+0.82 V) to the NADP+/NADPH couple (−0.32 V), but each reaction center gives up a large part of that energy to make charge separation fast and stable. One photon per electron therefore cannot cover the gap, the overpotentials and a proton-motive force; two photons per electron can. PSII (D1/D2 core, homologous to the purple-bacterial reaction center) generates the most oxidizing species in biology: the radical cation P680•+, left after charge separation, at ~+1.2 V. Tuned by its protein environment, it drives the Mn4CaO5 cluster through four sequential one-electron oxidations, most of them coupled to proton release (typically 1:0:1:2 across the S-state transitions) — the Kok S-state cycle — storing oxidizing equivalents until S4→S0 releases O2. Charge separation is kinetically stabilized by the protein's spatial cascade of acceptors (pheophytin → QA → QB), outrunning recombination. Cytochrome b6f, the homolog of the cytochrome bc1 complex, runs a Q-cycle that doubles proton translocation per electron pair through the Rieske 2Fe-2S/cytochrome f high-potential chain and the b-hemes/low-potential chain. PSI then re-energizes electrons to reduce ferredoxin (~−0.42 V), and FNR (a plant-type FAD flavoenzyme, the prototype of its own reductase family) couples two sequential one-electron Fd oxidations to a hydride transfer onto NADP+. Under sustained light the thylakoid pmf is mostly ΔpH (counterion movements across the membrane dissipate much of ΔΨ, though the split varies with conditions), and that ΔpH both powers CF1Fo ATP synthase and triggers photoprotective NPQ via the xanthophyll cycle and PsbS. The branch point between linear and cyclic electron flow (PGR5/PGRL1- and NDH-dependent) tunes the ATP:NADPH output ratio to match Calvin-cycle stoichiometry (~3 ATP : 2 NADPH per CO2), a regulatory problem central to engineering improved photosynthetic efficiency."

    text: "Boil spinach a few minutes too long and the bright green slumps into a dull olive.   - title: "The antenna is a bucket brigade where no bucket moves"
    text: "Picture 200 to 300 pigment molecules arranged around a reaction center like a satellite dish.   - title: "Photosystem II acts first — the scientists numbered them backwards"
    text: "It looks like a typo: Photosystem II goes before Photosystem I. It isn't. The numbering is historical; it does not follow the order electrons run through them. Water feeds PSII first; PSII hands electrons down the chain to PSI; PSI lifts them the rest of the way to NADPH. The pause-and-think that makes the dependency click: what if PSI worked but PSII didn't? **Almost every oxygen molecule you have ever breathed was built from the oxygen atoms of water, split apart at a single protein in a leaf, an alga or a cyanobacterium. That protein is Photosystem II, and splitting water means pushing electrons uphill.**

In a mitochondrion, electrons from NADH fall to oxygen and the fall pays for ATP. A chloroplast runs that fall backwards, hauling electrons from water up to NADP⁺ to make NADPH, and makes ATP too: the two currencies the Calvin cycle spends to build sugar. The puzzle is the wiring. One red photon carries more energy than the climb needs, yet the chloroplast uses two photosystems and two photons for every electron. Why?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** At a reaction center, one photon lifts one electron uphill, and each lift keeps only about 0.7 to 0.8 V of the photon's roughly 1.8 eV. Moving an electron from water (+0.82 V) to NADP⁺ (−0.32 V) takes 1.14 V, more than one lift keeps, so the chloroplast lifts every electron twice, in series: 2 photons per electron, 8 photons and 2 NADPH per O₂. Between the lifts, cytochrome b6f pumps extra protons into the lumen; with the protons released from split water, they make the ATP.

This rule covers linear (non-cyclic) electron flow, with approximate potentials at pH 7; cyclic flow comes near the end.

## Where it happens: a captured bacterium

The chloroplast is a double-membrane organelle, and that second membrane is a fossil of an engulfed cyanobacterium (the evidence is in Going deeper). Inside the inner membrane sits the **stroma**, the soup where carbon gets fixed later. Floating in the stroma is a folded network of flattened sacs called **thylakoid membranes**, stacked like poker chips into **grana** and joined by unstacked stroma lamellae. The sacs enclose one connected interior space called the **lumen**. The light reactions run in and across the thylakoid membrane, and where the protons end up, the lumen, is the whole point.

## What a photon can do

Light is energy in discrete packets, photons, each carrying E = hν. When a pigment absorbs one, an electron jumps to an excited state, and that excitation can go one of three ways.

It can simply fall back down, releasing the energy as fluorescence or heat. It can hand its **energy** to a neighboring pigment without the electron going anywhere: **resonance energy transfer**. Or, at one special spot, it can hand off the **electron itself**.

The second fate is where most students stumble. An antenna of many light-harvesting complexes packs roughly 200 to 300 pigment molecules around each reaction center like a satellite dish. Wherever a photon lands, the *energy* hops pigment to pigment toward the center, a bucket brigade in which no bucket actually moves. **Energy travels by resonance; the electron transfers only at the reaction center.**

<LessonFigure src="/figures/light-reactions/antenna-energy-vs-electron.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a ring of antenna pigments around a central reaction center. A photon strikes one pigment at the edge, and dashed gold arcs show its energy hopping pigment to pigment inward while each pigment's own electron stays put. Only at the reaction center does an electron actually leave, moving out to an acceptor. A small inset shows the three fates of an excited pigment: falling back as light or heat, passing energy to a neighbor, or giving away its electron." caption="In the antenna, energy hops pigment to pigment by resonance and no electron moves. Only at the reaction center does the photon's energy lift an electron out to an acceptor: one photon, one electron, the step the whole lesson counts." />

The main pigment is **chlorophyll**, a magnesium-centered ring related to heme, helped by accessory **carotenoids**. The reaction-center chlorophylls are named for the wavelength they absorb best: **P680** in Photosystem II, **P700** in Photosystem I.

A blue photon carries about 2.8 eV. It is used too, but the extra energy is shed as heat within picoseconds, so every photon delivers about one red photon's worth, 1.8 eV, to the reaction center. "One photon, one lift" holds whatever the color, and the film's "photon with the right energy" includes blue as well as red.

At the reaction center, the third fate fires. An excited donor (D\*) gives its electron to an acceptor (A), creating a separated pair of charges, D⁺ and A⁻. The surrounding protein passes the electron on to the next acceptor fast enough that it rarely snaps back. That **photoinduced charge separation** is the actual conversion of light into chemistry, and Deisenhofer and Michel won a Nobel for being the first to see its machinery, atom by atom.

## Why two lifts

The **reduction potential**, E, in volts, measures how tightly a molecule holds electrons. A more positive E holds them tightly; a more negative E gives them up easily. Left alone, electrons flow from more negative to more positive E, the way water runs downhill. Diagrams of the light reactions draw the most negative values at the top, so on the page "uphill" means toward more negative E, and climbing costs energy.

Two numbers frame everything:

- **Water**, the electron source: O₂/H₂O at about **+0.82 V**, holding its electrons very tightly.
- **NADP⁺**, the destination: NADP⁺/NADPH at about **−0.32 V**.

So the chloroplast must push electrons 1.14 V uphill.

## Walking the Z

Follow one electron in three stages: a lift, a bridge, a second lift.

### Stage 1: Photosystem II, the first lift

Energy collected by the antenna reaches P680 and makes **P680\***, which at about −0.6 V gives its electron away easily. The electron goes to **pheophytin** (chlorophyll without its magnesium), then to a bound plastoquinone, **QA**, and then to a second one, **QB**. After two such electrons, and two protons taken from the stroma, QB becomes **plastoquinol (PQH₂)** and drifts off into the membrane.

The same photon leaves something behind: **P680⁺, at about +1.2 V the strongest biological oxidant known.** One photon has made a strong electron donor at the top of the Z and a strong electron grabber at the bottom.

**Predict.** P680⁺ sits at about +1.2 V and water at +0.82 V. Which way do electrons flow between them, and does that step need light?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

From water to P680⁺, and no. P680⁺ is about 0.4 V more positive than water, so the step is downhill. Light was needed only to make P680⁺. That 0.4 V drop is the driving force that makes water oxidation go.

</details>

P680⁺ takes its replacement electron from the **oxygen-evolving complex**, a cluster of four manganese atoms, one calcium and five oxygens (Mn₄CaO₅). Making one O₂ takes four electrons, and each photon removes only one, so the cluster counts: its **S-state (Kok) cycle** steps from S0 to S4, storing one oxidizing step per photon, and at S4 it releases O₂ and resets.

> **2 H₂O + 4 photons → O₂ + 4 H⁺ (into the lumen) + 4 e⁻**

### Stage 2: the bridge, downhill through cytochrome b6f

Plastoquinol carries the electrons to the **cytochrome b6f** complex. From about +0.1 V they fall to **plastocyanin**, a small copper protein in the lumen, at about +0.37 V. This is the stretch of the Z where a fall pumps protons: for each electron it passes on, b6f releases two protons into the lumen (roughly 4 H⁺ per pair). One rode in on plastoquinol, picked up from the stroma at QB. The other is moved by the Q-cycle you met in Complex III, which sends half the electrons back to make more plastoquinol. That is twice the one proton per electron that water-splitting releases.

### Stage 3: Photosystem I, the second lift

Plastocyanin hands the electron to P700⁺. A second photon makes **P700\***, at about −1.3 V among the most reducing species in the chloroplast. The electron runs through A₀, A₁ (phylloquinone) and iron-sulfur clusters to **ferredoxin**, a strong soluble reductant in the stroma, at −0.42 V. **Ferredoxin-NADP⁺ reductase (FNR)**, a FAD flavoprotein, collects two electrons from two ferredoxins, adds a proton, and makes **NADPH** on the stromal side, exactly where the Calvin cycle waits.

## The tally: protons, ATP and the shortfall

Now count, per O₂. Water gives up 4 electrons; two lifts each is **8 photons**, and the 4 electrons make **2 NADPH**. Protons delivered to the lumen: 4 from water plus 8 released at b6f, **12 H⁺**.

The protons drive ATP synthase by chemiosmosis: the same proton-gradient logic Peter Mitchell proposed for mitochondria, and the same rotary motor from the last chapter, run here with protons flowing out of the lumen into the stroma. So the lumen plays the mitochondrial intermembrane space, where protons pile up, and the stroma plays the matrix, where the synthase makes ATP.

That loop is **cyclic flow**. Arnon showed isolated chloroplasts can make ATP two ways: **non-cyclic** photophosphorylation (the linear path: ATP, NADPH and O₂) and **cyclic** photophosphorylation (electrons loop PSI → ferredoxin → b6f and back, making ATP only). It uses only the second lift, one photon per electron, and b6f still pumps protons. The chloroplast mixes it in, along with other balancing routes, to reach 3:2. ## Going deeper

### Which chlorophylls make up P680

The lesson treats each reaction-center pigment as one chlorophyll, but they differ. P700 is a chlorophyll pair. P680 is better pictured as a small cluster of four chlorophylls, and the first electron transfer often starts at one of the accessory chlorophylls (ChlD1) rather than at the central pair.

### Chlorophyll, heme and overcooked greens

Chlorophyll is a substituted tetrapyrrole related to heme, but with a magnesium ion at its center instead of iron, a fifth ring fused onto the ring system, one of its four pyrrole rings partly reduced (which makes it, strictly, a chlorin rather than a porphyrin), and a long greasy phytol tail that anchors it in the membrane. Chlorophylls a and b absorb slightly different colors, though together they still leave the green gap. Boil greens too long and they go drab olive: acid swaps the central Mg²⁺ for two protons, converting chlorophyll into pheophytin, a sort of zombie chlorophyll, the same molecule that is PSII's first electron acceptor.

### Carotenoids, sunscreen and autumn

Carotenoids absorb blue-green light chlorophyll misses, widening the range a leaf can use, and act as sunscreen, mopping up dangerous excited states. When light is too intense, certain carotenoids (xanthophylls such as zeaxanthin) work with the protein PsbS in **nonphotochemical quenching (NPQ)**, which dumps excess energy as harmless heat. They were in the leaf all along: autumn removes the masking green and lets the yellows and oranges show; the reds are freshly made anthocyanins.

### The rotor sets the shortfall

The tally's 0.4 ATP shortfall assumes 3 H⁺ per electron and the chloroplast's measured 14-subunit rotor. A 12-subunit rotor would make exactly 3 ATP from 12 H⁺, and linear flow alone would match the Calvin cycle.

### Is "two" a law?

No. It follows from how much each real reaction center loses to its charge separation. The rule predicts the exception: green sulfur bacteria start from sulfide, near −0.25 V, more than 1 V more negative than water and close to NADP⁺, so one lift is enough, and they use a single photosystem.

### Timing of oxygen and the planet it changed

When Photosystem II learned to pry electrons off water is still debated, but it was before oxygen began building up in the air. Oxygen built up only slowly, because iron, sulfur and volcanic gases soaked it up first; the Great Oxidation Event began about 2.4 billion years ago. Some of the iron dissolved in the oceans rusted out as banded iron formations, an ozone layer formed, and aerobic respiration became possible. Oxygen reached near-modern levels only about two billion years after that first rise. The biochemist Albert Szent-Györgyi put the larger picture this way: life runs on a small electric current, kept going by sunlight.

### The evidence that chloroplasts were bacteria

Why two membranes? Because more than a billion years ago a eukaryotic cell engulfed a free-living cyanobacterium and never digested it. The evidence: the double membrane (inherited from the cyanobacterium's own two membranes), the chloroplast's own DNA, and bacterial-style 70S ribosomes inside it. Your own mitochondria carry the same scar from a separate engulfment.
