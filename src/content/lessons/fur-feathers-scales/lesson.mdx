## The big idea

Run a finger along a feather and it feels smooth one way and rough the other. Stroke a fish from head to tail, then tail to head, and the same thing happens. Your child's hands notice these differences before any book does.

Animals have different body coverings: hair or fur, feathers, scales and shells. They also have different **appendages**, the parts that stick out from the body, like legs, wings, fins and tails. These parts do jobs. Feathers are light and help birds fly and stay warm. Scales and fins suit life in water. Fur and legs suit many animals on land. A shell protects a soft body.

Virginia's grade 1 standard asks children to describe these features, use them to guess where an animal lives and how it moves, and group animals by features they choose. It says children do not need to sort animals into groups like reptiles, birds or mammals. Let your child make the rules.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time. An adult handles the raw fish and keeps it on its plate or tray.
- Use craft-store feathers only. Wild feathers can carry germs, and federal law protects the feathers of most native wild birds, even ones found on the ground.
- Raw fish can carry germs. Do not let anyone touch their face or mouth while it is out, and keep it out of reach of younger brothers and sisters. Afterward, wash hands with soap and water for 20 seconds and wash the tray and table with soap and water. If anyone in the room has a fish allergy, use a picture instead, because touching fish can set off a reaction in some people.
- Pet fur can set off allergies, so use fake fur if anyone is allergic. Wash hands after touching pet fur.
- Check shells for sharp or broken edges before your child holds them. Keep small shells away from children under 4, who may put them in their mouths.
- On a beach, leave any shell that has an animal inside, and put living animals back where you found them.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Set the table.** Lay a feather, the fur, the fish on its plate and a shell on the tray, a hand's width apart. Keep the pictures face down for now.
2. **Touch and describe.** Your child touches each covering (you hold the fish) and finds two words for it: soft, hard, smooth, rough, slimy, light, bumpy. Write their words down.
3. **Look closer.** Gently pull a feather's edge apart, then smooth it back with your fingers. The parts hook together again. Stroke the fish's scales from head to tail, then backward. Ask why scales might point toward the tail. With a magnifier, look for rings on a shell.
4. **Predict, then drip.** Ask, "Which one will stay driest if it gets wet?" Then put three drops of water on the feather and three on the fur. Water often beads up on a feather. Fur varies: water may bead up on it or soak in, and craft feathers vary too.
5. **Match to animals.** Turn the pictures over. For each covering, your child finds the animals that wear it. Ask, "Does this animal live on land or in water? How does it move: walk, crawl, fly or swim?"
6. **Look at the parts that stick out.** Point to legs, wings, fins and tails in the pictures. Ask what each one is for.
7. **Sort three ways.** Your child sorts the pictures by covering, then by number of legs (0, 2, 4, 6 or 8), then by how the animal moves. Let them choose a fourth rule of their own.
8. **Clean up.** Wrap the fish and put it back in the fridge within 2 hours of taking it out (1 hour if the room is above 90 °F), or throw it away. Wash hands with soap and water, and wash the tray and table with soap and water.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder which covering would keep an animal warmest. Which would you want on a cold day?"
- "What do you think is under the slime on a fish?"
- "This animal has fins. Do you think it lives on land or in water? Why?"
- "The bat flies. Does it have feathers? What does it have?"
- "What do you think would happen if a turtle tried to take off its shell?"
- "Which animal was hardest to sort? Why?"

## Why it works

Each covering is built for different jobs. A flat outer feather has a central shaft with rows of branches, and tiny hooks hold the branches together into one smooth, flat surface. That makes a light, strong surface for flying, and fluffy down feathers underneath trap warm air. Fur also traps air near the skin. Most fish have thin, overlapping scales covered in mucus, which protects against infection and helps the fish slip through water. A turtle's shell is part of its skeleton. The top half is joined to its ribs and backbone, and the bottom half is made of other flat bones.

Touching the real thing lets your child find features to sort by, which is what grade 1 asks for. Some animals are puzzles on purpose. A frog has bare, damp skin, with no fur, feathers, scales or shell. A snake has scales but no legs. Those puzzles show that grouping animals depends on which feature you pick.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use three coverings (feather, fur, shell) and eight pictures. Sort only by covering, then only by land or water. Your child can point and say instead of writing. Keep the fish for the end, or show a photo of fish scales instead.

### Ages 8 to 10
Add a fourth sort by appendages: wings, fins, legs or none. Your child makes a table with each animal's covering, legs, home and way of moving. Then they write two yes-or-no questions that split the pictures into groups, such as "Does it have feathers?" That is the first step toward a key.

## If it doesn't go as planned

Some grocery fish come with the scales already scraped off. Ask at the counter for a piece with scales left on, or look for skin-on salmon, which sometimes keeps its small scales (run a finger along the skin to check). A clear close-up photo works too. If water soaks right into your craft feather, say so. Dyed feathers sometimes behave differently from a bird's own feathers.
