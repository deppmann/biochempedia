## The big idea

A food web is made of overlapping food chains. Your child can sort living things, predict which cards are linked, and follow the yarn to check.

> **Say it together:** "Plants use sunlight, air, and water to make their own food. The first arrow shows sunlight reaching grass or algae. After that, each arrow points from food to the living thing that eats it."

Children already know everyday meals. A bird pulls up a worm. A cat stalks a mouse. This activity turns those scenes into a chain and then an overlapping **food web**.

Every chain here starts with the sun. **Producers** are living things, like grass and algae, that make their own food using sunlight. **Consumers** eat other living things: some eat plants, some eat animals, and some eat both. **Decomposers**, such as mushrooms and bacteria, break down dead plants and animals.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time. You do all cutting with the scissors and put them out of reach afterward.
- Never put yarn around anyone’s neck or wrap it tightly around fingers or wrists. Hold it loosely in an open hand, keep tugs gentle, and do not walk while holding yarn. Hand the ball over instead of throwing it, and clear yarn and loose cards before anyone moves.
- Keep yarn, string, tape, paper clips, marker caps, and small crayon pieces away from children under 4. These can be choking hazards. Yarn and string can also wrap around the neck. **Call 911 if choking.** Wind up the yarn and put the supplies away afterward.
- Outdoors: choose a spot with permission away from roads, check the weather, wear sun protection, and check for ticks afterward.
- Never taste or eat a wild plant, berry, or mushroom, even one you drew. Wash hands with soap and water after touching any of them. If someone swallows one, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222. Watch real snakes, other wildlife, and stinging or biting insects from a few big steps away. Leave them where you find them. Keep your hands off, and wash them with soap and water afterward.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Make the land cards.** Draw six cards: sun, grass, grasshopper, frog, snake, and hawk. Write each name under the picture.
2. **Work one pair first, then line up the chain.** Lay down only grass and grasshopper. Ask, *"Where does the grass go when the grasshopper eats it?"* Place a paper arrow pointing to the grasshopper. Your child will notice the arrow points *into* the eater. Then ask, *"Who eats whom next?"* Let your child finish lining up sun → grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk. Draw paper arrows together and put one between each pair.
3. **Make the Bay chain.** Draw sun, **phytoplankton** (tiny floating algae using sunlight), **zooplankton** (tiny floating animals), menhaden (a small fish that strains plankton from the water), striped bass, and osprey. Line up sun → phytoplankton → zooplankton → menhaden. Ask, *"What if two animals eat the same fish?"* Your child will notice two arrows split from menhaden: one to striped bass and one to osprey.
4. **Predict before building the web.** Add five cards: rabbit, mouse, fox, mushroom, and earthworm. Ask, *"Who eats the rabbit? What happens to a dead leaf?"* Let your child guess first.
5. **Build the yarn web.** Each person holds one to three land cards. Start at grass and pass yarn from food to eater: grass to grasshopper, rabbit, or mouse (mice eat seeds); grasshopper to frog; frog to snake or hawk; mouse to snake, fox, or hawk; rabbit to fox or hawk. Last, run yarn from living cards to mushroom and earthworm, since every living thing dies in the end. Your child will notice several strands meet in one hand. With a class, give each child one card in a circle.
6. **Predict, then pull one out.** Ask, *"What do you think will happen if the snakes are gone? Who will feel the tug?"* The snake holder gives one small tug; anyone who feels it raises a hand. Before you remove the grass card, ask, *"Who loses a food link first? Who might be affected next?"* Then remove it and trace the links together. Your child will see every land animal here depends on plants. Yarn shows who is linked, not whether a group will grow or shrink.
7. **Place the tadpole card.** Draw a tadpole and ask, *"Does a tadpole eat what a frog eats?"* Most tadpoles graze on algae and plant bits in water; adult frogs eat insects and other small animals. Put the tadpole near the producers.
8. **Two-question debrief.** Ask, *"Where did the energy in our web start?"* (The sun.) Then ask, *"Which way does the arrow between grass and grasshopper point, and why?"* (To the grasshopper, because it gets energy from the grass it eats.)

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder where the grass gets its food. Does it eat?"
- "Which way should the arrow point? Where does the food end up?"
- "What do you think will happen to the frogs if all the grasshoppers disappear?"
- "If the hawks were gone, what might happen to the snakes and the mice?"
- "I wonder what eats the osprey’s leftovers, or a fish that dies."
- "Which card do you think everyone needs? Let’s follow the yarn and see."

## Why it works

The cards make energy flow visible. Producers capture energy from sunlight and store it in their bodies. Each eater gets some of that energy, but uses most of it to live and move. Birds and mammals, like the hawk, also use it to stay warm. That is why a meadow holds far more grasshoppers than hawks.

The web also shows that most animals eat more than one thing. A red fox eats mice, rabbits, fruits, and seeds. When one food runs short, a fox can eat more of another. Changes still spread, though, and scientists cannot always predict which way.

The tadpole shows that an animal’s role, its **niche**, can change as it grows. Most tadpoles eat algae and plant material, and some eat other food too. Adult frogs eat mostly insects. In the Bay, the osprey only joins the web part of the year. It arrives in early spring and heads south in late summer.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use only the six land cards and skip the Bay chain. Let your child trace arrows with a finger before using tape. Lay the cards on the floor in a circle and run yarn between them. Ask one question per card: *"What does this one eat?"*

### Ages 8 to 10
Use both chains and all extra cards. Have your child label each living card producer, consumer, or decomposer (the sun is nonliving). Ask them to note which consumers eat only plants, only animals, or both. Play "what if" three times, changing one card each time. Have them write a prediction first, then use the tug test to check links. Remember, yarn shows who is connected, not whether a group will grow or shrink.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If the yarn tangles, lay the cards on a table and connect them with pencil lines or strips of tape instead. If your child gets stuck on arrow direction, act it out: they are the frog, you hand them a paper "grasshopper," and the arrow points to them. In a small family, each person can hold three or four cards.
