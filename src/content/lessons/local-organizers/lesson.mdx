summary: >-
  Hox genes give the hindbrain and spinal cord their addresses, but the front of the neural tube
  needs other instructions. This lesson follows one of them, the isthmic organizer at the
  midbrain-hindbrain boundary, and one idea carries it: a line becomes a source. Two repressors,
  Otx2 and Gbx2, position the line; cells there send out FGF8 and Wnt1; on the simplest reading,
  how much signal a neighbouring cell receives tells it which part to build, and the repressor it
  carries sets which region it can become. Grafts and beads showed the source to be sufficient,
  and mouse and fish mutants in which the boundary genes start and then fade showed it to be
  necessary and, in the standard model, held on by a small loop of genes. Afterwards you can predict the brain of an embryo in which one part of the loop
  is missing, moved or placed somewhere new.

techniques:
  - name: "Quail-chick grafting"
    blurb: >-
      A piece of neural tube is cut from a quail embryo and placed into a chosen spot in a chick
      embryo of similar stage. Quail cells look different from chick cells under the microscope,
      so you can tell whether a new structure is made of donor cells or of host cells that the
      graft has instructed.
  - name: "Protein-soaked bead implantation"
    blurb: >-
      A small bead is soaked in a purified protein and pushed into embryonic tissue, where it
      releases the protein locally. Control beads soaked in buffer alone show what the wound and
      the bead do by themselves. A positive result tests sufficiency.
  - name: "Gene targeting in mice"
    blurb: >-
      A gene is altered in embryonic stem cells, which are then used to make mice carrying the
      change. A knockout removes the gene, a hypomorph reduces it, and a knock-in puts another
      coding sequence under its control. These test necessity and position.
  - name: "In situ hybridization"
    blurb: >-
      A labelled RNA probe binds the messenger RNA of one gene in a fixed embryo, showing where that
      gene is on. Comparing the domains of Otx2, Gbx2, Fgf8 and Engrailed in normal and mutant
      embryos shows where a boundary lies and whether it has moved.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "Which description fits a local organizer such as the isthmus?"
    choices:
      - "A group of cells in the gastrula that induces the entire neural plate"
      - "A cluster of early neurons whose axons are the first to cross a region"
      - "A small signaling center inside the neural tube that patterns its neighbours"
      - "A row of cells that stops every signal from crossing between two regions"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      A local organizer sits within the neural tube after induction and sends signals that give
      nearby tissue its regional identity and polarity. The group of gastrula cells that induces
      the whole neural plate is Spemann's organizer, a different and earlier center. A row of cells
      that only blocks signals would be a barrier; the isthmus does more, since its tissue can
      instruct forebrain cells to become midbrain. Pioneer axons guide later axons but do not set
      the identity of a region.
  - stem: "How do Otx2 and Gbx2 set the position of the midbrain-hindbrain boundary in the mouse?"
    choices:
      - "Each represses the other, so the line falls where the two domains meet"
      - "Each activates the other, so both genes are on across the whole region"
      - "Otx2 alone forms a gradient, and the line falls at one threshold value"
      - "Both are secreted, so they diffuse toward each other and meet midway"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Otx2 is on in the forebrain and midbrain, Gbx2 in the anterior hindbrain, and each represses
      the other, so the interface between the two domains is sharp and marks the boundary. If they
      activated each other they would be on together, not in adjacent domains. A model built on an Otx2
      gradient alone gives Gbx2 no role, yet moving Gbx2 into the midbrain moved the line in the
      mouse experiments. Both are transcription factors that work in the nucleus; they do not diffuse toward each other like secreted
      proteins.
  - stem: "Engrailed is called a 'cousin of Hox'. What does that mean? (Covered in the Going deeper section.)"
    choices:
      - "It encodes a secreted protein that binds the same receptor as Hox proteins"
      - "It encodes a homeodomain protein but lies outside the four Hox clusters"
      - "It sits inside a Hox cluster but is expressed out of the usual order"
      - "It is a Hox gene that was renamed when it was found in the mouse midbrain"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Engrailed encodes a homeodomain transcription factor, so it binds DNA the way Hox proteins
      do, but the gene is not part of a Hox cluster. It is not in a Hox cluster at all, so it
      cannot be one expressed out of order. Neither Engrailed nor Hox proteins are secreted
      ligands that bind receptors; both work mainly in the nucleus. It was named in the fly for a segment defect and was never
      a Hox gene renamed later.
  - stem: "In Wnt1 mutant mice, Engrailed turns on at the boundary, is then lost, and the region disappears. Suppose En1 is now made in the Wnt1 domain from the Wnt1 gene's own control elements in these mutants. What do you predict?"
    choices:
      - "No rescue, since Wnt1 works only by binding FGF receptors at the isthmus"
      - "Rescue of the forebrain only, since Engrailed acts far from its source"
      - "No rescue, since Engrailed has to be made before Wnt1 is ever expressed"
      - "Large rescue, since keeping Engrailed on is a main job of Wnt1 there"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Engrailed starts and is then lost in Wnt1 mutants, which suggests that Wnt1 is needed to
      maintain it, so supplying Engrailed should restore much of the region, as Danielian and
      McMahon found. Wnt1 binds Frizzled receptors, not FGF receptors. Engrailed does not need to
      come before Wnt1; in these mutants it turns on without Wnt1 and fails only later. The
      forebrain is not missing in Wnt1 mutants, and Engrailed is a transcription factor that acts mainly in
      the cells that make it, not at a distance.
  - stem: "Which result shows that FGF8 is sufficient to induce midbrain character?"
    choices:
      - "Mice that make much less Fgf8 lose much of the midbrain and cerebellum"
      - "Zebrafish lacking Fgf8 first switch on boundary genes and then lose them"
      - "An FGF8 bead placed in chick caudal forebrain induces an extra midbrain"
      - "Fgf8 RNA is found in a narrow band just behind the midbrain boundary"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Sufficiency means that adding the factor where it is normally absent produces the structure,
      and the FGF8 bead did that in the chick forebrain. The mouse hypomorph shows necessity: take
      Fgf8 away and the region is lost. The zebrafish result also tests necessity, and shows that
      Fgf8 is needed to maintain the boundary genes more than to start them. Where a gene is
      expressed is a correlation and tests neither.

  - stem: "In the 1996 experiment, an FGF8 bead placed in the chick caudal forebrain made extra midbrain, not cerebellum, although FGF8 is normally made on the cerebellar side of the line. Which explanation fits best?"
    choices:
      - "FGF8 can only make midbrain tissue, wherever it is placed"
      - "The forebrain cells are Otx2-positive and competent, so the bead at this dose gave midbrain"
      - "Midbrain cells migrate from the real midbrain to the bead"
      - "The bead's FGF8 switches Otx2 on in the cells around it"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The bead sat in the caudal diencephalon, part of the forebrain, whose cells carry Otx2.
      Otx2-positive, competent tissue responded as midbrain at this dose and duration, while the
      Gbx2-positive roof of rhombomere 1 normally builds cerebellum. FGF8 cannot be limited to
      midbrain: stronger or longer FGF8 can switch Otx2 off and Gbx2 on, and in later bead
      experiments isthmic and cerebellum-like tissue formed. Migration
      from the real midbrain does not fit the result that Engrailed came on in forebrain tissue
      around the bead, and in the graft experiments the new midbrain was made of host cells
      beside the graft. The idea that FGF8 switches Otx2 on has it backwards: near a strong
      source FGF8 turns Otx2 off, and the diencephalon already carries Otx2.

      The isthmic organizer is a local signaling center at the midbrain-hindbrain boundary. Its
      position is set by the interface between Otx2, expressed in forebrain and midbrain, and Gbx2,
      expressed in anterior hindbrain, which repress each other. Fgf8 is expressed just behind the

  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      Positioning and maintenance are separable. The Otx2-Gbx2 interface fixes where the isthmic
      program runs: forcing Otx2 into the En1 domain shifts the boundary and Fgf8 caudally and
      shrinks the cerebellum, while Gbx2 misexpressed in posterior midbrain shifts it rostrally.

**Push a small bead soaked in one protein, FGF8, into the back of a chick embryo's forebrain, and a second midbrain can grow there, built back to front.**

That result, from 1996, is the end of a story this lesson follows. By the time the neural tube has closed, its cells need to know where along the tube they sit, so that some build forebrain, some midbrain, some hindbrain. Who tells them? And why would one bead build a midbrain that faces the wrong way, with the parts that belong at its back, next to the hindbrain, sitting at its front?

## Beyond the Hox border

In [the lesson on Hox genes and the hindbrain](/lessons/hox-and-hindbrain/) you met one answer. Hox genes, switched on in a nested order along the tube, give each segment of the hindbrain and spinal cord an address. But Hox expression stops near the front of the hindbrain. The midbrain and forebrain have no Hox code at all.

First, the parts. Picture the young tube as a flat strip, front at the left.

| Region, front to back | What it is | What it gives rise to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forebrain | the front of the tube; its back part, the caudal forebrain, is the **diencephalon** | the diencephalon gives, among other parts, the thalamus and prethalamus |
| Midbrain | the next region back; its roof is the **tectum** | on top the tectum (in mammals, the colliculi), below it the tegmentum |
| Isthmus | a narrowing of the tube where midbrain meets hindbrain | the site of the local organizer of this lesson; its own derivatives are small, and the cerebellum arises from the roof just behind |
| Hindbrain | a row of segments called **rhombomeres**, numbered from the front; the front of the hindbrain, including **rhombomere 1**, is the **metencephalon** | the roof of rhombomere 1 gives rise to the cerebellum |

**Predict.** The midbrain still needs an address. Suppose you had to design one for the front of the tube, using a secreted signal instead of a Hox code. Where would the signal come from, and what would a cell have to read to know which part to build?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

One design that works: put a source of signal inside the region you want to pattern, at a fixed place. The signal falls off with distance, so how much a cell receives tells it how far it sits from the source. A cell's own transcription factors then decide what it makes of that signal, so the same signal can mean different things on two sides of the source. Embryos use centers of this kind, called local organizers.

</details>

An organizer, in embryology, is a group of cells that changes what its neighbours become. The first one found was Spemann's organizer, the lip of the newt gastrula that can induce a whole second body axis, nervous system included ([the lesson on the organizer](/lessons/the-organizer/)). A **local organizer** (also called a secondary organizer) is smaller and later: a small signaling center inside the neural plate or tube that patterns the tissue around it. Think of it as a loudspeaker standing at a property line. It does not build the houses, but it tells the houses nearby what to build. The best studied sits at the isthmus.

One idea carries the lesson. It uses three names met properly below: **Otx2** and **Gbx2**, two transcription factors that repress each other, and **FGF8**, a secreted growth factor.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A line becomes a source. Where Otx2 meets Gbx2, the boundary cells become a signaling source, with FGF8 as the chief signal. On the simplest reading, how much signal a neighbouring cell receives tells it which part to build, and the repressor it carries sets which region it can become: midbrain in front; behind, rhombomere 1, whose roof builds the cerebellum.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which tissue and when.** The midbrain and rhombomere 1 while they are being patterned, before the parts take shape. Timed deletions of Fgf8 suggest that structures nearer the isthmus need the signal longer (Sato and Joyner, 2009).
- **Which step.** Where the line falls and which way the parts face. It does not cover how the isthmic genes first come on: they start in a broad region, in the mouse even in embryos lacking both Otx2 and Gbx2 (Li and Joyner, 2001), and the two repressors then refine them into bands at the line. Nor does it cover the border at the line that cells seldom cross, or the exact wiring that keeps the source on.
- **"Distance" is shorthand** for how much signal a cell receives, in level and in duration. And Otx2 and Gbx2 set what tissue can become, not everything it becomes, nor are they fixed: FGF8 feeds back on them (see the bead section).
- **Other organizers** follow related logic with other signals (see the end of the lesson).

## Where the line falls

Start with the address system itself. Two transcription factors divide the front of the neural tube between them. **Otx2** is on in the forebrain and midbrain. **Gbx2** is on in the front of the hindbrain, including rhombomere 1. They do not do everything at the boundary, but they position it: in the neural plate, the isthmus normally forms where their domains meet.

The two are not just neighbours. Each represses the other. A cell that starts with a little more Otx2 makes less Gbx2, which lets it make still more Otx2, and its neighbour across the line does the opposite. So cells near the line come down firmly on one side or the other, and an edge that began blurred, with the two domains briefly overlapping, becomes sharp. (How sharp depends on how strong and how switch-like the repression is; see [Going deeper](#what-makes-the-edge-sharp).)

**Predict.** In a mouse, force Otx2 on in the front of the hindbrain, the territory that would normally carry Gbx2 and build the cerebellum. Where should the line go? What should happen to the cerebellum?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The line should move back with the edge of Otx2, and the cerebellum should shrink, because less tissue is left behind the line. In 1999 Broccoli, Boncinelli and Wurst did this by putting the Otx2 coding sequence into the En1 gene, so that Otx2 was also made in territory that would become anterior hindbrain. The boundary, and the band of Fgf8 that marks it, moved toward the tail, and the cerebellum was smaller. In a paper published alongside it, Millet and colleagues in Alexandra Joyner's laboratory did the reverse. They made Gbx2 in the back of the midbrain, and the boundary moved forward. The line follows the edge of each repressor.

</details>

This is a **move-it test**: keep the players, change where they are, and ask whether the pattern follows. You will meet the same logic, under other names, in [the lesson on the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/), where changing where NGF is made tells permissive from instructive.

## The line becomes a source

A boundary that only separated two regions would be a fence. This one does more. Right at the line, cells switch on genes for secreted signals:

- **Fgf8**, a growth factor made in a band just behind the line, in rhombomere 1.
- **Wnt1**, a signal made in a ring just in front of it, in the back of the midbrain.

Across the line, a broader band of cells makes the transcription factors **Engrailed-1 and -2** and **Pax2 and Pax5**. Engrailed is a homeodomain transcription factor, a cousin of the Hox proteins whose gene sits outside the Hox clusters. It is needed for the midbrain and cerebellum to form. It is graded across the midbrain, highest next to the isthmus, and that gradient later gives the tectum its polarity, a topic for [the lesson on topographic maps](/lessons/topographic-maps/). The gradient fits the idea that the tissue around the line reads something that comes from it, though by itself it does not show that.

So here is the claim: the line is a source, and its neighbours take their identity from it. Two more kinds of experiment test it. A factor is **sufficient** if adding it where it is normally absent produces the structure. It is **necessary** if removing it takes the structure away. You need both: add the source somewhere new, and take it away.

## Add it: a graft, then a bead

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The first sufficiency test used tissue, not molecules. Alvarado-Mallart's group in Paris grafted pieces of young bird brain into new positions, often quail into chick, since quail cells can be told apart from chick cells under the microscope. In 1991 Martínez, Wassef and Alvarado-Mallart reported that metencephalic tissue from just behind the midbrain-hindbrain junction, grafted into the forebrain of a 2-day chick embryo, kept its own identity. The surprise was in the host. Forebrain cells next to the graft turned on Engrailed and later developed as midbrain. The junction could instruct its neighbours.

What was the message? Fgf8 was a candidate, since it is made right at the boundary. In 1996 Philip Crossley, Salvador Martínez and Gail Martin soaked small beads in FGF8 protein and placed them in the diencephalon of chick embryos. The paper reports that FGF8 protein had the same midbrain-inducing effect as isthmic tissue, and that in embryos allowed to develop further an extra midbrain formed.

**Predict.** The bead sits in the diencephalon, in front of the real midbrain, which still has its own isthmus behind it. Which way should the new midbrain face? Which of its parts should sit next to the bead?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It was built in reverse, a mirror image of the normal midbrain: parts that normally lie nearest the isthmus formed nearest the bead, and parts that lie farther forward formed farther away.

One source is enough to reverse the order. In a normal midbrain, the cells that receive the most signal sit at the back, next to the isthmus. Cells next to the bead also receive a lot, so they read "close to the isthmus" and build back-of-midbrain parts; as the bead's signal falls with distance, the parts run toward front-of-midbrain. This holds for signal below the level that reprograms the tissue. Right at a strong source, as at the isthmus itself, tissue takes isthmic character, and the back of the midbrain lies a little farther out.

</details>

The real isthmus adds a second source behind, and a toy model shows what it does. Put the bead at 0 micrometres and the real isthmus at 300, and give each the same steady gradient, C = C0 × e^(−x/λ) with λ = 50 micrometres, where x is the distance from that source. A cell cannot tell which source a molecule came from, so it receives the sum. The numbers are illustrative, not measurements.

| Distance from the bead (µm) | Bead's signal | Real isthmus's signal | Total signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1.00 | 0.00 | 1.00 |
| 50 | 0.37 | 0.01 | 0.37 |
| 100 | 0.14 | 0.02 | 0.15 |
| 150 | 0.05 | 0.05 | 0.10 |
| 200 | 0.02 | 0.14 | 0.15 |
| 250 | 0.01 | 0.37 | 0.37 |
| 300 | 0.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |

The total falls from the bead to the midpoint, then rises toward the real isthmus. The second source does not cause the reversal; it sets where the two midbrains meet, at the point of lowest total signal. With two equal sources that point falls halfway; a weaker bead would move it toward the bead.

One caution. The bead switched on Engrailed-2 in the tissue around it (Crossley and colleagues, 1996), and in later bead experiments also Fgf8 and Wnt1 (Martínez and colleagues, 1999), a sign that it had started an isthmus-like program. The new midbrain was probably patterned by that induced organizer as well as by the bead's own protein, so the result does not show that the bead's gradient alone set the order.

**Predict.** FGF8 is normally made just behind the line, on the side that builds cerebellum. Yet in the 1996 experiment the forebrain tissue around the bead built midbrain, not cerebellum. Why? (Ask which repressor the diencephalon carries.)

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The diencephalon is part of the forebrain, so its cells carry Otx2 and are competent to respond to FGF8. In that Otx2-positive tissue, at the dose and duration used in 1996, the response was midbrain. Otx2 versus Gbx2 is one of several factors, with the form of FGF8, its dose, its duration and the tissue's competence, that decide between midbrain and cerebellum.

A strong or lasting source can reprogram the tissue. In later bead experiments FGF8 switched Otx2 off and Gbx2 on, and isthmus and cerebellum-like tissue formed (Martínez and colleagues, 1999). Later chick misexpression work found that the stronger form, FGF8b, is the one that can turn midbrain roof into cerebellum (Sato, Araki and Nakamura, 2001).

</details>

## Take it away: started, then lost

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Sufficiency is half the case. In the mouse, a gene can be removed by gene targeting: the gene is altered in embryonic stem cells, which are then used to make mice carrying the change. For each signal at the line the question is the same: what happens if you knock it out?

**Wnt1** came first. In 1990 Andrew McMahon and Allan Bradley, and independently Kirk Thomas and Mario Capecchi, reported mice lacking Wnt1. They lose most of the midbrain and much of the cerebellum.

**Predict.** Wnt1 is made in a ring just in front of the line. In Wnt1-null mice, does Engrailed never come on in the region, or does it come on and then fade? Sketch Engrailed against time for normal and mutant embryos, and say what each shape would tell you about Wnt1's job.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Engrailed turns on in the region and is then lost, and the tissue disappears. (Its RNA is seen by in situ hybridization, a labelled probe that stains a gene's RNA where it is made.) Had Engrailed never come on, one possibility would have been that Wnt1 starts the program. It came on, so Wnt1 is not needed to turn Engrailed on; its loss, and the rescue below, say Wnt1 is needed to keep it on. Call this signature **started, then lost**. The same shape returns for neurons in the lesson on the neurotrophic hypothesis: made, then lost.

</details>

If a main job of Wnt1 here is to keep Engrailed on, then supplying Engrailed should bring the region back. In 1996 Paul Danielian and McMahon made En1 from the Wnt1 gene's own control elements in Wnt1 mutants, and early development of the missing midbrain and anterior hindbrain was restored. A signal keeps a transcription factor on, and the transcription factor keeps the territory.

**Fgf8** gives the same signature. In zebrafish, the mutant *acerebellar* carries a broken Fgf8 gene. Its boundary genes switch on and then fade, so in the fish Fgf8 is needed more to maintain the program than to start it. Mice with no Fgf8 fail at gastrulation, so the brain question needs an allele that reduces the gene (a hypomorph) or removes it only in that region (a conditional deletion). Mice that lose Fgf8 early from the region lack the midbrain and cerebellum. With a reduced dose, how much is lost depends on how much Fgf8 is left: milder reductions spare the front of the midbrain and the sides of the cerebellum (Chi and colleagues, 2003).

**Engrailed and Pax.** Removing both Engrailed genes, or both Pax2 and Pax5, takes away more of the region than removing either one alone. Some single losses, such as En2 or Pax5 alone, are milder because a related gene partly covers for them.

## Why the source stays on

Now put the two halves together. Removing Wnt1 in the mouse, or Fgf8 in the fish, does not stop the program from starting; it stops it from lasting, and supplying Engrailed rescues the Wnt1 mutant. So the signals are needed to keep the program running. The standard model, built on other genetic experiments too, adds the reverse arrow: the transcription factors help keep the signals on, so the genes keep one another on and the loop holds once started. Remove a key part, such as Wnt1 or most of Fgf8, and the rest fade. The two results above test only the first arrow.

The parts of the loop work through pathways you have met elsewhere. FGF8 acts through FGF receptors, which are receptor tyrosine kinases ([that lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)), and through the Ras-MAPK relay ([that lesson](/lessons/ras-to-mapk/)); Wnt1 is a secreted Wnt ligand ([that lesson](/lessons/wnt-signaling/)).

### Design problem

Using add it, take it away and move it, predict the brain in each case, and say which kind of test each one is. (a) A mouse that loses Fgf8 early from the region. (b) A mouse in which Otx2 is forced on in the territory that would become anterior hindbrain. (c) A chick embryo with an FGF8 bead in the caudal forebrain.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

(a) Without Fgf8, the loop fails: Engrailed, Wnt1 and Pax2 are not maintained, and the midbrain and cerebellum are lost. This takes it away, so it tests necessity.

(b) The boundary is drawn where Otx2 meets Gbx2. Forcing Otx2 backward pushes Gbx2 back, so the boundary and the Fgf8 band shift toward the tail, and less rhombomere 1 is left to make cerebellum, which shrinks. This is a move-it test, a gain-of-function change. Extending Otx2 is enough to shift the line in this tissue, though it does not show that Otx2 can build an organizer anywhere.

(c) The bead adds FGF8 where it is normally absent. The forebrain tissue around it, which carries Otx2, turns on Engrailed and later forms a midbrain whose order is reversed, with isthmus-like parts next to the bead. This adds it, so it tests sufficiency.

</details>

## Other organizers along the tube

The isthmus is one of several. At the very front of the neural plate, the **anterior neural ridge** expresses Fgf8; in mouse explants, removing it blocked the forebrain gene Foxg1 (also called BF1), and FGF8 protein could induce that gene. In the diencephalon, a thin stripe called the **zona limitans intrathalamica** makes Sonic hedgehog and, in chick experiments, patterns the thalamus on one side and the prethalamus on the other.

Not every organizer works by adding an activating signal. In zebrafish, a small strip of cells at the front edge of the neural plate, the anterior neural boundary, is needed for normal forebrain markers. One of its products, Tlc, dampens Wnt signaling nearby so that the telencephalon can form.

The three principles from the Hox lesson hold here, now in concrete form. Genes switched on at specific places and times: the line where Otx2 meets Gbx2. Graded signals set up the pattern: FGF8 and Wnt1 from that line. ## Going deeper

### Engrailed, a cousin of Hox

Engrailed was named in the fly for a defect in body segments. Its **homeodomain**, the DNA-binding unit it shares with the Hox proteins, is about 60 amino acids long and recognizes short DNA sequences, but the gene is not part of the Hox code. A cousin, not a sibling. The structure of the Engrailed homeodomain on DNA is among the structures for this lesson.

### What makes the edge sharp

In a toy model, along a stretch of tube from x = 0 to 1, Otx2 is made at a rate a = 2(1.2 − x) and Gbx2 at b = 2(0.2 + x), and each represses the other: dO/dt = a/(1 + G^n) − O and dG/dt = b/(1 + O^n) − G. The exponent n, the Hill coefficient, measures how switch-like the repression is. The input alone would take 1.12 of the stretch to shift from 90 to 10 percent Otx2. At steady state, the zone where Otx2's share, O/(O + G), falls from 90 to 10 percent narrows somewhat with n = 1 (0.82 of the stretch) and much more with switch-like repression (0.51 with n = 2, 0.17 with n = 4); stronger repression sharpens it at any n. Each steady state is reached from a start at zero. With n = 4 the system has two stable states in a narrow band at the edge (about 0.44 to 0.56), so a different start can move the switch point within that band. Adding 1.5 to Otx2's production between 0.5 and 0.8, a stand-in for the En1-Otx2 knock-in, moved the point where Otx2's share falls below half (n = 2) from 0.50 to 0.80: the edge follows the repressor. These are model numbers, not measurements. In the embryo the two domains overlap briefly at first.

### A weaker source

What should a partial loss do? Take one steady source with the gradient used above (λ = 50 micrometres), and give three parts three thresholds. The part nearest the source forms where the signal is at least 0.6 of the full source level, the middle part where it lies between 0.25 and 0.6, and the outer part between 0.08 and 0.25. Each column gives how far that part reaches from the source.

| Source strength | Nearest part (0.6 and up) reaches | Middle part (0.25 to 0.6) reaches | Outer part (0.08 to 0.25) reaches |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full | 26 µm | 69 µm | 126 µm |
| Half | lost | 35 µm | 92 µm |
| One fifth | lost | lost | 46 µm |

Two things follow, for this single steady source with λ and the thresholds fixed. The part that needs the most signal goes first. And halving the source pulls every remaining boundary toward it by the same distance, λ × ln 2, about 35 micrometres, not by a fixed fraction. The timed Fgf8 deletions in the mouse point the same way, in time rather than level.

### The chick Otx2 map

In the chick, Millet and colleagues in Alvarado-Mallart's group reported in 1996 that the back edge of Otx2 lies at the midbrain-hindbrain junction.

### Single mutants and strain background

Mice lacking En1 lose most of the colliculi and cerebellum (Wurst, Auerbach and Joyner, 1994), though on one other mouse strain most of the cerebellum is spared, apparently because En2 covers for it. Mice lacking En2 have a milder cerebellar defect. Putting the En2 coding sequence in place of En1's rescues the En1 defect (see the anecdote below). Losing both Pax2 and Pax5 removes the posterior midbrain and cerebellum; each alone has a milder or strain-dependent effect.

### Two forms of FGF8

FGF8b, the form that can induce cerebellum, binds FGF receptors more strongly than FGF8a. The FGF8b structure among this lesson's structures shows the extra receptor contact proposed for its front end.

### The fish anterior neural boundary

Corinne Houart, Monte Westerfield and Stephen Wilson described the fish anterior neural boundary in 1998; moved, its cells can induce forebrain markers. In 2002 Houart, Wilson and colleagues identified Tlc as a secreted relative of the Wnt receptor Frizzled. In the fish, Wnt from behind pushes tissue toward a posterior fate, and Tlc holds it off. The same three principles apply: a place, a signal opposed by another, and transcription factors restricted by the result.

### An open question: a border cells seldom cross

The FGF8 bead induces mirror-image midbrain, so the tissue reads something like distance from the source. Yet the boundary also acts as a lineage restriction that cells of the two sides seldom cross. How the same signal sets graded identity on one side, a fate choice on the other and a cell-sorting border between them is still not fully explained.
