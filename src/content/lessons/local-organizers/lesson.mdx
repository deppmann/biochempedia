**Push a small bead soaked in one protein, FGF8, into the back of a chick embryo's forebrain, and a second midbrain can grow there, built back to front.**

That result, from 1996, is the end of a story this lesson follows. The question behind it is simple. By the time the neural tube has closed, its cells need to know where along the tube they sit, so that some build forebrain, some midbrain, some hindbrain. Who tells them?

## Beyond the Hox border: the brain needs other organizers

In the lesson on Hox genes and the hindbrain you met one answer. Hox genes, switched on in a nested order along the tube, give each segment of the hindbrain and spinal cord an address. But Hox expression stops near the front of the hindbrain. The midbrain and forebrain have no Hox code at all. Beyond the Hox border, other organizers are needed.

An organizer, in embryology, is a group of cells that changes what its neighbours become. The first one found was Spemann's organizer, the lip of the newt gastrula that can induce a whole second body axis, nervous system included. The centers in this lesson are smaller and later. A **local organizer** (also called a secondary organizer) is a small signaling center inside the neural plate or tube that patterns the tissue around it. Think of it as a loudspeaker standing at a property line: it does not build the houses, but it tells the houses nearby what to build.

The best studied sits at the junction between midbrain and hindbrain, at a narrowing of the tube called the **isthmus**. In front of it, the midbrain gives rise to the tectum (in mammals, the colliculi). Just behind it, the first hindbrain segment, rhombomere 1, gives rise to the cerebellum.

Keep the three principles from the Hox lesson in view, because this organizer follows all three. Genes are switched on at specific places and times. Graded signals from opposing sources set up patterns. Those signals restrict transcription factors, which in turn control the genes of each region.

## A line drawn by two repressors, then held by a loop

Two transcription factors decide where the organizer sits. They divide the front of the tube. **Otx2** is on in the forebrain and midbrain. **Gbx2** is on in the front of the hindbrain. Each represses the other, so a cell cannot keep both, and the edge between the two domains becomes sharp. In the chick, Millet and colleagues in Alvarado-Mallart's group reported in 1996 that the back edge of Otx2 lies at this junction.

Is the line really set by where the two meet? Two mouse studies published side by side in 1999 moved it. Broccoli, Boncinelli and Wurst made Otx2 in territory that would become anterior hindbrain; the boundary and Fgf8 moved back and the cerebellum shrank. Millet and colleagues in Joyner's laboratory made Gbx2 in the back of the midbrain; the boundary moved forward. The line follows the edge of each repressor.

These genes first turn on in a broad region, and in the mouse they do so even in embryos lacking both Otx2 and Gbx2. The two repressors then sharpen them into bands at the line, where they keep themselves running:

- **Fgf8**, a secreted growth factor, in a band just behind the boundary. It acts through FGF receptors, which are receptor tyrosine kinases (see [that lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)), and through the Ras-MAPK relay ([that lesson](/lessons/ras-to-mapk/)).
- **Wnt1**, a secreted Wnt ligand, in a ring just in front of the boundary (see [that lesson](/lessons/wnt-signaling/)).
- **Engrailed-1 and -2** and **Pax2 and Pax5**, transcription factors on in a broad band across the boundary.

In the standard model these genes help keep one another on, so the loop holds once started. Remove a key part, such as Wnt1 or most of Fgf8, and the rest fade. Some single losses, such as En2 or Pax5 alone, are milder because a related gene partly covers for them.

Next: engrailed, a cousin of Hox, is a key determinant of neural identity. A cousin, not a sibling. Engrailed was named in the fly for a defect in body segments. Like the Hox genes, it encodes a **homeodomain**, a DNA-binding unit of about 60 amino acids that recognizes short DNA sequences. Unlike them, it does not sit in a Hox cluster and is not part of the Hox code. In the midbrain, Engrailed is graded, highest near the isthmus, and that gradient later tells the tectum which end is which, a topic for the lesson on topographic maps.

## Sufficient: the graft and the bead

The logic for every experiment in this unit has two halves. A factor is **sufficient** if adding it where it is normally absent produces the structure. It is **necessary** if removing it takes the structure away. You need both.

The first sufficiency test used tissue, not molecules. Alvarado-Mallart's group in Paris grafted pieces of young bird brain into new positions, often quail into chick, since quail cells can be told apart from chick cells under the microscope. In 1991 Martínez, Wassef and Alvarado-Mallart reported that metencephalic tissue from just behind the midbrain-hindbrain junction, grafted into the forebrain of a 2-day chick embryo, kept its own identity. The surprise was in the host. Forebrain cells next to the graft turned on Engrailed and later developed as midbrain. The junction could instruct its neighbours.

What was the message? Fgf8 was a candidate, since it is made right at the boundary. In 1996 Philip Crossley, Salvador Martínez and Gail Martin soaked small beads in FGF8 protein and placed them in the caudal forebrain, the diencephalon, of chick embryos. The paper reports that FGF8 protein had the same midbrain-inducing effect as isthmic tissue, and that in embryos allowed to develop further an extra midbrain formed.

The extra midbrain was built in reverse. The parts that normally lie nearest the isthmus formed nearest the bead, and parts that lie farther forward formed farther away, so the new midbrain was a mirror image of the normal one. That is what you expect if cells read their distance from a source. The bead also switched on genes normally expressed in the isthmus in the tissue around it, a sign that it had started the loop rather than only standing in for it.

## Necessary: "What happens if you knock it out?"

In the mouse, a gene can be removed by gene targeting: the gene is altered in embryonic stem cells, which are then used to make mice carrying the change. The question for each gene in the loop is the same, and the answers line up.

- **Wnt1.** In 1990 Andrew McMahon and Allan Bradley, and independently Kirk Thomas and Mario Capecchi, reported mice lacking Wnt1. They lack the midbrain and much of the cerebellum. In these mutants Engrailed turns on and is then lost. Making En1 from Wnt1's own control elements rescues early development of the region, so a main job of Wnt1 here is to keep Engrailed on.
- **Engrailed.** Mice lacking En1 lose most of the colliculi and cerebellum (Wurst, Auerbach and Joyner, 1994), though on one other mouse strain most of the cerebellum is spared, apparently because En2 covers for it. Mice lacking En2 have a milder cerebellar defect. Mice lacking both lose more of the region than either single mutant.
- **Pax2 and Pax5.** Losing both genes removes the posterior midbrain and cerebellum; each alone has a milder or strain-dependent effect.
- **Fgf8.** Mice with no Fgf8 fail at gastrulation, so the brain question needs an allele that reduces the gene or removes it only in that region. Mice that make greatly reduced Fgf8, or lose it early from the region, lack midbrain and cerebellum.

In zebrafish, the mutant *acerebellar* carries a broken Fgf8 gene. Its boundary genes switch on and then fade, so in the fish Fgf8 is needed more to maintain the program than to start it.

## Other organizers along the tube

The isthmus is one of several. At the very front of the neural plate, the **anterior neural ridge** expresses Fgf8; in mouse explants, removing it blocked the forebrain gene Foxg1 (also called BF1), and FGF8 protein could induce that gene. In the diencephalon, a thin stripe called the **zona limitans intrathalamica** makes Sonic hedgehog and, in chick experiments, patterns the thalamus on one side and the prethalamus on the other.

Not every organizer adds a signal. In zebrafish, Corinne Houart, Monte Westerfield and Stephen Wilson found in 1998 that a small strip of cells at the front edge of the neural plate, the anterior neural boundary, is needed for normal forebrain markers, and can induce them when moved. In 2002 Houart, Wilson and colleagues identified one of its products, Tlc, a secreted relative of the Wnt receptor Frizzled that dampens Wnt signaling nearby. In the fish, Wnt from behind pushes tissue toward a posterior fate, and Tlc lets the telencephalon form by holding it off. The same three principles apply: a place, a signal opposed by another, and transcription factors restricted by the result.
