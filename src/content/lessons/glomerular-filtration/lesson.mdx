      Carl Ludwig, held that the glomerulus filters plasma and the tubules reabsorb part of the

techniques:
  - name: "Clearance measurement (inulin and PAH)"
    blurb: >-
      Bring a marker to a steady plasma level, collect timed urine, and compute U x V / P.
      Inulin clearance gives GFR; para-aminohippurate (PAH) clearance gives effective renal
      plasma flow, which becomes true plasma flow only after dividing by an extraction that is
      measured with a renal vein sample or assumed.
  - name: "Glomerular micropuncture with servo-nulling pressure measurement"
    blurb: >-
      In rats with surface glomeruli, a fine pipette filled with concentrated saline is placed
      in a capillary or tubule, and a servo pump applies whatever pressure keeps the fluid
      interface at the tip from moving; that pressure equals the pressure in the vessel.
      Combined with timed tubular collections and blood sampled before and after the glomerulus,
      it gives single-nephron GFR, pressures and oncotic pressures.
  - name: "Loop of Henle perfusion with stop-flow pressure"
    blurb: >-
      Perfuse the loop of one nephron from the late proximal tubule at set rates while recording
      early proximal flow or the pressure in a proximal tubule blocked downstream. The fall in
      that pressure as loop flow rises measures the strength of tubuloglomerular feedback.
  - name: "Knockout mice"
    blurb: >-
      Delete one gene, here the adenosine A1 receptor, in every cell and compare the mutants with
      wild-type mice in the same preparation. It tests whether a pathway needs that gene, but
      cannot say in which cell the gene acts, and development may compensate.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "Which two Starling forces favour filtration across the glomerular capillary wall?"
    choices:
      - "Bowman's-space hydrostatic and Bowman's-space oncotic pressure"
      - "Capillary hydrostatic and capillary oncotic pressure"
      - "Capillary hydrostatic and Bowman's-space oncotic pressure"
      - "Capillary oncotic and Bowman's-space hydrostatic pressure"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Hydrostatic pressure in the glomerular capillary pushes fluid out, and the oncotic
      pressure of protein in Bowman's space, normally close to zero, would draw fluid in. The
      pair of capillary oncotic and Bowman's-space hydrostatic pressure is the two opposing
      forces, so it reverses every sign. Pairing capillary hydrostatic with capillary oncotic
      pressure mixes one force for and one against. The two Bowman's-space forces also act in
      opposite directions, so they cannot both favour filtration.
  - stem: "In hydrodynamic models of the glomerular capillary wall, which two structures together supply most of the resistance to water flow?"
    choices:
      - "The endothelial fenestrae and the glycocalyx"
      - "The endothelial fenestrae and the slit diaphragm"
      - "The glycocalyx and the podocyte cell bodies"
      - "The basement membrane and the filtration slits"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Drumond and Deen's (1994) model of the capillary wall found that the endothelium adds
      little resistance to water, while the basement membrane and the filtration slits each
      supply roughly half, with the slit diaphragm the main resistance within the slit. The
      fenestrae are wide pores, so any pair that includes them leaves out one of the two main
      resistances. The share of the glycocalyx is still unresolved, and podocyte cell bodies are
      not in the path water takes through the slits. Size selectivity, in contrast, comes
      mostly from the cell layers.

    choices:

      - "About 900 mL per min"
      - "About 1,000 mL per min"

    rationale: >-
      PAH clearance is effective renal plasma flow. True plasma flow is 600 / 0.90 = 667 mL per
      min, and blood flow is plasma flow divided by the plasma fraction of blood: 667 / (1 -

  - stem: "In the simple two-resistor model of the glomerular circulation, the afferent arteriole constricts while arterial pressure stays constant. Which set of changes follows?"
    choices:
      - "P_GC rises, RPF rises, GFR rises, filtration fraction about unchanged"
      - "P_GC falls, RPF falls, GFR falls, filtration fraction about unchanged"
      - "P_GC rises, RPF falls, GFR rises, filtration fraction rises"
      - "P_GC falls, RPF rises, GFR falls, filtration fraction falls"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Raising upstream resistance lowers flow through both arterioles and lowers the pressure
      left in the glomerulus, so P_GC, RPF and GFR all fall; because GFR and RPF fall roughly
      together, their ratio changes little in the simple model. The set with P_GC rising, RPF
      falling and filtration fraction rising describes mild efferent constriction. Plasma flow
      rising with P_GC falling describes efferent dilation. Everything rising describes afferent
      dilation, the reverse arrow.
  - stem: "In an anesthetized rat, the loop of Henle of one surface nephron is perfused from the late proximal tubule at a higher rate, so more NaCl reaches its macula densa. What happens next in that nephron?"
    choices:
      - "Renin release rises and single-nephron GFR rises"
      - "Afferent arteriole dilates and single-nephron GFR rises"
      - "Efferent arteriole dilates and single-nephron GFR rises"
      - "Afferent arteriole constricts and single-nephron GFR falls"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      High NaCl at the macula densa triggers tubuloglomerular feedback: ATP and adenosine act on
      the afferent arteriole, which constricts, so glomerular pressure and single-nephron GFR
      fall. Afferent dilation with a rise in GFR reverses the arrow; that is the response to low

      dilation is not the feedback response, because feedback acts mainly on the afferent side
      (Carlstrom 2015).

**At rest, an adult human's two kidneys, well under 1% of body mass, receive about a quarter of the blood the heart pumps, and they extract little of its oxygen.**

That flow supplies a filter that passes about 125 mL of plasma a minute in an adult at rest. [The kidney](/lessons/kidney/) traced the nephron and introduced clearance, C = U x V / P. This lesson puts numbers on its pressures, flows and controls.

## Where the filtrate goes: kidney, ureter and bladder

Each kidney has an outer **cortex**, where the glomeruli lie, and an inner **medulla** of loops and collecting ducts, which drain into the **renal pelvis** and the **ureter**. Peristalsis carries urine down the ureter. Each ureter crosses the bladder wall obliquely, so rising bladder pressure presses it shut and limits backflow.

The bladder wall, the **detrusor**, is smooth muscle with features of both single-unit and multi-unit types ([smooth muscle](/lessons/smooth-muscle/)). The **internal urethral sphincter** is smooth muscle at the bladder neck, not under voluntary control (a distinct ring in males; in females the bladder neck and proximal urethra act as a functional sphincter); the **external urethral sphincter** is voluntary skeletal muscle.

During storage, sympathetic fibres in the hypogastric nerve relax the detrusor through beta-3 adrenoceptors and contract the bladder neck through alpha-1 adrenoceptors, while the pudendal nerve keeps the external sphincter contracted. Voiding is a brainstem reflex, worked out mainly in cats and supported in humans by imaging: bladder stretch afferents reach the periaqueductal gray, which relays to the **pontine micturition centre** (Barrington's nucleus). Its output excites sacral preganglionic neurons, conventionally called parasympathetic ([a label now debated](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/)), whose pelvic nerve fibres excite neurons in pelvic and bladder-wall ganglia; these postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine, which contracts the detrusor through muscarinic M3 receptors, while sympathetic and pudendal outflow are inhibited and both sphincters relax. Cortical control of the reflex matures in childhood.

## A capillary that stays at high pressure

Unlike most systemic capillaries ([capillary exchange and lymph](/lessons/capillary-exchange-and-lymph/)), the glomerular capillary sits between two resistances, the afferent and efferent arterioles, and its hydrostatic pressure barely falls along its length.

Four **Starling forces** set net filtration pressure. Glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure (P_GC) and the oncotic pressure in Bowman's space (pi_BS, normally near zero) favour filtration. Bowman's-space hydrostatic pressure (P_BS) and capillary plasma oncotic pressure (pi_GC) oppose it.

> **SNGFR = Kf x (P_GC - P_BS - pi_GC + pi_BS)**, averaged along the capillary

SNGFR is single-nephron GFR. **Kf**, the ultrafiltration coefficient, is filtration per unit net pressure: water permeability times filtering area. The equation treats the wall as almost impermeable to protein. As water leaves and protein stays, pi_GC rises while P_GC stays nearly flat, so net pressure shrinks along the capillary.

The direct numbers come from rats. Brenner, Troy and Daugharty (1971) used Munich-Wistar rats, which have some glomeruli at the kidney surface, and measured P_GC with a servo-nulling micropipette: about 60 cmH2O (about 44 mmHg), roughly half of mean arterial pressure, with net filtration pressure averaging about 14 cmH2O (about 10 mmHg). In hydropenic rats, oncotic pressure typically rose to equal the hydrostatic difference before the efferent end: **filtration pressure equilibrium**. Past that point nothing filters, so more plasma flow lengthens the filtering stretch and SNGFR depends strongly on flow. Human P_GC is inferred rather than measured this way, and whether human glomeruli reach equilibrium is unknown.

The filter has three layers. **Fenestrated endothelium**, with pores about 60 to 100 nm wide covering, in different estimates, a fifth to a half of its surface, is lined by a negatively charged glycocalyx. In hydrodynamic models the endothelium adds little resistance to water flow; the **basement membrane** and the filtration slits, mainly the slit diaphragm, each supply roughly half. Size selectivity comes mostly from the cell layers. **Podocytes** wrap the capillary in foot processes bridged by the **slit diaphragm**. Human genetics implicates it: NPHS1 mutations (nephrin) cause congenital nephrotic syndrome of the Finnish type, and NPHS2 mutations (podocin) segregate with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome, both with heavy protein loss in urine. Basement-membrane charge was long taught as the albumin barrier, but mice with reduced membrane charge did not develop overt proteinuria. Whether charge elsewhere in the barrier, such as the glycocalyx, helps hold back albumin is still debated.

## Clearance measures the whole kidney

Clearance gives the sum over both kidneys, and its logic was worked out in a live dispute: does urine begin as a filtrate that the tubules mostly reabsorb (the line from Carl Ludwig), or do kidney cells secrete it (the line of William Bowman and Rudolf Heidenhain)? Arthur Cushny's 1917 book argued for filtration and reabsorption, and Heidenhain's urea-based argument that about 70 L of filtrate a day would have to be reabsorbed already seemed a large objection.

In 1926 Poul Brandt Rehberg, in August Krogh's Copenhagen laboratory, tested the idea on himself. If a substance is filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted, U x V = GFR x P, and its clearance is the filtration rate. Urea is partly reabsorbed, so it runs low; creatinine is concentrated in urine far more, so it should come closer. Rehberg took a load of creatinine by mouth and measured it in plasma and timed urine hourly. His clearance varied between periods, from about 50 to 200 mL per min, and a 2025 review summarises it as about 180 L of filtrate a day; with 1 to 2 L of urine, about 99% of filtered water must be reabsorbed. That made filtration with massive reabsorption quantitatively workable in one human. It did not show that creatinine is only filtered; Rehberg could not exclude tubular handling, and a 2023 history argues that the high plasma creatinine from his oral load caused tubular secretion that inflated the values. Homer Smith's work led to inulin (Shannon and Smith, 1935) and to para-aminohippurate, PAH, for plasma flow (Smith and colleagues, 1945); see [osmoregulation](/lessons/osmoregulation/).

PAH is filtered and secreted, and one pass removes roughly 90% of it from arterial blood, so its clearance is **effective renal plasma flow (ERPF)**. Dividing by the extraction gives **renal plasma flow (RPF)**; dividing by the plasma fraction of blood gives **renal blood flow (RBF)**. The **filtration fraction (FF)** is the fraction of glomerular plasma flow that is filtered.

> **RPF = C_PAH / E_PAH; RBF = RPF / (1 - Hct); FF = GFR / RPF**

### Worked problem: filtration, flow and a drifting marker

Made-up round numbers, adult human at rest, steady state, both kidneys. Assume inulin is freely filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted, PAH extraction is 0.90 (assumed), hematocrit 0.45 and cardiac output 5.0 L per min.

1. *GFR.* U_inulin = 25 mg per mL, V = 1.0 mL per min, P_inulin = 0.20 mg per mL. C = 25 x 1.0 / 0.20 = 125 mL per min, or 125 x 1,440 = 180 L per day, the size of Rehberg's estimate.
2. *Flows.* U_PAH = 12.5 mg per mL, P_PAH = 0.020 mg per mL. ERPF = 12.5 x 1.0 / 0.020 = 625 mL per min. RPF = 625 / 0.90 = 694 mL per min. RBF = 694 / 0.55 = about 1,260 mL per min, about 25% of cardiac output.
3. *Filtration fraction.* 125 / 694 = 0.18, or 125 / 625 = 0.20 using ERPF.
4. *Along one capillary* (illustrative, human-like). P_GC = 55 mmHg throughout, P_BS = 15, afferent pi_GC = 25, pi_BS = 0 (all mmHg). Afferent end: 55 - 15 - 25 = +15 mmHg. Because 18% of the plasma filters out, the remaining 82% holds the same protein, so protein is concentrated 1 / (1 - 0.18) = 1.22-fold by the efferent end; if oncotic pressure were proportional to concentration, pi_GC = 30.5 mmHg and net pressure = +9.5 mmHg. Real oncotic pressure rises faster than linearly, so this underestimates efferent pi_GC.
5. *Creatinine.* Take production as a constant 1.0 mg per min (illustrative) and creatinine as filtered only. At steady state excretion equals production, so P_cr = 1.0 / 125 = 0.008 mg per mL = 0.8 mg per dL. If GFR halves, P_cr rises over days to 1.6 mg per dL, when excretion again matches production.

But creatinine is also secreted. Proximal tubule cells take it up through OAT2, OCT2 and OCT3 and release it through MATE1 and MATE2-K, so creatinine clearance exceeds GFR, and a drug that blocks those transporters, such as cobicistat, raises plasma creatinine without changing GFR. Creatinine-based estimates also depend on muscle mass, and scaling GFR to 1.73 m2 of body surface is a convention.

## Two resistors in series: afferent and efferent arterioles

Think of a hose with a tap upstream (afferent) and a nozzle downstream (efferent). RBF = (arterial pressure - renal venous pressure) / (afferent + efferent resistance), and P_GC is arterial pressure minus the drop across the afferent arteriole ([resistance, compliance and local flow](/lessons/resistance-compliance-local-flow/)).

- **Afferent constriction:** RPF and P_GC fall, so GFR falls; in the simple model FF changes little. Dilation does the reverse.
- **Efferent constriction:** RPF falls, P_GC and FF rise, and GFR is biphasic. Mild constriction raises GFR slightly. Severe constriction lowers RPF and GFR, because the rise in oncotic pressure outruns the rise in hydrostatic pressure.
- **Efferent dilation:** RPF rises; P_GC, GFR and FF fall.

When perfusion pressure falls, angiotensin II is described as acting preferentially on efferent AT1 receptors, supporting GFR better than plasma flow. Blocking its formation (ACE inhibitors) or receptor (ARBs) dilates the efferent arteriole and can lower GFR, most when perfusion pressure is low. Prostaglandins dilate the afferent arteriole; NSAIDs, which block their synthesis, can let it constrict and lower GFR, most when angiotensin II and other constrictors are high.

## Holding GFR steady: myogenic response and tubuloglomerular feedback

In mammals RBF and GFR stay nearly constant over renal perfusion pressures of about 80 to 180 mmHg: **autoregulation**. Two mechanisms, both acting mainly on the afferent arteriole, do most of it.

In the **myogenic response**, stretch of arteriolar smooth muscle depolarizes it, cytosolic calcium rises and it contracts. Bayliss described the reaction in arteries in 1902 ([smooth muscle](/lessons/smooth-muscle/)); the stretch sensor is still debated.

**Tubuloglomerular feedback** runs through the **macula densa**, a plaque of cells at the end of the thick ascending limb where the tubule touches its own glomerulus. NaCl enters them through NKCC2, the cotransporter loop diuretics block. When NaCl delivery rises, the cells release ATP; adenosine formed from it constricts the afferent arteriole through adenosine A1 receptors (A1 is Gi-coupled; [G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/)), and P_GC and SNGFR fall; in some preparations ATP may also act directly on P2X receptors. When delivery falls, the afferent arteriole dilates and granular cells release renin. A 2015 review credits Goormaghtigh with the juxtaglomerular idea (1937), Thurau and others with the feedback hypothesis in the early 1960s, and the Navar and Schnermann laboratories with demonstrating its role in autoregulation in the 1970s.

{/* DIAGRAM: vascular pole with both arterioles, renin cells and macula densa */}

Sun and colleagues in Schnermann's group (2001) used micropuncture in anesthetized wild-type mice and mice lacking A1 receptors. They perfused the loop of Henle at 0 and then 30 nl per min and recorded proximal stop-flow pressure, which follows P_GC. If A1 receptors carry the signal, stop-flow pressure should fall in wild type only. It fell 6.8 mmHg in wild type and 0.4 mmHg in knockouts; baseline GFR, blood pressure and heart rate did not differ. So A1 receptors are required for the feedback response in these mice, and a mediator bypassing A1 signalling is ruled out in this preparation. It does not show where the adenosine comes from or that feedback sets resting GFR, and tests no human; the knockout is global, and development may compensate.

Diabetes gives a second test. High plasma glucose raises filtered glucose, and the proximal tubule reabsorbs more of it with sodium through SGLT2. Less NaCl reaches the macula densa, the afferent arteriole dilates, and P_GC and GFR rise: **hyperfiltration**. (Whether blunted feedback or other preglomerular dilation dominates is open.) Blocking SGLT2 should reverse the chain. In rats with early streptozotocin diabetes, dapagliflozin raised early distal chloride by about 70% and sharply lowered SNGFR. In Zucker diabetic fatty rats, luseogliflozin reduced afferent dilation without changing efferent diameter, and an A1 antagonist blocked the effect. In adults with type 1 diabetes under clamped glycemia, Cherney and colleagues (2014) measured inulin and PAH clearance before and after eight weeks of empagliflozin; in the high-GFR group GFR fell from about 172 to 139 mL per min per 1.73 m2, ERPF fell and renal vascular resistance rose. That study had no control arm, and clearances cannot say which arteriole changed. SGLT2 at the tubule is in tubular transport and diuretics.

### Design problem

Filtration pressure equilibrium hides Kf. You study one surface glomerulus in an anesthetized, hydropenic Munich-Wistar rat, as Brenner's group did. Pipettes give P_GC and tubule pressure, arterial and efferent arteriolar blood give oncotic pressures, and a timed proximal collection gives SNGFR. Made-up values: SNGFR 30 nl per min, P_GC 45 mmHg, P_BS 10 mmHg, afferent oncotic pressure 19 mmHg, efferent 35 mmHg. What can you say about Kf? Design a way to measure it.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

SNGFR = 0.50 nl per s. The hydrostatic difference is 45 - 10 = 35 mmHg, so net pressure is 16 mmHg at the afferent end and 0 at the efferent end. Equilibrium was reached at an unknown point. If net pressure falls fastest where it is highest, as the usual curves show, mean net pressure is at most about (16 + 0) / 2 = 8 mmHg. So Kf is at least 0.50 / 8 = 0.0625 nl per s per mmHg: a minimum, not a value.

Move the capillary out of equilibrium. Raise glomerular plasma flow, for example with an infusion of rat plasma, until efferent oncotic pressure stays below 35 mmHg, then integrate net pressure along the capillary using a model of oncotic pressure against protein concentration. Illustrative result: SNGFR 0.80 nl per s, net pressure 19 mmHg falling to 7 mmHg, integrated mean 10 mmHg, so Kf = 0.80 / 10 = 0.08 nl per s per mmHg, the value Deen and colleagues (1973) reported. Repeat at a second plasma flow: Kf should not change, and SNGFR's rise with flow should come from mean net pressure, as they found.

Limits: compare the same rats before and after; anesthesia and surgery alter hemodynamics; surface glomeruli may not represent deep nephrons; this is a rat value. Do not infer filtration fraction from the oncotic ratio, because oncotic pressure is not proportional to protein concentration.

</details>
