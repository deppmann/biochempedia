## Explain: how an enzyme speeds up a reaction

<WordBank terms={['metabolism', 'enzyme', 'catalyst', 'substrate', 'active site', 'product', 'activation energy', 'denature', 'optimum', 'pH', 'saturation']} />

Drop a piece of raw potato into hydrogen peroxide, the kind sold in brown bottles at a drugstore, and it fizzes. Hydrogen peroxide breaks down by itself, but slowly. Something in the potato makes it break down fast, and the bubbles are oxygen gas.

> hydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen
>
> 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂ (sped up by catalase)

The something is catalase, found in potato cells, liver cells and other animal cells. Your cells run a huge number of chemical reactions like this one. **Metabolism** is all the chemical reactions in a living thing. Many of them would be far too slow at body temperature without help.

The help comes from **enzymes**: molecules, almost all of them proteins, that speed up specific reactions in living things. An enzyme is a **catalyst**, a substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up.

- The **substrate** is the molecule an enzyme acts on. For catalase it is hydrogen peroxide.
- The substrate fits into the **active site**, a small pocket on the enzyme where the reaction happens.
- The enzyme changes the substrate into **products**, the molecules a reaction makes. For catalase they are water and oxygen.
- The products leave. The enzyme is unchanged and can take in another substrate molecule.

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/enzymes-at-work/enzyme-cycle.webp" width={1200} height={676} keyItems={["enzyme", "active site", "substrate", "products"]} given={[0, 2]} alt="AI-generated illustration: four frames of the same large block with a notch; a smaller block approaches, sits in the notch, splits into two pieces, and the pieces leave. Four numbered circles mark parts to name." caption="A model of one enzyme working again and again: the same enzyme takes in a molecule, changes it, lets the products go and is ready for another." />

The active site's shape and electric charges fit only certain molecules. So each enzyme usually speeds up one reaction, or a few similar ones. Bromelain in pineapple cuts protein chains. Catalase does not touch protein; it splits hydrogen peroxide.

A common model is a lock and key: the substrate is a key that fits one lock, the active site. The model breaks in two places. A real enzyme is not rigid. When the substrate binds, the enzyme often shifts its shape a little to grip it more tightly, which biologists call induced fit. And charge matters as well as shape.

Why does fitting into a pocket make a reaction faster? Picture a ball that must be pushed over a hill before it can roll down the other side. **Activation energy** is the energy needed to get a reaction started, like that hill. An enzyme does not add energy. Its active site holds the substrate in place and, with its shape and charges, steadies the in-between form the substrate passes through as bonds break or form. That lowers the hill. With a lower hill, many more substrate molecules react each second at body temperature. The analogy has a limit: the hill is a graph of energy, not a real place.

A few catalysts in cells are made of RNA instead of protein. You can meet them in Biology II.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** An enzyme binds its substrate at an active site that fits it, lowers the activation energy, and is not used up.</Support>

## Explain: why temperature, pH and substrate matter

An enzyme works only while its active site keeps its shape. Three conditions change how fast it works.

**Temperature.** Warmer molecules move faster, so more of them have enough energy to get over the activation-energy hill. Up to a point, a warmer reaction runs faster. But a protein enzyme is a folded chain held in shape by many weak attractions. Too much heat shakes those attractions apart. To **denature** is for a protein to lose the folded shape it needs to work. A denatured enzyme is not dead. It is a molecule whose active site no longer fits. Heat damage is often permanent, the way a cooked egg white stays white.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/enzymes-at-work/denatured-enzyme.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: on the left a block with a pocket holding a smaller block; on the right the same block bent out of shape after heat, with the smaller block no longer fitting the pocket." caption="Heat does not kill an enzyme. It unfolds it, so the pocket no longer fits the substrate, and the damage is often permanent." />

The **optimum** is the condition at which an enzyme works fastest. Most human enzymes have an optimum near 37 °C, your body temperature. Enzymes from heat-loving microbes can have much higher ones.

This answers half the pineapple puzzle. Fresh pineapple contains bromelain, which cuts gelatin's protein chains so they cannot tangle into a net. Canning heats the fruit enough to denature bromelain, so canned pineapple leaves the gelatin alone.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/enzymes-at-work/pineapple-gelatin.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two bowls of gelatin with pineapple, the fresh one runny and the canned one firm, with zoom insets of bead-string protein chains, cut in the fresh case and tangled in the canned case." caption="Bromelain in fresh pineapple cuts gelatin's protein chains, so they cannot tangle into a net. Canning heats the fruit and denatures bromelain, so the gelatin sets." />

**pH.** **pH** is a scale from 0 to 14 that measures how acidic or basic a solution is; 7 is neutral. Changing the pH changes the electric charges on the active site and the substrate, so they may no longer attract each other. Very acidic or very basic conditions can also unfold the enzyme for good.

| Enzyme | Where it works | Temperature it works at | Best pH |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pepsin | Human stomach | Body temperature, about 37 °C | About 2 (strong acid) |
| Many enzymes inside human cells | Inside cells | Body temperature, about 37 °C | Near 7 |
| Catalase from Geobacillus strain CHB1 | A heat-loving bacterium, tested in a lab | Fastest at 60 to 70 °C | 6 to 7 |

**Substrate concentration.** With a fixed amount of enzyme, adding a little substrate speeds the reaction, because substrate meets active sites more often. Keep adding, and the rate levels off. **Saturation** is the point at which nearly every active site is busy, so more substrate barely raises the rate. Picture a store with four checkout lanes: more shoppers mean more sales, until every cashier is busy. The comparison breaks because cashiers get tired and enzymes do not. Under the same conditions, only more enzyme raises the top rate.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Heat speeds an enzyme up to its optimum, and more heat can denature it; the wrong pH changes its charges or shape; more substrate helps until the active sites are full.</Support>

## Elaborate: an enzyme tested at five temperatures

Scientists made an enzyme called Sfβgly in bacteria, using the gene of the fall armyworm caterpillar, purified it, and measured how active it was at five temperatures. They followed each reaction for a long time and worked out the activity at the 20 minute mark and again at the 60 minute mark. Every other condition stayed the same.

| Minutes into the test | Temperature (°C) | Relative activity (%, mean of 3 tests ± spread) |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 29 | 64 ± 7 |
| 20 | 33 | 79 ± 4 |
| 20 | 37 | 87 ± 6 |
| 20 | 42 | 100 ± 10 |
| 20 | 46 | 74 ± 5 |
| 60 | 29 | 74 ± 7 |
| 60 | 33 | 91 ± 3 |
| 60 | 37 | 100 ± 5 |
| 60 | 42 | 86 ± 5 |
| 60 | 46 | 51 ± 2 |

Source: Almeida and Marana, PLOS ONE, 2019, supplementary S1 Table. One lab sample of purified enzyme, with the same substrate concentration (6 mM, millimolar) at pH 6. In each time block the highest value is set to 100%: 42 °C at 20 minutes and 37 °C at 60 minutes. Because the two blocks have different 100% marks, compare temperatures within a block, not numbers across blocks. The rises at 29 to 37 °C between 20 and 60 minutes come from this rescaling; at those temperatures the enzyme's activity stayed steady. This is not catalase, and it is a test-tube measurement, not a field measurement.

1. In the 20-minute tests, which temperature gave the highest activity? What happened between 42 and 46 °C?
2. Each block is scaled to its own best temperature, so do not subtract numbers across the two blocks. Compare 42 °C with 37 °C inside each block instead. Which was higher at 20 minutes, and which at 60 minutes? The enzyme stays stable at 37 °C, so what does the switch tell you happened at 42 °C?
3. Each value is the mean of three tests. What does "± 10" at 42 °C tell you, and why is a mean of three more trustworthy than one test?

### Design problem

Plan a fair test of how temperature changes the rate of catalase from potato. Name your independent and dependent variables, what you keep constant, your control and how many trials you run. Say how you will stay safe.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** How does the temperature of the reacting mixture change the rate at which potato catalase makes oxygen?
- **Independent variable:** temperature, set with water baths your teacher approves (for example, several baths from cold to hot), measured with a thermometer in the tube.
- **Dependent variable:** the rate, as millilitres of oxygen collected per minute over the first few minutes.
- **Constants:** one batch of potato extract, the same volume of extract, the same strength and volume of hydrogen peroxide, the same pH, the same equipment, and the same warm-up time in the bath for both liquids before mixing.
- **Control:** at every temperature, a tube of hydrogen peroxide with water instead of extract. It shows how fast peroxide breaks down with no enzyme.
- **Trials:** at least three fresh tubes at each temperature, in a mixed-up order. Work out each rate, then the mean, and report the spread.
- **Safety:** goggles, a peroxide strength your teacher approves, and care with hot water.

If the rate rises and then falls, the fall shows lower activity. It does not prove the enzyme denatured. To test lasting damage, heat extract samples first, cool them all to one temperature, and measure them there.

</details>
