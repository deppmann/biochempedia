summary: >-
  Neighbouring cells in the retina connect to neighbouring cells in the brain, so the eye's image
  is laid out in order on its target. How does a retinal axon know where to stop? One rule carries
  the lesson: an axon's place is a rank set against a slope. You follow it from Sperry's rotated
  newt eyes and his idea of chemoaffinity, through the stripe assay and the first ephrins, to mouse
  knockouts and a knock-in that showed an axon's position cannot be read from its own receptor
  level alone, and on to the fine sharpening by correlated retinal activity that the next unit
  takes up. Afterwards you can predict how a map changes when a ligand gradient or a receptor level
  is altered, and say what gradients cannot do alone.

scientists:
  - name: "Roger W. Sperry"
    contribution: >-
      Rotated the eyes of adult newts and let the cut optic nerve regrow. The animals' visual
      responses came back reversed and were never corrected, so he proposed that retinal fibres
      and their targets carry matching chemical differences, an idea he set out in full as chemoaffinity in 1963.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Sperry, J. Comp. Neurol. 79:33 (1943); Sperry, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 50:703 (1963); Nobel Foundation, Roger W. Sperry: Biographical (1981)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Sperry trained under Paul Weiss, whose work suggested that a nerve's function could be
      matched to its target after growth. In 1943 Sperry published two papers on adult
      red-spotted newts, Triturus viridescens. In one he turned an eye 180 degrees in its socket
      and left the optic nerve intact. In the other he also cut the optic nerve and let it
      regenerate, which adult newts can do. Once sight returned, the animals behaved as if the
      world were upside down and reversed: a lure held above the head made the animal turn its head downward.
      The error did not improve with experience for as long as the animals were watched, up to 50 days after sight returned. Because the regrown fibres
      had rebuilt the old, now useless, map, Sperry argued that each fibre carries a property
      tied to its retinal position and finds the matching place in the brain. In 1963 he gathered
      this into the chemoaffinity hypothesis, with crossing gradients as a way to supply
      coordinates. He shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his separate work
      on the divided cerebral hemispheres. His newts made it reasonable to search for molecules
      that label position.
  - name: "Friedrich Bonhoeffer"
    dates: "1932–2021"
    contribution: >-
      With Jochen Walter and colleagues built the stripe assay, in which temporal retinal axons
      chose anterior over posterior tectal membranes, and with Uwe Drescher and colleagues
      purified RAGS, the posterior tectal repellent now called ephrin-A5.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Walter, Kern-Veits, Huf, Stolze & Bonhoeffer, Development 101:685 (1987); Drescher et al., Cell 82:359 (1995)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Sperry's idea needed an assay in which a growing axon could be asked, again and again, which
      of two surfaces it preferred. Bonhoeffer's group made one. In 1987 Jochen Walter, Brigitte
      Kern-Veits, Julita Huf, Bernd Stolze and Bonhoeffer laid membranes from the anterior and
      posterior chick optic tectum in alternating narrow lanes, a striped carpet, and placed a
      strip of retina across them. Axons from temporal retina grew along the anterior lanes and
      avoided the posterior ones. Axons from nasal retina showed no preference. On either
      membrane alone, both kinds grew. A companion paper by Walter, Sigrid Henke-Fahle and
      Bonhoeffer found that heating the posterior membranes abolished the avoidance, which pointed
      to a heat-sensitive repellent at the back of the tectum. The assay then served as a guide
      for purification. In 1995 Uwe Drescher and colleagues in Bonhoeffer's laboratory reported a
      25 kilodalton posterior tectal protein, RAGS, that collapsed and repelled retinal growth
      cones in culture. It is now called ephrin-A5, and the stripe assay became a standard test
      for guidance molecules.
  - name: "John G. Flanagan"
    contribution: >-
      With Hwai-Jong Cheng and colleagues found complementary gradients of the ligand ELF-1
      (ephrin-A2) in the chick tectum and its receptor Mek4 (EphA3) in the retina, and with David
      Feldheim, Jonas Frisén and colleagues showed that mice lacking ephrin-A2 and ephrin-A5 lose
      much of their retinocollicular map.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Cheng, Nakamoto, Bergemann & Flanagan, Cell 82:371 (1995); Feldheim et al., Neuron 25:563 (2000)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Flanagan's route to the map was a reagent he had helped devise with Philip Leder in 1990. His laboratory fused the binding part of a
      receptor or ligand to alkaline phosphatase, an enzyme that turns a colourless substrate
      coloured, so that wherever the probe bound, the tissue stained. In 1995 Hwai-Jong Cheng,
      Masaru Nakamoto, Andrew Bergemann and Flanagan used such probes in the chick. RNA for an
      Eph-family ligand, ELF-1, rose from anterior to posterior across the optic tectum, and
      binding sites for its receptor followed the same pattern. RNA for the receptor Mek4 ran the
      opposite way across the retina, high in temporal and low in nasal ganglion cells. Probes made from the two proteins also showed complementary gradients of binding. The paper appeared next to the RAGS paper
      in the same issue of Cell. In 2000, with David Feldheim, Jonas Frisén and others, the
      laboratory disrupted the genes for ephrin-A2 and ephrin-A5 in mice. Axons from one small
      patch of retina then ended at several scattered places in the superior colliculus. That
      result showed the ephrins are required for a normal map, though the knockout removed them
      from the retina as well as the target.
  - name: "Greg Lemke"
    contribution: >-
      With Arthur Brown, Paul Yates, Dennis O'Leary and colleagues gave a scattered half of mouse
      retinal ganglion cells extra EphA3 and found two maps, showing that an axon's position
      depends on its EphA signalling relative to other axons', not on its own level alone.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Brown et al., Cell 102:77 (2000); Reber, Burrola & Lemke, Nature 431:847 (2004)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      A gradient can be read in two ways. An axon could stop where the ligand reaches a fixed
      value for its own receptor level, or it could compare its receptor level with that of its
      neighbours. In 2000 Arthur Brown and Paul Yates, with Patrick Burrola, Dan Ortuño, Ashish
      Vaidya, Thomas Jessell, Samuel Pfaff, Dennis O'Leary and Lemke, tested the two. They placed
      the coding sequence of EphA3, a receptor that mouse retinal ganglion cells do not normally
      make, under the control of the gene Isl2, which is active in about half of these cells,
      scattered across the retina. Neighbouring cells at the same retinal position now differed in
      EphA level. Tracing showed two orderly maps in the superior colliculus: the cells with extra
      EphA3 in the anterior part, their neighbours pushed into the posterior part. The title gave
      the conclusion: relative, not absolute, levels. In 2004 Michael Reber, Burrola and Lemke
      combined this allele with an EphA4 knockout and fitted a relative signalling model, which
      allowed quantitative predictions of how a map shifts when receptor levels change. Ephrin
      dosage results showed that the amount of ligand counts too, so current models combine the two.

techniques:
  - name: "Stripe assay"
    blurb: >-
      Membranes from two regions of a target are laid down in alternating lanes, and a strip of
      neural tissue is placed across them. If axons grow along one kind of lane, the two regions
      differ in a cue the axons can read. Treating one membrane (by heat or an enzyme) first shows
      whether the cue is an attractant on one or a repellent on the other.
  - name: "Alkaline phosphatase fusion probes"
    blurb: >-
      The extracellular part of a receptor or ligand is joined to an enzyme that makes a coloured
      product. Applied to tissue, the probe stains wherever its binding partner sits, so a
      gradient of binding sites can be seen directly.
  - name: "Anterograde axon tracing with DiI"
    blurb: >-
      A small crystal or injection of a fluorescent lipid dye is placed in one patch of retina.
      The dye spreads along the membranes of those axons to their endings, showing where that
      patch terminates in the tectum or superior colliculus.
  - name: "Knock-in and knockout mice"
    blurb: >-
      A knockout disables a gene, usually by deleting or interrupting part of it. A knock-in inserts a chosen sequence at a targeted gene; this one placed a coding sequence under the control of
      another gene, so it is made only where that gene is active. Here a knock-in gave half of the
      ganglion cells extra EphA3 while their neighbours kept normal levels.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "In the chick, axons from the temporal retina normally end in which part of the optic tectum?"
    choices:
      - "The medial tectum, where ephrin-B is highest"
      - "The anterior tectum, where ephrin-A is lowest"
      - "The posterior tectum, where ephrin-A is highest"
      - "The entire tectum, spread evenly at first"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Temporal retina maps to anterior tectum, where ephrin-A ligands are lowest, and temporal
      axons carry the most EphA receptor, so their endings form before the repellent becomes strong. The
      posterior tectum receives nasal axons, which carry little EphA. The medial tectum is set
      along the other axis and receives ventral retina. Temporal axons do first grow past this zone into
      posterior tectum, but their branches form mainly in the right place and the overshooting parts
      are removed, so the endings are never spread evenly over the whole tectum.
  - stem: "In mice lacking the beta2 subunit of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, retinal waves are abnormal. What happens to the retinal axons in the superior colliculus?"
    choices:
      - "They reach roughly the right region, but their termination zones stay large and diffuse"
      - "They fail to enter the colliculus at all"
      - "They reach exactly the right place and form termination zones as sharp as in normal mice"
      - "They grow to the wrong end of the colliculus, with the map reversed"
    answer: 0
    rationale: "In these mice the axons reach roughly the right region, so molecular gradients have done their work, but the termination zones stay large and diffuse, so correlated activity is needed to sharpen the map. They do enter the colliculus, so they are not blocked from it. Sharp zones would mean activity was not needed. The map is not reversed, since the gradients are intact."
  - stem: "Sperry rotated an adult newt's eye, cut the optic nerve and let it regrow. Once sight returned the newt aimed in the wrong direction for weeks. Which idea did this argue against most directly?"
    choices:
      - "That connections form at random and are then matched by use"
      - "That retinal axons carry chemical labels tied to their position"
      - "That retinal axons in newts end in the midbrain optic tectum"
      - "That newts can see moving objects after the optic nerve regrows"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      If connections formed at random and were matched by use, weeks of missed prey should have
      corrected the error; the persistent error supported position-specific reconnection rather
      than correction by experience. Position labels are what the result supported, not what it refuted. The newts
      did see and respond to moving lures, so the experiment confirmed recovery of vision. The
      retinal projection to the tectum was the system under study, not a hypothesis it tested.
  - stem: "Why did Sperry's experiment with the optic nerve cut and regrown add something that rotating the eye with the nerve intact could not?"
    choices:
      - "Cutting the nerve undid the eye's rotation, so the lens could be tested"
      - "Regrowing fibres had to find their targets anew and rebuilt the old map"
      - "Cutting the nerve blinded the newt for good, so it could not learn"
      - "Regrowing fibres entered both tecta, so the map was doubled in size"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      With the nerve intact, the old connections were never broken, so a fixed map was no
      surprise. With the nerve cut, every fibre had to grow back and choose an ending, and the
      reversed behaviour suggested that they chose their original places. The newts were not blinded
      for good; vision returned, which is what made testing possible. Cutting the nerve did not
      undo the rotation of the eye. The fibres did not enter both tecta; the map that returned
      was single and inverted.
  - stem: "According to the relative signalling model, what happens if every retinal ganglion cell in a mouse is given the same extra amount of EphA receptor?"
    choices:
      - "The axons spread at random, since the gradient has been erased"
      - "Every axon shifts to the anterior edge of the superior colliculus"
      - "The order is kept, though spacing could change as the ratios between cells change"
      - "The map duplicates, as it does in the Isl2-EphA3 knock-in mouse"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      In the relative signalling model an axon's ending depends on the ratio of its EphA signal to
      that of the axons around it. Adding the same amount to every cell keeps every axon in the same
      order, so the map keeps its order, but the ratios between neighbouring cells shrink, most where
      EphA is low, so spacing could change. An absolute reading would predict that every axon shifts
      anteriorly, piling up at the anterior edge only if the increase were large. Duplication needs two intermingled populations with
      different levels, as in the knock-in, where only half the cells were changed. The retinal
      gradient is not erased, because the endogenous differences between cells remain.

  - stem: "In an experiment modelled on the stripe assay, strips of chick retina grow across alternating lanes of anterior and posterior tectal membrane. Temporal axons put 90% of their length on anterior lanes; nasal axons put 50%. On a single membrane type alone, both grow equally well. When the posterior membranes are heated before the lanes are made, temporal axons put 50% of their length on anterior lanes. Which conclusion fits all the data?"
    choices:
      - "Nasal axons lack every receptor for proteins on tectal membranes"
      - "Anterior membranes carry a heat-stable cue that temporal axons follow"
      - "Posterior membranes cannot support the growth of temporal axons"
      - "Posterior membranes carry a heat-labile cue that temporal axons avoid"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Heating only the posterior membranes removed the temporal axons' preference, so the active
      cue is on the posterior lanes and is destroyed by heat; since temporal axons grow well on
      posterior membrane alone, the cue acts in the choice, as avoidance. A heat-stable attractant
      on anterior membrane would have kept the preference, because the anterior lanes were not
      heated. Posterior membrane does support temporal growth when offered alone. Nasal axons
      grow on both membranes; showing no preference does not mean they lack all receptors.
  - stem: "In a model mouse retina, EphA level rises linearly from 1 unit in the most nasal ganglion cells to 3 units in the most temporal. Each axon's ending is set by its rank among all axons: the highest level ends at the anterior edge of the colliculus (0% of the axis) and the lowest at the posterior edge (100%), evenly spaced by rank. A knock-in adds 4 units to a random half of the cells. Where does the most temporal axon without the knock-in end?"
    choices:
      - "At 25%, within the anterior half"
      - "At 0%, the anterior edge of the axis"
      - "At 100%, the posterior edge"
      - "At 50%, the middle of the axis"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Knock-in cells now range from 5 to 7 units, all above the unchanged cells at 1 to 3, so the
      knock-in half takes the top ranks and fills 0 to 50%, and the unchanged half fills 50 to
      100%. The most temporal unchanged axon has the highest rank in its half, so it ends at
      about 50%. The anterior edge is where it ends in a normal mouse or under an absolute rule,
      which ignores the neighbours. The 25% answer assumes the two populations interleave
      rank by rank. The posterior edge belongs to the most nasal unchanged axon.
  - stem: "DiI is placed in a small patch of temporal retina. In wild-type mice the label forms one dense zone in the anterior superior colliculus. In ephrin-A2/ephrin-A5 double knockouts, the label forms three zones spread along the anterior-posterior axis, one at the normal site.     choices:
      - "The retina loses its EphA gradient when the ephrins are missing"
      - "The ephrins act as a switch that fails only when both genes are gone"
      - "Graded ephrin levels set position, and lowering them makes mapping less accurate"
      - "The ephrins are needed for retinal axons to reach the colliculus"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Axons still reach the colliculus in every genotype, but with less ephrin some of their

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm five"
    text: >-
      Your eye sends wires to your brain, and the wires keep their places, like seats in a
      theatre. Each wire wears a badge with a number. The brain has a slope of a "keep out"
      signal, weak at the front and strong at the back. Wires with big numbers mind the signal a
      lot and stop near the front, and wires with small numbers go further back.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Cells next to each other in the retina send axons to cells next to each other in the brain,
      so the picture on the retina is copied in order. Roger Sperry turned newts' eyes upside
      down, cut the optic nerve, and let it regrow. The newts then struck in the wrong direction
      and never learned to correct it, supporting Sperry's interpretation that regrowing axons
      find position-specific targets. He proposed chemical labels; later experiments identified
      molecular guidance gradients. The labels turned out to be gradients of proteins. Retinal cells carry different amounts of
      a receptor called EphA, and the brain target carries a gradient of a repellent called
      ephrin-A. Axons with more receptor are pushed away sooner, so they end nearer the front.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      In the retinotectal projection, temporal retina maps to anterior tectum (superior
      colliculus in mammals) and nasal retina to posterior. EphA receptor tyrosine kinases are
      graded high temporal to low nasal across retinal ganglion cells, and the GPI-anchored ligands
      ephrin-A2 and ephrin-A5 are graded low anterior to high posterior in the target. Forward
      signalling through EphA is repulsive, so axons with high receptor end at low ligand; in chick
      and mouse this works mainly by controlling where axons branch after they overshoot. The stripe assay showed temporal axons avoiding posterior membranes, the
      ligands were cloned in 1994 and 1995, and the ephrin-A2/A5 double knockout mouse showed they are
      required, since its map loses much of its anterior-posterior order. The Isl2-EphA3 knock-in
      showed that an axon's position cannot be read from its own EphA level alone: it depends on
      its signalling relative to other axons, while the extra, misplaced termination zones in
      ephrin-A2/A5 compound heterozygotes show that ligand amount counts too. How axons compare themselves is not
      settled. EphB receptors and ephrin-B ligands contribute to
      the other axis, and activity refines the map, partly while the molecular cues are still acting.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      A single repulsive gradient cannot by itself produce a complete map: if every axon is
      repelled by ephrin-A and simply moves away from it, with nothing pushing back, all should
      pile up anteriorly, with temporal axons farthest forward. (A fixed stopping threshold would
      spread them out, but it fails the Isl2-EphA3 knock-in, where unchanged axons move.)
      Counter-gradients help. Ephrin-As are also expressed in the retina, high nasally, and EphAs
      in the tectum, high anteriorly, and ephrin-A reverse signalling in axons has been proposed
      to supply a posterior push. Competition among axons probably supplies much of the rest: the knock-in data, fitted by the
      relative signalling model of Reber, Burrola and Lemke (2004), behave as if each axon's
      endpoint depends on the ratio of its net EphA signal to that of the axons
      around it, so the map is a solution that all axons reach together, set against a ligand
      gradient whose absolute level also matters. Interstitial branching
      complicates the picture, because in chick and mouse the map forms by overshoot and
      topographically biased branching, not by growth cones stopping at the right place.

      An open question is how relative comparison is implemented. Axon-axon interactions
      through EphA and ephrin-A on neighbouring axons, competition for a limited trophic or
      synaptic resource, and activity-dependent stabilisation could each produce it, and they are
      not mutually exclusive. Which dominates, and whether it differs between fish, chick and
      mouse, is not settled.

anecdotes:
  - title: "Two papers, one issue, one answer"
    source: "Drescher et al., Cell 82:359 (1995); Cheng, Nakamoto, Bergemann & Flanagan, Cell 82:371 (1995)."
    text: >-
      In August 1995 the journal Cell printed two papers back to back. Uwe Drescher and
      colleagues in Friedrich Bonhoeffer's laboratory in Germany had started from the stripe
      assay and purified a 25 kilodalton repellent from posterior chick tectum. Hwai-Jong Cheng
      and colleagues in John Flanagan's laboratory in Boston had started from a ligand of the Eph
      family and asked where it, and its receptor, were found. Both arrived at relatives of the
      same ligand family, graded across the same tectum. The two names, RAGS and ELF-1, became
      ephrin-A5 and ephrin-A2 under the shared nomenclature of 1997.
  - title: "Worse with sight than without"
    source: "Sperry, J. Comp. Neurol. 79:33 (1943)."
    text: >-
      In one group of Sperry's newts, the eye was rotated, the optic nerve cut, and the other eye
      removed, so each animal was completely blind while the nerve regrew. Blind, they moved
      about the tank using their other senses. When sight came back through the rotated eye, they
      circled and bumped into things more than they had while blind. The restored map supplied
      precise information pointing the wrong way, and in this animal, at least, it could not be
      overridden by experience.
  - title: "A gradient that did not need the eye"
    source: "Walter, Kern-Veits, Huf, Stolze & Bonhoeffer, Development 101:685 (1987)."
    text: >-
      If retinal axons were what put the label into the tectum, the gradient would be a record of
      innervation, not an instruction for it. Walter and colleagues tested this with chick
      embryos grown without retinal innervation of the tectum.
      Membranes from these tecta still separated temporal axons into stripes. The target had
      built its gradient by itself, as chemoaffinity requires. The same paper found that the
      difference between anterior and posterior membranes faded with age in the chick embryo.

**Turn an adult newt's eye upside down in its socket, cut the optic nerve, and let it regrow. When the newt can see again and you hold a lure above its head, it turns its head downward. It keeps making that mistake for as long as anyone watched.**

The newt is not blind and not confused. Its vision has come back in its original order, only rotated, as if the image had been flipped before it reached the brain. So the question for this lesson is: **how does an axon know where to stop?**

## The map, and three ways to build it

The retina sends its axons to a target in the midbrain called the optic tectum in fish, amphibians and birds, and the superior colliculus in mammals. The projection is a **topographic map**: neighbouring ganglion cells in the retina connect to neighbouring places in the target, so the image on the retina is laid out in order on the brain.

The map has two axes. This lesson follows one of them and states it the same way throughout: **temporal retina maps to the anterior target, nasal retina to the posterior target.** (The temporal retina is the side nearer the temple.) The second axis runs at right angles: dorsal retina maps to the lateral tectum, ventral retina to the medial tectum.

One word needs care. In chick and mouse the main axon does not simply halt at the right place. It first grows past it, then sprouts side branches there. So in this lesson, where an axon "stops" means where it keeps its branches, its **termination zone**.

A growth cone (the moving tip of a growing axon) can sense only what is close to it, yet the map spans the whole target. There are three ways an axon could know where to stop.

1. **Individual labels.** One matching tag per cell and per target site.
2. **Gradients.** Smooth slopes in the amount of a molecule, which give each position a value.
3. **Neighbours.** Each axon places itself relative to the other retinal axons arriving in the target.

Picture seats in a theatre. Every ticket could name a seat. The lights could dim toward the back, and each person could sit where it is as dark as they like. Or people could line up by height, tallest at the front, and take the seats that order gives them. The rest of the lesson shows that the retina uses the second and third together.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson. An axon's place in the map is a rank set against a slope.** Each retinal axon carries an amount of a receptor, EphA; the target carries a slope of its ligand, ephrin-A, which repels the axon and is low at the front and high at the back (both are introduced below). Rank the axons by how much receptor they carry: the axon with the most ends where the repellent is lowest. Where exactly each axon lands depends both on how much ligand is there and on how its signal compares with the other axons'. In short: more receptor, less ligand, and no axon's place is an address in its own receptor level alone.

**What the rule covers.** The anterior-posterior axis of the coarse map in chick and mouse. It does not cover the other axis, which uses other cues (Going deeper), or the fine scale, where correlated activity sharpens termination zones (the last section, and the next unit). And how much weight falls on the rank differs between preparations: in zebrafish, a retinal axon with no neighbours can still find roughly the right place, so competition among axons is not essential for coarse targeting there.

## Sperry's newts: does the wiring follow the eye or the world?

Roger Sperry put this question to adult red-spotted newts, *Triturus viridescens*, in 1943. An adult newt's optic nerve regenerates, so these were experiments on regrowth, which were then read as a window on how the map is built in the embryo. Many textbooks draw his later frog and goldfish versions, but the 1943 papers used adult newts.

In one paper he rotated the eye 180 degrees and left the optic nerve intact. The newts' responses to moving objects were inverted and reversed, and stayed that way, which proves little, since the old connections were never broken. In the companion paper he also cut the optic nerve. In one group he cut through the nerve's sheath as well and twisted the two stumps out of line, so that fibres could not simply follow old channels back. Vision returned after the nerve regrew.

**Predict.** Suppose regrowing fibres connect more or less at random, and useful function is then matched to them by experience. What should a newt with a rotated eye be doing after weeks of missed prey?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It should have learned to aim correctly. It had not. Animals with a rotated eye aimed in the wrong direction, by an amount that matched the rotation (those with an unrotated eye aimed correctly), and experience did not correct them. The result argued against random wiring matched by use.

</details>

Sperry favoured the simplest reading: the regrown fibres had rebuilt the old map. The behaviour supported position-specific reconnection without proving a molecular mechanism (his adviser Paul Weiss's resonance idea could also fit it; see Going deeper). Sperry proposed that retinal cells acquire chemical differences according to their position and that their targets carry matching differences. He developed this into the **chemoaffinity** hypothesis, set out in full in 1963, and suggested that two crossing gradients, one per axis, could provide the coordinates without a separate label for every cell. He did not have a molecule.

## Is the cue in the target? The stripe assay

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Chemoaffinity predicts that the tectum itself carries a position-dependent cue that retinal axons can read. Friedrich Bonhoeffer's group in Germany built an assay to ask the axons directly. In 1987 Jochen Walter, Bonhoeffer and colleagues made a **striped carpet**: membranes from anterior and posterior chick tectum laid down in alternating narrow lanes. A strip of retina was placed across the lanes so that each growing axon met border after border.

| Axons from | Offered | Where they grew |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temporal retina | Alternating anterior and posterior lanes | Along the anterior lanes, off the posterior ones |
| Nasal retina | The same lanes | On both kinds |
| Either part of the retina | One kind of membrane alone | Well on either kind |

The last row matters: posterior membrane was not simply a poor surface. So either anterior membrane carries something temporal axons follow, or posterior membrane carries something they avoid.

**Predict.** A companion paper heated the posterior membranes before making the lanes. If the cue were an attractant on anterior membrane, what would happen to the temporal axons' choice? If it were a repellent on posterior membrane?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Heating the posterior membranes abolished the temporal axons' avoidance. An attractant on the anterior lanes, which were not heated, would have kept the preference. So the result pointed to a heat-sensitive repellent on posterior membranes rather than an attractant on anterior ones.

</details>

## The molecules: two opposite slopes

The stripe assay also gave biochemists a test to follow during purification. In 1995 two papers appeared back to back in *Cell*. Uwe Drescher, Bonhoeffer and colleagues purified a membrane-anchored protein from the posterior tectum that collapsed and repelled retinal growth cones in culture, now called ephrin-A5. Hwai-Jong Cheng, John Flanagan and colleagues reported that a ligand of the Eph receptor family, now called ephrin-A2, rose in a gradient from anterior to posterior across the chick tectum, while its receptor, now called EphA3, ran the opposite way in the retina, high in temporal ganglion cells and low in nasal ones.

Eph receptors are the largest family of receptor tyrosine kinases ([that lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/) covers how such receptors are switched on). Their ligands, the ephrins, are themselves membrane proteins. Ephrin-As are held to the outer surface by a GPI anchor (a lipid tail) and bind mainly EphA receptors; ephrin-Bs cross the membrane and bind mainly EphB receptors. Because both partners sit on cell surfaces, signalling needs contact, and it can run both ways: the receptor signals forward into its own cell, and the ephrin can signal in reverse into the cell that carries it (Egea and Klein reviewed this bidirectional signalling in 2007; [that lesson](/lessons/reverse-signaling/) explains how).

### Why more receptor means less ligand

Build the pairing of opposite slopes with toy numbers, chosen to make the arithmetic easy, not measured.

- Let retinal position *t* run from 0 at the nasal pole to 1 at the temporal pole, with equal numbers of ganglion cells in equal intervals of *t*. EphA rises in a straight line, 1 + 2*t*: from 1 unit in the most nasal ganglion cells to 3 in the most temporal. These are toy assumptions, not measured receptor profiles or cell densities.
- Ephrin-A rises in a straight line from 1 at the front edge of the target to 3 at the back edge.
- An axon enters at the front and grows back. The repulsion it feels is its EphA level times the local ephrin-A (more receptor or more ligand, more push). It keeps its branches where that product reaches a limit of 3. An axon whose limit falls outside the target is drawn at the nearest edge.

**Predict.** Three axons carry 1, 2 and 3 units of EphA. At what ephrin-A level does each reach the limit, and which one ends furthest forward?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

| EphA on the axon | Ephrin-A where EphA × ephrin-A = 3 | Where it keeps its branches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 (temporal) | 1 | At the front edge |
| 2 (middle) | 1.5 | A quarter of the way back |
| 1 (nasal) | 3 | At the back edge |

The axon with the most receptor reaches the limit soonest, so a little ephrin-A repels it and it ends in front. The nasal axon carries little EphA, tolerates more ephrin and ends at the back. In the toy, two slopes running in opposite directions put the axons in order.

</details>

Two cautions about the toy. First, ephrin-A keeps branches from forming and surviving behind the right zone, and the overshooting part of the axon is later removed, so the "limit" is where branches can survive, not a wall the growth cone hits. Second, a repellent alone says only how far back an axon may branch, not why it does not branch further forward. The toy simply places each axon at its limit; what pushes axons back toward it, counter-gradients and comparison among axons, comes later. The gradients fit the map; proving them necessary took a mouse.

## Is the slope needed? Ephrin knockouts

In 2000 David Feldheim, Jonas Frisén, John Flanagan and colleagues disrupted the genes for ephrin-A2 and ephrin-A5 in mice and traced axons from small patches of retina.

- **With either gene gone,** the map in the superior colliculus was moderately abnormal.
- **With one working copy of each gene,** mice still had a defect.
- **With both gone,** axons from one small patch of retina ended at several places scattered along the axis.

The map loses much of its anterior-posterior order, but not all of it. Termination zones are **ectopic** (in the wrong place) and multiple, and the projection still reaches the colliculus. The defect grows as ligand is lowered, which says that the amount of ephrin, not merely its presence, carries position. One caveat: the knockout removed the ephrins from the retina as well as the target.

## Absolute or relative? Two maps from one retina

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

The rule so far can be read in two ways.

- **Absolute reading (a fixed threshold).** Each axon grows into the target from the front, meets more ephrin-A the further back it goes, and keeps its branches where EphA × ephrin-A reaches a fixed limit. Its place is an address written in its own receptor level, whatever its neighbours do.
- **Relative reading, in its simplest form (pure rank).** Each axon's place depends on how its EphA signal compares with the other axons'. The axon with the most signal takes the front of the target, the next takes the space behind it, and so on, spaced evenly by rank. Its place is a rank.

Both readings produce the normal nasal-to-temporal order, so order alone cannot tell them apart. Their spacing can differ: in the toy below, an axon with 2 units ends a quarter of the way back under the fixed threshold and halfway back under pure rank. The direct test of whether neighbours matter is an animal in which some axons change their receptor level and their neighbours do not.

**The experiment.** Arthur Brown, Greg Lemke and colleagues built one in 2000. *Isl2* is a transcription-factor gene that is switched on in about half of mouse retinal ganglion cells, scattered across the retina. They inserted the coding sequence of EphA3, a receptor mouse ganglion cells do not normally make, into *Isl2* (a **knock-in**, which places a chosen coding sequence in a gene so it is made wherever that gene is on). Every *Isl2* cell now made EphA3 on top of its usual EphA receptors. At every point in the retina, cells with extra receptor now lay intermingled with cells without it.

**Numbers to work with.** Use the toy map from the molecules section. The knock-in adds 4 units to a scattered half of the cells, so they now carry 5 to 7; assume an exactly equal random split at each retinal position.

Look at the unchanged axons: nothing about them was altered, yet they ended somewhere new, which a purely absolute reading cannot explain. Their place depended on the other axons.

**The other half of the answer.** Is the amount of ligand irrelevant, then? Run the reverse test on the same toy map.

**Predict.** Halve the ephrin-A everywhere in the target. What does the fixed threshold predict? Pure rank?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Fixed threshold: every axon must grow further back to reach its limit, so the map shifts backward. In the toy the most temporal axon now ends in the middle of the target, and axons from the nasal half of the retina run out of target. Pure rank: nothing moves, because no axon's rank has changed. (This is a prediction of the target-only halving toy, not a measured displacement or magnitude in the mutant mice.)

Real mice are not a clean halving. Mice with one working copy of each of the ephrin-A2 and ephrin-A5 genes (the other copy disabled) have less of those two ligands, in the retina as well as the target, and other ephrin-As remain. Feldheim, Flanagan and colleagues found that temporal axons in these mice still formed their main termination zone at the normal site, but in some animals also formed an extra patch further back. They read the defect as an effect of absolute ligand level. Neither toy predicts exactly that, but pure rank predicts no change at all, so it cannot give it.

</details>

The knock-in rules out a fixed address in the axon's own receptor level; the dosage result rules out pure rank. Together they give the rule this lesson started with: **an axon's place is a rank set against a slope.** The ligand gradient supplies the scale, and the comparison among axons decides who goes where on it. Real relative models, such as the ratio model in Going deeper, can include the amount of ligand. This is the "neighbours" mode working inside a gradient: local rules produce global order partly because axons also interact with each other.

**What the knock-in does not tell you** is how the axons compare themselves. Contact between neighbouring axons through their own EphA and ephrin-A, competition for space or for a limited trophic or synaptic resource, and activity-dependent stabilisation could each produce it. They are not mutually exclusive; which dominates, and whether it differs between fish, chick and mouse, is not settled.

## Where slopes stop being enough

Early on, a retinal axon's branches in the colliculus spread over a broad zone around the right place. Correlated spontaneous activity in the retina then sharpens them. In mice lacking the β2 subunit of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, whose retinal waves are abnormal, axons reach roughly the right region but their termination zones stay large and diffuse, as McLaughlin and colleagues found in 2003 and Cang and Feldheim reviewed in 2013. Chemistry lays out the map and activity sharpens it, though the two act at overlapping times and interact: in the EphA3 knock-in mice, retinal waves help decide how the two maps are organised. [The next unit](/lessons/spontaneous-activity/) takes up how those waves work.

<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Absolute reading: every axon reaches its limit sooner, so the whole map shifts toward the front. In the toy, adding 1 unit sends every axon that had 2 units or more, the temporal half, to the front edge.

Pure rank reading: no axon changes rank, so the map stays normal.

Ratio reading: the order is kept, but a fixed increment shrinks the ratio between neighbours most where EphA is low. Two nasal neighbours at 1 and 1.1 units differ by 10%; after the increment, at 2 and 2.1, by 5%. Two temporal neighbours at 2.9 and 3 differ by about 3.4%; at 3.9 and 4, by about 2.6%. An experiment: change only a scattered half of the cells, with a small increment (for example one copy of the knock-in instead of two), and trace axons from nasal and temporal retina separately. One extra unit changes the receptor ratio between changed and unchanged cells from 2 at the nasal pole to 4/3 at the temporal pole. Whether their projections then stay apart needs an explicit discrimination rule. For example, a model that tells two populations apart only when their receptor ratio exceeds 1.5 would keep nasal populations distinct and merge temporal ones; that threshold is invented, not fitted. Predicting physical spacing needs a further rule turning ratios into positions. Under pure rank, separation depends only on how many axons have levels between the two populations; in the toy that is the same at the two ends of the retina and larger in the middle. Compare the two predictions across several retinal positions. Reber, Burrola and Lemke fitted one to crosses of a related kind (see Going deeper); this particular comparison is the toy's prediction, not their test.

## Going deeper

### The other axis

EphB receptors, high in ventral retina, and ephrin-B ligands, high in the medial target, help sort axons along it, together with Wnt signalling and adhesion molecules, and much of its order is set before axons reach the target. Loss of ephrin-A2 and ephrin-A5 has been described as disturbing this axis too, though the main defects reported in 2000 were along the anterior-posterior axis. Ephrin-B also acts at the optic chiasm, in [that lesson](/lessons/crossing-the-midline/).

### How might axons compare themselves?

In 2004 Michael Reber, Patrick Burrola and Lemke combined the *Isl2*-EphA3 allele with a knockout of EphA4 to vary receptor levels further and fitted the results with a **relative signalling model**, in which each axon's ending depends on the ratio of its net EphA signal to that of the axons around it. The model allowed quantitative predictions of how a map shifts when receptor levels change. It describes what the axons do; it does not say how they compare themselves.

One proposal for the missing counterforce uses counter-gradients: ephrin-As are also expressed in the retina, high nasally, and EphAs in the tectum, high anteriorly, and ephrin-A reverse signalling in axons has been proposed to supply a posterior push. Without some counterforce, axons that simply moved away from a repellent would all pile up at the front.

### Weiss, resonance and the Nobel Prize

In Paul Weiss's resonance principle, fibres connect diffusely but each centre responds only to matching signals. Sperry shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, with David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel, for his separate work on the divided cerebral hemispheres.

### The first ephrin names

The 25 kilodalton protein Drescher, Bonhoeffer and colleagues purified in 1995 was called RAGS. Cheng, Flanagan and colleagues called their ligand ELF-1 and its receptor Mek4. Cheng and Flanagan had cloned ELF-1 in 1994, as a ligand for the receptors Mek4 and Sek; the 1995 paper reported its gradient across the tectum. Under the names agreed in 1997, RAGS is ephrin-A5, ELF-1 is ephrin-A2, and Mek4 is EphA3.
