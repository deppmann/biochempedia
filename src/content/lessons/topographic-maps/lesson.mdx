    story: >-
      Sperry trained under Paul Weiss, whose work suggested that a nerve's function could be
      matched to its target after growth. In 1943 Sperry published two papers on adult
      red-spotted newts, Triturus viridescens. In one he turned an eye 180 degrees in its socket
      and left the optic nerve intact. In the other he also cut the optic nerve and let it
      regenerate, which adult newts can do. Once sight returned, the animals behaved as if the
      world were upside down and reversed: a lure held above the head made the animal turn its head downward.
      The error did not improve with experience for as long as the animals were watched, up to 50 days after sight returned. Because the regrown fibres
      had rebuilt the old, now useless, map, Sperry argued that each fibre carries a property
      tied to its retinal position and finds the matching place in the brain. In 1963 he gathered
      this into the chemoaffinity hypothesis, with crossing gradients as a way to supply
      coordinates. He shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his separate work
      on the divided cerebral hemispheres. His newts made it reasonable to search for molecules
      that label position.

    story: >-
      Sperry's idea needed an assay in which a growing axon could be asked, again and again, which
      of two surfaces it preferred. Bonhoeffer's group made one. In 1987 Jochen Walter, Brigitte
      Kern-Veits, Julita Huf, Bernd Stolze and Bonhoeffer laid membranes from the anterior and
      posterior chick optic tectum in alternating narrow lanes, a striped carpet, and placed a

      cones in culture. It is now called ephrin-A5, and the stripe assay became a standard test
      for guidance molecules.

  - name: "Greg Lemke"
    contribution: >-

    story: >-
      A gradient can be read in two ways. An axon could stop where the ligand reaches a fixed
      value for its own receptor level, or it could compare its receptor level with that of its
      neighbours. In 2000 Arthur Brown and Paul Yates, with Patrick Burrola, Dan Ortuño, Ashish
      Vaidya, Thomas Jessell, Samuel Pfaff, Dennis O'Leary and Lemke, tested the two. They placed
      the coding sequence of EphA3, a receptor that mouse retinal ganglion cells do not normally
      make, under the control of the gene Isl2, which is active in about half of these cells,
      scattered across the retina. Neighbouring cells at the same retinal position now differed in
      EphA level. Tracing showed two orderly maps in the superior colliculus: the cells with extra
      EphA3 in the anterior part, their neighbours pushed into the posterior part. The title gave
      the conclusion: relative, not absolute, levels. In 2004 Michael Reber, Burrola and Lemke
      combined this allele with an EphA4 knockout and fitted a relative signalling model, which
      allowed quantitative predictions of how a map shifts when receptor levels change.

techniques:
  - name: "Stripe assay"
    blurb: >-
      Membranes from two regions of a target are laid down in alternating lanes, and a strip of
      neural tissue is placed across them. If axons grow along one kind of lane, the two regions
      differ in a cue the axons can read. Treating one membrane (by heat or an enzyme) first shows
      whether the cue is an attractant on one or a repellent on the other.
  - name: "Alkaline phosphatase fusion probes"
    blurb: >-
      The extracellular part of a receptor or ligand is joined to an enzyme that makes a coloured
      product. Applied to tissue, the probe stains wherever its binding partner sits, so a
      gradient of binding sites can be seen directly.
  - name: "Anterograde axon tracing with DiI"
    blurb: >-
      A small crystal or injection of a fluorescent lipid dye is placed in one patch of retina.
      The dye spreads along the membranes of those axons to their endings, showing where that
      patch terminates in the tectum or superior colliculus.
  - name: "Knock-in and knockout mice"
    blurb: >-
      A knockout disables a gene, usually by deleting or interrupting part of it. A knock-in inserts a chosen sequence at a targeted gene; this one placed a coding sequence under the control of
      another gene, so it is made only where that gene is active. Here a knock-in gave half of the
      ganglion cells extra EphA3 while their neighbours kept normal levels.

**Turn an adult newt's eye upside down in its socket, cut the optic nerve, and let it regrow. When the newt can see again and you hold a lure above its head, it turns its head downward. It keeps making that mistake for as long as anyone watched.**

The newt is not blind and not confused. Its vision has come back in its original order, only rotated, as if the image had been flipped before it reached the brain. That tells you something about how the wiring was rebuilt. The regrowing axons did not connect at random and then get sorted out by use. The simplest reading, and the one Sperry favoured, is that each one went back to its original target in the brain, even though that target now gave the wrong answer. So the question for this lesson is the one Chris Deppmann asks his class: **"How does a neuron know its position and know where to go?"**

## The idea: an axon finds its place in a map

The retina sends its axons to a target in the midbrain called the optic tectum in fish, amphibians and birds, and the superior colliculus in mammals. The projection is a topographic map: neighbouring ganglion cells in the retina connect to neighbouring places in the target, so the image on the retina is laid out in order on the brain. In the chick, axons from the temporal retina (the side nearer the temple) end in the anterior tectum, and axons from the nasal retina end in the posterior tectum. A second axis runs at right angles: dorsal retina maps to the lateral tectum, ventral retina to the medial tectum.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/topographic-maps/retinotectal-crossed-order.webp" width={1200} height={778} alt="AI-generated illustration: a retina slab, nasal end up and temporal end down, sends five parallel axons to a tectum slab, posterior end up and anterior end down; an inset redraws the tectum anterior-up so the gold temporal and slate nasal axons cross." caption="Retinal axons map in order onto the optic tectum: temporal retina ends in the anterior tectum and nasal retina in the posterior. Drawn with the tectum flipped, the same map appears crossed." />

A growth cone (the moving tip of a growing axon) can sense only what is close to it, yet the map spans the whole target. His lecture sets out three ways a neuron could know where to stop. Its endpoint could be **"determined by individual labels"**, one matching tag per cell and per target site. It could be set **"by gradients"**, smooth slopes in the amount of a molecule that give each position a value. Or it could be set **"by neighbors"**, each axon placing itself relative to the axons around it. A useful picture is seats in a theatre. Every ticket could name a seat, every seat could be numbered by row, or people could simply sit in the order they arrived. The rest of the lesson shows that the retina uses the second and third, together.

## Sperry's classic experiment: eye rotation

Chris puts the experiment as a question: **"What happens when you flip structure A around?"** Roger Sperry flipped it in 1943, working with adult red-spotted newts, *Triturus viridescens*. Sperry later repeated this kind of experiment in frogs and goldfish, which is how many textbooks draw it, but the 1943 papers used adult newts, and they depended on the fact that an adult newt's optic nerve regenerates. These were experiments on regrowth, which were then read as a window on how the map is built in the embryo.

In one paper he rotated the eye 180 degrees and left the optic nerve intact. The newts' responses to moving objects were inverted and reversed, and they stayed that way. That alone proves little, since the old connections were never broken. In the companion paper he also cut the optic nerve and, in one group, cut through the nerve's sheath as well and twisted the two stumps out of line, so that fibres could not simply follow old channels back. Vision returned after the nerve regrew. Animals with an unrotated eye aimed correctly. Animals with a rotated eye aimed in the wrong direction, by an amount that matched the rotation, and experience did not correct them.

The result argued against the view that fibres from the retina connect more or less at random and that useful function is then matched to them by experience. If use did the matching, weeks of missed prey should have taught the newt to aim correctly. Sperry noted that his doctoral adviser Paul Weiss's resonance principle, in which fibres connect diffusely but each centre responds only to matching signals, could also fit the result. He favoured the simpler reading: the regrown fibres had rebuilt the old map. Sperry proposed that retinal cells acquire chemical differences according to their position and that their targets carry matching differences. He developed this into the **chemoaffinity** hypothesis, set out in full in 1963, and suggested that two crossing gradients, one per axis, could provide the coordinates without a separate label for every cell. He did not have a molecule. He shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, with David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel, for his separate work on the divided cerebral hemispheres.

## Chemospecificity: asking an axon to choose

Chemoaffinity predicts that the tectum itself carries a position-dependent cue that retinal axons can read. Friedrich Bonhoeffer's group in Germany built an assay to ask about it directly. In 1987 Jochen Walter, Bonhoeffer and colleagues made a **striped carpet**: membranes from anterior and posterior chick tectum laid down in alternating narrow lanes. A strip of retina was placed across the lanes so that each growing axon met border after border.

Axons from temporal retina grew along the anterior lanes and stayed off the posterior ones. Axons from nasal retina grew on both. On a single kind of membrane, both nasal and temporal axons grew, so posterior membrane was not simply a poor surface. A companion paper found that heating the posterior membranes abolished the temporal axons' avoidance. That pointed to a heat-sensitive repellent on posterior membranes rather than an attractant on anterior ones.

The stripe assay also gave biochemists a test to follow during purification. In 1995 two papers appeared back to back in *Cell*. Uwe Drescher, Bonhoeffer and colleagues purified **RAGS**, a 25 kilodalton protein anchored to the membrane by a lipid (a GPI anchor), found in the posterior tectum, that collapsed and repelled retinal growth cones in culture. Hwai-Jong Cheng, John Flanagan and colleagues reported that **ELF-1**, a ligand of the Eph receptor family, rose in a gradient from anterior to posterior across the chick tectum, while its receptor **Mek4** ran the opposite way in the retina, high in temporal ganglion cells and low in nasal ones. Under the names agreed in 1997, RAGS is ephrin-A5, ELF-1 is ephrin-A2, and Mek4 is EphA3.

## The molecular answer: opposing Eph and ephrin gradients

Eph receptors are the largest family of receptor tyrosine kinases ([that lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/) covers how such receptors are switched on). Their ligands, the ephrins, are themselves membrane proteins. Ephrin-As are held to the outer surface by a GPI anchor and bind mainly EphA receptors; ephrin-Bs cross the membrane and bind mainly EphB receptors. Because both partners sit on cell surfaces, signalling needs contact, and it can run both ways: the receptor signals forward into its own cell, and the ephrin can signal in reverse into the cell that carries it (Egea and Klein reviewed this bidirectional signalling in 2007; [that lesson](/lessons/reverse-signaling/) explains how).

Along the anterior-posterior axis the logic is a pair of opposite slopes. Temporal axons carry the most EphA, so a little ephrin-A repels them, and their endings form in the anterior target. Nasal axons carry little EphA, tolerate more ephrin, and end in the posterior target. In chick and mouse the main axon does not simply stop at the right place. It first grows past its final zone, then sprouts side branches, and ephrin-A keeps branches from forming and surviving behind the right zone; the overshooting part is later removed. The gradients fit the map; proving them necessary took a mouse.

In 2000 David Feldheim, Jonas Frisén, John Flanagan and colleagues disrupted the genes for ephrin-A2 and ephrin-A5. With either gene gone, the map in the superior colliculus was moderately abnormal; even mice with one working copy of each gene had a defect. With both gone, axons from one small patch of retina ended at several places scattered along the axis. Chris gives his class this map with a prompt: **"Here's a retinotectal map from an ephrin-A knockout mouse. What went wrong in the projection pattern? What does this tell you about how gradients encode position?"** The careful answer is that the map loses much of its anterior-posterior order, but not all of it: termination zones are ectopic and multiple, the projection still reaches the colliculus, and some order remains. The defect grows as ligand is lowered, which says that the amount of ephrin, not merely its presence, carries position. The other axis uses the other class among several cues, and ephrin-A2/A5 loss disturbs it too. EphB receptors, high in ventral retina, and ephrin-B ligands, high in the medial target, help sort axons along it, together with Wnt signalling and adhesion molecules, and much of its order is set before axons reach the target. (Ephrin-B also acts at the optic chiasm, in [that lesson](/lessons/crossing-the-midline/).)

## Big Question 2: How do local rules create global order?

A repulsive gradient read against a fixed threshold has a problem. The same receptor level could rank high among one animal's axons and low among another's, and since every axon is repelled, something must spread them out. Arthur Brown, Greg Lemke and colleagues tested how the gradient is read in 2000. They put the coding sequence of EphA3, which mouse ganglion cells do not normally make, under the control of the gene *Isl2*, active in about half of the ganglion cells and scattered across the retina. At every retinal position, some cells now carried extra EphA and their neighbours did not.

### Design problem

In this knock-in mouse, a scattered half of the retinal ganglion cells carry extra EphA3 on top of their normal gradient. If each axon reads an absolute level of EphA against the ephrin gradient, what map do you predict? What if each axon compares itself with its neighbours?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Absolute reading: each axon settles (in practice, keeps its branches) where the ephrin level crosses the repulsion threshold set by its own receptor level. The knock-in cells, with more receptor, settle sooner, so they shift anteriorly. The wild-type cells have the same receptor levels as in a normal mouse and meet the same ephrin gradient, so they end exactly where they normally would. You predict one normal map with a second, anteriorly shifted map laid over its front part.

Relative reading: what matters is an axon's EphA signal compared with that of the others. Every knock-in cell now outranks the wild-type cells from the same region, so the knock-in cells take the anterior part of the colliculus, compressed into it in their own order, and the wild-type cells are pushed into the posterior part. You predict two maps, each covering only part of the target, with the wild-type map displaced posteriorly.

The second is what Brown and colleagues saw in mice with two copies of the knock-in: two orderly maps, with the knock-in population anterior and its wild-type neighbours posterior, and both together filling the colliculus. Wild-type cells ending somewhere new is the observation a purely absolute model cannot explain. Absolute levels still count, though: Feldheim and colleagues read the defect in mice with one copy of each ephrin gene as an effect of absolute ligand level, so current models combine relative comparison with the amount of ligand.

In 2004 Michael Reber, Patrick Burrola and Lemke combined this allele with a knockout of EphA4 to vary receptor levels further and fitted the results with a **relative signalling model**: each axon's ending depends on its EphA signal relative to that of its neighbours, so axons compete for space and the map is the outcome. This is the "by neighbors" mode working inside a gradient. Local rules, each growth cone sensing only a short distance, produce global order partly because axons also interact with each other. How much this matters differs between preparations; in zebrafish, a retinal axon with no neighbours can still find roughly the right place.

Gradients stop being enough at the fine scale. Early on, a retinal axon's branches in the colliculus spread over a broad zone around the right place. Correlated spontaneous activity in the retina then sharpens them. In mice lacking the β2 subunit of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, whose retinal waves are abnormal, axons reach roughly the right region but their termination zones stay large and diffuse, as McLaughlin and colleagues found in 2003 and Cang and Feldheim reviewed in 2013. Chemistry lays out the map and activity sharpens it, though the two act at overlapping times and interact; in the EphA3 knock-in mice, retinal waves help decide how the two maps are organised. That is where [the next unit](/lessons/spontaneous-activity/) begins.
