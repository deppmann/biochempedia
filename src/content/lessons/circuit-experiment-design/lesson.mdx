techniques:
  - name: "Optogenetic gain and loss of function"
    blurb: >-
      Express a light-sensitive channel or pump in genetically specified cells, then drive or
      suppress their activity at a chosen time while an animal behaves. Driving tests whether that
      activity is sufficient for the measured change; suppressing tests whether it is necessary.
      Each conclusion is scoped to those cells, that timing and that assay.
  - name: "Whole-cell patch clamp"
    blurb: >-
      Record current and voltage from one neuron with a pipette sealed to its membrane. In an
      optogenetic experiment it is the validation step: it shows that light actually produced, or
      prevented, the spikes you assumed it did.
  - name: "Task fMRI with test-retest reliability"
    blurb: >-
      Measure the blood-oxygenation signal while a person performs a task, and scan the same people
      twice to ask whether their individual scores are stable. An intraclass correlation summarizes
      that stability, and a group-average activation map does not substitute for it.
  - name: "Held-out prediction and cross-validation"
    blurb: >-
      Fit a model on part of a dataset and evaluate it on observations not used in fitting. It
      estimates prediction, not causation, and it is only honest if feature selection happens
      inside the training data and the split is by person.

    choices:

      - "That the freezing seen in untreated mice is driven by those neurons"
      - "That those neurons are the only source of freezing in the mouse"
      - "That activity in those neurons is sufficient for more freezing in that assay"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Driving activity in chosen cells is a gain-of-function manipulation, so it tests sufficiency
      under the tested conditions. Necessity needs a loss-of-function arm in which the same
      activity is suppressed. The idea that these cells are the only source does not follow from a
      single positive manipulation, since other populations were never tested. Nor does the result
      show that naturally occurring freezing uses this pathway, because artificial activation can
      produce a behaviour that the circuit does not normally produce that way.

The first film leaves us with an interaction, not a verdict. The text now asks how far that contrast lets us speak, then how much evidence the result deserves.

> **The one idea.** Every causal circuit claim is only as specific as its identifying contrast: change the proposed cause, use a matched control for everything else, and limit the conclusion to the cells, projection, time window and assay that contrast isolates.
>
> *Scope:* this rule organizes the acute circuit experiments here. A matched control reduces alternative explanations; it does not make them impossible. An observational human scan can establish association, and careful validation can establish prediction, but neither alone identifies a cause.

## From a manipulation to a claim

The first film used manipulation to cross the causal gap that a recording leaves. A lesion changes the question again: it asks what is lost when tissue is damaged, which speaks to necessity, but lesion extent, connected networks and compensation complicate the answer. In [attention and decisions](/lessons/attention-and-decisions/), choice-related activity in LIP looked important, yet reversible inactivation showed that it was not needed for the choice under the tested conditions.

With **gain of function** already tied to sufficiency and **loss of function** to necessity, the useful next case is a partial result. Both conclusions hold only under the tested conditions.

**Predict.** Silencing a population reduces a behaviour but does not abolish it. Has the experiment shown that the population is necessary?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The result shows that the population contributes under the tested conditions. Calling it necessary would require showing that the specified behaviour cannot occur without its activity in that design.

</details>

A usable claim therefore names four things: the cells, the projection, the timing and the measured behaviour. It does not say that the manipulated cells are the only route to the behaviour, or that artificial activation copies the route the unmanipulated animal normally uses.

## Read the two-by-two as two differences

The film built a construct-by-light design: opsin or fluorophore only, each tested with light on and off. To read it, calculate the effect of light in each construct group, then compare those effects:

> **interaction = (opsin light − opsin no light) − (fluorophore light − fluorophore no light)**

The second difference estimates what the matched light does without an opsin. Subtracting it leaves the extra light-linked effect in animals carrying the opsin. This is stronger than comparing only the two light-on groups or only light on with light off in opsin animals.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/circuit-experiment-design/two-by-two-light-design.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: Four mice with head-mounted optical fibres in a two-by-two grid: opsin or fluorophore only, crossed with light on or light off; the opsin-plus-light arena is outlined in gold." caption="Cross opsin or fluorophore with light on or off, calculate the light effect within each construct group, and compare those two effects." />

Owen, Liu and Kreitzer showed why the matched light control is not optional. In 2019 they illuminated wild-type mice with no opsin, in vivo and ex vivo. Continuous striatal illumination at powers used in experiments suppressed firing, and one-sided illumination biased turning toward the lit side. The protocols they discuss raised local temperature by a fraction of a degree up to about two degrees. They did not show that every optogenetic effect is thermal. They showed that light-on versus light-off, by itself, does not identify an opsin effect.

Tye and colleagues' 2011 experiment shows how the claim narrows again. In freely moving mice they illuminated basolateral-amygdala terminals in the central amygdala. Activating those terminals acutely reduced anxiety-related behaviour; inhibiting them with eNpHR3.0 increased it. Illuminating basolateral-amygdala cell bodies instead had the opposite effect of terminal activation: open-arm time fell, an anxiogenic effect. Where the light went changed the answer. You met these structures in [emotion and stress](/lessons/emotion-and-stress/).

But illumination in a terminal field is not yet proof that local transmission at the named synapse caused the behaviour. It can excite axons of passage, labelled axons that cross the field without ending in it, and can drive activity back toward the parent cell bodies. Tye and colleagues therefore blocked glutamate receptors locally in the central amygdala. The light-induced reduction disappeared.

**Predict.** Activating basolateral-amygdala cell bodies reduced open-arm time, while activating their terminals in the central amygdala increased it. Can one region-level claim explain both results?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The basolateral amygdala contains neurons with several outputs, and manipulating the cell bodies recruits them together. The terminal experiment names one output, so the claim must distinguish region-level activation from that projection-specific manipulation.

</details>

That local block is one strong way to test whether transmitter action at the terminal field is required. The study also included a control virus carrying only a fluorescent protein with the same illumination. Its stimulation used 5 millisecond pulses at 20 hertz.

**Predict.** How many milliseconds was the light on during each second, and what duty cycle is that?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Twenty pulses times 5 milliseconds is 100 milliseconds of light per second. That is a 10 percent duty cycle.

</details>

The remaining checks make the contrast believable, and published work very often reports them incompletely. Verify expression and fibre placement blind to group. Record during and after illumination to show that the chosen protocol produced the intended physiological change. Randomize allocation, counterbalance repeated-condition order, define exclusions in advance and score behaviour blind. If timing matters, add an off-window condition. If movement could explain the result, add a locomotor control.

Once a contrast identifies an effect, the next question is how precisely it was estimated and how far its claim can travel.

## Effect size, power and the experimental unit

The second film named the planning formula and kept trials inside animals; here is the arithmetic. The numbers below are invented for teaching.

Suppose a pilot gives a mean avoidance time of 60 seconds in the opsin-plus-light group and 48 seconds in the fluorophore-plus-light group, with a pooled standard deviation of 12 seconds. The **standardized mean difference** is the group difference divided by that pooled spread:

> **d = (60 s − 48 s) / 12 s = 1.0**

For 80 percent power at a two-sided alpha of 0.05, the normal approximation for two independent groups is:

> **n per group = 2 (1.96 + 0.84)^2 / d^2**

At `d = 1.0`, this gives 15.7, so the pilot implies 16 animals per group, or about 17 after allowing for a t distribution rather than a normal one. That is not automatically the planning answer. Small pilots estimate effects imprecisely, and the pilots that overshoot are more likely to be followed up. The effect carried into a confirmatory study therefore tends to be too large.

Plan for the smallest effect worth detecting. If that effect is `d = 0.5`, the same approximation gives 15.7 divided by 0.25, or 62.7: 63 animals per group.

The film's nested dots become an analysis choice. Reporting guidelines ask investigators to identify whether the independent **experimental unit** is an animal, litter or cage. Trial-level scoring is fine when the model preserves the nesting: repeated observations share expression, placement and state within an animal. Treating them as independent hides that shared variation and makes uncertainty too small, which is an easy route to a significant result that will not replicate.

## Association, prediction and causation in human imaging

The second film separated association, held-out prediction and causation. The measurement and validation tests below decide which of those verbs a human imaging study has earned.

First ask what was measured. The conventional imaging signal indexes blood-oxygenation changes associated with neural activity, not spikes. In monkey visual cortex, Logothetis and colleagues recorded electrically and with that signal at the same time. Local field potentials estimated it better than multi-unit activity did, which led them to suggest that it reflects input and intracortical processing rather than spiking output. For recording many neurons directly, see [recording many neurons](/lessons/large-scale-neuroscience/).

Next ask how precise and stable the individual differences are. In Marek and colleagues' rigorously denoised analysis of 3,928 ABCD participants, the median absolute univariate brain-wide association with behaviour was 0.01. The largest univariate correlation that replicated out of sample had an absolute value of 0.16. Replication improved as samples grew into the thousands, and independent subsamples of 25 could support opposite conclusions about the same association.

Elliott and colleagues pooled 90 task-fMRI experiments with 1,008 participants and reported a mean test-retest intraclass correlation of 0.397. A group-average activation can be clear while individual rankings are too unstable for a useful biomarker.

Keep the scope. These results concern brain-wide studies of individual differences. They do not say imaging is broken or set a minimum sample for every within-subject design, where each person is their own comparison. Marek and colleagues reported larger associations for functional than structural measures, cognitive tests than questionnaires, and multivariate than univariate methods. The univariate values above do not set a ceiling across designs. Sampling scheme, behavioural reliability and separating within-person change from between-person differences also change what can be detected at a given sample size.

Here is the precision calculation to run before trusting an association. Suppose the true correlation is 0.10, the order of magnitude Marek and colleagues report. With the Fisher transformation, `atanh`, the sample size for 80 percent power at a two-sided alpha of 0.05 is:

> **n = (2.80 / atanh r)^2 + 3 = (2.80 / 0.1003)^2 + 3 = about 780**

Now take 25 people. The standard error on the transformed scale is 1 divided by the square root of `n − 3`, or `1 / sqrt(22) = 0.213`.

**Predict.** An observed correlation is 0.10. Before calculating its interval, can this sample establish even the sign of the association?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The 95 percent interval on the transformed scale is `0.100 ± 1.96 × 0.213`, from −0.318 to +0.518. Converting back gives correlations from about −0.31 to +0.48. The sign is unknown. That is the arithmetic behind two subsamples of 25 disagreeing.

</details>

Held-out prediction adds a different test, but only if the outcome remains unseen. Feature selection and model tuning must happen inside the training data, and all scans from one person must stay in the same fold.

**Predict.** A model selects features using all participants, then cross-validates only the model fitting. Is its test performance genuinely held out?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The test-fold outcomes already influenced feature selection, so the reported performance is optimistic. Repeat feature selection and model tuning inside each training fold.

</details>

Return to the rule: the claim cannot outrun the contrast. A validated predictor may generalize to new people while still leaving the cause of the predicted outcome unknown.

### Design problem

A paper reports the following. Twenty patients and twenty controls are scanned. The authors search all connections and symptom measures, pick the strongest relationship, and report cross-validated prediction after splitting each person's two scans across different folds. They name a mouse projection as the cause of the disorder, then activate that projection with light, comparing light-on with light-off in opsin-expressing animals, scoring avoidance unblinded, and counting each trial as a replicate. Their discussion proposes stimulating the human homologue as a treatment. Rewrite the study.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start by separating three claims: an association in humans, a prediction target and a causal intervention in mice. A cross-sectional scan plus an acute mouse assay can suggest a mechanism, but it does not establish the mechanism of a human disorder.

On the human side, prespecify the imaging target or validate it in an independent sample. Do feature selection and model tuning inside the training folds. Split by person, so no participant appears in both training and evaluation. Report the effect size and uncertainty, not a p value alone, and measure test-retest reliability of the proposed marker. Forty participants cannot resolve an association of the size this literature reports, so join a large cohort or reframe the question as a within-person design. Check medication, head motion and recruitment as alternatives.

On the mouse side, add the opsin-negative arm and use the light-by-construct interaction, because light alone can change firing and behaviour. Verify expression, placement and physiology blind, including the period after illumination. Run activation for sufficiency and silencing for necessity in the same prespecified window. If the claim is projection-specific, test local transmission. Randomize allocation, blind scoring, define exclusions in advance, and analyze trials nested within animals.

Then write the conclusion the design supports: replicated, controlled evidence that activity in a named projection contributes to an assay-defined behaviour in mice during a defined window, alongside a separately reported human association with its uncertainty and reliability. A therapeutic claim would need human interventional evidence, which this study does not contain.

</details>

## Going deeper

### What the first channelrhodopsin experiments established

The tool has two separate histories. In 2003 Nagel, Bamberg and colleagues characterized channelrhodopsin-2 as a directly light-switched, cation-selective channel in Xenopus oocytes and mammalian cells. They also reported that continuous light desensitized it to a smaller steady-state conductance.

In 2005 Boyden, Zhang, Bamberg, Nagel and Deisseroth expressed that channel with a lentivirus in cultured rat hippocampal neurons and showed reliable millisecond-timescale control of spiking. The neurons came from newborn rats; there was no intact animal and no behaviour. Other groups expressed the channel in neurons around the same period, so there is no useful single “inventor” or “first” to award. The specific advance was control of spikes in chosen cells, which made a sufficiency experiment possible. The channel's gating and selectivity belong to [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/), and genetic targeting connects to [CRISPR and functional genomics](/lessons/crispr-and-functional-genomics/).

### When the construct name misleads

Three failure modes deserve recordings rather than assumptions. Long channelrhodopsin-driven depolarization can suppress spikes through depolarization block, so driving harder can silence the cell meant to be driven. Anion-conducting silencers can excite compartments with high internal chloride, including some axonal compartments. Abruptly ending silencing can produce rebound firing, so the post-light window belongs in the result. Validate the physiology in the preparation during and after illumination.
