techniques:
  - name: "Optogenetic gain and loss of function"
    blurb: >-
      Express a light-sensitive channel or pump in genetically specified cells, then drive or
      suppress their activity at a chosen time while an animal behaves. Driving tests whether that
      activity is sufficient for the measured change; suppressing tests whether it is necessary.
      Each conclusion is scoped to those cells, that timing and that assay.
  - name: "Whole-cell patch clamp"
    blurb: >-
      Record current and voltage from one neuron with a pipette sealed to its membrane. In an
      optogenetic experiment it is the validation step: it shows that light actually produced, or
      prevented, the spikes you assumed it did.
  - name: "Task fMRI with test-retest reliability"
    blurb: >-
      Measure the blood-oxygenation signal while a person performs a task, and scan the same people
      twice to ask whether their individual scores are stable. An intraclass correlation summarizes
      that stability, and a group-average activation map does not substitute for it.
  - name: "Held-out prediction and cross-validation"
    blurb: >-
      Fit a model on part of a dataset and evaluate it on observations not used in fitting. It
      estimates prediction, not causation, and it is only honest if feature selection happens
      inside the training data and the split is by person.

What causal conclusion does this support under the tested conditions?"
    choices:
      - "That activity in those neurons is necessary for freezing in that assay"
      - "That the freezing seen in untreated mice is driven by those neurons"
      - "That those neurons are the only source of freezing in the mouse"
      - "That activity in those neurons is sufficient for more freezing in that assay"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Driving activity in chosen cells is a gain-of-function manipulation, so it tests sufficiency
      under the tested conditions. Necessity needs a loss-of-function arm in which the same
      activity is suppressed. The idea that these cells are the only source does not follow from a
      single positive manipulation, since other populations were never tested. Nor does the result
      show that naturally occurring freezing uses this pathway, because artificial activation can
      produce a behaviour that the circuit does not normally produce that way.
  - stem: "In a light-on versus light-off design, which control group should also be tested with light on and off to estimate the light effect without an opsin?"
    choices:
      - "Animals with no surgery at all, given identical light"
      - "Animals expressing only the fluorescent marker, tested under the same light-on and light-off conditions"
      - "Opsin-expressing animals illuminated at a different frequency"
      - "Opsin-expressing animals tested in a second behavioural assay"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Estimate the light effect (light on minus light off) in opsin animals and in matched
      fluorophore-only controls tested under the same light-on and light-off conditions, then
      compare the two effects so shared illumination effects cancel. A second assay changes the
      question rather than the confound. A different frequency changes the stimulus but still
      leaves every animal expressing an opsin. Animals with no surgery cannot receive intracranial
      light at all, and they differ in handling, virus and implant as well as in opsin, so a
      difference cannot be attributed to the construct.
  - stem: "A study illuminates eight mice, runs 40 trials in each, and analyzes 320 trials as independent observations. What is wrong?"
    choices:
      - "Trials within one animal are not independent of each other"
      - "Trial counts must be equal across the two light conditions"
      - "Behaviour should be scored once per animal, never per trial"
      - "Forty trials is too few to estimate a behavioural mean"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Repeated trials from the same mouse share that animal's state, placement and expression, so
      treating them as 320 independent observations inflates the apparent sample. Reporting
      guidelines ask for the experimental unit to be identified, and here it is the animal, with
      trials nested inside it. Forty trials per animal is not itself a problem. Unequal trial
      counts can be handled in the analysis. Scoring per trial is fine, as long as the nesting is
      carried into the model.

**Light alone altered firing and turning in mice carrying no light-sensitive tool.** The first film introduced the construct-by-light grid, which estimates the extra light response associated with the opsin after subtracting shared illumination effects; here we ask how far a manipulation lets you speak, and how much evidence a claim needs.

## From manipulation to a scoped causal claim

A recording shows covariation; manipulation tests cause. Driving chosen cells (**gain of function**) tests **sufficiency**—whether their activity is enough for the measured change under the tested conditions. Suppressing them (**loss of function**) tests **necessity**—whether their activity is required under those conditions.

A **lesion** (damaging tissue) also probes necessity, but lesion extent, connected networks and compensation complicate the answer. In [attention and decisions](/lessons/attention-and-decisions/), choice-related activity in LIP looked important in recordings, yet reversible inactivation showed it was not needed for the choice under the tested conditions.

Loss of function often yields a partial drop rather than abolition.

**Predict.** Silencing a population reduces a behaviour but does not abolish it. Has the experiment shown that the population is necessary?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. A selective, validated perturbation that reduces behaviour supports a contribution to its normal level in this assay. If silencing was complete and some behaviour still occurs, the activity is dispensable for any occurrence; if silencing was incomplete, strict necessity remains unresolved.

</details>

Every result also stays inside its own preparation. A **tool demonstration** shows what a construct does to neurons, not what a circuit does to behaviour: expressing channelrhodopsin-2 in cultured newborn rat neurons in 2005 established millisecond spike control with no intact animal and no behaviour (*Going deeper*). Moving into a behaving animal adds a behavioural readout—and harder controls. A usable causal claim therefore names four things: the **cells**, the **projection**, the **time window** and the **measured behaviour**. It never says those cells are the only route, or that artificial activation copies the route an unmanipulated animal normally uses.

> **The one idea.** A circuit claim can go no further than what the contrast isolates and what the sample can resolve.
>
> *Scope:* This rule governs acute manipulation experiments and observational brain–behaviour studies. A matched control reduces alternative explanations without ruling out every confound or compensation; a large sample sharpens precision without fixing bias, low reliability or a missing intervention.

## Isolate the cause: light controls and pathway specificity

The **opsins** used here are light-sensitive proteins—naturally occurring or engineered variants—that act as ion channels or pumps; the **fluorophore**-only construct expresses a fluorescent marker without a light-gated channel or pump. In 2019 Owen, Liu and Kreitzer illuminated wild-type, opsin-free mice in vivo and ex vivo. Continuous striatal light at experimental powers suppressed firing, and one-sided illumination biased turning toward the lit side; typical protocols raised local temperature by a fraction of a degree up to about two degrees. They did not show that every optogenetic effect is thermal—only that light on versus light off in opsin animals cannot, alone, identify an opsin effect.

To apply the first film's two-by-two construct-by-light grid, read its difference of differences—the **interaction**—on concrete teaching numbers (avoidance time in seconds):

| Construct | Light off | Light on | Light effect (on − off) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| Opsin | 40 s | 60 s | +20 s |
| Fluorophore only | 40 s | 48 s | +8 s |

> **interaction = (opsin light − opsin no light) − (fluorophore light − fluorophore no light) = (+20 s) − (+8 s) = +12 s**

The light effects are +20 s with opsin and +8 s with the fluorophore alone, and their difference is +12 s. Comparing only the two light-on groups (60 s vs. 48 s) happens to match here only because the light-off means are equal, and fails whenever expressing the construct shifts baseline behaviour. Comparing the two light effects subtracts additive baseline differences and shared illumination effects; interpreting the +12 s remainder as an opsin-mediated activity effect also requires matched procedures and validation of the intended physiological change.

Where the light lands narrows the claim further. In 2011 Tye and colleagues tested freely moving mice in anxiety assays including the **elevated plus maze**, where less open-arm exploration is interpreted as greater anxiety-like behaviour (an **anxiogenic** effect) when changes in locomotion and other plausible explanations are controlled. Activating basolateral-amygdala terminals in the central amygdala acutely reduced anxiety-related behaviour; inhibiting them with **eNpHR3.0** (an inhibitory, light-driven chloride pump) increased it. Illuminating basolateral-amygdala cell bodies had the opposite effect of terminal activation: open-arm time fell. You met these structures in [emotion and stress](/lessons/emotion-and-stress/).

**Predict.** Activating basolateral-amygdala cell bodies reduced open-arm time, while activating their terminals in the central amygdala increased it. Can one region-level claim explain both results?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The basolateral amygdala contains neurons with several outputs, so somatic activation can recruit several pathways together. Terminal illumination targets one output field, and the physiology and local receptor block described next support a role for basolateral-amygdala input to the central amygdala.

</details>

As the first film noted, terminal light can also excite **axons of passage** (labelled axons crossing the field without ending there) or drive spikes back to parent cell bodies. Tye and colleagues therefore blocked glutamate receptors locally in the central amygdala, and the light-induced reduction disappeared—strong evidence that local transmitter action was required. In the ChR2 activation experiment, fluorophore-only controls received the same 5 millisecond pulses at 20 hertz; the eNpHR3.0 inhibition experiment used continuous amber light with a separate matched control. A pulse train's **duty cycle** is the percentage of time the light is on.

**Predict.** With 5 millisecond pulses at 20 hertz, how many milliseconds is the light on per second, and what duty cycle is that?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Twenty pulses times 5 milliseconds is 100 milliseconds of light per second. That is a 10 percent duty cycle.

</details>

Remaining checks make the contrast believable, yet published work often reports them incompletely: verify expression and fibre placement blind; record physiology during and after light (*Going deeper* covers depolarization block, chloride-driven excitation and rebound firing); randomize allocation; counterbalance repeated-condition order; predefine exclusions; score behaviour blind; and add off-window or locomotor controls when timing or movement could explain the result.

Even a clean contrast can mislead if the sample is too small or trials are counted wrong; the second film shows how independent units, effect size and study design bound the evidence.

## Size the sample and count the right unit

The second film introduced standardized effects, the sample-size curve and pilot overestimation. Its curve applies to a two-group contrast; using the table's two light-on values alone illustrates that calculation. (To power the four-cell construct-by-light interaction instead, use all four means and the variance of that contrast; with repeated light conditions in the same animals, compare animal-level on-minus-off changes across construct groups.)

With mean avoidance of 60 s in opsin-plus-light mice, 48 s in fluorophore-plus-light mice and a pooled standard deviation of 12 s, the **standardized mean difference** (*d*) is:

> **d = (60 s − 48 s) / 12 s = 1.0**

For one **two-sided** test of two equally sized independent groups with a common within-group SD at **α = 0.05** (critical *z* values ±1.96) and **80 percent power** (an 80 percent chance of rejecting the null at the specified true effect *d*, giving a normal offset of 0.84), the normal approximation is:

> **n per group = 2 (1.96 + 0.84)^2 / d^2**

At `d = 1.0`, this gives 15.7: 16 animals per group, or about 17 using a *t* distribution. Because *d* is squared in the denominator, halving the target effect quadruples the required sample. Small pilots estimate *d* noisily and overshooting pilots are preferentially followed up, so plan for the smallest effect worth detecting instead. At `d = 0.5`, the formula gives `15.7 / 0.25 = 62.7`: 63 animals per group.

Set a modest true effect (`d = 0.5`) and small pilot size below, draw 200 pilots, and compare the average `d` of pilots reaching `p < 0.05` against the true value.

Identify the **experimental unit** for the treatment being tested—the smallest entity that can receive treatment independently (such as a mouse, litter or cage, which reporting guidelines ask you to state)—and use a power calculation that reflects the design (clustered and repeated-measures calculations can also model observations within units). In the second film's eight-mouse, 40-trial example, repeated trials share one mouse's expression, placement and state. Trial-level scoring is valid when the statistical model preserves that nesting; pooling trials as independent hides shared variation, shrinks uncertainty artificially, and can inflate false positives and make a significant result less dependable.

## Association, prediction and causation in human imaging

The second film separated **association** (two measures varying together), **held-out prediction** (predicting unseen people) and **causation** (changing the cause changes the outcome). The four checks below assess association and prediction; a causal conclusion also requires a design that identifies the effect of changing the proposed cause.

First, check what was measured. Conventional fMRI tracks blood-oxygenation changes, not spikes. In 2001 Logothetis and colleagues recorded electrically and with fMRI simultaneously in monkey visual cortex: **local field potentials** (summed synaptic and slow local membrane activity) tracked the signal better than **multi-unit activity** (nearby spikes), suggesting it reflects input and intracortical processing rather than spiking output (for direct multi-neuron recording, see [recording many neurons](/lessons/large-scale-neuroscience/)).

Second, check effect size and stability. In 2022 Marek and colleagues denoised 3,928 participants from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study: the median absolute univariate brain-wide association with behaviour was 0.01, and the largest replicating out-of-sample univariate correlation was 0.16. Replication improved in the thousands, whereas two selected resamples of 25 from the same cohort yielded statistically significant correlations in opposite directions, illustrating the range small samples can produce. In 2020 Elliott and colleagues pooled 90 task-fMRI experiments (1,008 participants) and found a mean test-retest **intraclass correlation** (ICC)—a measure of test–retest reliability that can be estimated in several ways, where values below 0.40 indicate poor reliability under the convention they used—of 0.397. Clear group averages do not guarantee stable individual rankings for a biomarker.

Keep the scope: these results cover brain-wide studies of individual differences, not every MRI or within-subject design where each person is their own comparison. Marek and colleagues found larger associations for functional than structural measures, cognitive tests than questionnaires and multivariate than univariate methods, so univariate figures set no universal ceiling; sampling scheme, behavioural reliability and separating within-person from between-person variation also shift detectability.

Third, check precision. For a Pearson correlation with `|r| < 1` from `n` independent, approximately bivariate-normal pairs, **Fisher's transformation (`z = atanh r`)** is approximately normal with standard error `1 / sqrt(n − 3)`. For one prespecified two-sided Pearson test of zero correlation at α = 0.05 and 80 percent power, a true correlation of 0.10—an illustrative value toward the strong end of Marek and colleagues' univariate results—requires:

> **n = (2.80 / atanh r)^2 + 3 = (2.80 / 0.1003)^2 + 3 = about 780**

At `n = 25`, the standard error is `1 / sqrt(22) = 0.213`.

**Predict.** In 25 people, an observed correlation is 0.10. Before calculating its interval, can this sample establish even the sign?

Fourth, check held-out validation. **Cross-validation** splits data into non-overlapping subsets (**folds**), fitting on training folds and testing on the held-out fold. It stays honest only if feature selection and tuning stay inside the training folds and all scans from one person stay in the same fold.

**Predict.** A model selects features using all participants, then cross-validates only the model fitting. Is its test performance genuinely held out?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. If features were selected for their relationship to the outcomes using all participants, the test outcomes already influenced the model and performance can look too good. Fit data-dependent preprocessing and feature selection on training data only, and tune within the outer training data before scoring the outer test fold.

</details>

Return to the rule: match the claim to what the contrast isolates and what the sample resolves. A validated predictor can generalize to new people while still leaving the cause unknown.

### Design problem

A paper reports the following. Twenty patients and twenty controls are scanned. The authors search all connections and symptom measures, pick the strongest relationship, and report cross-validated prediction after splitting each person's two scans across different folds. They name a mouse projection as the cause of the disorder, then activate that projection with light, comparing light-on with light-off in opsin-expressing animals, scoring avoidance unblinded, and counting each trial as a replicate. Their discussion proposes stimulating the human homologue as a treatment. Rewrite the study.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Separate three claims: human association, held-out prediction and causal intervention in mice. A cross-sectional scan plus an acute mouse assay can suggest a mechanism, not establish the mechanism of a human disorder.

On the human side, prespecify the target or validate it in an independent sample. Keep feature selection and tuning inside training folds, and split by person so no participant spans training and test. Report effect size, uncertainty and test-retest reliability, not a p value alone. With 40 participants, power is low for between-person correlations of this size, so use a larger independent cohort or ask a distinct within-person question powered for that estimand, and check medication, head motion and recruitment.

On the mouse side, add an opsin-negative arm and use the construct-by-light interaction, because light alone alters firing and behaviour. Verify expression, placement and physiology blind, including after illumination. Test sufficiency (activation) and necessity (silencing) in the same prespecified window; for a pathway mechanism claim, verify that transmission from the targeted projection in the recipient region mediates the effect (a local receptor block combined with projection-targeted physiology is one way to test that). Randomize allocation, blind scoring, predefine exclusions and nest trials within animals.

Match the conclusion to the design: if independently replicated, controlled experiments support it, report the projection's contribution to the assay-defined mouse behaviour and time window, and report the human association separately with its uncertainty and test–retest reliability. A therapeutic claim requires human interventional evidence this study lacks.

</details>

## Going deeper

### What the first channelrhodopsin experiments established

The tool has two separate histories. In 2003 Nagel, Bamberg and colleagues characterized channelrhodopsin-2 as a directly light-switched, cation-selective channel in Xenopus oocytes and mammalian cells, and reported that continuous light desensitized it to a smaller steady-state conductance.

In 2005 Boyden, Zhang, Bamberg, Nagel and Deisseroth expressed that channel with a lentivirus in cultured rat hippocampal neurons and showed reliable millisecond-timescale control of spiking. The neurons came from newborn rats; there was no intact animal and no behaviour. Other groups expressed the channel in neurons around the same period, so there is no useful single “inventor” or “first” to award. The specific advance was control of spikes in chosen cells, which made a sufficiency experiment possible. The channel's gating and selectivity belong to [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/), and genetic targeting connects to [CRISPR and functional genomics](/lessons/crispr-and-functional-genomics/).

### When the construct name misleads

Three failure modes deserve recordings rather than assumptions. Long channelrhodopsin-driven depolarization can suppress spikes through depolarization block, so driving harder can silence the cell meant to be driven. Anion-conducting silencers can excite compartments with high internal chloride, including some axonal compartments. Abruptly ending silencing can produce rebound firing, so the post-light window belongs in the result. Validate the physiology in the preparation during and after illumination.
