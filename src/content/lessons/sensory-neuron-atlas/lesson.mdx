techniques:
  - name: "Single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      Dissociated cells, or nuclei isolated from frozen tissue, are each given a barcode so that
      their RNA can be sequenced and counted one cell at a time, then grouped by similarity.
      Whole cells and nuclei capture different RNA, so the method of preparation can change which
      groups appear.
  - name: "Cre, CreER and Cre/Flp intersectional genetics"
    blurb: >-
      A recombinase expressed under a marker gene's regulatory sequences switches on a reporter or an actuator in
      cells that expressed that marker at some time; CreER adds a tamoxifen-timed window, and
      requiring both Cre and Flp restricts the label to cells that expressed two markers. Each
      line is validated by its specificity and its efficiency.
  - name: "In vivo DRG calcium imaging"
    blurb: >-
      A genetically encoded calcium indicator such as GCaMP is expressed in sensory neurons, the
      ganglion is exposed in an anaesthetized mouse, and hundreds of cell bodies are imaged while
      the skin is stimulated. It reports which neurons respond, but the slow signal does not count
      spikes.
  - name: "Spatial transcriptomics with in situ hybridization"
    blurb: >-
      RNA from a tissue section is captured on an array of barcoded spots (55 µm across on the
      Visium platform), so expression keeps its position; single-molecule in situ hybridization
      then checks a marker in individual cells. A spot over a large neuron still mixes in RNA
      from neighbouring cells.

**Single-cell sequencing has split the sensory neurons of the dorsal root ganglion into many molecular kinds, finer than the C, Aδ and Aβ fibre classes. A cluster becomes a cell type only when something other than its RNA ties it to an ending, a response or a job.** One mouse data set found eleven kinds, a larger one seventeen, and a 2024 merger of 31 data sets from six species eighteen. None of the three counts is wrong, and none is final. This lesson asks what an atlas count measures, and what more it takes before a cluster of transcriptomes deserves to be called a cell type.

[Touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) sorted afferents into Aβ, Aδ and C fibres, and [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/) weighed labelled lines against population codes. [Sequencing and genomes](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/) and [recording many neurons](/lessons/large-scale-neuroscience/) explain how single-cell sequencing works and what a brain atlas is. Here the question is narrower: when is a cluster a type, and when is it a state, a resolution, or an artefact of how the cells were prepared?

## Eleven, seventeen, eighteen: why the number moves

The dorsal root ganglion (DRG) holds the cell bodies of the neurons that innervate skin, muscle and viscera. Usoskin, Ernfors and colleagues (2015) dissociated lumbar DRGs from mice, sequenced the RNA of single neurons, and let an unsupervised method group them into eleven classes. Some classes suggested a job. NP3, a non-peptidergic class, for example, was linked by the authors to itch. Expression is a hypothesis about function, not proof of it; [itch](/lessons/itch/) takes it further.

Zeisel and colleagues (2018) then profiled a far larger number of cells across the mouse nervous system and arranged them in a hierarchical taxonomy. Within peripheral sensory neurons they resolved eight peptidergic, six non-peptidergic and three neurofilament types: seventeen. The class names, sample sizes and the size of the whole census are under Going deeper.

Think of a map. A country-level map and a county-level map of the same land are both correct; they differ in resolution. Broad classes (peptidergic, non-peptidergic, neurofilament) and fine subtypes sit at different levels of one tree. More cells, deeper sequencing or a finer clustering setting can split branches; a different preparation can also hide or reveal one.

Bhuiyan, Renthal and colleagues (2024) integrated 31 DRG and trigeminal ganglion data sets from six mammalian species (human, mouse, rat, guinea pig and two macaque species) into a single reference with eighteen DRG and fifteen trigeminal neuronal subtypes. The authors stressed that preparation, sequencing depth and analysis change how many subtypes are resolved. One of their eighteen groups is defined by injury-induced transcription factors, the Atf3 group, which is a warning that an atlas can mix types with states. They also cautioned that one molecular subtype can behave differently at different innervation targets.

Keep the fibre labels apart from the clusters. Among low-threshold mechanoreceptors (LTMRs, neurons activated by innocuous touch), C, Aδ and Aβ mean unmyelinated, lightly myelinated and heavily myelinated axons (Li and colleagues, 2011). They describe axons, not single transcriptomic clusters. And C does not mean nociceptor: in adult mouse, neurons positive for tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) were identified as C-fibre LTMRs ([the LTMR lesson](/lessons/ltmr-classes/) builds the full class table).

## From a cluster to a driver line

A cluster becomes a functional type only when something other than its RNA ties it to a job. The usual route is genetic access. A **Cre driver** expresses Cre recombinase under a marker gene's regulatory sequences, either knocked into the gene's own locus or carried on a transgene, so a reporter marks cells that expressed the marker at some time; **CreER** is switched on by tamoxifen, so the experimenter chooses the window ([tools for development](/lessons/tools-for-development/) covers the genetics). An **intersectional** strategy requires two recombinases, Cre and Flp, so that only cells that expressed both markers are labelled.

Every line has two separate quality numbers.

> **specificity = labelled and marker-positive cells / all labelled cells**
> **efficiency = labelled and marker-positive cells / all marker-positive cells**

In a made-up validation, a line labels 200 neurons, 180 of which express the marker by in situ hybridization, and the sections hold 300 marker-positive neurons. Specificity = 180 / 200 = 90%. Efficiency = 180 / 300 = 60%. The line is good for imaging the right cells but weak for an ablation: killing 60% of a population and seeing no deficit does not show the population is unneeded.

Timing is part of the definition. With TrkC-CreER intersected with a Ret reporter (Ret-f(CFP)), Bai and colleagues (2015) found that 3 mg tamoxifen at E12.5 labelled Merkel-associated Aβ SA1 endings, while 0.1 mg at P5 favoured Aβ field-LTMR circumferential endings. Age and dose both differed, so this is not an age-only comparison, but the same driver gave two populations. A CreER label records expression history, not a fixed identity.

The mouse DRG toolkit of Qi, Iskols, Sharma, Ginty and colleagues (2024) gathered such lines for the main subtypes: Th2A-CreER for C-LTMRs, TrkB-CreER for Aδ-LTMRs, Ret-CreER for Aβ rapidly adapting LTMRs, and new knock-ins such as Trpm8-T2a-FlpO. An intersection of Smr2-Cre with Calca-FlpE restricted labelling to an SMR2- and CGRP-positive population with free endings. The team then imaged calcium in the L4 DRG of anaesthetized young mice while indenting the skin with forces from 1 to 75 mN, heating it or cooling it. The authors reported that each genetically defined subtype had its own threshold and range of responses to mechanical or thermal stimuli. Together the subtypes tiled the range of forces and temperatures tested, so more subtypes were recruited as a stimulus grew stronger. Some variety remained inside a group: TRPM8 neurons, for example, showed three kinds of cooling response. On this evidence, the transcriptomic subtypes were also physiologically distinct.

Read such imaging with its limits. In adult mouse DRG imaged in vivo, the GCaMP6 signal rises and decays slowly compared with an action potential (Chisholm and colleagues, 2018). Single action potentials were detected in most cases, but spikes closer together than the indicator's rise and decay times summate, so a single transient does not give a spike count and its amplitude tracks firing rate only roughly. The firmest bridge between RNA and function is a measurement in the same cell. **Patch-seq** records a neuron with a patch electrode, then aspirates its contents for sequencing, so that physiology and gene expression come from the same cell; Cadwell and colleagues (2016) demonstrated it in mouse neocortex, and Parpaite and colleagues (2021) applied it to mouse DRG neurons, matching types of mechanically activated current to transcriptomic populations.

## How identity is built, and how injury takes it apart

DRG neurons arise from the neural crest ([the neural crest](/lessons/neural-crest/)). Sharma, Ginty and colleagues (2020) sequenced mouse DRG neurons from embryonic stages through to adulthood. Newborn sensory neurons coexpressed transcription factors that later became restricted to particular subtypes. Unspecialized here means unspecialized as to subtype; the cells were already neurons. Identity narrowed over weeks, like a sculpture cut from a shared block.

Does the target help cut? NGF from targets keeps TrkA-expressing neurons alive ([Trk and p75](/lessons/trk-and-p75/)). An Ngf knockout alone would therefore lose cells, and any change in the survivors could be selection rather than altered identity. Deleting Bax, which the neurons need to die by apoptosis, removes that confound. At P0, Ngf-null;Bax-null DRGs had fewer transcriptionally distinct populations than Bax-null controls. NGF contributes to subtype specialization; the experiment does not show that NGF alone sets every fate, and it is a mouse developmental result.

Identity can also be taken apart. Renthal and colleagues (2020) injured peripheral nerves in adult mice and sequenced single DRG nuclei. Injury suppressed the expression that marks each subtype. Over a time course associated with axons regrowing to their targets, identity re-emerged, and Atf3 was required for the full injury reprogramming. "Associated with" is the right verb: the timing fits reinnervation, but timing alone does not prove that reinnervation restores identity, and an apparent recovery could also come from which neurons survived or were sampled. Lineage tracing of injured neurons is what separates those readings, and the study used reporters for this: the share of neurons labelled by an Atf3 injury reporter was unchanged from one week to two months, which argues against selective death, and Mrgprd reporter tracing fit retained identity.

The lesson for atlases is direct. Tissue from an injured, inflamed or diseased ganglion will contain states. An Atf3-rich cluster is a condition that existing neurons enter and can leave, not a new permanent type, a point that matters for [chronic pain](/lessons/chronic-pain/).

## Mouse markers that do not transfer

Human DRG atlases are often built from nuclei or from tissue sections, because donor tissue is often frozen and large human neurons are hard to dissociate intact. Nguyen and colleagues (2021) sequenced 1,837 neuronal nuclei from lumbar DRGs of donors aged 34 to 55. Some human classes had no clear mouse equivalent in their comparisons. One, H9, coexpressed TRPM8 and PIEZO2 transcripts, which suggests a cell sensitive to both cold and touch but does not show it. MRGPRX1, though not strongly expressed, localized selectively to H10. Primate MRGPRX genes have no one-to-one mouse counterparts, so do not rename a mouse Mrgpr population after a human gene. The 2024 atlas linked mouse groups to function using rodent genetics: Mrgprd neurons to nociception, Mrgpra3+Trpv1 coexpressing neurons to itch, and Mrgpra3+Mrgprb4 coexpressing neurons to C-LTMR physiology.

Spatial data keep anatomy but add a trap. Tavares-Ferreira and colleagues (2022) used Visium, an array of barcoded spots 55 µm across, on lumbar DRG sections from eight organ donors, four women and four men, and validated markers by RNAscope in situ hybridization.

**Worked problem: what does a spatial atlas count?** The authors selected 4,356 barcodes (spots) overlapping single neurons and retained 3,952 after filtering. They curated twelve neuronal clusters, five annotated as C nociceptors.

1. Retention = 3,952 / 4,356 = 0.9073, or 90.73%.
2. Check: 4,356 × 0.9073 = 3,952.2, which rounds to 3,952.
3. C-nociceptor fraction of cluster labels = 5 / 12 = 0.4167, or 41.67%.
4. Is either the fraction of human DRG neurons that are C nociceptors? No. The first is a filtering statistic. The second counts labels and ignores that clusters differ in size. Per-cluster spot counts come closer, because each kept spot was chosen to overlap a single neuron. They still estimate only the neurons sampled in those sections: a spot also picks up RNA from satellite glia and neighbours, which can affect which cluster it joins, and a few neurons fell under more than one spot.

The same study found TAC1 and CALCA, the genes for substance P and CGRP, broadly expressed across its human nociceptor clusters, so the authors suggest the mouse split into peptidergic and non-peptidergic nociceptors may not carry over as a clean binary. Jung and colleagues (2023) compared mouse, guinea pig, macaque and human DRG with one workflow. Tafa4 was found mainly in mouse C-LTMRs, but human TAFA4 was strongest in NTRK2- and SCN5A-positive Aδ-LTMRs, confirmed by in situ hybridization. A mouse mechanism tied to a marker needs a human check of which cells carry that marker. [Nociceptors and sodium channels](/lessons/nociceptors-and-sodium-channels/) meets the same problem with drug targets.

So what is a type? A working answer: a molecular group that recurs across data sets and is anchored by at least one independent measure (ending, physiology, target or behaviour after manipulation), read with its state and its resolution in mind. Some variety of response can remain inside it.

### Design problem

Nguyen and colleagues (2021) reported human DRG classes with no clear mouse equivalent, using single-nucleus sequencing. Yu and colleagues (2024), using deep sequencing of single human DRG somata, also reported likely human-specific types. The 2024 harmonized integration of 31 data sets found no human-specific neuronal clusters, though an integration is built to align data sets and can merge real differences. Design the work that would decide whether one proposed human-only class is real.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with the known confound. Nguyen and colleagues noted that whole-cell sequencing of mouse DRG had found a Trpa1-rich group that a compared mouse nuclear data set missed, so a mismatch between species may partly reflect what each method captures.

1. Match the preparation. Compare human nuclei with mouse nuclei (and, where possible, human whole cells with mouse whole cells), at similar sequencing depth and with the same clustering pipeline and resolution. If the class finds a mouse partner once the methods match, a method difference becomes the likely explanation, but check that the integration step has not simply forced the two species together.
2. Test robustness. Check that the class appears in several donors, not one, and survives reclustering at a few resolutions and integration methods. A class carried by one donor or one setting is fragile.
3. Validate in tissue. Pick two or three markers that define the class and do multiplex in situ hybridization on human DRG sections, counting single neurons that coexpress them and measuring their soma size. Do the same with the orthologous genes in mouse DRG. A coexpressing population present in human sections and absent from mouse ones supports a real difference in expression.
4. Tie it to function. Expression alone does not make a functional type. Record from human DRG neurons in culture or by Patch-seq, or use microneurography data where an afferent class can be matched, and ask whether cells with the marker profile share a physiology.

Expected outcomes: if the class disappears when methods are matched, and the matching did not rely on an integration that could merge real differences, it was probably an artefact; if it survives matching and tissue validation but has no distinct physiology, it is a real molecular difference of unknown function; only all four together would make it a functional human-mouse difference, and calling it human-specific would also need comparison with other species such as macaque.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Class lists and sample sizes in the first two atlases

Usoskin and colleagues sequenced the RNA of 622 single neurons from dissociated lumbar DRGs. The eleven classes were five neurofilament-rich classes (NF1 to NF5) with myelinated profiles, three non-peptidergic (NP1 to NP3), two peptidergic (PEP1, PEP2), and one marked by tyrosine hydroxylase (TH).

Zeisel and colleagues profiled roughly half a million cells across the mouse nervous system. The half million is the whole census, not a count of DRG neurons.

In adult mouse, TH-positive neurons were identified as C-fibre LTMRs, with a mean conduction velocity of 0.58 m/s in ex vivo skin-nerve recordings from five neurons.

### Calcium-indicator constants

In adult mouse DRG imaged in vivo under urethane, the GCaMP6 signal had a typical rise of about 200 ms and a decay time constant of about 700 ms (Chisholm and colleagues, 2018).

### Sampling schedule and cohort details

Sharma, Ginty and colleagues sequenced mouse DRG neurons at E11.5, E12.5, E15.5, P0, P5 and P28 to 42. Renthal and colleagues injured peripheral nerves in mice, mostly males aged 8 to 12 weeks.
