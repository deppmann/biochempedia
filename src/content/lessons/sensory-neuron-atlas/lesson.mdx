    profileSourceAnchor: "Usoskin, Furlan, Islam, Abdo, Lönnerberg et al., 'Unbiased classification of sensory neuron types by large-scale single-cell RNA sequencing', Nature Neuroscience 18:145–153 (2015), doi 10.1038/nn.3881."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Before single-cell sequencing, dorsal root ganglion neurons were sorted by a few markers at a
      time: peptides, lectin binding, neurofilament, Trk receptors. Dmitry Usoskin, Patrik Ernfors
      and colleagues, in work with Sten Linnarsson's sequencing group, took the opposite route.
      They dissociated lumbar ganglia from mice, captured single neurons,
      sequenced each one's RNA and let an unsupervised method group the 622 neuronal
      transcriptomes without being told the answer. Eleven classes came out. Five were named NF
      for neurofilament-rich, myelinated profiles, three NP for non-peptidergic, two PEP for
      peptidergic, and one TH for the tyrosine hydroxylase class that matches the C-fibre
      low-threshold mechanoreceptors. Some classes suggested a function, such as NP3, which the paper linked to itch. The paper is cited in the
      2015 issue, though it appeared online in 2014. What it made possible was a
      transcript-level parts list that many later DRG atlases compared themselves against.

    story: >-
      An atlas of adult neurons tells you what exists, not how it got that way. Nikhil Sharma and
      David Ginty, whom the paper's contribution statement credits jointly with its conception
      and design, sequenced mouse DRG neurons at E11.5, E12.5, E15.5, P0, P5 and P28 to 42, with

      factors that, later, were restricted to particular subtypes. Identity was not fixed at a
      neuron's birth; it narrowed over weeks. To ask whether the target contributes, the team

      deletion keeps neurons alive that would otherwise die without NGF, so a difference reflects
      identity rather than survival. The double mutants had fewer transcriptionally distinct
      populations. In 2025 the authors corrected a duplicated image in one extended data figure. The work made subtype identity a developmental process that can be
      perturbed and measured.

    story: >-
      A cluster in an atlas is a list of genes. To ask what the cells do, you need a way to mark
      them in a living animal. The 2024 Cell paper on which Lijun Qi is first author, from the laboratories of
      David Ginty and Nikhil Sharma, set out to provide that for the main mouse DRG subtypes. It drew on
      recombinase lines keyed to marker genes from the transcriptomic data: Th2A-CreER for
      C-fibre low-threshold mechanoreceptors, TrkB-CreER for Aδ ones, Ret-CreER for Aβ rapidly
      adapting ones, an intersection of Smr2-Cre with Calca-FlpE for one peptidergic population
      with free endings, and new knock-ins such as Trpm8-T2a-FlpO. The team followed labelled
      endings in the skin, then imaged calcium in the L4 ganglion of anaesthetized
      young mice while touching, heating or cooling the skin. In the sampled mouse hairy skin, ten indentation-responsive groups had overlapping
      threshold distributions across the tested 1 to 75 mN force range, with additional groups
      recruited at higher forces, while TRPM8-positive and SSTR2-positive groups did not respond
      to step indentation; thermally responsive groups covered different tested temperature
      ranges, with some responding to temperature change rather than an absolute temperature and
      some groups unresponsive in each modality. The toolkit turned atlas clusters into populations that can be labelled,
      imaged and manipulated one at a time.
  - name: "William Renthal"
    contribution: >-
      First author of a 2020 Neuron study showing that peripheral nerve injury suppresses subtype
      identity in mouse DRG neurons and that subtype-associated expression returns over a time
      course associated with regrowth after sciatic crush, and corresponding author of the 2024
      harmonized cross-species atlas of sensory ganglia.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Renthal, Tochitsky, Yang, Cheng, Li, Kawaguchi et al., Neuron (2020), doi 10.1016/j.neuron.2020.07.026; Bhuiyan, Xu, Yang et al., Science Advances 10:eadj9173 (2024), doi 10.1126/sciadv.adj9173."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      If identity is a transcriptional program, can it be undone? William Renthal and colleagues
      studied several peripheral axotomy models, including nerve cut and crush, in mice (mostly
      males aged 8 to 12 weeks) and sequenced single nuclei from the DRG. After injury, injured
      neurons of many subtypes lost much of the expression that marked their subtype, while
      injury-induced genes rose. After sciatic crush, subtype-associated expression re-emerged
      over a time course associated with axonal regrowth, whereas more neurons remained in the
      injury state after ligated transection. Conditional deletion of Atf3, a transcription factor
      induced by injury, showed that it was required for the full transcriptional reprogramming. Four years later Renthal was the
      corresponding author, with first author Bhuiyan and colleagues, of an atlas that harmonized
      31 data sets from six mammalian species into one reference for the dorsal root and
      trigeminal ganglia. Together the two papers made the distinction between a cell type and a
      cell state something that can be measured, rather than argued about.

techniques:
  - name: "Single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      Dissociated cells, or nuclei isolated from frozen tissue, are each given a barcode so that
      their RNA can be sequenced and counted one cell at a time, then grouped by similarity.
      Whole cells and nuclei capture different RNA, so the method of preparation can change which
      groups appear.
  - name: "Cre, CreER and Cre/Flp intersectional genetics"
    blurb: >-
      A recombinase expressed under a marker gene's regulatory sequences switches on a reporter or an actuator in
      cells that expressed that marker at some time; CreER adds a tamoxifen-timed window, and
      intersectional strategies—such as pairing Cre and Flp with a dual-dependent reporter, or
      combining CreER with a reporter under a second gene's regulatory control—restrict the label
      to cells sharing two expression histories. Each line is validated by its specificity and its
      efficiency.
  - name: "In vivo DRG calcium imaging"
    blurb: >-
      A genetically encoded calcium indicator such as GCaMP is expressed in sensory neurons, the
      ganglion is exposed in an anaesthetized mouse, and hundreds of cell bodies are imaged while
      the skin is stimulated. It reports which neurons respond, but the slow signal does not count
      spikes.
  - name: "Spatial transcriptomics with in situ hybridization"
    blurb: >-
      RNA from a tissue section is captured on an array of barcoded spots (55 µm across on the
      Visium platform), so expression keeps its position; single-molecule in situ hybridization
      then checks a marker in individual cells. A spot over a large neuron still mixes in RNA
      from neighbouring cells.

    choices:
      - "The full set of somatosensory neuron types in mammals"
      - "The subtypes that survived later cross-species merging"
      - "The number of Trk receptors multiplied by fibre classes"
      - "The resolution reached by one early mouse data set"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Eleven is what unsupervised clustering resolved in 622 dissociated mouse neurons; a later
      mouse census with a different sample and analysis resolved seventeen peripheral sensory
      types (Zeisel et al., 2018), so eleven is not the full set of mammalian types. The count
      has nothing to do with Trk receptors multiplied by fibre classes. The cross-species
      integration of Bhuiyan et al. (2024) produced eighteen DRG neuronal groups, not eleven.
  - stem: "A CreER line is induced in mice and labels 150 DRG neurons in a set of sections. Of those, 120 express the target marker by in situ hybridization. The same sections contain 240 marker-positive neurons in all. What are the line's specificity and efficiency?"
    choices:
      - "Specificity 50%, efficiency 80%"
      - "Specificity 80%, efficiency 62.5%"
      - "Specificity 80%, efficiency 50%"
      - "Specificity 62.5%, efficiency 50%"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Specificity is labelled-and-marker-positive cells over all labelled cells, 120 / 150 = 80%.
      Efficiency is the same numerator over all marker-positive cells, 120 / 240 = 50%. Specificity
      50% with efficiency 80% swaps the two definitions. Efficiency 62.5% divides the 150 labelled
      cells by the 240 marker-positive cells, which counts off-target labelled cells as hits.
      Specificity 62.5% makes the same error in the other quantity.
  - stem: "Sharma et al. (2020) compared P0 DRGs from Ngf-null;Bax-null mice with DRGs from Bax-null littermates. Why was Bax deleted in both groups?"
    choices:
      - "To stop NGF from reaching the neurons' targets"
      - "To label NGF-dependent neurons for sequencing"
      - "To keep alive neurons that would die without NGF"
      - "To replace NGF signalling through the TrkA receptor"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Without NGF many DRG neurons die by Bax-dependent apoptosis, so an Ngf knockout alone would
      lose cells and could not show whether the survivors' identity changed. Deleting Bax in both
      groups keeps those neurons for profiling. Bax deletion does not block NGF delivery to
      targets, does not act as a genetic label for sequencing, and does not substitute for TrkA
      signalling; it removes a death effector.
  - stem: "A cluster of mouse DRG neurons is defined only by its RNA profile. A group builds a driver line, confirms reporter overlap with the cluster's defining markers and validates coverage, then images calcium in the labelled neurons while indenting the skin. The labelled neurons respond within a distinct range of forces compared with a neighbouring subtype. What does this add to the claim that the cluster is a cell type?"
    choices:
      - "An independent physiological anchor, so the cluster is more than a result of clustering settings"
      - "Proof that the cluster will have the same count in every future atlas"
      - "Proof that the cluster is a permanent type and not a state"
      - "A direct count of the spikes fired by each neuron in the cluster"
    answer: 0
    rationale: "Once overlap and coverage are validated, a distinct stimulus range measured in the labelled cells provides an independent physiological anchor supporting a functional claim under the conditions tested. It does not fix how many clusters a future atlas will resolve, since the count depends on resolution and preparation. It does not exclude a state, which needs separate state or lineage checks. Calcium transients do not give exact spike counts."
  - stem: "After sciatic nerve crush in adult mice, single-nucleus sequencing of the DRG shows a cluster rich in Atf3 that is absent from uninjured ganglia and shrinks as axons regenerate. How is this cluster best classified?"
    choices:
      - "A new permanent subtype made by adult neurogenesis"
      - "A state that injured neurons enter and later leave"
      - "Contaminating satellite glia in the neuronal data"
      - "A human-specific class revealed by the mouse injury"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Renthal et al. (2020) found that injury suppressed subtype identity and that after sciatic
      crush subtype-associated expression returned over a time course associated with axonal
      regrowth (whereas more neurons remained in the injury state after ligated transection), so
      an Atf3-rich group is best read as a state of existing neurons. The harmonized atlas lists
      such a group among its eighteen, a reminder that atlases can mix types and states. Nothing
      here indicates new neurons being born. Atf3 is induced in injured neurons themselves, so
      glial contamination does not explain the cluster. The data are from mice, so a
      human-specific class is not at issue.

  - stem: "In a made-up spatial transcriptomic study of human DRG sections, 5,000 capture spots overlapping neurons were selected and 4,500 passed quality filtering. The retained spots fell into 10 clusters, 4 of them annotated as nociceptor clusters that together contained 1,200 spots. A student reports that the study shows 40% of human DRG neurons are nociceptors. Which statement is best supported?"
    choices:
      - "26.7% of kept spots fall in nociceptor clusters, not cells"
      - "40% of the neurons are nociceptors, from 4 of 10 clusters"
      - "24% of spots are nociceptors, from 1,200 of 5,000 spots"
      - "90% of the neurons are nociceptors, from 4,500 of 5,000"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The fraction of retained spots in nociceptor clusters is 1,200 / 4,500 = 26.7%, and even this is an
      estimate from sampled spots, not a direct count of neurons: a spot also picks up RNA from
      neighbouring cells, and one neuron can fall under more than one spot. Four of
      ten is a fraction of cluster labels and ignores that clusters differ in size. Dividing by
      5,000 uses spots that were filtered out before clustering. The ratio 4,500 / 5,000 is a
      filtering statistic that says nothing about cell type.
  - stem: "In single-nucleus data, Jung et al. (2023) found Tafa4 transcripts mainly in C-fibre low-threshold mechanoreceptors in mouse DRG, whereas in human DRG the strongest TAFA4 expression was in the NTRK2- and SCN5A-positive cluster annotated as Aδ low-threshold mechanoreceptors, with in situ hybridization supporting the species shift by confirming TAFA4 and NTRK2 coexpression. A group plans a human study based on a mouse experiment that manipulated Tafa4-expressing C fibres. What should they conclude first?"
    choices:
      - "TAFA4 protein must perform a new function in humans"
      - "The mouse cell-type result needs checking in human cells"
      - "Nuclear RNA cannot detect TAFA4 in human C fibres"
      - "Human Aδ cells are C fibres mislabelled by sequencing"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The same gene marks different populations in the two species, so a mechanism shown in mouse
      Tafa4-positive C fibres cannot be assumed to act in the same human cells. In situ
      hybridization supported the human shift by confirming TAFA4 and NTRK2 coexpression and
      soma-size differences on tissue sections, so sequencing mislabelling or a nuclear-detection
      artefact alone is not the simplest reading. Expression data say where the transcript is,
      not that the protein has a new function.
  - stem: "In mice carrying TrkC-CreER and a Ret reporter, Bai et al. (2015) gave 3 mg tamoxifen at E12.5 in one group and 0.1 mg at P5 in another. E12.5 induction labelled Merkel-associated Aβ SA1 endings, while P5 induction mainly labelled Aβ field-LTMR circumferential endings. Which conclusion do the data support?"
    choices:
      - "Field-LTMRs form one subset of the SA1 afferent population"
      - "SA1 neurons turn into field-LTMRs during the first postnatal week"
      - "TrkC protein is required for Merkel endings to form in the skin"
      - "Induction conditions, including timing, change which neurons are labelled"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      CreER labels the cells expressing the driver when tamoxifen is given, so different induction
      conditions (here, ages and doses) capture different populations, and the label is a record of expression history rather
      than a fixed type. Nothing in the data tracks the same neurons changing class, so a switch

      A transcriptomic cluster is best treated as a candidate type: check that it recurs and
      assess whether it reflects a shared state, use independent anatomy (such as ending

      evidence to support a specific functional claim under the conditions tested. Each atlas

      atlases show broad TAC1 and CALCA expression across nociceptor clusters, and a gene such as

**Two mouse studies resolved eleven and seventeen sensory neuron groups, respectively, and a later six-species integration resolved eighteen DRG groups—each count shaped by its sample and analysis.** The first film showed how resolution, preparation and cell state can change a cluster count without adding new neurons; here we examine the experiments behind those maps, how genetic access tests function, how identity shifts in development and injury, and where mouse maps break down in humans.

First, two definitions. A **cluster** is a group of cells with similar measured RNA profiles. A **state** is a condition an existing cell can enter and leave. ([Touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) introduced Aβ, Aδ and C fibres; [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/) compared labelled lines with population codes; [sequencing and genomes](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/) and [recording many neurons](/lessons/large-scale-neuroscience/) cover the sequencing and atlas methods.)

> **Guiding idea.** Treat a transcriptomic cluster as a candidate type: check that it recurs and assess whether it reflects a shared state; independent anatomy can anchor the population, and physiological or causal evidence can support a specific functional claim under the conditions tested.
>
> **Scope:** This rule is for interpreting sensory-neuron atlases, not a universal definition of cell type. The explicit state check qualifies the first film's two checks (recurrence and an independent anchor), because a recurrent state can also show distinct physiology, while shared endings or targets anchor anatomy without by themselves establishing function. Counts and boundaries still depend on sample size, preparation, clustering resolution and cell state; an unanchored molecular class remains useful for a narrower claim.

## What each atlas actually counted

The **dorsal root ganglion (DRG)** holds cell bodies of sensory neurons innervating skin, muscle and viscera. The first film showed why higher clustering resolution splits branches; here are the three atlases behind eleven, seventeen and eighteen:

| Atlas | Sample | Groups resolved | Key reading lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Usoskin, Ernfors et al. (2015) | 622 dissociated mouse lumbar DRG neurons | 11 classes (`NF`, `NP`, `PEP`, `TH`) | Unsupervised clustering; `NP3` suggested itch without proving it ([itch](/lessons/itch/) adds later evidence) |
| Zeisel et al. (2018) | ~500,000 mouse nervous-system cells (whole census, not a DRG count) | 17 peripheral sensory types (8 peptidergic, 6 non-peptidergic, 3 neurofilament) | Hierarchical tree: broad classes and fine subtypes sit at different levels |
| Bhuiyan, Renthal et al. (2024) | 23 DRG data sets from human, mouse, rat, guinea pig, cynomolgus macaque and rhesus macaque, plus 8 trigeminal data sets from human and mouse (31 total) | 18 DRG and 14 trigeminal groups | Includes an `Atf3` injury-state group; one subtype can behave differently at different innervation targets |

More cells or deeper sequencing can reveal previously unresolved groups (or intermediates, or no change). With the data fixed, a finer clustering setting can split a group, as in the first film's schematic, whereas real clusterings across different settings need not nest cleanly—and the eleven and seventeen groups came from studies with different samples and analyses. By contrast, a preparation difference can hide RNA or cells rather than merely changing zoom (Bhuiyan, Renthal and colleagues stressed that preparation, depth and analysis shape resolution). The `Atf3` group, defined by injury-induced transcription factors, warns that an atlas can mix a state alongside types.

Do not equate fibre labels with transcriptomic clusters. Among **low-threshold mechanoreceptors (LTMRs)**—neurons activated by innocuous touch—C, Aδ and Aβ mean unmyelinated, lightly myelinated and heavily myelinated axons (Li and colleagues, 2011). C does not mean nociceptor: adult mouse tyrosine-hydroxylase-positive (TH+) neurons were identified as C-fibre LTMRs ([the LTMR lesson](/lessons/ltmr-classes/) builds the full class table; Going deeper lists class codes and conduction velocity).

## From a candidate cluster to a functional test

In mice, researchers can link a cluster to physiology through **genetic access**—by validating reporter overlap and coverage against the RNA-defined population, then measuring the labelled cells ([tools for development](/lessons/tools-for-development/) covers the genetics). A **Cre driver** expresses Cre recombinase under a marker gene's regulatory sequences (from the gene's locus or a transgene), switching on a reporter in cells that expressed that marker. **CreER** adds a tamoxifen-controlled time window. One **intersectional** strategy pairs Cre and Flp with a reporter requiring both recombination events; labelled cells share both driver expression histories, which can occur at different times. Other intersectional strategies combine CreER with a reporter under a second gene's regulatory control.

Every line needs two quality numbers—**specificity** (how clean the label is) and **efficiency** (how much of the target population it catches):

> **specificity = labelled and marker-positive cells / all labelled cells**
>
> **efficiency = labelled and marker-positive cells / all marker-positive cells**

**Predict.** A made-up line labels 200 neurons. Of those, 180 express the target marker, while the sections contain 300 marker-positive neurons altogether. Can the line be highly specific but only moderately efficient?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes. Specificity is 180 / 200 = 90%, because 180 of 200 labelled cells express the marker (leaving 20 off-target labelled cells). Efficiency is 180 / 300 = 60%, because 120 of 300 marker-positive cells (40%) go unlabelled—and the captured subset may be biased. The line could support imaging after checking off-target labelling and which target cells it misses. Reporter coverage alone does not measure the fraction killed in an ablation experiment; even if ablation independently verified removal of 60% of target cells, the remaining 40% could still support the behaviour.

</details>

### Anchoring clusters with physiology

The first film previewed how labelled endings and skin stimulation anchor clusters; Qi, Iskols, Sharma, Ginty and colleagues (2024) built that toolkit across the major mouse DRG subtypes (Going deeper lists lines). Intersecting `Smr2-Cre` with `Calca-FlpE` (`Calca` encodes α-CGRP) labelled an SMR2- and CGRP-positive population with free endings. Exposing the L4 DRG in anaesthetized young mice, they imaged calcium while applying 1 to 75 mN step indentations to hairy thigh skin or thermal steps (5 to 55 °C from a 32 °C baseline). In the sampled hairy skin, ten indentation-responsive groups had overlapping threshold distributions across the tested force range, with additional groups recruited at higher forces, while TRPM8-positive and SSTR2-positive groups did not respond to step indentation. Thermally responsive groups covered different tested temperature ranges, and some responded to a temperature change rather than an absolute temperature, with unresponsive groups in each modality. Heterogeneity remained within groups—TRPM8 neurons showed three kinds of cooling response—so physiology supported functional claims under the conditions tested without making every cell identical.

### Two limits: induction history and calcium kinetics

First, **CreER records expression history, not a fixed identity.** Bai and colleagues (2015) combined `TrkC-CreER` with `Ret-f(CFP)`, a Cre-dependent CFP reporter at the *Ret* locus (requiring `TrkC-CreER` activity during the tamoxifen window and `Ret` expression, without Flp). Giving 3 mg tamoxifen at embryonic day 12.5 (E12.5) labelled Merkel-associated Aβ SA1 endings; giving 0.1 mg at postnatal day 5 (P5) favoured Aβ field-LTMR circumferential endings around hair follicles.

**Predict.** Can that difference be attributed to induction age alone when both age and tamoxifen dose changed?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Age and dose changed together, so the experiment does not isolate an effect of age; it shows that induction conditions change which expression history is captured. Nor did it track the same neurons changing class, so a CreER label is not evidence that SA1 neurons turn into field-LTMRs.

</details>

Second, **calcium imaging does not count spikes.** In adult mouse DRG imaged in vivo, GCaMP6 rises and decays slowly compared with an action potential (Chisholm and colleagues, 2018; Going deeper gives time constants). It detected single action potentials in most cases, but nearby spikes summate, so amplitude tracks firing rate only roughly. **Patch-seq** links physiology and RNA directly: record a neuron with a patch electrode, then aspirate and sequence that same cell (demonstrated by Cadwell and colleagues, 2016, in mouse neocortex; applied by Parpaite and colleagues, 2021, in mouse DRG to match mechanically activated currents to transcriptomic populations).

### Development: the `Bax`-null control behind NGF

Mouse DRG neurons arise from [the neural crest](/lessons/neural-crest/) before the post-mitotic stages shown in the second film. From those post-mitotic neurons ("unspecialized" refers only to subtype identity), broad transcription-factor coexpression narrowed into subtype combinations over weeks (Sharma, Ginty and colleagues, 2020; Going deeper gives ages). The second film showed why the researchers kept the affected neurons alive; in the experiment itself, `Bax-null` littermates provided the matched genetic control.

Target-derived NGF keeps TrkA-expressing neurons alive ([Trk and p75](/lessons/trk-and-p75/)). Deleting `Ngf` alone causes Bax-dependent apoptosis among NGF-dependent neurons, confounding altered identity with selective survival. `Bax-null` is the matched control. At P0, double mutants had fewer transcriptionally distinct populations: NGF contributes to subtype specialization, though this mouse experiment does not show NGF alone sets every fate.

### Peripheral nerve injury: necessity and lineage tracing

The second film depicted a schematic axon cut and recovery. In the underlying study, Renthal and colleagues (2020; cohort details in Going deeper) tested several peripheral axotomy models in adult mice, including nerve cut and crush, pulling DRG neurons into a shared `Atf3`-rich injury state. After sciatic crush, subtype-associated expression returned over a time course associated with axonal regrowth, whereas more neurons remained in the injury state after ligated transection. The study paired single-nucleus sequencing with targeted genetic and lineage experiments:

- **Necessity:** conditional `Atf3` deletion, profiled by single-nucleus sequencing after crush, tested whether full transcriptional reprogramming depends on ATF3.
- **Survival vs. recovery:** as the second film noted for the injury-reporter time comparison, after sciatic crush the fraction of `Atf3`-reporter-positive neurons measured by in situ hybridization showed no detectable decline from one week to two months (arguing against widespread selective death), and separate `Mrgprd` lineage tracing tested whether labelled neurons predominantly recovered their former molecular identity.

Still, **"associated with" matters.** Timing alone does not show that target reinnervation restores identity (hence the dashed arrow in the figure below).

## Human atlases: methods, markers and denominators

Keep the guiding rule in view: **treat a cluster as a candidate, check recurrence and cell state, use independent anatomy to anchor the population, and look for physiological or causal evidence to support a specific functional claim under the conditions tested.** Human DRG atlases often rely on nuclei or tissue sections because donor tissue is frequently frozen and large human neurons are hard to dissociate intact.

### Why mouse markers do not transfer by name

- **Same gene, different cell type.** Building on the second film's `TAFA4` example, Jung and colleagues (2023) ran one single-nucleus workflow across mouse, guinea pig, macaque and human DRG: `Tafa4` was mainly in mouse C-LTMRs, whereas in human single-nucleus data `TAFA4` was strongest in the `NTRK2`- and `SCN5A`-positive cluster annotated as Aδ-LTMR and also appeared in clusters annotated as cold-sensing neurons and C-LTMRs. In situ hybridization supported the human shift by confirming `TAFA4` and `NTRK2` coexpression and soma-size differences. A mouse mechanism tied to a marker needs a human cell-type check—the same drug-target issue met in [nociceptors and sodium channels](/lessons/nociceptors-and-sodium-channels/).
- **Blurry peptide binary.** Tavares-Ferreira and colleagues (2022) found `TAC1` (substance P) and `CALCA` (CGRP) broadly expressed across human nociceptor clusters, suggesting the mouse peptidergic/non-peptidergic split may not transfer cleanly.
- **No one-to-one counterparts.** Nguyen and colleagues (2021) sequenced 1,837 neuronal nuclei from lumbar DRGs of donors aged 34 to 55: several classes lacked clear mouse matches. `H9` coexpressed `TRPM8` and `PIEZO2` transcripts (suggesting cold and touch sensitivity, not demonstrating it), and `MRGPRX1`, though weakly expressed, localized selectively to `H10`. Primate `MRGPRX` genes have no one-to-one mouse counterparts, so mouse `Mrgpr` groups should not be renamed with human genes. The 2024 atlas annotated rodent groups using prior evidence about `Mrgprd`-associated nociception, `Mrgpra3`/`Trpv1`-associated itch, and a proposed C-LTMR-like `Mrgpra3`/`Mrgprb4` group (though marker-line labelling can span more than one population, and Qi and colleagues observed high-threshold responses in populations targeted with `Mrgprb4` and `Mrgpra3` strategies). Those annotations summarize rodent evidence rather than measuring physiology by coexpression alone, and they require independent functional testing in humans.

### Spatial transcriptomics and the denominator trap

Spatial data preserve anatomy but change the denominator. Tavares-Ferreira and colleagues (2022) used 55 µm Visium spots on lumbar DRG sections from eight organ donors (four women, four men), validating markers by RNAscope in situ hybridization. They selected 4,356 spots overlapping single neurons, retained 3,952 after filtering and curated twelve neuronal clusters, five called C nociceptors.

**Predict.** A student divides five C-nociceptor cluster labels by twelve labels and reports that 41.67% of human DRG neurons are C nociceptors. What did that denominator count?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It counted category labels, not neurons. The valid calculations are:

1. Retention = 3,952 / 4,356 = 0.9073, or 90.73% (check: 4,356 × 0.9073 = 3,952.2, rounding to 3,952).
2. 5 / 12 = 0.4167, or 41.67% **of cluster labels**—clusters need not be equal in size.

Per-cluster spot counts come closer to an abundance estimate because each kept spot overlapped one neuron, yet they still describe sampled sections rather than all human DRG neurons: spots pick up RNA from satellite glia and neighbours, and a few neurons lay under more than one spot.

</details>

### Design problem: is a proposed human-only class real?

Nguyen and colleagues (2021, single nuclei) and Yu and colleagues (2024, deep sequencing of single human DRG somata) both reported human classes without clear mouse equivalents, whereas the 2024 cross-species integration found no human-specific neuronal clusters—though an integration is built to align data sets and can merge real differences.

**Predict.** Why should tissue validation test two or three defining markers in the same neurons rather than one marker alone?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A cluster is defined by a pattern of RNA expression, and one marker may be shared across groups. Look for two or three markers whose combined expression distinguishes the group from neighbours; coexpression in tissue supports the population assignment when the panel discriminates that group, probe performance and sampling are adequate, and the pattern recurs across donors.

Nguyen and colleagues noted a preparation warning: mouse whole-cell data contained a `Trpa1`-rich group that a compared mouse nuclear data set missed. If a proposed human-only group gains a mouse partner once preparation is harmonized (without forced integration), a technical explanation becomes more plausible—though you should still check whether sequencing depth, donor or anatomical sampling, or analysis choices could also account for the match.

To test the claim fully:

1. **Match preparation.** Compare nuclei with nuclei and, where possible, whole cells with whole cells, at similar depth with the same pipeline and resolution.
2. **Test robustness.** Require the class across several donors, clustering resolutions and integration methods.
3. **Validate in tissue.** Use multiplex in situ hybridization for two or three defining markers in human and orthologous mouse genes, counting coexpressing neurons and measuring soma size. A coexpressing population present in human but absent in mouse sections supports a real expression difference.
4. **Anchor function.** Link recordings directly to the marker-defined cells (for example by Patch-seq, or by marker identification after recording in culture; microneurography generally offers an indirect match) and compare like physiological measurements in the corresponding mouse population.

If the class disappears once methods match (without forced integration), a technical or preparation explanation is more plausible, alongside potential effects of depth, donor or anatomical sampling, or analysis choices. Reproducible tissue expression supports a molecular difference; if the functional assays do not detect a difference, scope that conclusion to the properties tested, since other physiology, anatomy, projection targets or behaviour could still differ. All four steps support a human–mouse functional difference when comparable measurements in both species actually differ; checking another species such as macaque extends the comparison without proving universal human specificity, so report the proposed class as absent from the species and preparations examined.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Class lists, conduction velocity and toolkit lines

- **Usoskin et al. (2015) eleven classes:** five neurofilament-rich groups (`NF1` to `NF5`) with myelinated profiles, three non-peptidergic (`NP1` to `NP3`), two peptidergic (`PEP1`, `PEP2`) and one tyrosine-hydroxylase (`TH`) group.
- **Mouse C-LTMR conduction velocity:** adult mouse TH-positive C-fibre LTMRs had a mean conduction velocity of 0.58 m/s in ex vivo skin-nerve recordings from five neurons (Li and colleagues, 2011).
- **Qi et al. (2024) toolkit lines:** included `Th2A-CreER` (C-LTMRs), `TrkB-CreER` (Aδ-LTMRs), `Ret-CreER` (Aβ rapidly adapting LTMRs) and new knock-ins such as `Trpm8-T2a-FlpO`.

### Calcium-indicator constants

In adult mouse DRG imaged in vivo under urethane, GCaMP6 had a typical rise of about 200 ms and a decay time constant of about 700 ms (Chisholm and colleagues, 2018).

### Sampling schedule and cohort details

Sharma, Ginty and colleagues (2020) sampled mouse DRG neurons at E11.5, E12.5, E15.5, P0, P5 and P28 to 42. Renthal and colleagues (2020) injured peripheral nerves in mice, mostly males aged 8 to 12 weeks.
