  - name: "Activity-guided purification"
    blurb: >-
      Pass a tissue extract through a series of columns, and after each one measure how much
      protein is left and how much activity. Fold purification is activity per milligram compared
      with the starting extract. When one activity survives and another is lost as purity rises,
      the columns have separated two components.

**Adrenaline is still sitting in its receptor, and within minutes the cell's response to it has faded.** Nothing has taken the hormone away. What switched the receptor off? A kinase marked the active receptor, and a protein called arrestin read the mark and blocked the G protein. This lesson shows how that was proved, then follows the cell's other off switches down the chain, from the G protein to the messenger to the phosphates, and ends with a case where a steady signal shuts a system down and pulses do not.

## The idea: every step that starts a signal needs its own way to end

You followed the "on" pathway in the biochemistry course: adrenaline, receptor, G protein, adenylyl cyclase, cyclic AMP, protein kinase A, phosphorylated targets. Each step leaves something behind that would keep signaling if nothing removed it, so the cell has an off switch at each one. From the outside in:

1. **The ligand is removed.** The hormone falls off the receptor and is carried away.
2. **The receptor is uncoupled.** It is still occupied but can no longer reach its G protein.
3. **The receptor is taken inside.** It leaves the surface in a vesicle.
4. **The G protein's off step is sped up.** G-alpha cuts its own GTP, and for many G proteins a helper protein makes it cut sooner. The best-known helpers, RGS proteins, work mainly on the Gi and Gq families, not on the Gs of this pathway.
5. **The messenger is destroyed.** A phosphodiesterase breaks the ring of cyclic AMP.
6. **The phosphates are taken off.** Phosphatases reset the target proteins.

The second is the puzzle in the opening line. A response that fades while the hormone is still bound is called desensitization, and its explanation came from a purification that seemed to be going wrong.

## The purer kinase still adds phosphate but no longer switches the receptor off

Robert Lefkowitz's laboratory at Duke University had one firm clue: phosphate is added to the beta-adrenergic receptor as its response fades. The obvious suspect was protein kinase A, the kinase that cyclic AMP itself switches on.

Jeffrey Benovic, a graduate student in the laboratory, tested the suspect with the kin⁻ line of S49 mouse lymphoma cells, which has no working protein kinase A. He mixed an extract of those cells with hamster lung receptor, rebuilt into lipid vesicles, and radioactive ATP. Phosphorylation of the receptor increased when the agonist isoproterenol was present, and the antagonist alprenolol blocked the increase. So another kinase was at work, and it recognized the receptor's active shape.

He purified that kinase from cow brain cortex. The pure kinase still added phosphate to the receptor, as the crude material had. But a cruder batch had cut the receptor's ability to activate its G protein by a large amount, and the pure enzyme cut it by only a little.

Stop and work out what that means. The amount of phosphate on the receptor is the same and the effect has nearly vanished, so the phosphate cannot be the off switch by itself. Something else in the cruder batch was doing the switching, and each column removed more of it.

The missing piece came from studies of vision. Hermann Kühn, at Jülich in West Germany, had described an abundant retinal protein, then called the 48K protein, that works with rhodopsin kinase to shut off rhodopsin, the light receptor of the rod cell. It was later renamed arrestin, because it arrested rhodopsin's function. Lefkowitz arranged for Kühn to send some to Duke.

Benovic added it to the reconstituted system. With the pure kinase alone, the receptors were phosphorylated and could still switch on Gs. With kinase and arrestin together, the receptor's activity fell by about 40%. The controls show the mechanism:

- Bovine serum albumin, an unrelated protein, could not replace arrestin.
- Heat-denatured arrestin could not replace it either.
- Arrestin added to a receptor the kinase had never marked did nothing.

So there are two separate requirements, a mark and a reader. The kinase writes the mark, and only on an active receptor; enzymes of this kind are now called G-protein-coupled receptor kinases, or GRKs. Arrestin reads the mark and sits where the G protein needs to sit. Arrestin can also hang on by the marked tail alone, leaving room for a G protein, and some receptors keep signaling from inside the cell this way.

The retinal protein was a stand-in. Three years later Martin Lohse, a fellow in the same laboratory, found the version that works outside the retina, now called beta-arrestin. It reduced signaling by the kinase-marked receptor by more than 75% and did not act on rhodopsin.

## Arrestin carries the receptor into the cell

Arrestin also throws the third switch on the list. Clathrin is the protein that cages a patch of surface membrane and pulls it inward as a coated pit. In 1996 Benovic's laboratory, by then at Thomas Jefferson University in Philadelphia, reported with James Keen and Vsevolod Gurevich that the two non-visual arrestins, beta-arrestin 1 and beta-arrestin 2, bind clathrin directly and tightly. Hybrid arrestins that could not bind the receptor, or could not bind clathrin, carried the receptor into the cell less well. In intact cells the receptor, arrestin and clathrin came together in an agonist-dependent way. So the protein that blocks the G protein's binding site also pulls the receptor into a coated pit.

One line fits here: GPCRs can be unfaithful to trimeric G proteins. Once arrestin has taken the G protein's place, the receptor has a new partner with contacts of its own. Besides clathrin and the adaptor AP-2, arrestin can bind the kinase Src and parts of the ERK and JNK kinase cascades. How much signaling this allows with no G protein at all is still argued.

Going inside does not settle the matter either. A receptor in an endosome, the compartment that receives incoming vesicles, was long assumed to be out of service. In 2013 Roshanak Irannejad, in Marc von Zastrow's laboratory, found the receptor and its G protein switched on in the early endosome membrane too, which a later lesson takes up.

## The other off switches, and one rule for how receptors are switched off

**RGS proteins.** You met these in the lesson on GTPase switches: an RGS protein binds a G-alpha subunit while it holds GTP and makes it cut the GTP sooner. RGS4 and GAIP speed the cut at least 40 times for Gi alpha-1, and act on other members of the Gi family too, so a cell can shorten a response by making more of them.

**Phosphodiesterases.** A phosphodiesterase cuts one bond of the cyclic phosphate ring, and cyclic AMP becomes ordinary AMP, which does not switch on protein kinase A. At steady state, synthesis and breakdown run at the same rate. If breakdown is proportional to the amount of messenger present, then

> **messenger level at steady state = rate of synthesis ÷ rate constant of breakdown**

An inhibitor makes the bottom of that fraction smaller, so the messenger climbs higher and lingers longer. It cannot start a signal: if synthesis is zero, there is no messenger for it to protect.

Two drugs show this. Caffeine inhibits phosphodiesterases in a test tube. Be careful with the coffee story, though: at the concentration a few cups produce, most of its effect is thought to come from blocking adenosine receptors. Sildenafil is the cleaner case. It inhibits phosphodiesterase 5, which breaks down cyclic GMP, a close relative of cyclic AMP. In the smooth muscle of some blood vessels, nitric oxide switches on cyclic GMP synthesis and the muscle relaxes. Sildenafil makes that signal larger and longer once nitric oxide has started it.

**Phosphatases.** A protein that protein kinase A has phosphorylated stays switched until a phosphatase removes the phosphate, as you saw in the lesson on the phosphorylation switch.

**One rule.** An occupied receptor loses its G protein only when a dedicated protein acts on the receptor, and where the receptor sits does not tell you whether it is still signaling. A response can also fade farther down the chain, at the G protein, the messenger or the targets. The GnRH system, taken up in the next section, goes quiet under a constant signal even though its receptor has no tail to mark, and the block there seems to lie mostly below the receptor.

Two findings would break this rule. The first is an occupied receptor that loses its coupling to the G protein with nothing acting on it: no mark, no reader, no other bound protein. The second is a receptor that stops signaling every time it leaves the surface, in every cell you test.

## Constant GnRH shuts the pituitary down

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone, GnRH, is a peptide from the hypothalamus that tells the pituitary gland to release the gonadotropins, the hormones that drive the ovaries and testes. In 1978 Belchetz and colleagues studied rhesus monkeys whose hypothalamic lesions had abolished gonadotropin release. They gave GnRH either continuously or once an hour. Hourly pulses restored gonadotropin secretion, and a constant infusion did not. Clinics use the same effect on purpose: they keep a GnRH agonist present to shut gonadotropin output down.

Why would a steady signal fail where pulses work? One general possibility is that a cell's off switches build up while a signal is on and need a gap to reverse. Pulses would give the cell that gap, and a constant signal never would.

Which off switch does it in the pituitary? Not the one this lesson has taught. The mammalian GnRH receptor has no C-terminal tail, so it resists phosphorylation in response to agonist and resists rapid desensitization. A kinase mark and an arrestin cannot be the whole explanation.

Two kinds of candidate remain. A failure at the receptor would show up as fewer receptors on the surface, or as less second messenger made per dose. A failure downstream would leave the receptor's own output normal while hormone release falls. Count the receptors, measure second messenger output, and test an agent that bypasses the receptor, and you can tell the two apart. In gonadotrope cell lines the evidence points mainly downstream. The receptor resists rapid desensitization, but sustained GnRH causes a fast loss of the IP3 receptors that release calcium, so the calcium response fades. How much this explains in a patient on a GnRH agonist is less settled.

### Design problem

Design a scheme to down-regulate a receptor using PKC and PKA. Say which signals each kinase reports, why this differs from the kinase that marks only the occupied receptor, and what the cell gains by having both.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with what each kinase reports. Protein kinase A is switched on by cyclic AMP, so it reports that Gs-coupled receptors somewhere on the cell have been active. The conventional and novel forms of protein kinase C are switched on by diacylglycerol, with calcium also needed for the conventional forms, so it reports activity in the phospholipase C branch. Neither knows which receptor did the work.

Now the scheme. Give the receptor serines on its cytoplasmic side that match the target sequences of the two kinases. When either messenger rises, its kinase phosphorylates those sites and the receptor couples less well to its G protein. That is negative feedback, the motif from the lesson on signaling circuits.

A GRK reads the receptor's shape, so it marks mainly receptors that hold an agonist. Kinases A and C read the messenger, so they can mark any receptor that carries their sites, occupied or empty, including receptors for a different hormone. The first is called homologous desensitization and the second heterologous.

So the cell gains two controls with different reach. The messenger-reading kinases turn down every receptor that carries their sites when messenger levels are high. A GRK and arrestin silence only the receptors being hit, and leave the cell able to answer a different hormone.

## Going deeper

### The reconstitution numbers

He purified that kinase from cow brain cortex, enriching it 20,000 times. A batch purified only 350 times had cut the receptor's ability to activate its G protein by up to 80%, and the pure enzyme cut it by only 10 to 15%. With the pure kinase alone, the receptors carried an average of about 8 phosphates each and could still switch on Gs. With kinase and arrestin together, activity fell to about 59% of the untreated receptor's, a loss of about 40%. Arrestin added to a receptor the kinase had never marked left activity at 98.1% of the value without arrestin, across 13 measurements.

### Where GAIP sits

Marilyn Farquhar's laboratory asked where in the cell GAIP sits. It is held on a membrane by palmitate, a fatty acid bonded to the protein, and in 1998 the laboratory reported where it sits: on clathrin-coated vesicles, some budding from the surface and some from the Golgi. It was the first GTPase-activating protein found on clathrin-coated vesicles.

### The GnRH receptor has no tail

In 1999 Willars and colleagues showed that the missing C-terminal tail makes the GnRH receptor resist phosphorylation in response to agonist, and resist rapid desensitization. It works mainly through Gq and phospholipase C, so cyclic AMP is not the main messenger here.
