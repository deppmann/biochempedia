techniques:
  - name: "Comparative morphology"
    blurb: >-
      Record the same characters (symmetry, germ layers, body cavity, gut openings, segments) in
      many species and compare them. It describes body plans well, but a character that is gained
      or lost more than once will group animals by grade rather than by ancestry.
  - name: "Fluorescent lineage tracing with live imaging"
    blurb: >-
      Inject a fluorescent tracer into one identified early cell and follow its descendants as the
      embryo develops. It shows what a cell actually becomes, which is stronger evidence than
      where structures happen to lie next to each other.
  - name: "Molecular phylogenetics"
    blurb: >-
      Compare the sequences of the same gene, or of hundreds of genes, across species and build
      the tree that best explains the differences. Results depend on sampling and on the model of
      sequence change, so fast-changing sequences can mislead.
  - name: "Chromosome-scale synteny comparison"
    blurb: >-
      Compare which genes share a chromosome across whole genomes, using outgroups to tell an
      ancestral linkage from a derived fusion. Because gene sets that fuse and mix rarely separate
      again, a shared fusion is evidence of shared ancestry.

**A slipper snail embryo makes its mouth from the first opening of its gut. Its anus appears about 12 days after fertilization, from a different set of cells. For more than a century, that kind of fact has been used to sort animals into two great groups. Does it work?**

This lesson gives you the vocabulary for describing an animal's body, then shows where it agrees with ancestry and where it misleads. It builds on [what an organism has to do](/lessons/organism-problems/) and [size, scale and surface](/lessons/size-and-scaling/), which explained why a flatworm can manage with no heart.

## Characters you can see: symmetry, layers, cavities

A **body plan** is the basic layout an animal shares with its relatives. Four characters do most of the work.

**Symmetry.** A sponge has no consistent symmetry. **Radial symmetry** arranges body parts around a central axis, like slices of a pie; any cut through that axis gives similar halves. **Bilateral symmetry** allows only one plane that divides the body into roughly equivalent left and right halves, usually with a front end where sense organs cluster.

**Germ layers.** A **germ layer** is one of the sheets of cells in the early embryo from which tissues form. A **diploblastic** animal has two, an outer **ectoderm** and an inner **endoderm**. A **triploblastic** animal adds a middle **mesoderm**, which forms muscle and much of the body wall.

**Body cavity.** A **coelom** is a fluid-filled body cavity lined entirely by mesoderm. A **pseudocoelom** is a fluid-filled cavity that lacks a complete mesodermal lining around the gut. An **acoelomate** animal has tissue filling the region between gut and body wall. Keep the spaces apart: the gut lumen, where food goes, is not the coelom. A free-living acoelomate flatworm such as a planarian still has a gut.

**Early development.** In **cleavage**, the first cell divisions of the embryo, many annelids, molluscs and other spiralians divide in a tilted, **spiral** pattern; echinoderms and many other deuterostomes divide in a **radial** pattern, with cells stacked directly above one another. During **gastrulation** the embryo folds or spreads to form its germ layers and a primitive gut, and the **blastopore** is the opening that connects that forming gut to the outside.

Compare a few animals. A sponge has no true tissues: its feeding chambers are lined with **choanocytes**, collar cells that drive water through the body and catch food particles. The sea anemone *Nematostella vectensis* is usually described as diploblastic, has a gut with one opening and catches prey with **cnidocytes**, stinging cells. Yet it is not simply radial: it has a second body axis at right angles to its mouth-to-foot axis, patterned by Chordin and BMP signalling molecules, a story taken up in [evolution of development](/lessons/evolution-of-development/). A planarian flatworm is bilateral and acoelomate, with a one-opening gut. The nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans* has a pseudocoelom and a **through gut**, one that runs from mouth to anus. The earthworm *Lumbricus terrestris* has a coelom and a through gut too.

## Mouth first or mouth second, and the snail that tested it

In 1908 the Austrian zoologist Karl Grobben proposed splitting bilaterian animals by the fate of the blastopore. In **protostomes** ("mouth first") the blastopore becomes the mouth. In **deuterostomes** ("mouth second") the mouth forms separately, and the blastopore often becomes the anus. Textbooks pair protostomes with spiral cleavage and **schizocoely** (a coelom opening within masses of mesoderm), and deuterostomes with radial cleavage and **enterocoely** (pouches budding off the gut wall).

Deirdre Lyons, Kimberly Perry and Jonathan Henry checked the blastopore rule in 2015 in the slipper snail *Crepidula fornicata*, a marine gastropod whose early cells can be identified individually and given names such as 2d2. The team raised embryos at about 20 degrees C, injected fluorescent lineage tracers into chosen cells, and followed each cell's descendants by live imaging.

The textbook protostome picture predicted that the blastopore would become the mouth and that cells long called the "anal cells" would make the anus. Gastrulation happened by **epiboly**, a sheet of small cells spreading over the large, yolky ones. The blastopore did become the mouth. The anus formed separately and late, at about 12 days of development, from descendants of the 2d2 cell. The so-called anal cells did not form the anus.

So *Crepidula* is protostomic by fate map. The study does not tell you what happens in other spiralians, or how the gut of the first bilaterian formed. Across animals, blastopore fate varies even among close relatives, and Lyons and colleagues note that it is not a reliable test of the major bilaterian clades.

## From grades to a tree

In 1940 Libbie Hyman published the first volume of *The Invertebrates*, and by its second and third volumes (1951) her treatment ordered the bilaterians by body cavity: acoelomates, then pseudocoelomates, then coelomates. A group defined like that is a **grade**, animals that share a level of organization. A **clade** is different: an ancestor and all of its descendants. Grades match clades only if the character arose once and was never lost; see [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/).

Molecular trees tested the grades. In 1997 Anna Marie Aguinaldo, James Lake and colleagues compared sequences of the 18S ribosomal RNA gene and found nematodes grouped with arthropods, not with other worms. Analyses of many genes since then broadly support this picture.

- **Outside Bilateria:** sponges (Porifera), comb jellies (Ctenophora), placozoans (Placozoa) and cnidarians (Cnidaria). Which of these branched off first is unsettled. Chromosome-linkage evidence from Darrin Schultz, Steven Haddock, Daniel Rokhsar and colleagues (2023) supports comb jellies as the sister group of all other animals, one line of evidence among several.
- **Bilateria, Protostomia, Spiralia:** flatworms, annelids, molluscs, nemerteans and rotifers. In one major analysis (Laumer and colleagues, 2019), the name Lophotrochozoa refers to a subclade inside Spiralia, so do not use the two names as synonyms.
- **Bilateria, Protostomia, Ecdysozoa:** nematodes and arthropods, the animals that moult a cuticle.
- **Bilateria, Deuterostomia:** echinoderms, hemichordates and chordates. This is the traditional grouping, but a 2021 analysis by Paschalia Kapli and colleagues found equivocal support for it and showed that systematic error in the methods could produce the support that is seen.

Flatworms sit inside Spiralia, among coelomate relatives, so their acoelomate body may well be a secondary simplification, though some analyses suggest the first spiralians had no coelom, and the ancestral bilaterian state is still debated. And no living phylum is an ancestor of another: sponges and cnidarians are branches with as long a history as ours, not steps on a ladder.

## Phyla in one line each

- **Porifera:** choanocytes drive water through the body and catch food; no true tissues.
- **Ctenophora:** swim with eight rows of ciliary combs.
- **Placozoa:** *Trichoplax* is a flat plate of cells that digests algae outside its body, at its lower surface.
- **Cnidaria:** cnidocytes, the stinging cells.
- **Platyhelminthes:** flat, acoelomate; free-living forms such as planarians have a gut with one opening.
- **Rotifera:** a ciliated crown, the corona, that draws in food.
- **Nemertea:** an eversible proboscis housed in its own cavity, the rhynchocoel.
- **Mollusca:** a mantle, a fold of tissue over the visceral mass, which in many species secretes a shell.
- **Annelida:** a body of repeated segments.
- **Nematoda:** unsegmented roundworms with a pseudocoelom and a cuticle that is moulted.
- **Arthropoda:** jointed appendages and a chitinous exoskeleton that is moulted.
- **Echinodermata:** a water vascular system that works the tube feet; radial adults develop from bilateral larvae.
- **Hemichordata:** adult acorn worms have a body divided into proboscis, collar and trunk.
- **Chordata:** a notochord, a flexible rod along the back, during at least part of development.

Compare a sea star with a mouse. The sea star's five-part radial adult develops from a bilateral larva, so its radial form is a later change within Bilateria. The mouse embryo forms a notochord beneath the developing nerve cord, a step covered in [the organizer](/lessons/the-organizer/) and [neurulation](/lessons/neurulation-and-tube-defects/).

## What a coelom, a through gut and segments allow

A coelom puts fluid between gut and body wall. Organs inside it can grow and move with some independence from the body wall, and the fluid can act as a **hydrostatic skeleton**: a confined, incompressible fluid that muscles push against, like water in a balloon.

A through gut lets food move one way, from mouth to anus, so different regions can do different jobs in sequence. In the earthworm, food passes a muscular pharynx, a storage crop, a grinding gizzard and a long absorbing intestine. A planarian takes in food and expels waste through one opening. See [digestive systems](/lessons/digestive-systems/).

Segmentation adds compartments. In *Lumbricus terrestris*, walls called **septa** divide the coelom, and in textbook accounts each segment acts as a largely independent hydraulic unit.

**Worked problem.** These numbers are illustrative. Treat one earthworm segment as a cylinder 4.0 mm in diameter and 2.0 mm long, sealed by septa and filled with incompressible fluid. Assume the volume stays constant.

> **Volume = pi x r^2 x L**

1. *Starting volume.* With r = 2.0 mm and L = 2.0 mm, V = pi x 2.0^2 x 2.0 = 8 pi, about 25.1 mm3.
2. *Circular muscles contract.* The diameter narrows to 3.0 mm, so r = 1.5 mm and the cross-section is pi x 1.5^2 = 2.25 pi, about 7.07 mm2.
3. *New length.* L = 25.1 / 7.07 = 3.56 mm. The segment is 1.78 times longer, which is (4/3)^2: narrowing by a factor of 4/3 lengthens by its square.
4. *Longitudinal muscles contract.* The reverse happens. Shorten the segment to 1.5 mm and r^2 = 25.1 / (pi x 1.5) = 5.33 mm2, so r = 2.31 mm and the diameter grows to about 4.6 mm.

Because the septa isolate segments, one segment can be long and thin while its neighbour is short, fat and anchored to the soil by bristles called setae. Waves of this alternation push the worm forward. Now ask what changes with one shared cavity, as in a nematode. A squeeze anywhere raises pressure along the whole cavity, so there is no sealed compartment that can lengthen on its own the way an earthworm segment does, and a nematode has no circular muscle layer to squeeze one region in the first place. *C. elegans* moves instead by bending, using longitudinal muscles against its pressurized body and cuticle. More in [muscle and movement](/lessons/muscle-and-movement/).

### Design problem

Annelids and arthropods both have segments and a coelom, and older schemes grouped them together. The 18S rRNA trees of Aguinaldo and colleagues (1997) instead grouped nematodes, which are unsegmented and pseudocoelomate, with arthropods, and found no support for grouping annelids with arthropods. What does this mean for classifying animals by body cavity and segments, and how would you test which grouping is right?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

If the molecular tree is right, then either segmentation arose more than once (in annelids and in arthropods), or it was present in a common ancestor and lost in many lineages. Whether segmentation has one origin or several is still debated. The same reasoning applies to the coelom: a nematode's pseudocoelom could be a change from a coelomate ancestor. Either way, cavity and segments describe grades, not clades.

A good clade marker is shared and derived, and unlikely to arise twice or to reverse. Moulting of a cuticle, shared by nematodes and arthropods, is the candidate that named Ecdysozoa.

Then test with independent data, since a single gene can mislead when some lineages' sequences change fast. Sample hundreds of genes from many species, use models that allow for uneven rates, and check whether the grouping survives. Add a different kind of evidence, such as which genes share chromosomes. If several independent sources agree, keep Ecdysozoa and treat segments and cavities as descriptions of body plans.

</details>
