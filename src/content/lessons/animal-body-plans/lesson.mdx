techniques:
  - name: "Comparative morphology"
    blurb: >-
      Record the same characters (symmetry, germ layers, body cavity, gut openings, segments) in
      many species and compare them. It describes body plans well, but a character that is gained
      or lost more than once will group animals by grade rather than by ancestry.
  - name: "Fluorescent lineage tracing with live imaging"
    blurb: >-
      Inject a fluorescent tracer into one identified early cell and follow its descendants as the
      embryo develops. It shows what a cell actually becomes, which is stronger evidence than
      where structures happen to lie next to each other.
  - name: "Molecular phylogenetics"
    blurb: >-
      Compare the sequences of the same gene, or of hundreds of genes, across species and build
      the tree that best explains the differences. Results depend on sampling and on the model of
      sequence change, so fast-changing sequences can mislead.
  - name: "Chromosome-scale synteny comparison"
    blurb: >-
      Compare which genes share a chromosome across whole genomes, using outgroups to tell an
      ancestral linkage from a derived fusion. Because gene sets that fuse and mix rarely separate
      again, a shared fusion with mixing is strong evidence of shared ancestry.

      - "About 3.0 mm, unchanged"
      - "About 6.8 mm, longer"
      - "About 2.0 mm, shorter"

      Volume is pi x r^2 x L, so pi x 3.0^2 x 3.0 = 27 pi, about 84.8 mm3. With r = 2.0 mm the

## Characters you can see: symmetry, layers, cavities

A **body plan** is the basic layout an animal shares with its relatives: its symmetry, tissue layers, body cavity, gut and embryonic development.

**Symmetry.** Most sponges have no consistent symmetry. **Radial symmetry** arranges body parts around a central axis, like slices of a pie; several cuts through that axis give similar halves. **Bilateral symmetry** allows only one plane that divides the body into roughly equivalent left and right halves, usually with a front end where sense organs cluster.

**Germ layers.** A **germ layer** is one of the sheets of cells in the early embryo from which tissues form. A **diploblastic** animal has two, an outer **ectoderm** and an inner **endoderm**. A **triploblastic** animal adds a middle **mesoderm**, which forms muscle and much of the body wall.

**Body cavity.** The film's three cross-sections, defined: a **coelom** is a fluid-filled body cavity lined entirely by mesoderm; a **pseudocoelom** is a fluid-filled cavity that lacks a complete mesodermal lining around the gut; an **acoelomate** animal has tissue filling the region between gut and body wall. None is the **gut lumen**, where food goes: an acoelomate planarian still has a gut.

Applied to five animals: a sponge has no organs; the sea anemone *Nematostella vectensis*, usually described as diploblastic, has a one-opening gut and catches prey with **cnidocytes**, stinging cells; a planarian flatworm is bilateral and acoelomate, with a one-opening gut; the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans* (pseudocoelom) and the earthworm *Lumbricus terrestris* (coelom) each have a **through gut**, from mouth to anus. Whether sponge cell sheets are true tissues, and why the anemone is not simply radial, are in Going deeper.

## Grades, clades and the tree

The film called Hyman's staircase a **grade**, animals that share a level of organization, and set it against a **clade**, an ancestor and all of its descendants ([reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/)), but did not say which characters can mark one.

A **shared, derived** character, a new state that arose in a common ancestor and passed to its descendants, can mark a clade, as the moulted cuticle does. A few descendants may later lose it; annelids that have lost their segments are still annelids. A missing coelom cannot mark a clade by itself, though: the absence may be the ancestral state, or the same loss may have happened more than once.

How would anyone know whether a character arose once? Build the tree from other evidence (many other anatomical characters, many genes or chromosome linkage), then lay the character on it. In 1997 Anna Marie Aguinaldo, James Lake and colleagues compared 18S ribosomal RNA genes and grouped nematodes with arthropods and other moulting animals, not with annelids; analyses of many genes since broadly support this and place flatworms inside **Spiralia**. The table counts the fewest changes each character needs on that tree, for eight phyla coded by textbook states.

| Branch on the gene tree | Phylum | Body cavity | Segments | Moulted cuticle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outside Bilateria | Cnidaria | none | no | no |
| Spiralia | Platyhelminthes (flatworms) | none (acoelomate) | no | no |
| Spiralia | Annelida | coelom | yes | no |
| Spiralia | Mollusca | coelom | no | no |
| Ecdysozoa | Nematoda | pseudocoelom | no | yes |
| Ecdysozoa | Arthropoda | coelom (reduced in adults) | yes | yes |
| Deuterostomia | Echinodermata | coelom | no | no |
| Deuterostomia | Chordata | coelom | yes (vertebrates coded as segmented) | no |
| **Fewest changes on this tree** | | **3** | **3** | **1** |
| Fewest any tree could need | | 2 | 1 | 1 |

**On the gene tree, body cavity needs one change more than any tree must, segments need three where one would do, and the moulted cuticle needs only one.** So the body-cavity groups are grades, the segmented animals do not form a clade, and the moulted cuticle fits a single origin that marks **Ecdysozoa**, named after ecdysis, the moulting of a **cuticle** (a tough, non-living outer layer). This is a worked count on a coarse coding, not a published analysis, and not a history: three segment changes could be three origins or one origin and later losses (Design problem). It also shows why Hyman's staircase looked right: built from cavity type, it needs only two cavity changes, the fewest possible.

> **The one idea.** A body plan tells you how an animal is built and how it can live; on its own it does not tell you whom the animal descends from. A shared derived character can mark a clade, but the same state can arise twice or be lost, so test each character on a tree built from other evidence.
>
> *Scope:* animal body-plan characters (symmetry, germ layers, body cavity, gut openings, segments, blastopore fate). Some do mark clades: the notochord marks Chordata and the moulted cuticle Ecdysozoa. Whether bilateral symmetry has a single origin is still debated (Going deeper), and no single character is an infallible clade test.

Outside **Bilateria**, a clade named for its left and right sides (some members, such as adult sea stars, have since modified them), sit sponges, comb jellies, placozoans and cnidarians; which of these split off first is unsettled (Going deeper). Inside, **Protostomia** splits into Spiralia (flatworms, annelids, molluscs, nemerteans and rotifers) and Ecdysozoa (nematodes and arthropods). **Deuterostomia** (echinoderms, hemichordates and chordates) is the traditional third group, but one recent analysis found its support equivocal (Going deeper). No living phylum is an ancestor of another: sponges and cnidarians are branches with as long a history as ours, not steps on a ladder.

**Predict.** An adult sea star is radial, like a jellyfish. Does it sit with the cnidarians or inside Bilateria, and what would you look at to decide?

Inside Bilateria, but no single character decides it: trees built from many other anatomical and molecular characters place echinoderms there. The larva then explains the shape: it is bilateral, and the five-part radial adult develops from it later, a change within a bilaterian lineage.

## Testing an old rule: the blastopore

The names Protostomia and Deuterostomia come from an embryonic character long used as a clade test. Does it pass?

In **cleavage**, the first cell divisions of the embryo, many annelids, molluscs and other spiralians divide in a tilted, **spiral** pattern; echinoderms and many other deuterostomes divide in a **radial** pattern, with cells stacked directly above one another. During **gastrulation** the embryo folds or spreads to form its germ layers and a primitive gut, and the **blastopore** is the opening that connects that forming gut to the outside.

The old rule splits bilaterian animals by the fate of the blastopore. In **protostomes** ("mouth first") the blastopore becomes the mouth. In **deuterostomes** ("mouth second") the mouth forms separately, and the blastopore often becomes the anus. Textbooks pair protostomes with spiral cleavage and deuterostomes with radial cleavage, plus a pairing for how the coelom forms (Going deeper).

The rule can be checked one species at a time with a **fate map**, a record of what each early cell becomes. In 2015 Deirdre Lyons, Kimberly Perry and Jonathan Henry traced labelled cells in embryos of the slipper snail *Crepidula fornicata*, a marine gastropod and a spiralian whose early cells can be identified one by one.

**Predict.** If the textbook protostome picture holds, what should the snail's blastopore become? And Conklin's classic study of this snail named two early cells the "anal cells". Should they make the anus?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The blastopore did become the mouth, as the rule predicts, so this snail is protostomic by fate map. The anus formed separately and late, at about 12 days of development, from descendants of the 2d2 cell. The so-called anal cells did not form the anus.

</details>

One species is not a clade test: blastopore fate varies even among close relatives, and Lyons and colleagues note that it is not a reliable test of the major bilaterian clades (method in Going deeper).

## Phyla in one line each

Read these as thumbnail descriptions: each line gives a characteristic feature or combination, but identifying an unknown animal usually takes several characters.

- **Porifera:** **choanocytes**, collar cells that line the feeding chambers, drive water through the body and catch food; no organs, only simple cell layers.
- **Ctenophora:** swim with eight rows of ciliary combs.
- **Placozoa:** *Trichoplax* is a flat plate of cells that digests algae outside its body, at its lower surface.
- **Cnidaria:** cnidocytes, the stinging cells.
- **Platyhelminthes:** flat, acoelomate; free-living forms such as planarians have a gut with one opening.
- **Rotifera:** a ciliated crown, the corona, that draws in food.
- **Nemertea:** an eversible proboscis, one that turns inside out and shoots forward, housed in its own cavity, the rhynchocoel.
- **Mollusca:** a mantle, a fold of tissue over the visceral mass (the soft body that holds most organs), which in many species secretes a shell.
- **Annelida:** a body of repeated segments, though a few lineages have lost them.
- **Nematoda:** unsegmented roundworms with a pseudocoelom and a cuticle that is moulted.
- **Arthropoda:** jointed appendages and a chitinous exoskeleton that is moulted.
- **Echinodermata:** a water vascular system that works the tube feet; radial adults, bilateral larvae.
- **Hemichordata:** adult acorn worms have a body divided into proboscis, collar and trunk.
- **Chordata:** a notochord, a flexible rod along the back, during at least part of development; a mouse embryo forms one beneath the developing nerve cord, a step covered in [the organizer](/lessons/the-organizer/) and [neurulation](/lessons/neurulation-and-tube-defects/).

## What a coelom, a through gut and segments allow

The second film showed which way a segment changes shape; it left out by how much, how literal the sealed segment is, and what an unsegmented worm does instead.

In *Lumbricus terrestris* the **hydrostatic skeleton** is incompressible coelomic fluid held in by the muscular body wall; **septa** largely partition it, and in textbook accounts each segment acts as a largely independent hydraulic unit. The septa are not perfect seals (the gut runs through them), so a sealed, constant-volume segment is a model. The coelom also lets the gut and other organs grow and move with some independence from the body wall, within the limits of their attachments.

### Design problem

Annelids and arthropods both have segments, and older schemes grouped them together as the Articulata. (Arthropods are counted as coelomates, but in adults the coelom is reduced and the main body cavity is a blood-filled haemocoel.) The 18S rRNA trees of Aguinaldo and colleagues (1997) instead grouped unsegmented, pseudocoelomate nematodes with arthropods and found no support for annelids with arthropods. What does this mean for classifying animals by body cavity and segments, and how would you test which grouping is right?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

If the molecular tree is right, segmentation either arose more than once (in annelids and in arthropods) or was present in a common ancestor and lost in many lineages. Whether segmentation has one origin or several is still debated. The same reasoning applies to the coelom: a nematode's pseudocoelom could be a change from a coelomate ancestor. Either way, groups built on cavity or segments are grades, not clades.

Then test with independent data. A single gene can mislead when some lineages' sequences change fast; sample hundreds of genes from many species with models that allow for uneven rates, and add a different kind of evidence, such as which genes share chromosomes. If the sources agree, keep Ecdysozoa.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Two more textbook pairings

Textbooks also pair protostomes with **schizocoely**, a coelom opening within masses of mesoderm, and deuterostomes with **enterocoely**, pouches budding off the gut wall.

### Are sponge cell layers true tissues?

A sponge's cell sheets were long said not to be true tissues, although many biologists now count them as simple epithelia.

### The slipper snail fate map

Lyons, Perry and Henry raised *Crepidula fornicata* embryos at about 20 degrees C, injected fluorescent lineage tracers into chosen cells, such as the one named 2d2, and followed each cell's descendants by live imaging. Gastrulation happened by **epiboly**, a sheet of small cells spreading over the large, yolky ones. The study does not tell you what happens in other spiralians, or how the gut of the first bilaterian formed.

### Where the old schemes came from

In 1908 the Austrian zoologist Karl Grobben proposed splitting bilaterian animals by the fate of the blastopore, the origin of the names protostome and deuterostome. Hyman's body-cavity ordering followed in *The Invertebrates* (first volume 1940; second and third volumes 1951).

### The deepest branches: sponges or comb jellies?

Chromosome-linkage evidence from Darrin Schultz, Steven Haddock, Daniel Rokhsar and colleagues (2023) supports comb jellies as the sister group of all other animals, one line of evidence among several. They compared which gene sets share a chromosome in animals and their unicellular relatives; gene sets that fuse and then mix are very unlikely to separate again, so a shared fused, mixed state marks shared ancestry.

### Names and uncertain nodes

In one major analysis (Laumer and colleagues, 2019), the name Lophotrochozoa refers to a subclade inside Spiralia, so do not use the two names as synonyms. For Deuterostomia, a 2021 analysis by Paschalia Kapli and colleagues found equivocal support and showed that systematic error in the methods could produce the support that is seen.

### Was the first bilaterian coelomate?

Because flatworms sit among coelomate relatives, their acoelomate body may well be a secondary simplification. Some analyses suggest the first spiralians had no coelom, though, and the ancestral bilaterian state is still debated.

### A second axis in a sea anemone

*Nematostella vectensis* is not simply radial: it has a second body axis at right angles to its mouth-to-foot axis, patterned by the signalling molecules Chordin and BMP. Whether that axis means bilaterality has one origin or arose by convergence is still open. The story is taken up in [evolution of development](/lessons/evolution-of-development/).
