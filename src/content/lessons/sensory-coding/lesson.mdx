    blurb: >-
      An animal trained on a perceptual task performs while one neuron is recorded. From the

      Deleting a gene only in chosen cells of an adult animal, such as Piezo2 in sensory neurons

      to transduction in the cell and to perception in the animal.

    choices:
      - "It reverses sign above a set strength"
      - "It keeps one fixed size, like a spike"
      - "It shrinks as more channels inactivate"
      - "It grows in a graded way with strength"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      A receptor potential is graded: a stronger stimulus changes more transduction channels (opening them in touch, closing cGMP-gated channels in rods and cones) and
      gives a larger depolarization or hyperpolarization. The fixed size belongs to the action
      potential, which the receptor potential later drives, so applying it here confuses the two
      signals. A sign reversal at a set strength describes no standard receptor potential; the
      sign depends on the receptor type, not on strength. Inactivation can reduce a response over
      time during adaptation, but it does not make stronger stimuli give smaller potentials.

  - stem: "With low noise that is the same at every angle, a neuron's tuning curve gives 60, 55, 30 and 10 spikes/s at 0, 10, 20 and 30 degrees. Where should a 2-degree change in angle be easiest to detect from its firing?"
    choices:
      - "Between 20 and 30 degrees, the lowest firing"
      - "Between 10 and 20 degrees, the steepest part"
      - "At 0 degrees, where the firing rate is highest"
      - "Equally well anywhere the neuron is firing"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The rate changes by 5, 25 and 20 spikes/s across the three 10-degree steps, so the slope is
      steepest between 10 and 20 degrees, and with equal low noise a small change there moves the
      rate most. At the peak the curve is nearly flat, so the highest rate is the worst place for

      to 30 degree flank is steep but less so than 10 to 20. Discrimination is not equal
      everywhere, because it depends on the slope, not on whether the cell fires.
  - stem: "In a two-alternative forced-choice task, an observer must say which of two intervals held the stimulus. At the smallest stimulus difference tested, she is correct on 50% of trials. What does this score mean?"
    choices:
      - "She detects the stimulus on half of the trials"
      - "She is performing at the level of pure guessing"
      - "She is lapsing on half of the trials at random"
      - "She is exactly at her discrimination threshold"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      With two choices a guess is right half the time, so 50% correct is chance, and the
      threshold in this design is usually set halfway between chance and perfect, at 75%. Calling
      50% the threshold borrows the convention for yes/no detection tasks, where threshold is the level

      performance down at large differences, not a fixed half of trials.

**In 1926 Edgar Adrian and Yngve Zotterman recorded the impulses from one stretch receptor in a frog muscle and found that a heavier load did not make the impulses bigger, only more frequent.**

Every sense you have ends in that same currency. A fingertip ending, a rod in the retina, a taste cell and an olfactory neuron all produce graded voltages, and the afferent neurons that carry their signals to the brain send trains of spikes that look alike. In vision those spiking cells are retinal ganglion cells, reached through bipolar cells rather than driven by the rods directly. So how does a train of identical spikes say what touched you, where, and how hard? This lesson builds the answer from recordings: transduction, receptive fields, tuning, lateral inhibition and the old argument between labelled lines and populations.

## The idea: a stimulus becomes a graded voltage, then a rate

**Transduction** is the conversion of a stimulus into an electrical signal at a sensory receptor. The first signal is the **receptor potential**, a change in membrane voltage whose size grows with stimulus strength. It is graded, like the synaptic potentials in [integration and inhibition](/lessons/synaptic-integration/), and it spreads passively, as in [the resting membrane](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/).

The machinery differs by sense. In touch, force opens mechanically activated channels in the ending of the sensory neuron itself. In mice, Ranade and colleagues (Nature, 2014) deleted the channel Piezo2 from adult sensory neurons and Merkel cells, skin cells that contact some touch endings, and the animals lost much of their touch sensation. That result is about touch. Vertebrate photoreceptors use a receptor protein and a cGMP cascade, and light hyperpolarizes them; smell and taste also use receptor proteins. The cascades belong to [senses and cGMP](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/); here we follow what the voltage does next.

Where the ending is part of the afferent neuron, the receptor potential depolarizes the spike-initiating zone, and the larger it is, the faster the neuron fires [action potentials](/lessons/action-potential/). Where the receptor is a separate cell, it changes transmitter release instead. Taste cells release onto the afferent fibres. Photoreceptors release onto bipolar cells, and the retinal circuit passes the signal on to ganglion cells, whose spikes leave the eye. Either way, intensity reaches the brain mostly as a **firing rate**, which is what Adrian and Zotterman saw. A stronger stimulus also recruits more afferents, so the number of active fibres carries intensity too.

## Which cells, which stimuli: receptive fields and adaptation

A **receptive field** is the region of the sensory surface, a patch of skin or of the visual field, where a stimulus can change a given cell's activity. Many fingertip afferents, such as those contacting Merkel cells, have small ones; afferents of the same kind on the back have much larger ones, which is one reason your fingertip resolves finer detail. Receptor type matters too: a Pacinian afferent's field can cover much of the hand.

Receptors also differ in time. **Rapidly adapting** endings fire at the start of a maintained stimulus and then fall quiet; many fire again when it is removed. They report change: a Pacinian corpuscle in the skin signals vibration. **Slowly adapting** endings keep firing for as long as the stimulus lasts, as Merkel-cell afferents do during steady indentation, so they report the stimulus itself. Not every receptor stops firing under a constant stimulus. Adaptation as a general circuit motif is taken up in [signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/).

To summarize a cell's preferences, plot its **tuning curve**: the mean firing rate against one stimulus parameter, such as the orientation of a bar or the frequency of a tone. Present the same stimulus many times and the spike count varies from trial to trial, so the curve is an average with spread around it.

## Reading a tuning curve and a threshold

You might expect a neuron to be most useful at its peak. For fine discrimination, telling two nearby stimuli apart, it is often the reverse.

**Worked problem 1, a tuning curve.** In this made-up recording, a neuron fires 50, 40, 20 and 8 spikes/s at 0, 15, 30 and 45 degrees. Assume the trial-to-trial noise is low and the same at every angle.

*Step 1: the change per step.* From 0 to 15 degrees the rate falls by 10 spikes/s; from 15 to 30 degrees by 20 spikes/s; from 30 to 45 degrees by 12 spikes/s.

*Step 2: the slope.* The steepest segment is 15 to 30 degrees: 20 spikes/s per 15 degrees, about 1.3 spikes/s per degree, against about 0.7 near the peak.

*Step 3: the reading.* A small change near 22 degrees moves the rate about twice as much as the same change near the peak, so it is easier to detect. Butts and Goldman (PLoS Biology, 2006) showed this in low-noise models of fine discrimination, and also that as variability grows the best-encoded stimulus can move back toward the peak. The answer depends on noise and task.

Behaviour has its own curve. A **psychometric function** plots an observer's performance against stimulus level; in the tasks here, that is the proportion of correct responses. In a **two-alternative forced-choice** (2AFC) task the observer must pick which of two options held the stimulus, so guessing gives 50% correct. That makes 50% chance, not threshold, and the usual threshold sits halfway between chance and perfect, at 75%. (A 50% convention comes from yes/no detection tasks, where threshold is often taken as the level reported on half the trials, a measure that also depends on how willing the observer is to say yes.)

**Worked problem 2, a psychometric function.** Take a model 2AFC observer judging an angular difference x, with chosen, not measured, parameters (midpoint 2 degrees, spread 1 degree, no lapses):

> **p(x) = 0.5 + 0.5 × Φ((x − 2°) / 1°)**

Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution.

*Step 1: threshold.* Set p = 0.75. Then Φ(z) = (0.75 − 0.5)/0.5 = 0.5, so z = 0 and x = 2 degrees.

*Step 2: 90% correct.* Φ(z) = (0.90 − 0.5)/0.5 = 0.8, so z = 0.84 and x = 2.84 degrees.

*Step 3: performance at 3 degrees.* z = (3 − 2)/1 = 1 and Φ(1) = 0.841, so p = 0.5 + 0.5 × 0.841 = 0.921, or 92.1% correct.

Now the two curves can be compared. Britten, Shadlen, Newsome and Movshon (Journal of Neuroscience, 1992) trained rhesus monkeys to report the direction of moving dots and recorded single neurons in visual area MT on the same trials, with the display matched to each neuron's receptive field and preferred direction. Under these conditions most neurons were about as sensitive as the monkey. That is a statement about signal. It does not show that one neuron made the choice: in a later model by the same group, pools of at least 100 weakly correlated neurons reproduced the observations best, and that number is a model estimate, not a count. Causal tests came from manipulation; Salzman, Britten and Newsome (Nature, 1990) biased monkeys' choices by microstimulating MT.

## Neighbours that suppress: lateral inhibition

The lateral eye of the horseshoe crab Limulus is a compound eye of ommatidia, each sending its own fibre into the optic nerve. Hartline, Wagner and Ratliff (Journal of General Physiology, 1956) recorded with a microelectrode inside one ommatidium. Lighting its neighbours suppressed its steady discharge, cutting the lateral connections to it abolished the inhibition, and the interaction exaggerated the difference between brightly and dimly lit regions. Think of each unit reporting its own light minus a share of its neighbours' light.

**Worked problem 3, an edge.** This is a feedforward simplification. The Hartline-Ratliff model is recurrent (each unit is inhibited by its neighbours' output, not their input) and has thresholds. Six receptors in a row receive excitation 10, 10, 10, 20, 20, 20 (made-up units). Each output equals its own excitation minus 0.2 times the excitation of each adjacent neighbour, and the row continues beyond both ends.

> **output = own excitation − 0.2 × (left neighbour + right neighbour)**

*Interior dim:* 10 − 0.2 × (10 + 10) = 6.

*Dim at the edge:* 10 − 0.2 × (10 + 20) = 4.

*Bright at the edge:* 20 − 0.2 × (10 + 20) = 14.

*Interior bright:* 20 − 0.2 × (20 + 20) = 12.

The plateaus differ by 6 units (12 against 6), but across the edge the difference is 10 (14 against 4). The dim side dips below its plateau and the bright side overshoots, the same kind of edge exaggeration as the light and dark Mach bands people see at the ends of a luminance ramp (an abrupt step does not ordinarily show them, so this is an analogy). Limulus is not the vertebrate retina, whose surrounds are built by other cells; see [the retina](/lessons/retina/).

## Labelled lines or populations

In a **labelled line**, a fibre's activity means one thing, say "sweet", however it was activated. In a **population code**, quality is read from the pattern across many fibres. Taste researchers still argue between the two (Ohla and colleagues, Chemical Senses, 2019). At the level of taste cells the evidence favours dedicated lines: in mice, sweet, umami, bitter and sour are detected by separate cell types with their own receptors, and making sweet cells express a receptor for a synthetic opiate made mice attracted to that drug (Zhao and colleagues, Cell, 2003). The argument is mainly about the afferent fibres and the brain.

Smell leans toward populations at the receptor level. In olfactory sensory neurons, Malnic, Hirono, Sato and Buck (Cell, 1999) found that one receptor recognizes several odorants and one odorant is recognized by several receptors, so an odorant is coded by a combination. How neurons expressing the same receptor are wired to the same targets in the olfactory bulb is a developmental question, covered in [partners, layers and self-avoidance](/lessons/partners-layers-self-avoidance/).

Taste offers a test. Wu, Dvoryanchikov, Pereira, Chaudhari and Roper (Nature Communications, 2015) imaged taste neurons in the geniculate ganglion of anaesthetized mice expressing GCaMP3 and found that some neurons narrowly tuned at low concentrations became broadly tuned at higher ones.

### Design problem

In that study, 28 of 101 neurons responded to more than one taste below the EC50 (the concentration giving half the maximal response), and 79 of 155 did so at about 1.5 to 2 times the EC50. A classmate says this refutes labelled lines. How would you evaluate the claim, and what experiment would test it?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with the counts. 28 of 101 is 28%, and 79 of 155 is 51%, but they come from different samples of neurons, so the rise could partly reflect which cells were imaged. The stronger evidence is the same neuron recorded at a low and a higher concentration (about 3.5 to 7 times the EC50 in that paired series), which the paper also shows for a subset of neurons.

Next, scope. Broad peripheral tuning says what the ganglion sends, not how the brain decodes it. A decoder could still read a narrowly tuned subset, or the combination.

Then test it causally. Silence or remove one receptor class, or one molecularly defined group of afferents, and measure behaviour at low and high concentrations. If a dedicated "sweet" line carries quality, losing it should remove sweet responses at both concentrations and leave the other tastes intact. A pattern decoder could also lose sweet when its main input is gone, so a loss of sweet alone does not separate the models. The more telling measure is discrimination among the remaining tastes at low and high concentrations: a pattern code that uses those fibres should blur these discriminations too, most where tuning is broad. The result is a scoped counterexample to strict, concentration-invariant labelled lines in mouse geniculate neurons, not a refutation of every labelled-line model.

</details>
