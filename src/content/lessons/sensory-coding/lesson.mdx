  - name: "Edgar Adrian"
    contribution: >-
      With Yngve Zotterman, recorded impulses from a single sensory ending and showed that a
      stronger stimulus raised the frequency of identical impulses, and that the frequency fell
      while a steady stimulus was held: rate coding and adaptation.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Adrian & Zotterman, J. Physiol. (1926), 'The impulses produced by sensory nerve-endings', Part 2; Adrian, Nobel Lecture, 12 December 1932 (nobelprize.org)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the 1920s physiologists knew that nerves carried electrical impulses, but a whole nerve
      mixes the activity of many fibres, so no one could say what a single sensory ending sent to
      the brain. Edgar Adrian, at Cambridge, combined a capillary electrometer with valve
      amplification sensitive enough to see the small impulses of single fibres. In 1926, working
      with the Swedish physiologist Yngve Zotterman, he cut successive strips from a frog muscle
      until its activity came from one stretch receptor. Each impulse was the same size whatever
      the load. What changed with a heavier load was how often the impulses came, and during a
      maintained stretch the frequency fell. Adrian shared the 1932 Nobel Prize in Physiology or
      Medicine with Charles Sherrington for their work on the functions of neurons. The single-
      fibre record made it possible to ask, for any sense, what the spike train of one receptor
      says about the stimulus.
  - name: "H. Keffer Hartline"
    contribution: >-
      With Henry Wagner and Floyd Ratliff, showed in the lateral eye of the horseshoe crab
      Limulus that each receptor unit is inhibited by its illuminated neighbours, and that this
      lateral inhibition exaggerates differences between light and dark regions.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Hartline, Wagner & Ratliff, J. Gen. Physiol. 39:651–673 (1956); Hartline, Nobel Lecture, 12 December 1967 (nobelprize.org)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Hartline trained in medicine and turned to the physiology of vision, and he chose an
      animal whose eye could be taken apart one unit at a time. The lateral eye of Limulus, the
      horseshoe crab, is a compound eye built of ommatidia, each with a receptor unit whose axon
      runs in the optic nerve, so a single fibre reports a single unit. In the 1956 Journal of
      General Physiology paper with Henry Wagner and Floyd Ratliff, a microelectrode inside one
      ommatidium recorded a steady discharge to steady light. Lighting neighbouring ommatidia
      suppressed that discharge. Cutting the lateral connections to the ommatidium abolished the
      inhibition, which placed it in a network of connections among the units rather than in the
      optics. The interaction exaggerated the difference in response between brightly and dimly
      lit regions of the eye. Hartline and Ratliff went on to describe the inhibition with
      equations in which each unit is inhibited by its neighbours' output. Hartline shared the
      1967 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Ragnar Granit and George Wald. The work made
      contrast enhancement a measurable computation done by a circuit.

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The question was old: how does the activity of neurons in a sensory area relate to what an
      animal can perceive? Britten, Shadlen, Newsome and Movshon trained rhesus monkeys to report
      the direction of motion in a display of moving dots, in which the experimenters could set
      what fraction of dots moved together. While a monkey worked, they recorded a single neuron
      in the middle temporal area, MT, and set the display to fit that neuron's receptive field

      about as sensitive as the monkey. A 1996 paper from the same group then modelled how pooled
      MT signals could produce the choices, and the simulations fitted the observations best with
      pools of at least 100 weakly correlated neurons. Microstimulation experiments from the
      Newsome laboratory tested the link by biasing MT activity. The work made it possible to put
      neuronal and behavioural sensitivity on one scale.

techniques:
  - name: "Intracellular and single-fibre recording from receptors"
    blurb: >-
      A fine electrode inside a receptor, or on a nerve dissected down to one active fibre,
      records the receptor potential or the spike train of one sensory unit. Changing the
      stimulus, or the cell's neighbours, while recording shows how that one unit encodes it.
  - name: "Single-unit recording paired with psychophysics"
    blurb: >-
      An animal trained on a perceptual task performs while one neuron is recorded. From the
      spike counts you build a neurometric function and compare it with the animal's psychometric
      function on the same trials, which tests whether the neuron carries enough signal to
      account for the behaviour.
  - name: "Calcium imaging of sensory ganglia"
    blurb: >-
      Neurons expressing a genetically encoded calcium indicator such as GCaMP3 brighten when
      they fire. Imaging a ganglion while you apply stimuli records the tuning of many neurons at
      once, though calcium signals are slower and less direct than recorded spikes.
  - name: "Conditional knockout with behavioural and afferent readouts"
    blurb: >-
      Deleting a gene only in chosen cells of an adult animal, such as Piezo2 in sensory neurons
      and Merkel cells, then testing behaviour and recording single afferents, links one molecule
      to transduction in the cell and to perception in the animal.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "A receptor cell is stimulated at three increasing strengths, and its membrane is recorded before any spike is generated. How does its receptor potential change?"
    choices:
      - "It reverses sign above a set strength"
      - "It keeps one fixed size, like a spike"
      - "It shrinks as more channels inactivate"
      - "It grows in a graded way with strength"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      A receptor potential is graded: a stronger stimulus changes more transduction channels (opening them in touch, closing cGMP-gated channels in rods and cones) and
      gives a larger depolarization or hyperpolarization. The fixed size belongs to the action
      potential, which the receptor potential later drives, so applying it here confuses the two
      signals. A sign reversal at a set strength describes no standard receptor potential; the
      sign depends on the receptor type, not on strength. Inactivation can reduce a response over
      time during adaptation, but it does not make stronger stimuli give smaller potentials.
  - stem: "A touch afferent fires a burst when a probe presses the skin, falls silent while the probe is held still, and fires again when the probe lifts. How is it best classified?"
    choices:
      - "A pressure line that has run out of transmitter"
      - "Slowly adapting, signalling steady indentation"
      - "Rapidly adapting, signalling change in contact"
      - "A pain fibre signalling damage at the onset"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Firing at onset and offset with silence during the held stimulus is the mark of a rapidly
      adapting afferent, which reports change rather than steady pressure. A slowly adapting
      afferent would keep firing during the hold, so it fits the opposite pattern. Nothing in a
      gentle press implies tissue damage, so a pain fibre is the wrong class. Running out of
      transmitter would not explain the renewed burst at removal; this pattern reflects how the
      ending responds to change, not exhaustion.
  - stem: "With the same low trial-to-trial standard deviation at every angle, a neuron has mean rates of 60, 55, 30 and 10 spikes/s at 0, 10, 20 and 30 degrees, changing in a straight line between those points. Where would a 2-degree change, lying wholly within one segment, be easiest to detect from its firing?"
    choices:
      - "Between 20 and 30 degrees, the lowest firing"
      - "Between 10 and 20 degrees, the steepest part"
      - "At 0 degrees, where the firing rate is highest"
      - "Equally well anywhere the neuron is firing"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The rate changes by 5, 25 and 20 spikes/s across the three 10-degree steps, so the slope is
      steepest between 10 and 20 degrees, and with equal low noise a small change there moves the
      rate most. At the peak the curve is nearly flat, so the highest rate is the worst place for
      a fine change, the common mistake of equating most spikes with best discrimination. The 20
      to 30 degree flank is steep but less so than 10 to 20. Discrimination is not equal
      everywhere, because it depends on the slope, not on whether the cell fires. This relies on
      the equal noise stated in the stem; real spike counts get noisier at higher rates, which
      would favour the lower flank, between 20 and 30 degrees.
  - stem: "In a two-alternative forced-choice task, an observer must say which of two intervals held the stimulus. At the smallest stimulus difference tested, she is correct on 50% of trials. What does this score mean?"
    choices:
      - "She detects the stimulus on half of the trials"
      - "She is performing at the level of pure guessing"
      - "She is lapsing on half of the trials at random"
      - "She is exactly at her discrimination threshold"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      With two choices a guess is right half the time, so 50% correct is chance, and the
      threshold in this design is commonly set halfway between chance and perfect, at 75%. Calling
      50% the threshold borrows the convention for yes/no detection tasks, where threshold is the level
      detected on half of the trials; that is the second misreading too, since chance performance
      gives no evidence that she can tell the two intervals apart. Lapses are occasional stimulus-independent errors that pull
      performance down at large differences, not a fixed half of trials.
  - stem: "Six receptors in a row receive excitation 5, 5, 5, 15, 15, 15 (arbitrary units). Each output equals its own excitation minus 0.2 times the excitation of each adjacent neighbour, and the row continues beyond both ends. What is the output of the first bright receptor, at the edge?"
    choices:
      - "1 unit"
      - "15 units"
      - "11 units"
      - "9 units"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The first bright receptor has one dim neighbour and one bright one, so its output is 15 −
      0.2 × (5 + 15) = 15 − 4 = 11, above the bright interior. The value 9 is the bright interior,
      15 − 0.2 × 30, which forgets that one neighbour is dim. The value 15 ignores the inhibition
      altogether. The value 1 is the dim receptor at the edge, 5 − 0.2 × 20, the other side of the
      boundary.

**In 1926 Edgar Adrian and Yngve Zotterman cut a frog muscle down until one stretch receptor was left, hung weights on it, and recorded what that single ending sent along its nerve fibre.**

Every sense you have ends in the same currency. A fingertip ending, a rod in the retina, a taste cell and an olfactory neuron all produce graded voltages. The afferent neurons, which carry signals toward the brain, send trains of spikes that look alike. So how does a train of identical spikes say what touched you, where, and how hard?

## One ending, three weights

In the 1920s no one could yet say what a single sensory ending sent to the brain, because a whole nerve mixes the activity of many fibres. Adrian and Zotterman kept cutting strips from the muscle until the record showed a single, regular series of impulses, their sign of one active ending.

**Predict.** A heavier weight stretches the receptor more. Will its impulses get bigger, come more often, or both?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

They come more often, and they do not get bigger. Picture the record: three loads, three trains of impulses, every impulse the same height, sitting closer together under the heavier load. While a load is held, the frequency slowly falls. These two facts, **rate coding** and **adaptation**, are still the first two taught about sensory coding.

</details>

If every spike looks alike, the record raises four puzzles.

1. **What.** How does the brain know a signal came from touch and not from light?
2. **Where.** How does it know which patch of skin was touched?
3. **How much.** How does it read the strength of the stimulus?
4. **How fine.** How can it detect a change so small that it barely moves the firing rate?

## What a spike means

Adrian's record answers the third puzzle. In his single fibre, heavier loads did not make the spikes bigger: stimulus strength was carried by their frequency, so how often spikes come tells you **how much**. A stronger stimulus also recruits more afferents, so the number of active fibres carries intensity too.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/sensory-coding/adrian-stretch-receptor.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a strip of frog muscle hangs from a stand with a weight on its end and a single nerve fibre leading from one stretch receptor to electrodes; beside it, three rows of identical impulses grow more frequent from light to heavy load." caption="Recording one stretch receptor's fibre, Adrian and Zotterman found that a heavier load did not make the impulses bigger, only more frequent." />

The first puzzle has a different answer: **which** fibre is firing tells you **what**. In a **labelled line**, a fibre's activity means one thing, say "sweet", however it was activated. The classic example, behind Müller's law of specific nerve energies, is that pressure on the closed eye is seen as light. The pressure is not light, but activity in the optic nerve means light to the brain, whatever started it. Whether every quality of every sense runs on dedicated lines is still argued (see the end of the lesson).

To put "which" and "how much" into one rule, you need two terms. A neuron's **tuning curve** plots its mean firing rate against one property of the stimulus, such as the orientation of a bar or the frequency of a tone. Present the same stimulus many times and the spike count in a fixed time window fluctuates from trial to trial. The standard deviation of that fluctuation is the neuron's **noise**.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A spike means nothing by itself. It means what its neuron is tuned to.
>
> - Count a sensory neuron's spikes over a fixed stretch of time. On each trial its firing rate is its tuning curve read at the stimulus, plus a fluctuation that averages to zero: **rate = tuning curve(stimulus) + fluctuation**.
> - *Which* neurons fire and their *relative rates* together tell you what, where and how much.
> - For otherwise similar responses, a small change is easiest to detect where it moves the mean rate a lot compared with the noise.

The rule has two limits. Some senses also carry information in spike timing, such as the latency of the first spike in touch and phase locking to the sound wave in hearing. And the rule says what information a neuron *carries*, not how the brain reads it out.

## From stimulus to rate

**Transduction** is the conversion of a stimulus into an electrical signal at a sensory receptor. The first signal is the **receptor potential**, a change in membrane voltage whose size grows with stimulus strength.

The machinery differs by sense. In touch, force opens mechanically activated channels, mostly in the ending of the sensory neuron itself (Merkel cells, skin cells that contact some touch endings, also transduce; see Going deeper). Removing the channel Piezo2 from adult mouse sensory neurons and Merkel cells removed much of touch (Ranade and colleagues, Nature, 2014). Vertebrate photoreceptors use a receptor protein and a cGMP cascade, and light hyperpolarizes them, making the inside more negative. Smell, and sweet, umami and bitter taste, also use receptor proteins, while sour taste and one pathway for salt work through ion channels (see Going deeper). The cascades belong to [senses and cGMP](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/); here we follow what the voltage does next.

### Why the receptor potential is graded

Use the rule from [the action potential](/lessons/action-potential/): with no current injected, the voltage moves toward a balance point, the average of the equilibrium potentials weighted by their conductances, and settles there. Take a simple passive model: the resting membrane's channels, which together reverse at −65 mV, plus **transduction channels**, cation channels whose current reverses near 0 mV. With two kinds of channel, the balance point is

> **Vm = (g_rest × −65 mV + g_trans × 0 mV) ÷ (g_rest + g_trans)**

where g_rest is the resting conductance and g_trans the transduction conductance. Check the ends: with no transduction channels open (g_trans = 0) it gives −65 mV; with g_trans huge it approaches 0 mV.

- Each extra open transduction channel pulls the balance point toward 0 mV. A stronger stimulus opens more channels, so the voltage moves further. The response is graded.
- Put numbers on it (made-up units). With g_rest = 1 and g_trans = 1, the balance point is (1 × −65 + 1 × 0) ÷ (1 + 1) = −32.5 mV. Raise the transduction conductance to 2, 3 and 4 and it moves to about −22, −16 and −13 mV. Each equal increase moves it less, by about 11, 5 and 3 mV, because the voltage can approach 0 mV but never pass it. The response saturates. The model shows only this driving-force limit; real receptors also have channel kinetics, adaptation and voltage-gated currents.
- In a rod, cGMP-gated cation channels are open in the dark, and light closes them. That moves the balance point the other way, which is why light hyperpolarizes the rod.

### Why it must become spikes

Like the synaptic potentials in [integration and inhibition](/lessons/synaptic-integration/), the receptor potential spreads passively, as in [the resting membrane](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/). A passive potential fades as it spreads, over about a length constant. To travel to the spinal cord or brain, it must be turned into spikes, which regenerate along the way.

There are two routes.

- **The ending is part of the afferent neuron**, as in most touch. The receptor potential depolarizes the spike-initiating zone, where the axon's spikes start, and the larger it is, the faster the neuron fires [action potentials](/lessons/action-potential/).
- **The receptor is a separate cell.** It changes transmitter release instead. Taste cells release onto the afferent fibres. Photoreceptors release onto bipolar cells, and the retinal circuit passes the signal on to retinal ganglion cells, whose spikes leave the eye. The rods do not drive the ganglion cells directly.

Either way, intensity reaches the brain mostly as a **firing rate**, which is what Adrian and Zotterman saw.

## Where and when: receptive fields and adaptation

Which neuron fires also tells you **where**. A **receptive field** is the region of the sensory surface, a patch of skin or of the visual field, where a stimulus can change a given cell's activity.

Many fingertip afferents, such as those contacting Merkel cells, have small receptive fields. Afferents of the same kind on the back have much larger ones. Receptor type matters too: a Pacinian afferent's field can cover much of the hand.

**Predict.** Two blunt points touch the skin a short distance apart. Where are you more likely to feel two points rather than one: on a fingertip or on the back?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

On the fingertip, for two reasons. Its receptive fields are small, so two nearby points can fall in different afferents' fields and drive different lines. And the fingertip has many more afferents per square millimetre. On the back, afferents are sparser and their fields larger and overlapping, so the spatial picture is coarser and two nearby points are harder to resolve. Firing can still differ between one point and two, and the classic two-point test can also be passed using intensity and other non-spatial cues (Tong, Mao and Goldreich, 2013).

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/sensory-coding/receptive-field-size.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two equal patches of skin: the fingertip is tiled with many small receptive fields, each with its own afferent fibre, while the back has a few large, overlapping fields." caption="Afferents on the fingertip have small, densely packed receptive fields, while those of the same kind on the back have larger, overlapping fields and are fewer." />

Neurons are tuned in time as well as in space. **Rapidly adapting** endings fire at the start of a maintained stimulus and then fall quiet; many fire again when it is removed. They are tuned to *change*: a Pacinian corpuscle in the skin signals vibration. **Slowly adapting** endings keep firing for as long as the stimulus lasts, as Merkel-cell afferents do during steady indentation (there the Merkel cell, a separate receptor cell, helps sustain the firing), so they report the stimulus itself. Adaptation as a general circuit motif is taken up in [signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/).

## Neighbours compare: lateral inhibition

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

So far each neuron reports on its own patch. Neighbouring receptors can also compare their inputs, and the comparison needs only subtraction.

The evidence comes from the lateral eye of the horseshoe crab Limulus, a compound eye of ommatidia, each sending its own fibre into the optic nerve. Hartline, Wagner and Ratliff (Journal of General Physiology, 1956) recorded with a microelectrode inside one ommatidium, and also from single fibres dissected from the optic nerve. Picture the record.

- Steady light on the ommatidium gives a steady discharge.
- Light its neighbours as well, and the discharge falls.
- That could have been stray light or optics. So they cut the lateral connections to the recorded ommatidium, and the neighbours' light no longer inhibited it.

The inhibition ran through connections among the units, and it exaggerated the difference in response between brightly and dimly lit regions of the eye. Think of each unit as reporting its own light minus a share of its neighbours'.

**Predict.** Now the dim unit right at the edge, which has one bright neighbour. Will its output be above or below the dim plateau of 6? What about the bright unit at the edge?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

*Dim at the edge:* 10 − 0.2 × (10 + 20) = 4, below its plateau.

*Bright at the edge:* 20 − 0.2 × (10 + 20) = 14, above its plateau of 12.

</details>

Now compare input and output. Across the edge, the output still steps by 10 (14 against 4), the same as the input step. What shrank are the uniform regions: the two plateaus now differ by 6 (12 against 6), not 10. So lateral inhibition **discounts uniform regions, and the edge stands out against them**.

Contrast, (bright − dim) ÷ (bright + dim), puts a number on standing out. It is 0 when the two sides are equal and approaches 1 when the dim side is near zero.

- Input: (20 − 10) ÷ (20 + 10) = 10 ÷ 30 = 0.33.
- Output plateaus: (12 − 6) ÷ (12 + 6) = 6 ÷ 18 = 0.33.
- Across the edge: (14 − 4) ÷ (14 + 4) = 10 ÷ 18 = 0.56.

The step at the edge keeps its size while the levels around it fall, so its contrast rises.

This rule is a feedforward simplification. The Hartline-Ratliff model of the real eye uses each neighbour's output, not its input, and has thresholds; the result still holds (numbers in Going deeper). The model predicts bands at a step. People see the light and dark **Mach bands** mainly where a luminance ramp meets a plateau, and an abrupt step does not ordinarily show them, so the band pattern here is an analogy, and lateral inhibition is thought to be part of that story, not all of it. Limulus is not the vertebrate retina, whose surrounds are built by other cells; see [the retina](/lessons/retina/).

## From a tuning curve to a threshold

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Now the fourth puzzle: how fine a change can one neuron report? A tuning curve tells you what the neuron prefers, but telling two nearby stimuli apart needs the noise as well as the mean.

Picture one neuron shown a bar at 20 degrees on many trials, and at 24 degrees on many more. Count the spikes on each trial and draw two histograms, one for each angle. Each histogram has a centre, the mean rate that the tuning curve gives, and a width, the trial-to-trial noise. If the two histograms sit far apart, one trial's count tells you which bar was shown. If they overlap, often it cannot.

What matters is the gap between the two means in units of noise, called **d′** ("d-prime").

*Step 1: the slope.* From 0 to 15 degrees the rate falls by 10 spikes/s, a shallow 0.7 spikes/s per degree near the peak. From 15 to 30 degrees it falls by 20, about 1.3 spikes/s per degree, the steepest segment. From 30 to 45 degrees it falls by 12.

*Step 2: d′.* Near the peak, a 4-degree change moves the mean about 2.7 spikes/s: d′ ≈ 2.7 ÷ 5 ≈ 0.5. On the flank, 4 degrees moves the mean about 5.3 spikes/s: d′ ≈ 5.3 ÷ 5 ≈ 1.1.

The flank pair is about twice as far apart in noise units, so it is easier to tell apart, even though the neuron fires less there: the guiding idea at work. The peak is where a neuron fires most, not where it discriminates best.

</details>

That answer assumed the noise was the same at every angle. Real spike counts are noisier when the rate is higher, with the variance roughly tracking the mean, and that counts against the high-rate peak even more. The task matters too: for coarse judgements between very different stimuli, the most useful part of the curve can shift toward the peak (see Going deeper).

### The observer's curve

A **psychometric function** plots an observer's performance against the size of the stimulus difference; in the tasks here, that is the proportion of correct responses. In a **two-alternative forced-choice** (2AFC) task the observer must pick which of two options held the stimulus, for example which of two intervals. A guess is right half the time, so the curve starts at 50% correct and climbs toward 100% as the difference grows.

That makes 50% chance, not threshold. An observer who scores 50% gives no evidence that she can tell the two intervals apart. A common convention puts the **threshold** halfway between chance and perfect, at 75% correct; other tasks and procedures use other levels (see Going deeper).

The neuron's curve and the animal's curve can share one scale. Imagine an ideal observer that sees one neuron's spike count from each interval and picks the interval whose count better fits the stimulus. Suppose the counts are bell-shaped (Gaussian), independent from one interval to the next, and equally noisy for both stimuli. Then its proportion correct depends on d′ alone:

Now find the d′ that gives threshold, in two steps.

1. Φ(z) = 0.75 when z is about 0.67 (a quarter of the bell curve lies between 0 and 0.67).
2. d′ ÷ √2 = 0.67, so d′ = 0.67 × 1.41, about 0.95.

So a neuron's threshold is roughly the stimulus change that moves its mean rate by one noise unit. To get it in degrees, rearrange d′ ≈ slope × change ÷ noise into change ≈ d′ × noise ÷ slope. For the made-up neuron, the slope is 20/15 = 1.33 spikes/s per degree on the flank and 10/15 = 0.67 near the peak. So 0.95 × 5 ÷ 1.33 is about 3.6 degrees on the flank, and 0.95 × 5 ÷ 0.67 is about 7 degrees near the peak. A curve built this way from one neuron's spike counts is a **neurometric function**, and you can draw it on the same axes as the animal's psychometric function.

### One neuron against one monkey

Britten, Shadlen, Newsome and Movshon (Journal of Neuroscience, 1992) made that comparison. Rhesus monkeys watched a field of dots and reported which of two opposite directions it drifted, and the experimenters set what fraction of the dots moved together, the motion's **coherence**. While a monkey worked, they recorded one neuron in visual area MT, a cortical area where most neurons are tuned to the direction of motion. They matched the display to that neuron's receptive field and preferred direction (the direction that drives it best), and built the neuron's neurometric function from its spike counts on the same trials. Under these conditions, most neurons were about as sensitive as the monkey.

**Predict.** Does this show that one MT neuron made the monkey's choice?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. One neuron's spike count holds about as much signal as the monkey used: a statement about signal, not cause. Many MT neurons respond to the same dots. If many similarly sensitive neurons had independent noise, averaging them would make the monkey far more sensitive than any one of them, and it is not. In a later model by the same group, part of the reason is that neighbouring neurons' noise is weakly correlated. Noise they share rises and falls together, so averaging cannot cancel it. Pools of at least 100 weakly correlated neurons reproduced the observations best, a model estimate, not a count. Whether MT activity *causes* the judgement takes a manipulation. Salzman, Britten and Newsome (Nature, 1990) biased monkeys' choices by microstimulating MT.

</details>

## Lines or patterns: coding what kind of stimulus

Back to the first puzzle, now within one sense: what kind of taste or smell? Quality can be carried by a labelled line or by a **population code**, read from the pattern of rates across many fibres. Taste researchers still argue between the two (Ohla and colleagues, Chemical Senses, 2019).

At the level of taste cells the evidence favours dedicated lines. In mice, sweet, umami, bitter and sour are detected by separate cell types with their own receptors, and making sweet cells express a receptor for a synthetic opiate made mice attracted to that drug (Zhao and colleagues, Cell, 2003). The argument is mainly about the afferent fibres and the brain.

Smell leans toward populations at the receptor level. In olfactory sensory neurons, Malnic, Hirono, Sato and Buck (Cell, 1999) found that one receptor recognizes several odorants and one odorant is recognized by several receptors, so an odorant is coded by a combination. How neurons expressing the same receptor are wired to the same targets in the olfactory bulb is a developmental question, covered in [partners, layers and self-avoidance](/lessons/partners-layers-self-avoidance/).

Taste offers a test. Wu, Dvoryanchikov, Pereira, Chaudhari and Roper (Nature Communications, 2015) imaged taste neurons in the geniculate ganglion of anaesthetized mice expressing GCaMP3, a calcium indicator that brightens when neurons fire, and found that some neurons narrowly tuned at low concentrations became broadly tuned at higher ones.

### Design problem

In that study, 28 of 101 neurons responded to more than one taste below the EC50 (the concentration giving half the maximal response), and 79 of 155 did so at about 1.5 to 2 times the EC50. A classmate says this refutes labelled lines. How would you evaluate the claim, and what experiment would test it?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with the counts. 28 of 101 is 28%, and 79 of 155 is 51%, but they come from different samples of neurons, so the rise could partly reflect which cells were imaged. The stronger evidence is the same neuron recorded at a low and a higher concentration (about 3.5 to 7 times the EC50 in that paired series), which the paper also shows for a subset of neurons.

Next, scope. Broad peripheral tuning says what the ganglion sends, not how the brain decodes it. A decoder could still read a narrowly tuned subset, or the combination.

Then test it causally. Silence or remove one receptor class, or one molecularly defined group of afferents, and measure behaviour at low and high concentrations. If a dedicated "sweet" line carries quality, losing it should remove sweet responses at both concentrations and leave the other tastes intact. A pattern decoder could also lose sweet when its main input is gone, so a loss of sweet alone does not separate the models. The more telling measure is discrimination among the remaining tastes at low and high concentrations: a pattern code that uses those fibres should blur these discriminations too, most where tuning is broad. The result is a scoped counterexample to strict, concentration-invariant labelled lines in mouse geniculate neurons, not a refutation of every labelled-line model.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Touch transduction in more detail

In the Ranade study, the loss of touch was profound, and single primary afferents were recorded in an ex vivo preparation of skin with its nerve attached. Merkel cells are themselves mechanosensitive through Piezo2 and signal to their afferents (Woo and colleagues, Nature, 2014; Maksimovic and colleagues, Nature, 2014), so touch is transduced mostly in the ending itself, but not only there.

### Receptors for smell and taste

The receptor proteins for smell and for sweet, umami and bitter taste are G-protein-coupled receptors. Sour taste is transduced through the proton channel OTOP1. In mice, the sodium channel ENaC carries the amiloride-sensitive sodium taste that makes low salt attractive; the aversive response to high salt recruits other pathways, including the sour- and bitter-sensing cells.

### When noise moves the best stimulus

Take a bell-shaped (Gaussian) tuning curve with no baseline firing, and let its tuning width be the curve's standard deviation. With the same noise at every stimulus, small changes are best detected at the steepest point, one tuning width from the peak. If the spike-count variance equals the mean count, close to the rule for cortical neurons, the best point moves farther out, to about 1.4 tuning widths. Other curve shapes, or a baseline, change these distances. At the peak itself the slope is zero, so a small change there barely moves the mean rate. For the made-up neuron in the lesson, variance-equals-mean noise still favours the 15-to-30-degree segment over the 30-to-45-degree one, but only narrowly.

Butts and Goldman (PLoS Biology, 2006) asked how much a neuron's response says about each stimulus across a broad range. With low noise, where fine distinctions dominate, the best-encoded stimuli lie on steep flanks; as variability grows, coarse distinctions matter more and the most informative stimulus can move back toward the peak. That does not reverse a fixed fine-discrimination comparison like the lesson's: raising the same noise everywhere scales every d′ equally. And d′ itself works for any two stimuli; only the slope shortcut needs a small change.

### Noise that is not only noise

In awake mice, part of the ongoing activity in visual cortex tracks the animal's own behaviour (Stringer and colleagues, 2019), so some of what looks like noise may be signal about something else.

### Threshold conventions

A 50% convention comes from yes/no detection tasks, where threshold is often taken as the level reported on half the trials, a measure that also depends on how willing the observer is to say yes. With equal up and down steps, two-down/one-up and three-down/one-up staircases converge on about 70.7% and 79.4% correct, and the scale parameter of a common 2AFC Weibull fit sits at about 82%. **Lapses** are occasional stimulus-independent errors that pull performance down at large differences.

### Reading a psychometric function in numbers

Take a model 2AFC observer judging an angular difference x, with chosen, not measured, parameters (midpoint 2 degrees, spread 1 degree, no lapses):

> **p(x) = 0.5 + 0.5 × Φ((x − 2°) / 1°)**

Read it in pieces. p(x) is the proportion correct at difference x. Φ, "phi", is the standard normal cumulative distribution. The bracket, z = (x − 2°) / 1°, says how many spreads x sits above the midpoint. The 0.5 + 0.5 × makes the curve run from chance (0.5) up to perfect (1). The shape is a fit over positive differences, so at x = 0 it gives about 51% rather than exactly 50%.

*Step 1: threshold.* Set p = 0.75. Subtract 0.5 and divide by 0.5: Φ(z) = (0.75 − 0.5)/0.5 = 0.5, so z = 0 and x = 2 degrees.

*Step 2: 90% correct.* Φ(z) = (0.90 − 0.5)/0.5 = 0.8, so z = 0.84 and x = 2.84 degrees.

*Step 3: performance at 3 degrees.* z = (3 − 2)/1 = 1 and Φ(1) = 0.841, so p = 0.5 + 0.5 × 0.841 = 0.921, or 92.1% correct.
