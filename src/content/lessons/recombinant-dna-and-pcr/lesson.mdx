  - name: "Hamilton O. Smith"
    contribution: >-
      With Kent Wilcox, purified and characterized a restriction enzyme from Haemophilus
      influenzae Rd, an enzyme that cuts DNA at a defined sequence, which made sequence-specific
      cutting a laboratory tool.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Smith & Wilcox, J. Mol. Biol. 51:379 (1970); Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1978, nobelprize.org."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the late 1960s it was known that some bacteria destroy foreign DNA that enters them while
      sparing their own, a pattern called restriction. What was missing was an enzyme that did the
      cutting at a fixed address. In 1970 Hamilton Smith and Kent Wilcox reported in the Journal of
      Molecular Biology the purification and characterization of a restriction enzyme from
      Haemophilus influenzae strain Rd. A companion paper from Smith's laboratory with Thomas Kelly
      worked out the sequence at which it cuts. An enzyme that always cuts at the same short
      sequence turns a long DNA molecule into a reproducible set of pieces. Daniel Nathans, also at
      Johns Hopkins, soon used such an enzyme to cut the DNA of the virus SV40 into fragments that
      could be ordered into a map. Smith shared the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with
      Werner Arber and Nathans. Every restriction digest in this lesson, from cloning to the
      Southern blot, uses the kind of enzyme his work made available.

    profileSourceAnchor: "Cohen, Chang, Boyer & Helling, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 70:3240 (1973); Cohen, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 110:15521 (2013)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Cohen's laboratory at Stanford studied how bacterial plasmids acquire antibiotic-resistance
      genes. In 1972, with Annie Chang and Leslie Hsu, it reported that E. coli treated with
      calcium chloride could take up purified plasmid DNA and become resistant to antibiotics.
      Boyer's laboratory at the University of California, San Francisco, worked on the restriction
      enzyme EcoRI, whose ends are cohesive. Together, in a PNAS paper published in November 1973
      with Chang and Robert Helling, they cut two different plasmids, joined fragments in vitro,
      transformed E. coli and recovered plasmids that replicated and carried genetic properties
      and sequences from both parents. Paul Berg's group had already joined DNA from different
      sources in a test tube in 1972; the 1973 work showed that a recombinant plasmid could be
      propagated in living bacteria. In 1974 a paper with Cohen and Boyer among its authors
      reported frog ribosomal DNA carried in an E. coli plasmid. That made it possible to grow many
      pieces of DNA you could cut out.
  - name: "Kary B. Mullis"
    contribution: >-
      Conceived the polymerase chain reaction at Cetus Corporation, in which two primers and
      repeated rounds of copying amplify the DNA between them; shared the 1993 Nobel Prize in
      Chemistry with Michael Smith.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Mullis, Nobel Lecture, 8 December 1993; Saiki et al., Science 230:1350 (1985); Saiki et al., Science 239:487 (1988)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Mullis worked at Cetus Corporation, where the laboratory next door, Henry Erlich's, was
      developing ways to detect point mutations. In his Nobel lecture he describes how his own
      attempt to read a single base in human DNA, hard because one gene is a tiny fraction of the
      genome, grew into PCR: use two primers that face each
      other, so that the products of one round of copying become templates for the next. The first
      published application came in 1985 from Randall Saiki and colleagues at Cetus, Mullis among
      them. They amplified a stretch of the human beta-globin gene about 220,000-fold and used it
      to tell the normal and sickle-cell alleles apart. That reaction used the Klenow fragment of
      E. coli DNA polymerase I, which heat destroys, so fresh enzyme was added every cycle. In
      1988 the same group reported PCR with a heat-stable polymerase from Thermus aquaticus,
      which needed no replenishment. Mullis received the 1993 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. PCR made it possible to study a gene from very small samples, down to a single human sperm.
  - name: "Edwin M. Southern"
    contribution: >-
      Developed the transfer of gel-separated DNA fragments to a filter for hybridization with a
      labeled probe, so that a specific sequence can be found among thousands of fragments.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Southern, J. Mol. Biol. 98:503 (1975); Southern, Nature Protocols 1:518 (2006)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      A restriction digest of genomic DNA run on a gel gave a smear, because
      there were too many fragments to see one. Southern, at the MRC Mammalian Genome Unit in
      Edinburgh, wanted to identify the fragments complementary to a given RNA without losing the
      separation the gel had achieved. His 1975 paper in the Journal of Molecular Biology describes
      the method. The DNA in the gel is denatured, then carried by capillary flow onto a cellulose
      nitrate filter laid against it, so the filter holds a copy of the gel's pattern. The filter is
      soaked in radioactive RNA, washed and exposed to film, and only the fragments that pair with
      the probe show up. He demonstrated it with fragments complementary to ribosomal RNA in DNA from
      E. coli, the frog Xenopus laevis and several mammals. The method gave both the size of a
      fragment and its identity, and it became the model for the RNA and protein blots that followed.

techniques:
  - name: "Restriction-ligation cloning"
    blurb: >-
      Vector and insert are cut with restriction enzymes to give compatible ends, joined with DNA
      ligase, and introduced into E. coli by transformation. Antibiotic selection keeps cells that
      carry a plasmid; screening, digestion and sequencing show which plasmid it is.
  - name: "PCR and real-time quantitative PCR"
    blurb: >-
      Two primers and a heat-stable polymerase copy the region between them in repeated cycles of
      denaturing, annealing and extension. Watching fluorescence each cycle gives a Ct value that
      falls as the starting amount of template rises.
  - name: "Southern blotting"
    blurb: >-
      Restriction fragments separated on an agarose gel are denatured and transferred to a
      membrane, then hybridized with a labeled probe. Bands show which fragment sizes carry the
      probe's sequence.
  - name: "Agarose gel electrophoresis"
    blurb: >-
      DNA is loaded into a gel and pulled toward the positive electrode by its negatively charged
      backbone. Smaller linear fragments move faster, and a size ladder in a neighboring lane turns
      position into an estimate of length.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "EcoRI recognizes 5'-GAATTC-3' and cuts each strand between the G and the A. What ends does it leave?"
    choices:
      - "Four-base 5' overhangs reading AATT"
      - "Four-base 3' overhangs reading AATT"
      - "Blunt ends with no unpaired bases"
      - "Six-base 5' overhangs reading GAATTC"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The cut on each strand falls after the G, and because the site is a palindrome the cut on the
      other strand falls four bases away, so each end carries an unpaired 5' AATT. Calling them 3'
      overhangs comes from not tracking strand direction; the unpaired bases are at the 5' ends.
      Blunt ends are what enzymes such as SmaI leave, when both strands are cut at the same point.
      A six-base overhang would require the two cuts to sit at opposite edges of the site, which is
      not where EcoRI cuts.
  - stem: "Why must a cloning vector carry an origin of replication that works in the host?"
    choices:
      - "So the restriction enzyme has a site at which to cut it"
      - "So the host copies it and passes it to daughter cells"
      - "So transformed cells survive on plates with ampicillin"
      - "So RNA polymerase can transcribe the inserted gene"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The origin is where the host's replication machinery starts copying the plasmid; without it
      the plasmid is not copied and is lost as the cells divide. Restriction sites are the cloning
      site, a different element. Survival on ampicillin comes from the resistance gene, the
      selectable marker. Transcription of an insert needs a promoter, and a recombinant plasmid
      need not express its insert at all.
  - stem: "You clone into a lacZ-alpha vector, transform a host that complements lacZ-alpha, and plate on ampicillin with X-gal. What does a white colony most likely carry?"
    choices:
      - "No plasmid, so it cannot make beta-galactosidase"
      - "An empty vector that closed up without an insert"
      - "A plasmid whose lacZ-alpha segment an insert has disrupted"
      - "A plasmid whose ampicillin-resistance gene was disrupted"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      An insert in the cloning site, which lies inside lacZ-alpha, prevents complementation, so no
      active beta-galactosidase forms and X-gal stays colorless. A cell with no plasmid has no
      ampicillin resistance and does not form a colony. An empty vector keeps lacZ-alpha intact and
      gives a blue colony. A disrupted resistance gene would kill the cell on ampicillin. White is
      still only a likely answer: some white colonies carry no insert, so the clone must be checked.
  - stem: "Which description fits a primer pair that will amplify the region between two sites?"
    choices:
      - "Both bind one strand, with their 3' ends pointing the same way"
      - "They bind opposite strands, with 5' ends pointing toward each other"
      - "They bind opposite strands, with melting temperatures 15 °C apart"
      - "They bind opposite strands, with 3' ends pointing toward each other"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Polymerase extends only from a primer's 3' end, so each primer must point into the target on
      its own strand; then each new strand contains a site for the other primer. Two primers on one
      strand make only single-stranded copies that neither primer can use. With 5' ends facing each
      other the primers copy away from the target. Primers 15 °C apart in melting temperature
      rarely both anneal well at one annealing temperature, so primer pairs are matched.
  - stem: "One double-stranded molecule of genomic DNA is the template. Assuming perfect copying, how many double-stranded molecules exactly the length between the two primers exist after three cycles?"
    choices:
      - "Two"
      - "Zero"
      - "Four"
      - "Eight"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Products of exact length, bounded by both primers, first appear in cycle 3, and their number
      per starting duplex is 2^n - 2n after n cycles; for n = 3 that is 8 minus 6, or 2. Zero is the answer after
      two cycles, when every exact-length strand is still paired with a longer one. Eight is the total number
      of duplexes, which includes the longer products and the original strands paired with new copies. Four comes from
      assuming half of all molecules are exact length.

mcatQuestions:
  - stem: "A researcher measures the mRNA of gene X in liver from fasted and fed mice by RT-qPCR. Both assays are about 100% efficient, and the reference gene has been shown not to change between the two conditions. Gene X has a Ct of 22.0 in fasted and 25.0 in fed liver; the reference gene has a Ct of 17.0 in both. How does gene X mRNA compare between the conditions?"
    choices:
      - "About 3-fold higher in fasted liver"
      - "About 8-fold lower in fasted liver"
      - "About 8-fold higher in fasted liver"
      - "About 6-fold higher in fasted liver"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Delta Ct is 22.0 minus 17.0, or 5.0, in fasted liver and 25.0 minus 17.0, or 8.0, in fed
      liver; delta delta Ct is 5.0 minus 8.0, or minus 3.0, and the fold change is 2 to the 3, or
      8, higher in fasted liver because a lower Ct means more starting template. Reading 3-fold
      takes the Ct difference itself as the fold change, ignoring that each cycle doubles the
      product. Reading 8-fold lower reverses the sign, as if a lower Ct meant less template.
      Reading 6-fold multiplies 2 by 3 instead of raising 2 to the third power.
  - stem: "Two cDNA samples are amplified for 40 cycles with the same primers and give equally bright bands of the expected size on a gel. In a real-time run of the same samples, with equal efficiencies near 100%, sample A crosses the threshold at cycle 20 and sample B at cycle 26. What is the best conclusion?"
    choices:
      - "They started with equal target, as the endpoint bands show"
      - "Sample B started with about 6 times more target than A"
      - "Sample A started with about 6 times more target than B"
      - "Sample A started with about 64 times more target than B"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Sample A reaches the threshold six cycles earlier, and at about 100% efficiency each cycle
      doubles the product, so A started with about 2 to the 6, or 64, times more target. Equal
      endpoint bands do not show equal starting amounts, because reactions plateau and different
      inputs can converge on the same final amount. Saying B had more reverses the meaning of Ct,
      since a later crossing means less template. Saying 6 times treats the cycle difference as a
      linear ratio rather than an exponent.
  - stem: "A student measures a transcript in RNA from cultured cells. The reaction with reverse transcriptase gives a Ct of 24. A matched reaction without reverse transcriptase gives a Ct of 25. The no-template control shows no amplification by cycle 40. What is the best interpretation?"
    choices:
      - "The reagents are contaminated with the amplified product"
      - "About half the signal may come from genomic DNA in the RNA"
      - "The transcript is abundant, since both reactions amplified it"
      - "The RNA itself was copied, since reverse transcription failed"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Without reverse transcriptase no cDNA is made, so amplification in that reaction most likely comes
      from DNA already in the RNA preparation; if both reactions are about equally efficient, a Ct only one cycle later means about half as much
      template, so roughly half the signal in the main reaction may be genomic DNA. Contaminated
      reagents would also make the no-template control amplify, and it did not. Amplification in
      both reactions says nothing about abundance when one of them should have been blank. Taq
      amplifies DNA, so a failed reverse transcription would leave little to amplify from
      the RNA itself.
  - stem: "A researcher measures mRNA abundance using conventional reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Why is reverse transcription performed before PCR?"
    choices:
      - "To remove introns from genomic DNA before amplification"
      - "To copy DNA into RNA for the amplification cycles"
      - "To produce cDNA that the PCR polymerase can amplify"
      - "To attach the fluorescent reporter to the target protein"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Reverse transcriptase copies RNA into complementary DNA, providing a template for conventional
      DNA PCR. It does not splice introns from genomic DNA or label proteins. Its relevant direction
      here is RNA to DNA; DNA to RNA is transcription by RNA polymerase, not the preparation needed
      for RT-qPCR.
  - stem: "A plasmid contains an antibiotic-resistance marker and an origin functional in its bacterial host. Which statement explains why the origin is needed for plasmid maintenance?"
    choices:
      - "The origin guarantees segregation into every daughter cell"
      - "The origin permits replication using compatible host machinery"
      - "The resistance gene allows persistence without DNA replication"
      - "The resistance gene itself catalyzes chromosomal integration"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A functional origin enables plasmid replication in a compatible host. Antibiotic resistance
      provides selection but does not catalyze integration or make DNA replication unnecessary.
      Replication and segregation are distinct requirements: an origin alone does not guarantee that
      every daughter inherits the plasmid. Continued selection helps retain plasmid-bearing cells.
  - stem: "A researcher amplifies a DNA segment by PCR. Which component provides the heat-stable catalytic activity?"
    choices:
      - "RNA-dependent RNA polymerase"
      - "Restriction endonuclease"
      - "Thermostable DNA polymerase"
      - "DNA ligase"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      PCR uses a thermostable DNA polymerase to extend DNA primers during repeated temperature cycles.
      Ligase joins DNA ends but does not copy the target region. Restriction enzymes cut DNA.
      RNA-dependent RNA polymerase copies RNA, whereas this PCR amplifies a DNA template.
  - stem: "A standard PCR uses primers flanking opposite ends of a DNA target. One primer is omitted, preventing the usual exponential amplification. What best explains this result?"
    choices:
      - "DNA ligase must join the primers before polymerase can extend"
      - "Double-stranded DNA cannot melt unless both primers are bound"
      - "DNA polymerase can extend only when two primers touch each other"
      - "One primer can support extension, but both strands are needed as repeated amplification templates"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      A single primer can support repeated linear accumulation of new strands from appropriate
      templates. Two opposing primers let products from both strands become templates, generating
      exponential amplification. Primers do not need to touch, DNA melting is driven by temperature,
      and standard PCR does not require ligase to join primers before extension.
  - stem: "A restriction endonuclease cuts a plasmid and a DNA insert, producing compatible sticky ends. What enzyme seals the sugar-phosphate backbone after base pairing?"
    choices:
      - "RNA primase"
      - "DNA helicase"
      - "Type I topoisomerase"
      - "DNA ligase"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Ligase seals nicks by forming phosphodiester bonds between suitable 3′-OH and 5′-phosphate ends.
      Helicase separates strands, and primase synthesizes RNA primers. Topoisomerase transiently
      breaks and reseals DNA to relieve topological strain; it is not the cloning enzyme that joins
      annealed insert and vector ends.
  - stem: "Empty and recombinant plasmids carry the same antibiotic-resistance marker. Which screen most directly distinguishes colonies carrying the intended vector-insert junction?"
    choices:
      - "A blot detecting only the antibiotic-resistance transcript"
      - "Colony PCR with primers spanning a vector-insert junction"
      - "Growth on the plasmid's antibiotic-selection medium alone"
      - "Counting colonies on nonselective medium"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A PCR product spanning a vector-insert junction supports the presence of the intended
      recombinant construct. If empty and recombinant vectors share the resistance marker, antibiotic
      growth and detection of that transcript cannot distinguish them. Nonselective colony counts
      measure viable cells, not insert identity. Sequencing can subsequently verify the insert's full
      sequence.

    text: >-
      Some bacteria have scissors that cut DNA only at one special spelling. Scientists use those
      scissors to cut out a gene and glue it into a tiny ring that bacteria copy whenever they

  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Restriction enzymes come from bacteria, where they cut invading virus DNA at short, specific
      sequences. Many leave short single-stranded tails that stick to any other end cut by the same
      enzyme, and DNA ligase seals the join. Joining a gene into a plasmid and putting it into E.
      coli lets the bacteria make copies as they grow; an antibiotic-resistance gene on the plasmid
      lets only cells with a plasmid survive. PCR copies one region in a test tube: heat separates
      the strands, two short primers stick on either side of the region, and a heat-stable
      polymerase copies it. Each cycle can double the amount, so 30 cycles can make about a billion
      copies from one, until the reaction runs out of steam.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      Type II restriction enzymes cut at defined, often palindromic, sites of four to eight base
      pairs; EcoRI cuts GAATTC to leave 5' AATT overhangs, and others leave blunt ends. Ligase forms
      phosphodiester bonds between a 3'-OH and a 5'-phosphate. A vector needs an origin, a
      selectable marker such as ampicillin resistance and a cloning site; selection keeps
      transformed cells, while screening, for example by insertional inactivation of lacZ-alpha on
      X-gal, suggests which carry an insert, and digestion or sequencing confirms it. PCR repeats
      denaturation, annealing of two inward-facing primers on opposite strands, and extension. At
      100% efficiency the target doubles each cycle, N = N0 x 2^n, until the reaction plateaus. In
      real-time qPCR the threshold cycle Ct falls by about one for each doubling of starting
      template, and with equal, near-100% efficiencies the fold change is 2 to the minus delta
      delta Ct. RT-PCR adds a reverse-transcription step to start from RNA. Gels sort by size;
      Southern, Northern and Western blots add a probe or antibody to identify DNA, RNA or protein.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      The constant-efficiency model, N_c = N_0(1 + E)^c, holds only in the early cycles; product
      accumulation then slows, and experiments have implicated polymerase binding to accumulated
      product in one system and assay-dependent primer depletion in others. Because inputs converge
      at the plateau, quantification uses the cycle at which fluorescence crosses a threshold, and
      either a standard curve of known inputs (absolute) or the 2^-ΔΔCt method (relative), which
      assumes equal efficiencies near 100% and a reference gene validated for the experiment.
      Vandesompele and colleagues found large errors from single reference genes in a substantial
      fraction of human tissue samples, which is why geometric means of several validated
      references are used. MIQE guidelines (2009) and their précis (2010) ask for no-RT and no-template controls, and the 2025 revision asks for prediction intervals, detection limits and dynamic ranges for each target.
      On the cloning side, selection and color are phenotypes, not sequence: some white
      colonies carry no insert, so sequencing across the junctions
      and the insert remains the decisive check. An open question for any qPCR study is how much of the variance
      between replicates comes from efficiency differences that a single assumed E hides.

> **The one idea.** Amplification solves scarcity, not identity: cut sites and primers set what can be joined or copied, while readouts and controls test what actually was.
>
> *Scope:* this lesson's methods. A restriction site or primer pair can still admit an unintended product, and not every readout establishes sequence. Nor does making more DNA report the starting amount; only qPCR timing estimates that, under stated assumptions.

## Cut at a known site, then seal the join

Many bacteria fight phages, viruses that inject their DNA into bacterial cells, with a restriction enzyme that cuts unmodified copies of a short recognition sequence and a methylase that marks those sites in their own DNA. Host DNA is usually spared; matching sites in incoming unmarked phage DNA can be cut.

Laboratory **type II restriction enzymes** recognize defined sequences and cut at fixed positions in or near them. Many sites are palindromes of four to eight base pairs: they read the same 5' to 3' on both strands. EcoRI, from *E. coli*, recognizes 5'-GAATTC-3' and cuts each strand between G and A. The cuts sit four bases apart, leaving single-stranded 5' AATT overhangs that let EcoRI ends pair, so they are called **sticky**, or cohesive. SmaI cuts both strands at the same point in CCCGGG and leaves **blunt ends**.

**Predict.** EcoRI leaves AATT overhangs and BamHI leaves GATC. Will an EcoRI-cut insert pair with a BamHI-cut vector?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. “Sticky” means single-stranded, not universally compatible; AATT and GATC do not pair. For enzymes like these, which leave a fixed overhang, the same enzyme on vector and insert gives compatible ends; two enzymes whose ends are distinct and cannot pair, such as EcoRI at one end and BamHI at the other, also fix the insert's orientation.

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/recombinant-dna-and-pcr/sticky-ends-and-ligase.webp" width={1200} height={430} alt="AI-generated illustration: a three-step strip. Like matching jigsaw tabs, compatible ends fit but only loosely. **DNA ligase** forms the missing phosphodiester bond between a 3'-OH and a 5'-phosphate on each strand. T4 DNA ligase can also join blunt ends in vitro.

## Into bacteria: retain, screen, confirm

A **plasmid** is a small DNA circle copied separately from the bacterial chromosome (see [bacterial and phage genetics](/lessons/bacterial-and-phage-genetics/)). A cloning vector carries an **origin of replication**, without which the plasmid is lost as cells divide; a **selectable marker** such as ampicillin resistance; and a **cloning site** of unique restriction sites.

Bacterial uptake of naked DNA is **transformation**: cold calcium chloride and a brief heat shock, or an electric pulse (**electroporation**), let a fraction of cells take it up. It differs from **transfection** (DNA entry into animal cells) and **oncogenic transformation** (a cell becoming cancer-like).

**Selection** on ampicillin kills plasmid-free cells but spares empty vectors, so **screening** asks which plasmid survived. In blue-white screening, the cloning site lies inside **lacZ-alpha**, a short part of the beta-galactosidase gene from the [lac operon](/lessons/lac-operon/). The host makes the rest of the enzyme; together the parts turn colorless X-gal blue. An insert usually disrupts lacZ-alpha, so recombinant colonies stay white, but some white colonies carry no insert.

**Predict.** A colony on the same ampicillin plate is blue. What does it most likely carry?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A vector that closed up without an insert, leaving lacZ-alpha intact. Pick white colonies, but white is a strong hint, not proof: check each as the first film did.

</details>

Expressing the insert needs a promoter and other expression parts (see [hacking the cell](/lessons/hacking-the-cell/)).

## PCR: copying one bounded region

PCR needs a template, two primers, four dNTPs (see [nucleotide metabolism](/lessons/nucleotide-metabolism/)) and a DNA polymerase, which adds nucleotides only to the 3'-OH of a primer paired with a template, as in [DNA replication](/lessons/dna-replication/). Each cycle has three steps:

1. **Denature** at about 94 to 98 °C to separate the strands.
2. **Anneal** a few degrees below the primers' melting temperature so each primer pairs with its site.
3. **Extend**, typically at 72 °C for Taq polymerase, which lengthens each primer 5' to 3'.

Primers are commonly 15 to 30 nucleotides, with matched melting temperatures and no self-pairing or hairpins. Primer-BLAST can check a pair against a sequence database for unintended targets.

**Predict.** After two ideal cycles from one long genomic duplex, are any double-stranded molecules bounded by both primers?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The first copies start at one primer but run past the other site. Exact-length strands appear in cycle 2, paired with longer strands; exact-length duplexes first appear in cycle 3.

</details>

After *n* ideal cycles, exact-length duplexes number 2^n - 2n per starting genomic duplex. By cycle 30, they are nearly all the product.

<PcrCyclePlotter />

**Worked problem (a).** Starting from 10 double-stranded template molecules with perfect doubling every cycle, how many copies are there after 30 cycles?

> N = N0 x (1 + E)^n, with E = 1 for perfect doubling

1. Each cycle multiplies by 2, so 30 cycles multiply by 2^30 = 1,073,741,824.
2. N = 10 x 1,073,741,824 = 10,737,418,240, or about 1.07 x 10^10.

Amplification is exponential only early; in one model system, reactions with different starting amounts converged on the same plateau. Making more DNA neither identifies nor quantifies it.

### Counting from when the signal appears

The **threshold cycle, Ct**, from the second film, is the cycle at which fluorescence crosses a set threshold during exponential amplification. **Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)** reads fluorescence each cycle with a double-stranded-DNA dye or a sequence-specific probe. **RT-PCR** (reverse-transcription PCR) first copies RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA); **RT-qPCR** combines them to measure mRNA. With matched, near-perfect efficiency and the same threshold, a lower Ct means more starting template; at 100% efficiency, one cycle earlier means twice the starting template.

**Predict.** Target and reference Ct values both move one cycle earlier in a treated sample. Has target abundance changed relative to the reference?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Subtracting reference Ct from target Ct removes the shared shift, so delta Ct is unchanged; relative qPCR asks whether that gap changes between conditions.

</details>

**Worked problem (b).** Both reactions are about 100% efficient. Target gene Ct is 24.0 in treated cells and 26.5 in control; reference gene Ct is 18.0 in treated and 18.5 in control.

> fold change = 2^(-ΔΔCt), where ΔCt = Ct(target) - Ct(reference)

1. ΔCt treated = 24.0 - 18.0 = 6.0.
2. ΔCt control = 26.5 - 18.5 = 8.0.
3. ΔΔCt = 6.0 - 8.0 = -2.0.
4. Fold change = 2^2 = 4. Target mRNA is about 4-fold higher in treated cells.

The method assumes equal efficiencies near 100% for both assays and a reference gene shown not to change in this experiment.

## Reading the result: size, identity and controls

In an agarose gel, DNA's negatively charged backbone moves toward the positive electrode, and smaller linear fragments move faster through the mesh. A **size ladder** of known fragments in a neighboring lane turns position into an estimated length. Uncut circular plasmid runs differently from the same plasmid cut into a line. A right-sized band is evidence about size, not identity.

Blots add identity: in Southern's 1975 method, gel-separated DNA fragments are denatured, transferred to a membrane and paired with a labeled probe. Only complementary fragments light up. A Northern blot does the same for RNA. A Western blot transfers proteins separated by SDS-PAGE and detects one with an antibody (see [protein purification techniques](/lessons/protein-purification-techniques/)).

**Predict.** In an RT-qPCR run, the no-reverse-transcriptase control crosses the threshold ten cycles after the matched full reaction. Does genomic DNA explain much of the signal?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Very little. With no cDNA made, that signal comes from DNA already in the RNA. At about equal efficiency, ten cycles later means about 2^10, or roughly 1,000-fold, less template: about a tenth of a percent of the signal.

</details>

Match each control to a failure:

- a **size ladder** calibrates every gel;
- a **no-template control** detects PCR contamination, and a **no-RT control** detects genomic DNA in an RNA preparation;
- a **positive control** shows the assay works, and an inhibitor check such as a spike-in shows that a negative sample means the target was not detected rather than that the reaction failed;
- a **loading control** on a blot, such as a stable-RNA reprobe or total-protein stain, supports comparison within its linear range.

### Design problem

You cut a 5.0 kb vector and a 1.2 kb insert with EcoRI and BamHI, ligate, transform and select on ampicillin. Insert plus vector gives 200 colonies; vector alone with ligase gives 180. Interpret the result and redesign the experiment.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Interpret.** An EcoRI end (AATT overhang) cannot pair with a BamHI end (GATC overhang), so a vector cut by both enzymes cannot close on itself. Colonies from vector alone mostly come from vector that was cut by only one enzyme, whose matching ends re-ligate, or not cut at all, since uncut plasmid transforms well. Less often, the small piece cut out between the two sites is ligated back in. That background is 180 of 200, so most colonies on the insert plate probably lack the insert.

**Redesign.**
1. Digest the vector longer or with more enzyme, and check each single digest.
2. Gel-purify the cut vector away from uncut circles. When the two sites sit close together, as in a cloning site, singly and doubly cut vector run at almost the same size, so this step alone is not enough.
3. Dephosphorylate the vector with alkaline phosphatase. Without 5'-phosphates it cannot re-ligate, while the insert still supplies phosphates for each junction.
4. Add controls: cut vector without ligase, to measure carry-over of uncut plasmid (the vector-only ligation above adds singly cut vector that re-closes); and uncut plasmid, to measure transformation efficiency.
5. Screen colonies by colony PCR or by EcoRI plus BamHI digest, expecting bands of 5.0 and 1.2 kb, then sequence across both junctions and through the insert.

</details>

## Going deeper

### From test-tube joins to copied constructs

In 1972 David Jackson, Robert Symons and Paul Berg joined lambda phage genes and the *E. coli* galactose operon to SV40 DNA entirely in a test tube. That year Janet Mertz and Ronald Davis showed that EcoRI ends are complementary and that a restriction enzyme plus ligase can make hybrid molecules.

In 1973 Stanley Cohen, Annie Chang, Herbert Boyer and Robert Helling joined restriction fragments from separate plasmids, transformed *E. coli* and recovered plasmids with properties of both parents. Berg's group made recombinant DNA first; the Cohen and Boyer collaboration first showed a recombinant plasmid replicating in a cell.

### Why PCR no longer needs fresh enzyme each cycle

The first published PCR, in 1985, used the Klenow fragment of *E. coli* DNA polymerase I. Heating destroyed it, so fresh enzyme was added every cycle. That Cetus team amplified a short beta-globin region about 220,000-fold.

In 1988 the group reported PCR with polymerase from *Thermus aquaticus*, which survives denaturation. Taq lacks a 3' to 5' proofreading exonuclease, so its copies carry more errors. In one 1991 reporter assay, products made with Pfu polymerase carried fewer than a tenth as many mutations. Kary Mullis shared the 1993 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for conceiving PCR.
