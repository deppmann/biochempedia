  - name: "Southern blotting"
    blurb: >-
      Restriction fragments separated on an agarose gel are denatured and transferred to a

      probe's sequence.
  - name: "Agarose gel electrophoresis"
    blurb: >-
      DNA is loaded into a gel and pulled toward the positive electrode by its negatively charged
      backbone. Smaller linear fragments move faster, and a size ladder in a neighboring lane turns
      position into an estimate of length.

      - "Four-base 3' overhangs reading AATT"
      - "Blunt ends with no unpaired bases"
      - "Six-base 5' overhangs reading GAATTC"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The cut on each strand falls after the G, and because the site is a palindrome the cut on the
      other strand falls four bases away, so each end carries an unpaired 5' AATT. Calling them 3'
      overhangs comes from not tracking strand direction; the unpaired bases are at the 5' ends.
      Blunt ends are what enzymes such as SmaI leave, when both strands are cut at the same point.
      A six-base overhang would require the two cuts to sit at opposite edges of the site, which is
      not where EcoRI cuts.

  - stem: "Which description fits a primer pair that will amplify the region between two sites?"
    choices:
      - "Both bind one strand, with their 3' ends pointing the same way"
      - "They bind opposite strands, with 5' ends pointing toward each other"
      - "They bind opposite strands, with melting temperatures 15 °C apart"
      - "They bind opposite strands, with 3' ends pointing toward each other"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Polymerase extends only from a primer's 3' end, so each primer must point into the target on
      its own strand; then each new strand contains a site for the other primer. Two primers on one
      strand make only single-stranded copies that neither primer can use. With 5' ends facing each
      other the primers copy away from the target. Primers 15 °C apart in melting temperature
      cannot both anneal well at one annealing temperature, so primer pairs are matched.
  - stem: "One double-stranded molecule of genomic DNA is the template. Assuming perfect copying, how many double-stranded molecules exactly the length between the two primers exist after three cycles?"
    choices:
      - "Two"
      - "Zero"
      - "Four"
      - "Eight"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Products of exact length, bounded by both primers, first appear in cycle 3, and their number
      per starting duplex is 2^n - 2n after n cycles; for n = 3 that is 8 minus 6, or 2. Zero is the answer after

    rationale: >-
      Delta Ct is 22.0 minus 17.0, or 5.0, in fasted liver and 25.0 minus 17.0, or 8.0, in fed
      liver; delta delta Ct is 5.0 minus 8.0, or minus 3.0, and the fold change is 2 to the 3, or
      8, higher in fasted liver because a lower Ct means more starting template. Reading 3-fold
      takes the Ct difference itself as the fold change, ignoring that each cycle doubles the
      product. Reading 8-fold lower reverses the sign, as if a lower Ct meant less template.
      Reading 6-fold multiplies 2 by 3 instead of raising 2 to the third power.

**In 1985 a team at Cetus Corporation reported raising the number of copies of one short stretch of the human beta-globin gene about 220,000-fold, in a tube, with no living cell involved.**

Before then, the usual way to get many copies of one piece of DNA was to cut it out, paste it into a small circle of DNA that bacteria copy, and let the bacteria grow. Both routes are still in daily use. This lesson takes them in order, works through the arithmetic that makes PCR powerful and quantitative, and ends with how you read the result and which controls make it believable.

## Cut and paste: restriction enzymes and ligase

Bacteria are attacked by phages, viruses that inject their DNA. Many bacteria defend themselves with a restriction enzyme, which cuts DNA at one short sequence, paired with a methylase that marks the same sequence in the cell's own DNA so that it is spared. Unmarked phage DNA gets cut.

The restriction enzymes used in the laboratory are type II enzymes. Each recognizes a defined sequence, in many cases a palindrome of four to eight base pairs, and cuts at a fixed position in or near it. Palindrome here means the site reads the same 5' to 3' on both strands. EcoRI, from E. coli, recognizes 5'-GAATTC-3' and cuts each strand between the G and the A. The two cuts are four bases apart, so each end carries a single-stranded 5' overhang reading AATT. Any EcoRI end can pair with any other EcoRI end through those four bases, which is why such ends are called sticky, or cohesive. Other enzymes, such as SmaI at CCCGGG, cut both strands at the same point and leave blunt ends.

Think of a jigsaw cutter that always cuts the same tab shape: any two pieces cut by it fit, but only loosely. DNA ligase makes the join permanent by forming the missing phosphodiester bond between a 3'-OH and a 5'-phosphate on each strand. T4 DNA ligase can also join blunt ends in vitro.

The first recombinant molecules were made entirely in the test tube. In 1972 David Jackson, Robert Symons and Paul Berg joined lambda phage genes and the E. coli galactose operon to the DNA of the virus SV40. The same year Janet Mertz and Ronald Davis showed that EcoRI ends are complementary and that restriction enzyme plus ligase can make hybrid molecules. The next step was to have such a molecule copied by a living cell.

## Into bacteria: finding the colony that carries your insert

A plasmid is a small circle of DNA that a bacterium copies separately from its chromosome (see [bacterial and phage genetics](/lessons/bacterial-and-phage-genetics/)). A cloning vector is a plasmid reduced to three working parts. An **origin of replication** is the site where the host's replication machinery starts copying; without it the plasmid is not copied and is lost as cells divide. A **selectable marker**, such as a gene for ampicillin resistance, lets only plasmid-bearing cells grow. A **cloning site** holds unique restriction sites where the insert goes.

In 1973 Stanley Cohen, Annie Chang, Herbert Boyer and Robert Helling joined restriction fragments from separate plasmids, put the products into E. coli and recovered plasmids that replicated and carried properties of both parents. Berg's group had made recombinant DNA first; the Cohen and Boyer collaboration first showed a recombinant plasmid replicating in a cell.

Getting the plasmid in is **transformation**: uptake of naked DNA by a bacterial cell. Cells are made competent with cold calcium chloride or by electroporation, and only a fraction take up a plasmid. Bacterial transformation is a different process from transfection, which is putting DNA into animal cells, and from oncogenic transformation, which is a cell becoming cancer-like.

Now the two questions every cloning experiment must keep apart. **Selection** asks whether a cell has a plasmid: on ampicillin, cells without one die. But a vector that closed up without an insert also confers resistance. **Screening** asks which plasmid it is. In blue-white screening the cloning site sits inside lacZ-alpha, a short piece of the beta-galactosidase gene from the [lac operon](/lessons/lac-operon/). The host strain makes the rest of the enzyme, and the two pieces together are active. Active enzyme turns the colorless substrate X-gal blue. An insert in the cloning site breaks lacZ-alpha, so recombinant colonies stay white. White is a strong hint, not proof: some white colonies carry no insert. The final checks are a restriction digest or colony PCR, then sequencing across both junctions. Even then a correct plasmid need not express its insert; that needs a promoter and the other parts of an expression construct (see [hacking the cell](/lessons/hacking-the-cell/)).

## PCR: copying one region a billion times

PCR needs a template, two primers, the four dNTPs (see [nucleotide metabolism](/lessons/nucleotide-metabolism/)) and a DNA polymerase. Polymerase can only add nucleotides to the 3'-OH of a primer paired to a template, as you saw in [DNA replication](/lessons/dna-replication/). Each cycle has three steps:

1. **Denature** at about 94 to 98 °C, so the two strands separate.
2. **Anneal** at a lower temperature, set a few degrees below the primers' melting temperature, so each primer pairs with its complementary site.
3. **Extend**, typically at 72 °C for Taq polymerase, so each primer is lengthened 5' to 3'.

Primer design follows from the polymerase rule. The two primers bind **opposite strands**, with their 3' ends **pointing toward each other**, so each new strand contains a binding site for the other primer. Then products of one cycle become templates for the next, like a photocopier whose copies can be copied. Primers are commonly 15 to 30 nucleotides, the pair should have matched melting temperatures, and each should avoid pairing with itself or folding into a hairpin. Tools such as Primer-BLAST check a pair against a sequence database for unintended targets.

Exact-length double-stranded products do not appear at once. In cycle 1, copies of a genomic template start at a primer but run past the other site. Molecules bounded by both primers first appear in cycle 3, and after n cycles there are 2^n - 2n of them per starting duplex. By cycle 30 they are nearly all of the product.

<PcrCyclePlotter />

**Worked problem (a).** You start with 10 double-stranded template molecules. Assuming perfect doubling every cycle, how many copies are there after 30 cycles?

> N = N0 x (1 + E)^n, with E = 1 for perfect doubling

1. Each cycle multiplies by 2, so 30 cycles multiply by 2^30 = 1,073,741,824.
2. N = 10 x 1,073,741,824 = 10,737,418,240, or about 1.07 x 10^10.

A real reaction stops short. Amplification is exponential only in the early cycles; then it slows and reaches a plateau. In one model system, reactions with different starting amounts converged on the same final level, so a bright endpoint band does not tell you how much template you began with.

The first published PCR, in 1985, used the Klenow fragment of E. coli DNA polymerase I, which heat destroys, so fresh enzyme went in every cycle. In 1988 the Cetus group reported PCR with the polymerase of Thermus aquaticus, which survives denaturation. Taq lacks a 3' to 5' proofreading exonuclease, so its copies carry more errors; in one 1991 reporter assay, products made with Pfu polymerase carried fewer than a tenth as many mutations. Kary Mullis shared the 1993 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for conceiving PCR.

### Counting with qPCR

Two names are easily confused. **RT-PCR** is reverse-transcription PCR: reverse transcriptase first copies RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA), which is then amplified. **Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)** measures product during cycling with a fluorescent dye that binds double-stranded DNA or a sequence-specific probe. RT-qPCR combines the two to measure mRNA.

The readout is the **threshold cycle, Ct**: the cycle at which fluorescence crosses a set threshold during the exponential phase. A lower Ct means more starting template, because fewer doublings are needed to reach the same amount of product. At 100% efficiency, one cycle earlier means twice as much template.

**Worked problem (b).** Both reactions are about 100% efficient. Target gene Ct is 24.0 in treated cells and 26.5 in control; reference gene Ct is 18.0 in treated and 18.5 in control.

> fold change = 2^(-ΔΔCt), where ΔCt = Ct(target) - Ct(reference)

1. ΔCt treated = 24.0 - 18.0 = 6.0.
2. ΔCt control = 26.5 - 18.5 = 8.0.
3. ΔΔCt = 6.0 - 8.0 = -2.0.
4. Fold change = 2^2 = 4. Target mRNA is about 4-fold higher in treated cells.

The method assumes equal efficiencies near 100% for both assays and a reference gene shown not to change in this experiment.

## Reading the result: gels and blots

DNA's phosphate backbone is negatively charged, so in an agarose gel it moves toward the positive electrode. The gel is a mesh, and smaller linear fragments slip through faster. A **size ladder** of known fragments in a neighboring lane turns position into length. Uncut circular plasmid runs at a different position from the same plasmid cut to a line. A band of the right size is evidence about size, not identity.

Blots add identity. In Southern's 1975 method, fragments in the gel are denatured and transferred to a membrane, which is then soaked in a labeled probe; only fragments that pair with it light up. The Northern blot applies the same idea to RNA. The Western blot transfers proteins separated by SDS-PAGE and detects one with an antibody (covered in [protein purification techniques](/lessons/protein-purification-techniques/)).

Each readout needs its controls:

- a **size ladder** on every gel;
- a **no-template control** for every PCR, to detect contamination, and a **no-RT control** for RNA, to detect genomic DNA;
- a **positive control**, so a negative result means the target was not detected rather than that the reaction failed;
- a **loading control** on blots, such as a reprobe for a stable RNA or a total-protein stain, read within its linear range.

### Design problem

You cut a 5.0 kb vector and a 1.2 kb insert with EcoRI and BamHI, ligate, transform and select on ampicillin. Insert plus vector gives 200 colonies; vector alone with ligase gives 180. Interpret the result and redesign the experiment.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Interpret.** An EcoRI end (AATT overhang) cannot pair with a BamHI end (GATC overhang), so a vector cut by both enzymes cannot close on itself. Colonies from vector alone must come from vector that was cut by only one enzyme, whose matching ends re-ligate, or not cut at all, since uncut plasmid transforms well. That background is 180 of 200, so most colonies on the insert plate probably lack the insert.

**Redesign.**
1. Digest the vector longer or with more enzyme, and check each single digest.
2. Gel-purify the cut vector away from uncut circles. Singly and doubly cut vector run at almost the same size, so this step alone is not enough.
3. Dephosphorylate the vector with alkaline phosphatase. Without 5'-phosphates it cannot re-ligate, while the insert still supplies phosphates for each junction.
4. Add controls: cut vector without ligase, to measure carry-over of uncut plasmid (the vector-only ligation above adds singly cut vector that re-closes); and uncut plasmid, to measure transformation efficiency.
5. Screen colonies by colony PCR or by EcoRI plus BamHI digest, expecting bands of 5.0 and 1.2 kb, then sequence across both junctions.

</details>
