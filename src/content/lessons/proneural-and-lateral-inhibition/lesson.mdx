summary: >-
  In a small group of equivalent cells, how is it that usually only one cell becomes a neural
  precursor while its neighbours do not? One idea carries the lesson: in the fly, proneural factors
  say which cells may become neural and Notch says which one does, by comparing each cell with its
  neighbours, and for cells that start alike the comparison breaks the tie only if the feedback loop is steep. You follow it from fly clusters and mosaics to a
  worked model of the loop, then into mouse neural progenitors, where the same parts act as a brake
  and a clock, and you judge what a proneural gene does and does not decide.
hook: "Every cell in the cluster could become the neuron. Why does only one?"

scientists:
  - name: "Pat Simpson"
    contribution: >-
      With Pascal Heitzler, used genetic mosaics in the fly to show that a cell with less Notch
      than its neighbours tends to become a neural precursor, so a cell's choice depends on its
      signaling relative to the cells beside it and on competition between them.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Heitzler & Simpson, Cell 64:1083 (1991), The choice of cell fate in the epidermis of Drosophila."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the late 1980s it was clear that fly embryos lacking Notch or Delta make too many neural
      cells. What was not clear was how the decision is made between two neighbours that both
      carry the receptor and the ligand. Pascal Heitzler and Pat Simpson attacked this with
      genetic mosaics in the fly. They produced flies in which patches of cells carried mutant
      Notch or Delta, or a different level of Notch activity, beside normal cells, then asked
      which cells at the patch borders became neural precursors. Cells mutant for Notch became
      neural on their own beside normal cells, but cells mutant for Delta did not. A normal cell
      beside cells with less Notch activity than its own became epidermis, and beside cells with
      more Notch activity it became a neural precursor. In these mosaics, a cell's fate depended on its
      Notch activity relative to its neighbours. Their 1991 paper in Cell argued that the cells
      compete, and that Notch is the receptor for an inhibitory signal sent by the neural
      precursors. That picture of lateral
      inhibition with feedback was later written as a mathematical model and remains the
      standard way to reason about the choice.
  - name: "Yuh Nung Jan and Lily Jan"
    contribution: >-
      Their laboratory, with Andrew Jarman, identified atonal as the proneural gene for the fly's
      chordotonal organs and then for the founding photoreceptor of each unit of the eye,
      showing that different proneural genes start different kinds of sense organ.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Jarman, Grau, Jan & Jan, Cell 73:1307 (1993); Jarman, Grell, Ackerman, Jan & Jan, Nature 369:398 (1994)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The achaete-scute genes were known to start the fly's bristles, but not every fly sense
      organ is a bristle. Chordotonal organs are internal stretch receptors that sense the
      movement of the body wall and the joints. In 1993 Andrew Jarman, Yves Grau, Lily Jan and
      Yuh Nung Jan reported a gene, atonal, that encodes a bHLH protein related to the
      achaete-scute proteins and that is expressed in the clusters that give rise to chordotonal
      organs. Deleting the region that contains it removed those organs, and forcing it on made
      extra ones. A year later, with Ellsworth Grell and Larry Ackerman, they showed that atonal is
      also needed for R8, the first photoreceptor of each unit of the compound eye, which then
      recruits the others. In 1995 mutants of atonal confirmed that the chordotonal loss came from
      atonal itself. The work established that proneural genes form a family whose members start
      different sense organs, and it led to the mouse relative Atoh1, which is needed for the
      hair cells of the inner ear.
  - name: "Ryoichiro Kageyama"
    contribution: >-
      Showed that the Notch target Hes1 keeps mouse neural progenitors from differentiating too
      early, and that Hes1 levels oscillate with a period of a few hours, first in cultured cells
      and then in neural progenitors, with Neurog2 and Dll1 oscillating in turn.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Ishibashi et al., Genes Dev. 9:3136 (1995); Hirata et al., Science 298:840 (2002); Shimojo, Ohtsuka & Kageyama, Neuron 58:52 (2008)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Hes1 is a mouse relative of the fly genes hairy and Enhancer of split, and Kageyama's
      laboratory in Kyoto studied it from the early 1990s. In 1995, with first author M. Ishibashi,
      François Guillemot and others, it reported mice lacking Hes1. Their neural tubes made
      neurons too early, expressed more of the proneural factor Mash1 (now Ascl1), and had severe
      neural tube defects. So a Notch target was needed to hold progenitors back. In 2002 Hiromi
      Hirata and colleagues found that Hes1 messenger RNA and protein rise and fall with a period
      of about two hours in several kinds of cultured cells after a serum change, because Hes1
      represses its own gene, its messenger RNA and protein are short-lived, and making new protein
      takes time. In 2008
      Hiromi Shimojo, Toshiyuki Ohtsuka and Kageyama imaged reporters in mouse neural
      progenitors and found that Hes1 oscillates there too, with Neurog2 and Dll1 cycling
      opposite to it. The work made the timing of a signal, and not only its level, a variable
      that developmental biologists measure.
  - name: "François Guillemot"
    contribution: >-
      With Carol Fode, Carlos Parras and colleagues, swapped the coding regions of Ascl1 and
      Neurog2 in the mouse to ask whether these proneural factors carry dorsal or ventral
      forebrain identity, and found that they do so only partly and unequally.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Fode et al., Genes Dev. 14:67 (2000); Parras et al., Genes Dev. 16:324 (2002)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Guillemot's laboratory had shown in 1993 that mice lacking Mash1, the mouse relative of
      achaete-scute now called Ascl1, lose olfactory and autonomic neurons. In the embryonic
      forebrain, Neurog2 is high in dorsal progenitors that make cortical neurons and Ascl1 is
      high in ventral progenitors of the ganglionic eminences. Do the two proteins carry those
      identities, or does the territory? In 2000 Carol Fode and colleagues found that removing
      Neurog2 let Ascl1 rise dorsally and turned on a subset of ventral markers, such as Dlx1 and
      GAD67, in dorsal cells, a change that needed Ascl1. Placing the Ascl1 coding region at the
      Neurog2 locus induced some ventral markers as well. In 2002 Carlos Parras, Carol
      Schuurmans and colleagues made both swaps, including the reverse one. Neurog2 at the Ascl1 locus restored
      ventral neurogenesis but did not switch on dorsal markers. These experiments made the
      question of factor versus context testable, and later work compared the two factors' DNA
      binding directly.

techniques:
  - name: "Genetic mosaics"
    blurb: >-
      An animal is built so that patches of cells carry a different dose or version of a gene
      than the cells around them. Recording which cells at a patch border take which fate shows
      whether a gene acts in the cell that sends a signal or the cell that receives it, and
      whether the comparison between neighbours matters.
  - name: "In situ hybridization of proneural clusters"
    blurb: >-
      A labeled probe that pairs with one messenger RNA shows where a gene is expressed in a fixed
      embryo. Staging many embryos shows a gene expressed first in a group of cells and later in
      one, which is how competence and selection were told apart.
  - name: "Coding-region swap (knock-in)"
    blurb: >-
      In mouse embryonic stem cells, the protein-coding part of one gene is replaced with that of
      another while the original regulatory DNA is kept. Comparing the result with a plain
      knockout asks whether two related proteins can do each other's job in the same place and
      time.
  - name: "Live bioluminescence imaging"
    blurb: >-
      A gene's promoter drives a short-lived luciferase whose light is recorded over hours from
      single cells in culture or in tissue slices. It reveals oscillations that fixed samples
      cannot, since a snapshot of an oscillating population looks like random variation.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "What kind of protein does a proneural gene such as achaete, atonal or Ascl1 encode?"
    choices:
      - "A bHLH transcription factor that binds DNA as a dimer"
      - "A membrane receptor cut to release its inner domain"
      - "A repressor that Notch signaling turns on next door"
      - "A secreted morphogen that spreads across the cluster"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Proneural genes encode basic helix-loop-helix transcription factors that pair with an E
      protein (Daughterless in the fly) and bind E-box sequences in DNA. A membrane receptor cut to
      release its inner domain describes Notch, which is the inhibitory side of the circuit. A
      secreted morphogen describes signals such as BMP or Shh that pattern the tube; proneural
      proteins stay inside the cell that makes them. A repressor turned on by Notch describes the
      E(spl) and Hes proteins, which shut proneural genes off.
  - stem: "A fly embryo has no working Notch. What does its neurogenic ectoderm produce?"
    choices:
      - "Normal neuroblasts but no proneural clusters"
      - "Normal neuroblasts placed at random positions"
      - "Too few neuroblasts and too much epidermis"
      - "Too many neuroblasts and too little epidermis"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Without Notch no cell in a proneural cluster can be inhibited by its neighbours, so many or
      all of them become neuroblasts and too little epidermis is left; this is the neurogenic
      phenotype. Too few neuroblasts is what loss of proneural genes, not Notch, would give.
      Proneural clusters are placed by regulatory DNA and still form without Notch, so a loss of
      clusters is wrong. Normal neuroblast numbers in random places misreads Notch as a
      positioning signal; it selects within clusters and does not place them.
  - stem: "A student asks whether Notch is in the stem cell or the non-stem cell. In two neighbouring mouse neural progenitors, which statement is correct?"
    choices:
      - "Both make Notch; the one receiving more Delta stays a progenitor"
      - "Both cells keep exactly equal Notch activity throughout"
      - "Only the cell that becomes a neuron makes the Notch receptor"
      - "Only the cell that stays a progenitor makes the ligand Delta"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Both cells carry the receptor. What differs is how much Delta each receives: the cell whose
      Notch is activated more turns on Hes genes, keeps proneural genes low and stays a
      progenitor, while the cell that keeps sending more Delta, because its proneural factor stays
      on, goes on to become a neuron; a brief pulse of Delta is not a commitment. Saying only the
      future neuron makes Notch confuses having the receptor with having it active. Saying only
      the progenitor makes Delta reverses the roles, since in the standard model Delta is higher in
      the cell that differentiates. Equal Notch activity throughout would leave no difference to amplify, and
      the cells would not diverge.
  - stem: "In fly embryos lacking Notch, achaete clusters appear in their normal places, but high achaete expression is not restricted to one cell of each cluster. What does this show?"
    choices:
      - "Notch is needed for achaete to be expressed at all"
      - "Cross-activation among proneural genes places them"
      - "Notch narrows each cluster but does not place it"
      - "Notch places each cluster but does not narrow it"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The clusters are still positioned normally without Notch, so Notch is not what places
      them; it is needed to restrict high achaete expression to fewer cells. That separates
      competence, set by regulatory DNA around the gene, from selection, which depends on Notch.
      Saying Notch places clusters reverses the finding. Saying achaete needs Notch to be
      expressed is contradicted by the persistent expression. Cross-activation among proneural
      genes is a reasonable guess, but this experiment tests Notch, and other work showed that
      the cluster map comes from regulatory DNA.
  - stem: "Mouse embryos lack both Hes1 and Hes5 in the developing neural tube. What is the most likely result?"
    choices:
      - "Progenitors turn into glia in place of neurons"
      - "Neurons appear early and the progenitor pool runs down"
      - "Progenitors divide longer and neurons appear late"
      - "No change occurs, since the Notch receptor is still there"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Hes1 and Hes5 are the Notch targets that repress proneural genes. Without them proneural
      factors rise, cells differentiate as neurons too early and the progenitor pool is used up,
      as seen in Hes1 mutants and more severely when Hes5 is also removed. Holding cells back
      from becoming neurons is the effect of too much Hes, not too little. A switch to glia is wrong
      because during neurogenesis Hes activity holds cells back as progenitors, so losing it pushes
      cells toward neurons, not away from them; Hes activity can also favour glial fates, but later
      or in particular lineages. Having the receptor is not enough if its targets are missing, so the
      claim of no change fails.
  - stem: "In a model of two touching cells that start almost equal, cell A is given 1 percent more Delta than cell B. After one trip round the loop, the difference comes back as 0.5 percent. What happens to the pair?"
    choices:
      - "The difference grows and one cell becomes neural"
      - "Both cells become neural because inhibition is lost"
      - "The difference fades and both cells stay alike"
      - "The cells split, but only after a long delay"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      A round-trip factor below 1 shrinks the difference on every pass, so the even state is stable,
      like a marble in a bowl, and both cells stay alike. Growth with one neural cell would need the
      difference to come back larger than the 1 percent that left. Both cells becoming neural is
      what losing Notch does; here every part of the loop is present, and the loop is only shallow. A
      split after a long delay is what a small start on a steep loop gives; on a shallow loop the
      difference never grows.

  - stem: "Suppose researchers make fly mosaics in which cells with one working copy of Notch lie beside cells with two copies, and border cells of the two kinds are equally common. Of 120 bristle precursors that form exactly at patch borders, 90 come from one-copy cells and 30 from two-copy cells. What fraction come from one-copy cells, and what does it suggest?"
    choices:
      - "50 percent; the dose of Notch has no effect on which cell wins"
      - "25 percent; less Notch than a neighbour favors the epidermal fate"
      - "75 percent; less Notch than a neighbour favors the precursor fate"
      - "75 percent; less Notch than a neighbour favors the epidermal fate"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Ninety of 120 is 75 percent, three times the 30 from two-copy cells, although the two kinds
      of border cell are equally common, so a cell with less Notch than its neighbour is more
      likely to become the precursor. The 25 percent answer divides the wrong group by the total.
      Reading 75 percent as favoring the epidermal fate inverts the meaning, since these counts
      are precursors, not epidermal cells. Fifty percent would be expected only if dose did not
      matter, and the counts are far from even.
  - stem: "In cultured cells given fresh serum, Hes1 messenger RNA peaks about every two hours, and both the messenger RNA and the protein have half-lives near 20 minutes. When protein synthesis is blocked with cycloheximide, Hes1 messenger RNA rises and stays high instead of cycling. Which explanation fits these data best?"
    choices:
      - "Serum keeps the Hes1 gene silent between peaks"
      - "Hes1 protein represses transcription of its own gene"
      - "Hes1 protein stabilizes its own messenger RNA"
      - "Hes1 messenger RNA is translated only every other cycle"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Blocking protein synthesis removes Hes1 protein, and the messenger RNA then stays high, so
      the protein normally turns its own gene down. Short half-lives let the protein vanish and the
      gene restart, and the delay in making new protein keeps the loop cycling. If the protein stabilized its own messenger RNA, losing
      the protein would make the RNA fall, not rise. Serum silencing the gene between peaks would
      not explain why blocking protein synthesis keeps the RNA high. Translation in alternate
      cycles says nothing about why the RNA itself stops cycling.
  - stem: "In mice whose Ascl1 coding region is replaced by Neurog2, the ventral forebrain recovers neurogenesis and shows a normal pattern of the ventral markers Dlx1 and GAD67, unlike Ascl1 knockouts. The dorsal markers Tbr1 and NeuroD are not switched on in these ventral cells. Which conclusion is best supported?"
    choices:
      - "Ascl1 and Neurog2 carry the same information in every tissue"
      - "Ventral identity requires Ascl1 protein in particular"
      - "Neurog2 imposes dorsal identity wherever it is expressed"
      - "Neurog2 can drive neurogenesis here while the region sets identity"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Neurog2 rescued neuron production, and the cells kept ventral markers without gaining dorsal
      ones, so in this tissue the factor supplied neurogenesis and the local setting supplied
      identity. Identical information everywhere is too broad, since the reverse swap in the
      dorsal forebrain gave a different result. Dorsal identity imposed by Neurog2 is contradicted
      by the absence of Tbr1 and NeuroD. A requirement for Ascl1 protein in particular is
      contradicted by the ventral markers appearing with Neurog2 in its place.

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm five"
    text: >-
      Imagine a small group of children, and only one can be the captain. Each child whispers
      "not you" to the children beside them. The child who whispers a little louder makes the
      others quieter, so they whisper less, and soon one child is loud and the rest are quiet. The
      loud one becomes the captain, which in the body means a nerve cell.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      In a developing fly, small groups of skin cells switch on proneural genes, which make every
      cell in the group able to become a nerve cell. Then the cells signal to each other through a
      receptor called Notch. A cell that sends a bit more of the signal, a protein called Delta,
      pushes its neighbours away from the nerve fate, and as they back off they send less signal
      back. A tiny starting difference grows until one cell becomes the nerve precursor and the
      rest become skin. If Notch is missing, nothing holds the neighbours back, and the embryo
      makes far too many nerve cells.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      Proneural genes (achaete, scute and atonal in Drosophila; Ascl1, Neurog1/2 and Atoh1 in
      vertebrates) encode bHLH transcription factors that heterodimerize with E proteins, bind
      E-boxes. In the fly they confer neural competence on a proneural cluster; in vertebrates they act
      in cells that are already neural progenitors and drive neuronal differentiation. Within the cluster, proneural
      factors raise Delta expression and their own expression. Delta activates Notch in
      neighbouring cells; the released intracellular domain, with CSL and Mastermind, induces
      E(spl) or Hes bHLH repressors, which repress proneural genes and so lower Delta in the
      receiving cell. The resulting loop is a double negative between cells, which acts as
      positive feedback and, when the feedback is steep enough, makes the pair bistable. Loss of Notch or Delta gives the fly's
      neurogenic phenotype; loss of Hes1 in mouse causes premature neurogenesis.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      Lateral inhibition with feedback produces a fine-grained pattern only if the response of
      Delta to Notch activity is steep enough; in Collier and colleagues' 1996 model, a shallow
      response leaves the homogeneous state stable. In the fly embryo, competence
      (cluster placement by cis-regulatory modules of achaete-scute) and selection (Notch-dependent
      restriction) are separable, and Ruiz-Gómez and Ghysen showed clusters placed normally in
      Notch-null embryos. On the notum the steps overlap: Notch signaling also helps shape the
      proneural stripes themselves. Vertebrate neural progenitors add time. Hes1 represses its own
      promoter; short messenger RNA and protein half-lives (near 20 minutes in cultured cells) and
      the delay of transcription and translation make this negative loop oscillate, with a period of
      about two to three hours in mouse progenitors, driving
      out-of-phase oscillations of Neurog2, Ascl1 and Dll1. Sustained proneural expression
      accompanies differentiation, while oscillation accompanies maintenance.


      An open question is how much of fate information sits in the proneural protein and how much
      in the cell receiving it. The Ascl1 and Neurog2 swaps were asymmetric, and comparisons in
      programmed stem cells show the two factors open different chromatin, which raises the
      question of how much of the difference in vivo comes from binding partners, chromatin state
      and the duration of expression.

anecdotes:
  - title: "Competent, then determined"
    source: "Ruiz-Gómez & Ghysen, EMBO J. 12:1121 (1993)."
    text: >-
      Mar Ruiz-Gómez and Alain Ghysen followed the proneural gene achaete in staged fly embryos.
      It came on first in clusters of a few ectodermal cells. Later one
      cell in each cluster held more transcript and became the neural progenitor.
      They called the first state competent and the second determined. Notch was needed to narrow
      each cluster, not to place it. Where the clusters form is set by regulatory sites spread around
      the achaete-scute genes, a map worked out mostly on the notum and wing by Modolell and
      colleagues.
  - title: "A two-hour clock in a dish"
    source: "Hirata et al., Science 298:840 (2002)."
    text: >-
      Notch target genes were already known to cycle during somite formation. When Hiromi Hirata and colleagues in Ryoichiro
      Kageyama's laboratory gave cultured cells fresh serum and sampled them over the next hours,
      Hes1 messenger RNA and protein rose and fell with a period of about two hours, in several
      cell types. Hes1 protein represses its own gene, and both the
      messenger RNA and the protein have half-lives of only about 20 minutes. Models show that the
      delay in making the protein, from transcription to translation, is also needed for the cycle. Blocking protein
      synthesis stopped the cycle with the messenger RNA high. Six years later the same laboratory
      saw Hes1 oscillate in neural progenitors.
  - title: "One gene, two sensory beginnings"
    source: "Jarman, Grell, Ackerman, Jan & Jan, Nature 369:398 (1994); Jarman, Sun, Jan & Jan, Development 121:2019 (1995)."
    text: >-
      The proneural gene atonal is needed for the fly's chordotonal organs, internal sensors of
      stretch and vibration. It is also needed in the eye. There Atonal appears first in a stripe,
      then in small groups, and finally in single R8 cells, the founder photoreceptor of each eye
      unit. Without atonal no R8 forms, and the other seven photoreceptors, which R8 normally
      recruits, do not form either. One proneural factor starts two very different structures,
      so what a precursor becomes depends on the tissue around it as well.

**In the fly embryo, the cells that will make the nervous system sit in small groups. Every cell in a group can become a neural precursor, yet usually only one does.**

If the cells are equivalent at the start, something must break the tie. This lesson asks what does it, what it takes to work, and what changes when the same parts are used in the vertebrate neural tube.

## The layout

The arguments in this lesson happen on three maps, which the film at the top of the page draws. Keep track of which map each experiment uses.

- **The fly embryo's ectoderm.** On the underside lies the ventral neuroectoderm, a sheet of ectoderm that can make nerve cells or skin. Proneural genes come on in small clusters of a few cells. From each cluster one cell moves inward as a **neuroblast**, a neural stem cell of the fly embryo. The rest stay at the surface and become **epidermis**, the skin of the underside. Elsewhere in the ectoderm, clusters give the precursors of the larva's sense organs in the same way.
- **The fly notum**, the back of the fly's thorax, where bristles grow. Here each small proneural cluster that makes a large bristle gives one **sensory organ precursor (SOP)**, the cell that will build one bristle and its neuron. The rest of the cluster becomes epidermis. Small bristles come instead from broader proneural stripes, each giving many SOPs.
- **The vertebrate neural tube.** Here the cells are already neural: progenitors, dividing cells that make neurons.

The maps raise three puzzles.

1. **Who may?** Every cell in a cluster could become the precursor. What makes them able, and what decides where the clusters form?
2. **Who does?** If the cells start equal, what breaks the tie, and is the tie always broken?
3. **Does it carry over?** In the neural tube, where cells are already neural, what do the same parts do, and does the proneural factor also decide what kind of neuron forms?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** In the fly, proneural factors say which cells *may* become neural; Notch says which one *does*, by comparing each cell with its neighbours. Between two touching cells that start alike, the comparison breaks the tie only if the loop is steep: a small difference must come back round the loop larger than it left.

## Step 1: a cluster becomes competent

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The proneural genes were found in the fly. *achaete* and *scute*, neighbours in a stretch of chromosome called the achaete-scute complex, are needed for bristles: remove them and bristles are missing; switch them on in extra places and extra bristles form. *atonal*, reported in 1993 by Andrew Jarman, Yves Grau, Lily Jan and Yuh Nung Jan, does the same job for chordotonal organs, internal stretch receptors. In 1994 Jarman, Ellsworth Grell, Larry Ackerman and the Jans showed it is needed for R8, the founding photoreceptor of each unit of the eye.

All of these genes encode basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factors. The helix-loop-helix part lets two proteins pair; the basic part grips DNA at a short sequence called an E-box. A proneural protein works as a pair with a partner, Daughterless in the fly and E proteins in vertebrates. Vertebrates have relatives of both fly families: Ascl1 (once called Mash1) is related to achaete-scute, and the Neurogenins and Atoh1 to atonal.

A proneural gene first comes on in a cluster of cells. Think of it as a casting call: everyone in the cluster gets to audition. In 1993 Mar Ruiz-Gómez and Alain Ghysen followed *achaete* in the ectoderm of staged fly embryos. It came on in clusters of a few cells; later one cell in each cluster held much more *achaete* RNA and became the neural progenitor. They called the first state **competent** and the second **determined**.

Is Notch part of placing the cluster, or only of picking within it? Ruiz-Gómez and Ghysen looked at embryos lacking Notch.

**Predict.** In an embryo with no Notch, what happens to the *achaete* clusters? Do they form in the wrong places, fail to form, or something else?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The clusters still formed in their normal places, but high *achaete* expression was no longer restricted to one cell in each cluster. So Notch does not place the clusters; it narrows them. Placing the cluster and picking within it are separate events, and where the clusters form is set by regulatory DNA around the achaete-scute genes.

</details>

That answers the first puzzle.

## Step 2: one cell is picked

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

What picks one cell from the cluster? The cells signal to each other through Notch, and the signal says "not you".

You met Notch in [the Notch signaling lesson](/lessons/notch-signaling/). Delta, a membrane protein on one cell, binds Notch on the cell it touches and pulls. Two cuts then free the inner part of Notch, which enters the nucleus and, with the DNA-binding protein CSL (called Suppressor of Hairless in the fly), turns on target genes.

In a proneural cluster the targets that matter are the **Enhancer of split**, or E(spl), genes. They encode bHLH repressors that switch proneural genes off. Proneural factors do two things in return: they raise their own expression (self-activation), and, in the standard model, they raise Delta.

Now follow two neighbours, A and B, one hand-off at a time.

1. **A to B.** A has a little more Delta. B's Notch is pulled harder, so B makes more E(spl).
2. **Inside B.** E(spl) lowers B's proneural factor, so B makes less Delta.
3. **B to A.** A now receives less Delta. A's Notch is less active, so A makes less E(spl), keeps more proneural factor and makes more Delta.

The signal that returns to A pushes A's Delta up again, in the same direction as its original lead. Each cell inhibits its neighbour, so A inhibits the cell that inhibits A. Two inhibitions in a row act as positive feedback, the switch-making arrangement from [signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/). The signs say which way the lead moves; whether it comes back larger is a separate question.

**Predict.** Two neighbouring cells in a fly proneural cluster start with equal Delta, and both express the proneural genes. What happens in each case?

## When does the tie break? The loop must be steep

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

The first row of that table hides a condition. Positive feedback is not, by itself, enough to split two equal cells. What matters is how steep each response is. A response is steep when a small change in input gives a large change in output: how sharply a cell's Notch activity rises with its neighbour's Delta, and how sharply its own Delta falls as its Notch activity rises.

**Predict.** Give cell A 1 percent more Delta than cell B, and let the signal go once round the loop. Does the difference always come back larger?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Each step passes the change on, multiplied by a factor set by its steepness, so multiply the factors round the loop. In the worked example of Collier and colleagues' 1996 model of lateral inhibition, a 1 percent rise in A's Delta raises B's Notch activity by about 1.3 percent. Each 1 percent rise in Notch activity lowers that cell's Delta by about 1.85 percent, so B's Delta falls by about 2.4 percent. The same two steps back to A multiply by about 2.4 again (2.39 squared is 5.73), so the 1 percent comes back as about 5.7 percent. The product for the whole trip, here about 5.7, is the **round-trip factor**. Above 1, the difference grows and the pair splits.

With shallow responses in the same model, the 1 percent comes back as about 0.14 percent. The difference fades, and the two cells stay alike however long you wait. These are illustrative numbers for a small change near the even state, not measurements and not a step in time.

</details>

That is the test in the guiding idea. On a steep loop, a perfectly even pair is like a pencil balanced on its point. It could in principle stay in the middle, but the slightest tip grows, and there are two stable outcomes, A neural and B not, or the reverse. A system with two stable outcomes and an unstable even state between them is **bistable**. On a shallow loop the pair is a marble in a bowl: push it and it rolls back to the middle.

Two more things follow from the same model.

- **On a steep loop, the size of the starting difference matters only a little.** In the worked example a start ten times smaller delays the split by about a third. So ordinary noise can start the split, though the wait grows long as the loop nears its threshold, and a tissue has only so much time.
- **Geometry matters.** A cell in a sheet hears the average of its six neighbours, so one neighbour's change is diluted. In the model a pair splits when the round-trip factor is above 1, but an ideal sheet of hexagonal cells needs it above 4 (twice the gain at each hand-off). When such a sheet splits from a nearly even start, about one cell in three typically wins; other starts, edges and settings change the count.

So on an extended sheet the loop spaces winners apart; it does not by itself guarantee one winner per cluster. Whether a cluster gives exactly one depends on its size and shape: a small enough cluster can give one winner by itself, as the pair does. Real tissues add help: a head start, such as the uneven proneural levels on the notum, a longer reach, or timing.

The grid below is a simpler sketch of the same loop on a sheet of touching cells. At its starting setting nothing happens. Turn the feedback strength up to 4 or more and a spaced pattern appears, with roughly a third of the cells, or a little more, picked. The grid has its own settings, so its thresholds are not the model's.

## The evidence: neighbours compare

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

Two kinds of evidence support this picture. First, the phenotype, on the embryo map. Fly embryos lacking Notch or Delta make far too many neuroblasts and too little epidermis on their underside. Such genes are called **neurogenic**. Without the receptor, no cell can be told "not you".

Second, the comparison between neighbours, on the notum. In 1991 Pascal Heitzler and Pat Simpson made fly mosaics in which patches of mutant cells, or cells with a different level of Notch activity, lay beside normal cells. Then they asked which cells at the patch borders became precursors. Cells mutant for *Notch* became neural on their own. Cells mutant for *Delta* did not: they still have Notch, so their normal neighbours' Delta can still tell them "not you". Losing Delta removes only what a cell sends. A normal cell beside cells with less Notch activity than its own became epidermis, and beside cells with more Notch activity it became a neural precursor. In these mosaics, what decided a cell's fate was how its signaling compared with that of the cells beside it.

That answers the second puzzle.

## Vertebrates: a brake and a clock

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

In vertebrates the neural tube is already neural. Proneural factors here do not make cells able to become neural, as in the fly ectoderm. They push a progenitor to become a neuron, and Notch holds its neighbours back as progenitors.

The parts are the same. The vertebrate relatives of E(spl) are the **Hes** genes, and Hes inhibits proneural genes. Progenitors express Notch, its ligand Dll1 (a vertebrate Delta), the repressors Hes1 and Hes5, and proneural factors such as Ascl1 and Neurog2. A cell that starts to differentiate raises Dll1 and activates Notch in the progenitors around it, which keeps them as progenitors. The neural tube does not settle into the fly's fixed pattern. Neurons first appear spaced apart, new ones arise in the gaps, and cells leave the progenitor pool over days. Lateral inhibition here works as a brake on how fast progenitors are used up. Why a brake here and a fixed switch in the fly? One difference is that a cell held back here stays a dividing progenitor and can compete again later, while most of the fly's held-back cells become epidermis.

This answers a question students often bring: is Notch in the stem cell or the non-stem cell? Both cells have the receptor. The cell whose Notch is more strongly activated stays a progenitor; the cell that keeps sending more Delta, because its proneural factor stays on, goes on to differentiate. Progenitors also send Delta in pulses as they oscillate (below), so a high level at one moment is not a commitment. How a dividing progenitor can give its two daughters different amounts of Notch activity, through proteins such as Numb and other mechanisms, is the subject of [asymmetric division](/lessons/asymmetric-division/). The cells themselves are described in [radial glia as stem cells](/lessons/radial-glia-progenitors/), and the later role of Notch in making glia in [where glia come from](/lessons/origins-of-glia/).

The mouse genetics fits the brake. In 1995 Ryoichiro Kageyama's laboratory, with François Guillemot and others, reported mice lacking Hes1. Their neural tubes expressed more Ascl1, made neurons too early, and had severe neural tube defects. Later mutants lacking Hes5 as well lost progenitors still faster.

Then came a surprise about timing. In 2002 Hiromi Hirata and colleagues found that in several kinds of cultured cells Hes1 rises and falls with a period of about two hours. This happens because Hes1 protein represses its own gene, its messenger RNA and protein are short-lived, and making new protein takes time. In 2008 Hiromi Shimojo, Toshiyuki Ohtsuka and Kageyama imaged reporters in mouse neural progenitors. Hes1 oscillates there too, with a period of about two to three hours, and Neurog2 and Dll1 oscillate in the opposite phase. In cells becoming neurons, Hes1 falls and Neurog2 stays high.

So a progenitor can carry a proneural factor that comes and goes, or one that stays on. Does the difference matter?

**Predict.** Use light to switch an Ascl1 gene on and off in cultured mouse neural progenitors. In one set of cells, hold Ascl1 steady. In the other, make it oscillate. Which cells keep dividing as progenitors, and which become neurons?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In 2013 Imayoshi and colleagues in Kageyama's laboratory did this with optogenetics in culture. Steady Ascl1 pushed cells toward becoming neurons. Oscillating Ascl1 kept them dividing as progenitors. The same factor gave opposite results depending on its timing.

</details>

One reading is that oscillating Dll1 lets progenitors keep signaling to each other without any of them committing, and a steady high level of a proneural factor is what pushes a cell to differentiate.

## Do proneural genes carry identity? The Ascl1 and Neurog2 swap

Proneural factors start neurogenesis. Do they also say what kind of neuron to make? The mouse forebrain offered a test. Neurog2 is high in dorsal progenitors, which make the excitatory neurons of the cortex. Ascl1 is high in ventral progenitors of the ganglionic eminences (swellings of the ventral forebrain), which make inhibitory neurons, although some Ascl1 is present dorsally too.

A **coding-region swap** puts one factor's protein-coding part at the other's gene, under the other's regulatory DNA. Before the result, set out what each answer would predict.

| If identity sits in... | Ascl1 made in dorsal cells | Neurog2 made in ventral cells |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the factor | dorsal cells turn ventral | ventral cells turn dorsal |
| the territory | dorsal cells stay dorsal | ventral cells stay ventral |

**Predict.** Which row did the mouse follow?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Neither row, fully. In 2000 Carol Fode, Guillemot and colleagues found that removing Neurog2 let Ascl1 rise in the dorsal forebrain, and some dorsal cells then switched on a subset of ventral markers such as Dlx1 and GAD67, a change that required Ascl1. Other ventral markers did not appear. Placing the Ascl1 coding region at the Neurog2 locus induced some ventral markers in dorsal cells as well.

In 2002 Carlos Parras, Carol Schuurmans and colleagues also did the reverse, putting Neurog2 in place of Ascl1. In the ventral forebrain, Neurog2 restored neuron production and the ventral markers, yet did not switch on dorsal markers. Elsewhere it restored neurogenesis in the sympathetic ganglia, but not in the ventral spinal cord or the locus coeruleus.

The two swaps were not mirror images. Ascl1 could push dorsal cells partly ventral; Neurog2 did not push ventral cells dorsal.

</details>

So proneural factors carry partly different identity information, and how much depends on the factor and the tissue. The fly shows both sides. Forcing achaete-scute genes on makes only external sense organs, while forcing *atonal* on mostly makes chordotonal organs, so the factor itself carries identity. Yet *atonal* also starts photoreceptors in the eye, so the tissue matters too. A precursor's identity comes from the proneural factor together with where the cell is and what it has already been told. That answers the third puzzle. When in a lineage those instructions arrive is the subject of [birth order and fate](/lessons/birth-order-and-layers/).

## Putting it together

In the fly, proneural factors say who may and Notch, comparing neighbours on a steep loop, says who does. In the vertebrate neural tube, where cells are already neural, the proneural factor pushes a progenitor toward a neuron and Notch-Hes signalling holds its neighbours back, as a brake that also oscillates.

1. **Who may?** Proneural bHLH factors make a cluster competent. In the embryo, regulatory DNA places the cluster, and Notch-null embryos still form clusters in the right places; on the notum Notch also helps shape the proneural stripes.
2. **Who does?** Delta–Notch–E(spl) feedback between neighbours is a double negative, so it acts as positive feedback, and it splits two equal cells only on a steep loop. In Heitzler and Simpson's mosaics, fate followed relative Notch activity.
3. **Does it carry over?** In the neural tube the same parts brake how fast progenitors become neurons, and they oscillate; steady proneural expression, not only a high level, goes with commitment. The factor carries part of a neuron's identity; the tissue and the cell's history shape the rest.

**What the rule covers, and its limits.** The steepness test applies to touching cells that all express proneural factor and start alike: the fly embryo's clusters and the notum's clusters. In vertebrate progenitors the same parts also form a delayed oscillator, and the lesson does not test a steepness threshold there. On an extended sheet, touching-neighbour inhibition spaces winners rather than guaranteeing one per cluster. A difference imposed from outside, such as uneven proneural levels or a bias inherited through an [asymmetric division](/lessons/asymmetric-division/), can set fates without the tie having to break by itself.

## Going deeper

### The last cut is regulated proteolysis

The second of the two cuts that free the inner part of Notch is made inside the membrane by γ-secretase. It is a case of regulated proteolysis, the theme of the lesson on [ubiquitin and proteolysis](/lessons/ubiquitin-and-proteolysis/). The drug DAPT, one of this lesson's structures, blocks this cut and so switches Notch off.

### Where the cluster map comes from

The map of regulatory sites around the achaete-scute genes that places the clusters was worked out mostly on the notum and wing, by Modolell and colleagues.

### Delta level versus Delta activity

"Proneural factors raise Delta" is the standard model, and the lesson uses it. In the fly embryo, though, Delta RNA is broad across the neuroectoderm, and a good part of the difference between cells may lie in Delta's *activity*. In the embryo the main protein that makes Delta active as a ligand is Neuralized, an enzyme that tags Delta with ubiquitin and helps the cell take it in. A different E3 ligase with a similar job, Mind bomb, matters more in other fly tissues. A careful reader should not take "raise Delta" to mean only more Delta messenger RNA.

### Spacing, and one winner per cluster

The embryo makes one neuroblast per cluster, and SOPs on the notum are spaced several cells apart, more widely than nearest-neighbour inhibition alone would space them. The longer reach proposed is signaling beyond touching neighbours, through Delta-bearing filopodia, thin cell extensions (de Joussineau and colleagues, 2003; Cohen and colleagues, 2010).

### The model behind the numbers

Collier and colleagues wrote the loop for each cell as two equations. Notch activity n moves toward f(d̄), a rising function of the average Delta d̄ of the neighbours, and Delta d moves toward g(n), a falling function of the cell's own Notch activity. The worked example uses f(x) = x^k/(a + x^k) and g(x) = 1/(1 + b x^h), with a = 0.01 and b = 100; the Hill exponents k and h set how switch-like each response is. Near the even state, a small change passes through f and then g. Because g falls, the product of the two slopes is negative; its size is the gain G for one hand-off between cells, and G² is the round-trip factor. The thresholds in the lesson, round-trip factors of 1 for a pair and 4 for an ideal hexagonal sheet, are G = 1 and G = 2. The steep worked example has k = h = 2 (G about 2.39); the shallow one has k = h = 1 (G about 0.38). A steep exponent alone is not enough: with k = h = 2 but the weaker repression constant b = 10, G is about 0.93, just below 1, and the pair stays even.

Timing, with both rates set to 1 so that one time unit is the mean lifetime of Notch activity: start both cells with Notch activity at its even-state value and cell A's Delta 1 percent (or 0.1 percent) above B's; the two Delta levels differ by 0.5 after about 13 (or about 17) time units. A tenfold smaller start adds a fixed time, here about 4 units, and the wait grows without limit as G approaches 1 or the start approaches zero.

Receptor dose: if a cell's Notch activity is scaled by its share of the normal receptor, a cell with half the Notch of its partner wins from an equal start. A pair in which both cells have half the Notch still splits (G about 1.6), but a pair with a tenth in both stays even (G about 0.07), at the same 1:1 ratio. So the relative signal biases which cell wins, and the absolute signal decides whether the pair splits at all. All of these are illustrative, from published model forms.

### Why the Hes1 clock needs a delay

A loop of only two steps, messenger RNA that makes protein and protein that represses the messenger RNA, with each decaying at a steady rate, cannot keep oscillating; it settles to a steady level. Short half-lives, near 20 minutes for both, let Hes1 vanish and the gene restart, but the cycle also needs the delay of transcription and translation, or extra steps in the loop. The models agree with the experiment in which blocking protein synthesis stopped the cycle with the messenger RNA high.

### Open questions on identity

How much of fate information sits in the proneural protein and how much in the cell receiving it? Comparisons in programmed stem cells show the two factors open different chromatin, which raises the question of how much of the difference in vivo comes from binding partners, chromatin state and the duration of expression.

### The name "neurogenic"

Fly geneticists call Notch, Delta and their partners neurogenic genes, because losing them makes too much neural tissue. The name was used by José Campos-Ortega's laboratory in the early 1980s for the set, after Donald Poulson's 1930s descriptions of embryos lacking Notch.
