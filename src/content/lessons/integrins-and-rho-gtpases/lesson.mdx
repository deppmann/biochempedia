techniques:
  - name: "Microinjection with actin staining"
    blurb: >-
      A purified protein is injected into one cultured cell through a fine glass needle. The
      cell is later fixed and stained with fluorescent phalloidin, a mushroom toxin that binds
      actin filaments, so the microscope shows what the injected protein made the actin do.
      Ridley and Hall tested Rho and Rac this way, one protein at a time.
  - name: "Peptide competition"
    blurb: >-
      Short synthetic peptides that copy part of a matrix protein are added to cells in
      solution. If a peptide stops cells from attaching to the whole protein, and a control
      peptide with one residue changed does not, then its sequence is what the receptor
      recognises. This is how the RGD sequence was pinned down.
  - name: "Optical trap (laser tweezers)"
    blurb: >-
      A focused laser beam holds a small bead and pulls it back toward the focus with a force
      the experimenter sets, in the piconewton range. With a matrix-coated bead resting on a
      cell, the trap reports how the cell responds when the thing it is holding resists.
  - name: "Magnetic tweezers with single-molecule counting"
    blurb: >-
      One protein molecule is tethered between a glass surface and a magnetic bead, and a
      magnet pulls on the bead with a chosen force. Fluorescent partner proteins that bind the
      stretched molecule are counted one at a time, because each dye goes dark in a single step.

**Most receptors in this course read a soluble signal that drifts in from elsewhere; an integrin reads something solid—the surface a cell stands on.** The first film traced how this enzyme-free receptor switches grip from inside and builds force-sensing adhesions; here is the molecular evidence behind those steps, and then the actin switches that turn grip into crawling.

## The idea: a receptor for something solid

Most cells sit on or in the **extracellular matrix**, a mesh of protein fibres and sugar chains secreted around cells. Collagen is its rope; **fibronectin** is one of its glues, sticking to collagen with one part and to cells with another.

Which part do cells hold? In 1984 Michael Pierschbacher and Erkki Ruoslahti tested short synthetic fragments of fibronectin's cell-binding region and found that four amino acids, Arg-Gly-Asp-Ser, could stand in for it. The first three—**RGD** in one-letter code—are the handle. Add enough free RGD peptide to a dish and fewer cells hold on to fibronectin, because the peptide fills the site the matrix would have filled.

The receptor that holds that handle is an **integrin**—named in 1986 by Richard Hynes, Clayton Buck and Alan Horwitz for an integral membrane protein tying matrix outside to cytoskeleton inside. An integrin is a heterodimer of two different chains, α and β, each crossing the membrane once. Their outer parts form a head that grips the matrix, while inside each chain has only a short tail (a few dozen amino acids in nearly every case), with no kinase domain and no channel. Linker proteins tie those tails to actin filaments. Different αβ pairs have distinct targets: some recognise RGD, others bind collagen or laminin at non-RGD sites, and some white blood cell integrins grip proteins on other cells.

## A grip that is switched on from inside

A hormone receptor waits for its ligand; an integrin is often bathed in ligand continuously, so the cell controls the receptor instead.

In the integrins studied most closely—those of platelets and white blood cells—the resting α and β tails and transmembrane segments pack together, holding the outer legs folded at a knee so the closed head tips toward the membrane and grips weakly. As the first film animated, **inside-out signalling** throws this switch from the cytoplasm: **talin** binds the β tail and pries the α and β tails and membrane segments apart; the change passes through the membrane, straightening the legs and opening the head into a tight grip. A second β-tail-binding protein, **kindlin**, is needed as well.

**Predict.** A mutation breaks the packing between α and β transmembrane segments so the two tails drift apart without talin. What happens to the outer head and to a platelet carrying this integrin in blood?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Uncoupling the tails should shift the integrin toward its extended, high-affinity state without any inside signal. A platelet carrying it would be expected to bind circulating fibrinogen with no wound to call for it, the first step toward clumping where no plug is needed.

</details>

A normal platelet shows why this switch matters: it carries **αIIbβ3** switched off while blood rich in **fibrinogen** flows past. At a cut vessel, wound signals switch αIIbβ3 on within seconds, and two-ended fibrinogen bridges platelets into a plug. A white blood cell faces a ~1-second version of the same control problem, which you will design at the end of this lesson.

## The focal adhesion: a signalling machine that can feel a pull

Bound integrins cluster laterally and assemble a **focal adhesion**—the cytoplasmic complex linking clustered integrins to actin and driving **outside-in** signalling. Four core proteins gather on the β tails: **talin** joins the β tail to actin; **vinculin** binds both talin and actin to reinforce the join; **paxillin** is an enzyme-free scaffold crowded with docking sites; and **focal adhesion kinase (FAK)** is a tyrosine kinase recruited from the cytoplasm when an adhesion forms.

With no enzyme of its own, an integrin borrows one, much as cytokine receptors borrow JAKs in the JAK and STAT lesson. Resting FAK is folded shut, with its **FERM domain** lying against its kinase domain. At a cluster of integrins it opens and phosphorylates itself on **Tyr397** (how clustering opens it is not fully worked out). Phosphorylated Tyr397 docks the **SH2 domain** of **Src**; docked Src phosphorylates FAK further, and the pair phosphorylates neighbours such as paxillin to add more docking sites. One site on FAK binds **Grb2**, the adaptor leading to Ras (Going deeper shows how paxillin scaffolds the MAP kinase cascade there). Outside adhesions, FAK also has other jobs, some in the nucleus.

**Predict.** Cells kept in suspension are given soluble RGD peptide rather than fibronectin fixed to a dish, so integrins bind ligand one at a time without clustering. Would you expect strong FAK–Src signalling?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. One-to-one binding in solution does not cluster integrin tails, so FAK is not gathered to open, phosphorylate Tyr397 and recruit Src.

</details>

Because its parts sit in series—**matrix ↔ integrin ↔ talin ↔ actin pulled by myosin**—the adhesion also measures force. A soft surface yields under myosin pull; a stiff surface resists and builds tension across talin.

Two experiments from Michael Sheetz's laboratory pinned down how pull becomes binding. In 1997 Daniel Choquet, Dan Felsenfeld and Sheetz held fibronectin-coated beads on fibroblasts with an optical trap (laser tweezers): the harder the trap resisted, the more firmly the cell tied those integrins to actin. In 2009 Armando del Rio and colleagues tested one purified molecule at a time. Talin's rod is a string of helix bundles hiding vinculin-binding helices inside. Tethered between glass and a magnetic bead, an unpulled rod bound at most one fluorescent vinculin head (the part of vinculin that grips talin); pulled with about 12 piconewtons, it bound up to three.

No phosphate was added and no GTP was exchanged: force alone exposed buried sites. In cells, full-length vinculin recruited this way also grips actin, reinforcing pulled adhesions. Sheetz's laboratory later saw single talin molecules stretch and relax under myosin pull in living cells, though whether stretch uncovers sites widely outside talin remains open (Going deeper notes open questions on forces in intact adhesions).

**Predict.** Plate one fibroblast on a soft fibronectin gel and another on stiff fibronectin-coated glass. On which surface does talin stretch more and bind more vinculin—and what happens on stiff glass if myosin II is blocked?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Talin should stretch more and recruit more vinculin on stiff glass, where resistance builds tension across the rod. Blocking myosin II removes internal pull even on stiff glass, leaving talin slack so adhesions fail to reinforce.

</details>

## Three switches for the actin skeleton

The second film animated **Rho**, **Rac** and **Cdc42**—small GTPases of the Rho family that cycle between GTP-bound ("on") and GDP-bound ("off") states via GEFs and GAPs, like Ras. Here are the microinjection experiments that assigned each switch its job, their effectors, and the Cdc42 nuance in more detail.

In 1992 Anne Ridley and Alan Hall injected purified proteins into serum-starved fibroblasts, which have few stress fibres: active Rho filled them with thick actin cables and focal adhesions, and active Rac made their edges ruffle. In 1995 Catherine Nobes and Hall, and separately Louis Lim's laboratory, showed that Cdc42 produces thin spikes.

| Switch | Classic actin structure | Key effector(s) | Additional coupling / nuance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Rho** | **Stress fibres**: contractile actin–myosin II cables ending in focal adhesions | **Rho kinase (ROCK)** raises myosin II activity (formins also build unbranched filaments) | Also turned on by G12/13-coupled GPCRs |
| **Rac** | **Lamellipodia**: broad sheets pushed by a branching actin mesh | **WAVE complex** turns on **Arp2/3**, starting each new filament as a branch | FAK/Src-phosphorylated adhesion proteins recruit Rac GEFs |
| **Cdc42** | **Filopodia**: thin fingers with parallel actin cores | Opens folded **WASP-family** proteins (WASP in blood cells, N-WASP elsewhere) to activate **Arp2/3** | Parallel cores are built mainly by **formins** and **Ena/VASP**; some cells make filopodia without Cdc42 or N-WASP |

Notice the Cdc42 nuance: Arp2/3 nucleates *branched* actin, whereas filopodia have *parallel* cores. Cdc42-GTP frees autoinhibited WASP or N-WASP to activate Arp2/3 locally, while elongating parallel filopodial bundles relies mainly on formins and Ena/VASP proteins—and some cells form filopodia without Cdc42 or N-WASP.

**Predict.** Active Rac-GTP sits at a cell's leading edge, but a mutation deletes Arp2/3. Can Rac still build a branching lamellipodium—and separately, if Rho is locked in its GTP-bound state while a drug blocks myosin II directly, what happens to stress-fibre contraction?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Without Arp2/3, Rac-GTP and WAVE cannot nucleate the branching mesh of a lamellipodium. Likewise, active Rho cannot drive contraction if myosin II itself is blocked downstream of ROCK.

</details>

## How a cell crawls, and what happens when it cannot

Both halves of the lesson's rule now meet: inside-out integrin grip and force-sensing adhesions work with Rho, Rac and Cdc42 to drive crawling. As the second film showed, a fibroblast cycles through **push** (Rac lamellipodium), **grip** (new front adhesions feeding back through FAK/Src to Rac GEFs), **pull** (Rho, ROCK and myosin II hauling the body forward) and **let go** (rear adhesions dissolving so the tail draws in)—refining the "Rac in front, Rho behind" first model with live-cell sensors that also detect Rho at the leading edge.

Human genetics and hospital drugs reveal what happens when blood-cell integrins fail or are blocked:

- **Leukocyte adhesion deficiency type I (LAD-I):** A mutation in **β2** (*ITGB2*), shared by white-cell β2 integrins, keeps white cells from stopping on vessel walls, causing severe bacterial infections from infancy. A bone marrow transplant can cure it.
- **Glanzmann thrombasthenia:** People lacking working platelet **αIIbβ3** cannot bridge platelets with fibrinogen and bleed easily. A drug can copy that state on purpose.
- **Platelet integrin blockers:** **Abciximab**, an antibody fragment that blocked αIIbβ3, is no longer supplied, while **eptifibatide** and **tirofiban** are small molecules that sit in αIIbβ3's ligand pocket. Given by vein in hospital to some patients with a heart attack or a threatened one—most often around the catheter procedure reopening a blocked artery—they keep platelets from bridging. Their main risk is what Glanzmann thrombasthenia predicts: bleeding.

### Design problem

A white blood cell carried in flowing blood must keep its integrins off while flowing, yet stop and crawl out of the vessel at an infection within about a second. Design the control using a signal on the vessel wall and parts from earlier lessons, then predict what a blood count and an infected tissue site look like in a child whose white cells lack this integrin.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

A second is too short to make a new protein, so the integrin must already sit on the surface in its bent form, with the "on" signal restricted to the inflamed wall.

**The address.** Vessel-lining cells near an infection display **selectins**, whose short-lived bonds make passing white cells slow and roll, plus **chemokines**, small signal proteins that stay stuck to the wall.

**The receptor and relay.** A white-cell G-protein-coupled receptor, a fast architecture whose parts are already at the membrane, detects the chemokine; its G protein activates phospholipase C, raising calcium and diacylglycerol. Calcium activates a GEF for the Ras-family GTPase **Rap1** (CalDAG-GEFI in platelets and mouse neutrophils; other blood cells use other GEFs, and details differ between cell types). Rap1-GTP helps bring talin to the integrin β tail.

**The grip.** Talin binds the β tail, the integrin extends and grips **ICAM-1** on the vessel wall, and the cell stops and crawls out between wall cells—all in about a second without making new protein.

**The child.** White cells without this integrin roll but cannot stop. Trapped in circulation, the blood white-cell count (mostly neutrophils) is very high while infected tissue holds very few neutrophils, so you would predict repeated bacterial infections that form little pus, since pus is mostly dead white cells. That is what is seen.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The integrin family in numbers

Humans have 18 α chains and 8 β chains, pairing selectively into 24 known integrins (out of 144 possible combinations); about a third recognise RGD.

### A scaffold for the MAP kinase cascade

From Grb2 and Ras at FAK, the next step follows **"Scaffolds can localize ras-mapk"** (as with Ste5 and KSR). In cells responding to hepatocyte growth factor through c-Met, paxillin holds Raf, MEK and ERK next to FAK at the focal adhesion, running the familiar cascade at one address.

### Open questions on force inside living adhesions

The magnetic-tweezer count on talin rod residues 482 to 889 (up to three vinculin heads at ~12 piconewtons) used purified protein, and atomic force microscopy in the same study saw five unfolding peaks averaging 29 to 51 piconewtons, though the authors argued that a lower force held for longer could be as effective. In intact cells, Margadant and colleagues observed talin stretching in 2011, a vinculin tension sensor reported about 2.5 piconewtons in stable adhesions, and the weakest talin bundle, R3, has been reported to open near 5 piconewtons. Which bundles open in living cells, how often, and whether stretch-uncovered sites are common outside talin remain open.
