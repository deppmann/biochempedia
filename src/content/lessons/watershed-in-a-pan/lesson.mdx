## The big idea

Watch a puddle after a storm and you will see water moving, always downhill. This activity follows that water.

A **watershed** is all the land that drains to the same stream, river or bay. High ground called a **ridge** splits one watershed from the next. Rain on one side of the ridge flows one way, and rain on the other side flows the other way.

As water runs across the land, it picks things up: soil, litter, pet waste and lawn fertilizer. Much of it can end up downstream. More than half of Virginia drains to the Chesapeake Bay. Most of the rest drains south to the sounds of North Carolina or west toward the Gulf of Mexico.

Virginia’s grade 4 standard asks children to model a watershed, name the one they live in, and explain "we all live downstream." Younger children only need the idea that what we put on the land can reach the water.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay nearby the whole time. A child can spray alone once you have shown how, but you handle spills. Spray only at the paper, never at faces, eyes or pets.
- Use washable markers and a spray bottle of plain water. Never use a bottle that once held a cleaner, even after rinsing.
- Paper dots and torn bits can be swallowed or breathed in. Young children choke most easily. Count the dots out and back in when you finish, keep them away from babies, toddlers and anyone under 4, and never let a child put them in their mouth.
- Cocoa powder is toxic to dogs and cats. Keep pets out of the area, wipe up spilled cocoa, and put the cocoa paper in a bin pets cannot open. Cocoa and food colouring are pretend ingredients here, so nothing in this activity is for eating. If a child has a food allergy or asthma, use the food-colouring water instead of cocoa.
- Food colouring stains clothes, wood and skin. Cover the table and wear old clothes.
- Wipe up drips on the floor right away so nobody slips.
- Everyone washes hands with soap and water when you finish.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Crumple.** Crumple a sheet of paper into a tight ball. Open it gently, but don’t flatten it. You want hills and valleys. Set it in the tray.
2. **Find the high ground.** Ask your child to run a finger along the tops of the ridges. Trace each ridge with a brown or black marker. Colour one low corner blue and call it "the bay." Slip a folded towel under the opposite end of the tray so the bay corner sits lowest.
3. **Build a town.** Draw small houses, a farm field and a road with green and the other colour. Put some on hilltops and some in valleys.
4. **Predict.** Point to three spots, one on each side of a ridge and one by a house. Ask, "If rain lands here, where will it go?" Have your child draw a little arrow for each guess.
5. **Make it rain.** Mist the paper from about 30 cm (one foot) above, 10 to 20 squirts. It can take several squirts before anything moves. Watch the marker colour bleed and run down the creases. Most of it should collect in the valleys, and some in the bay. If water pools in more than one low spot, each pool is collecting rain from its own small patch of land, and some of those pools never reach the bay. Check the predictions.
6. **Add pollution.** Sprinkle a pinch of cocoa ("fertilizer" or "soil") near the farm, or drip a little of your food-colouring water there instead. Drop a few paper dots ("litter") on the road. Ask where they will go, then spray again. The cocoa usually streaks downhill. The paper dots may need more water before they move.
7. **Find your watershed.** Look at a map of Virginia’s watersheds together and find your town. Follow the river from your area to where it ends. Ask, "Who lives downstream from us?"
8. **Plan three things.** Talk about what your family can do: pick up pet waste (an adult job, with a bag and soap and water afterwards), keep trash out of storm drains, sweep spilled fertilizer and grass off driveways and back onto the lawn, never into the street (also an adult job, with hands washed afterwards), plant trees or a garden, and save water at home, such as turning off the tap while brushing teeth. Clean water is limited, and pollution leaves less of it fit to use. Have your child pick three and draw them.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder where this drop will go if it lands right on top of a ridge."
- "What do you think will happen to the farm if we spray a lot?"
- "Will the bay get more colour or less if we keep spraying?"
- "What do you think will happen if we put the litter on the hilltop instead?"
- "Where does the rain go after it lands on our roof?"
- "If crabs lived in our bay, what would be best to keep out of the water?"

## Why it works

Water flows downhill because of gravity, so the shape of the land sets its path. The crumpled paper is a small copy of real hills and valleys. The washable ink dissolves in the spray and rides along with it, showing the path the way a dye would.

On real land, rain soaks into soil, especially under plants. Water that runs off the surface is called **runoff**. Runoff carries soil and **nutrients**, such as nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilizer and animal waste. In the Chesapeake Bay, too many nutrients make algae grow too fast. Thick algae block sunlight from underwater grasses. When the algae die and rot, the rotting uses up oxygen and can leave low-oxygen "dead zones." Farms, lawns, wastewater and air pollution all add nutrients. In most places, storm drains carry rain straight to a creek without cleaning it, which is why "we all live downstream."

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Skip the map and the litter. Draw one house and one farm, trace the ridges with your child, and spray. Ask one question each time: "Where did the colour go?" End with one family action your child can do this week, such as putting trash in the bin. Your child can draw the bay before and after the rain.

### Ages 8 to 10
Make two landscapes from the same kind of paper. On one, lay a strip of paper towel ("a strip of plants") along the stream below the farm. Put the same pinch of cocoa in the same spot near each farm, spray both the same number of times, and compare how much cocoa reaches the bay. The two crumples never match exactly, so look for a big difference. Have your child find their watershed on a map, name the river, and write a short note to a neighbour about one thing everyone can do.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If nothing runs, keep spraying. The paper soaks up water first. If everything turns into one big blur, you sprayed too much at once; try a new sheet and spray in short bursts. If the marker does not move at all, it is probably not washable. Thin printer paper works better than card or construction paper.
