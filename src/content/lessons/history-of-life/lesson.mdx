techniques:
  - name: "Zircon uranium-lead geochronology"
    blurb: >-
      Zircon crystals from volcanic ash take up uranium but almost no lead when they form, so nearly all the
      lead they contain accumulates by decay. Measuring parent and daughter isotopes gives the age
      at which the ash crystallized, and ash beds above and below a fossil horizon bracket its age.
  - name: "Lipid biomarker analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Organic molecules are extracted from organically preserved fossils and identified by
      chromatography and mass spectrometry. Steroid skeletons characteristic of particular groups,
      such as the cholesteroids that dominate animal sterols, inform which lineage a puzzling fossil belongs to.
  - name: "Experimental evolution in microcosms"
    blurb: >-
      Replicate populations started from one genotype are grown under controlled conditions that
      differ in a single factor, such as spatial structure. Comparing what evolves across treatments
      tests a proposed cause of diversification, and frozen samples allow evolution to be replayed.
  - name: "Birth-death models fitted to fossil occurrences"
    blurb: >-
      Dated occurrences of fossil species are analysed with a model that includes speciation,
      extinction and the probability of preservation and sampling. Because extinct lineages are
      observed directly, such models can estimate extinction rates that a tree of living species
      alone cannot pin down.

**For most of its history, life on Earth was microbial, and over more than three billion years it remade the planet.** Traces read as microbial life appear in rocks 3.48 billion years old. Oxygen began to build up in the air by about 2.4 billion years ago, in the Great Oxidation Event. Eukaryotes, cells with a nucleus, evolved by 1.9 to 1.4 billion years ago, and a many-celled red alga is in the record by just over a billion years ago. Animals are present by about 558 million years ago and plants on land by about 470 million years ago. Along the way, mass extinctions removed most species in geologically short intervals, and survivors diversified into the space left behind. Transitions, extinctions and radiations are the main line of this lesson. How the dates are measured, and what kind of date each one is, comes after.

**Macroevolution** is evolution above the species level: the origin, diversification and extinction of species and larger groups. It is built from the processes in [speciation](/lessons/speciation/) and [molecular evolution](/lessons/molecular-evolution/), and in this lesson it plays out over hundreds of millions of years. Below, Ma means millions of years ago and Ga billions of years ago.

## Major transitions: how life changed the planet

Each entry gives what appeared, roughly when, and why it mattered.

- **Microbial life, by about 3.48 Ga.** Rocks in the Dresser Formation of the Pilbara, Western Australia, preserve layered mounds and microbial fabrics that Djokic and colleagues (2017) read as traces of life in hot springs on land. Reviews place a microbial biosphere on Earth by at least 3.4 billion years ago, and disputably earlier (Javaux, 2019).
- **Oxygen in the air, onset about 2,460 to 2,426 Ma.** The Great Oxidation Event (GOE) is the first significant buildup of oxygen in the atmosphere. It is not the origin of oxygenic photosynthesis, which the [light reactions](/lessons/light-reactions/) lesson explains; it marks when oxygen accumulated. The change reshaped surface environments: aerobic metabolisms expanded, an ozone layer formed that would have shielded organisms in shallow surface environments from ultraviolet light, and organisms had to contend with the harmful effects of free oxygen (Crockford and colleagues, 2026). Gumsley and colleagues (2017) bracketed the onset with dated volcanic rocks in southern Africa and inferred that oxygen rose and fell rather than climbing once; the exact timing is still debated.
- **Eukaryotes and many-celled algae, by about 1.05 Ga.** Eukaryotes evolved by 1.9 to 1.4 billion years ago. The red alga *Bangiomorpha pubescens*, from Baffin Island, Canada, dated to about 1.05 billion years, is the earliest known example of forms of multicellularity and of eukaryotic photosynthesis that still exist today (Gibson and colleagues, 2018). Its chloroplast began as an engulfed cyanobacterium (see [organelles as environments](/lessons/organelles-as-environments/)).
- **Animals, by about 558 Ma.** Bobrovskiy and colleagues (2018) extracted cholesteroid molecules, the steroid family that dominates animal sterols, from organically preserved *Dickinsonia* from the White Sea region, Russia. The result supported an animal affinity for *Dickinsonia* and its close relatives, though not for every Ediacaran fossil.
- **Plants on land, by about 473 to 471 Ma.** Rubinstein and colleagues (2010) recovered cryptospores, spores attributed to early land plants, from Middle Ordovician rocks in Argentina. Edwards and colleagues (2014) reviewed fossils that preserve such spores and found their source plants simple and millimetres high, though the affinities of cryptospores remain contentious; these plants belong to a pool of diversity from which bryophytes and vascular plants emerged.

**What kind of date each is.** The fossil entries are **minimum ages**. Read each as "by this time, at the latest": the lineage existed by then, and may be older, because older traces could have been destroyed, never formed or not yet found. Holland (2016) stressed that preservation is uneven in space and time, so a gap in the record is not a gap in life. The GOE entry is a different kind of date: it brackets an environmental change from both sides. How these ages are measured comes later in the lesson.

## Mass extinctions and the evidence for their causes

Transitions added new kinds of life. Mass extinctions took most of it away at once and changed the makeup of communities and biomes worldwide. A widely used benchmark (Barnosky and colleagues, 2011) defines a **mass extinction** as the loss of more than three-quarters of species in a geologically short interval, which has happened five times in about the past 540 million years. These five are called the "Big Five": the end of the Ordovician, the Devonian, the Late Permian, the Late Triassic and the end of the Cretaceous. How well the five stand out from background extinction is a matter of dispute, taken up under Going deeper. David Raup and J. John Sepkoski first picked them out in 1982 from a compilation of fossil marine families.

**End-Cretaceous, about 66 Ma: an impact triggered it.** Several independent lines of evidence point to one cause. Luis Alvarez, Walter Alvarez, Frank Asaro and Helen Michel (1980) found iridium, scarce in Earth's crust but more abundant in many meteorites, strongly enriched in deep-sea limestones at the Cretaceous-Tertiary boundary at three widely separated sites, in Italy, Denmark and New Zealand. They proposed, as a hypothesis, that an asteroid struck Earth and that dust darkened the sky and suppressed photosynthesis. That made a testable prediction: a crater of the right age should exist. A buried impact crater, Chicxulub, was found on Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, and argon-argon dating by Renne and colleagues (2013) placed the impact and the extinction within 32,000 years of each other. That figure is synchrony, not how long the dying took. Schulte and colleagues (2010) then drew the evidence together: a single ejecta-rich layer linked to Chicxulub is spread worldwide at the boundary, it matches the onset of the extinctions in time, and the fossil patterns fit modelled darkness and cooling. The extinction also fell within the time of large volcanic eruptions in India, the Deccan flood basalts, and Schulte and colleagues weighed both causes. They concluded that the Chicxulub impact triggered the mass extinction.

**End-Permian, about 252 Ma: the largest loss in the marine record.** Stanley (2016) estimated that about 81% of marine species were lost in the terminal Permian crisis, a figure inferred from data on higher taxa. Burgess, Bowring and Shen (2014) dated zircons from volcanic ash beds at Meishan, China, and estimated that the extinction there lasted 60 ± 48 thousand years. That is one section, so it constrains timing there, not the kill mechanism or worldwide synchrony. What it gives is a clock that any proposed cause has to fit.

One bias blurs every extinction. Because the record is incomplete, a species' last fossil usually lies below where it actually vanished, smearing a sudden extinction backwards. This is the **Signor-Lipps effect**.

## Radiations: ecological opportunity in a flask and in the record

An **adaptive radiation** is the rapid diversification of one lineage into types adapted to different ecological niches. A **niche**, here, is the set of conditions and resources a type uses. A leading candidate cause is ecological opportunity: niches that no one is using. An extinction that removes the incumbents is one way to create it.

In the rock record, placental mammal orders diversified mainly after the end-Cretaceous losses, although mammals were already diverse before it, and most molecular clocks place many splits among placental orders in the Cretaceous. That sequence is consistent with release from competitors but does not test it; a coincident change in climate is one alternative. The fossil record cannot be rerun. Bacteria can.

Paul Rainey and Michael Travisano (1998) grew *Pseudomonas fluorescens* from a single genotype in microcosms of broth. Left unshaken, where conditions differ from the surface down, new heritable types arose beside the ancestral smooth type, including a "wrinkly spreader" that forms a mat at the surface. Shaken to keep the broth uniform, they did not diversify. Spatial structure supplied ecological opportunity, and competition among the niche specialists kept them all present.

Keep the scope: these are heritable morphs of an asexual bacterium, not new species, and nothing here says every fossil radiation had the same cause. The flask shows the logic: unoccupied niches plus heritable variation gave repeatable diversification.

## How the dates are measured

Every date above has the same shape: a measured age, a method, an uncertainty, and a direction in which the truth could lie.

Fossils are rarely dated directly; minerals in the rocks around them are. **Radiometric dating** compares how much of a radioactive parent isotope remains in a mineral with how much of its daughter has built up. The **half-life** is the time for half of the parent atoms in a sample to decay. It is constant for each isotope, so the mineral works like an hourglass: the sand in the bottom bulb tells you the time since it was turned over.

Zircon, common in volcanic ash, takes up uranium as it crystallizes but almost no lead, so nearly all its lead comes from later decay.

**Worked problem.** A hypothetical zircon from an ash bed has stayed closed since it crystallized: no parent or daughter has entered or left, and it started with no radiogenic lead. Treat uranium-235 decaying to lead-207 as one parent and one daughter, with the rounded teaching half-life of 704 million years that the US Geological Survey uses. You measure a daughter-to-parent ratio D/P = 3.

1. *Recover the starting parent.* Every daughter atom was once a parent atom, so P0 = P + D = P + 3P = 4P. The fraction of parent left is P/P0 = 0.25.
2. *Count half-lives.* After n half-lives the fraction left is (1/2)^n. Since 0.25 = (1/2)^2, n = 2.
3. *Convert to years.* Age = 2 x 704 million years = 1,408 million years, or 1.408 billion years.
4. *Check.* After one half-life 50% of the parent remains; after two, 25%. Then 75% is daughter, and 75 / 25 = 3, the measured ratio.
5. *Date the fossils.* The ash lies above a fossil bed, and a second ash below the bed is dated to 1,450 million years. The fossils are younger than 1,450 and older than 1,408 million years. The answer is a bracket, not a point.

> **fraction of parent left = P / (P + D) = (1/2)^n,   age = n x half-life**

Real geochronology uses precisely measured decay constants with stated uncertainties, both uranium chains and corrections for initial lead; this exercise is a model, only as good as its assumptions. The clock must also match the age. Carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5,730 years, so after roughly 50,000 years too little remains to measure. Dinosaur bones are not carbon-dated.

Molecular clocks convert sequence differences among living species into divergence times, with fossils calibrating particular nodes (see [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/)). Parham and colleagues (2012) argued that each calibration needs an explicit justification of the fossil's place on the tree and the age of its rock, because a misplaced or misdated fossil carries its error into the dates.

## What rates the record can and cannot give

A clade's **net diversification rate** is speciation minus extinction:

> **r = λ − μ   (each in events per lineage per million years)**

where λ is the speciation rate and μ the extinction rate. A time tree of living species records when surviving lineages split. Lineages that went extinct leave no tips, and recent lineages have had less time to die out, so a tree of survivors shows a "pull of the present": the number of lineages climbs steeply near today even when rates were constant, as long as extinction is not negligible.

So, unless the rates are constrained in advance, a tree of survivors alone cannot tell rising speciation apart from steady speciation with high extinction. That information has to come from outside the tree.

Fossils supply that outside information. Silvestro and colleagues (2014) built a method, PyRate, that estimates speciation and extinction from fossil occurrences while modelling preservation and sampling explicitly.

### Design problem

Placental mammal orders diversified mainly after the end-Cretaceous extinction. A student concludes that the extinction caused the radiation by removing competitors and opening niches. What else could produce this pattern, and what data would test the student's idea?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Alternatives.** A change in climate at about the same time could have opened niches without any release from competitors. The radiation may also have begun earlier than the fossils show: most molecular clocks place many splits among placental orders in the Cretaceous, and the oldest fossil of each order is only a minimum age. Unlike the flask, this event cannot be rerun with a control.

**Data that would decide.**

1. *Date the pattern.* Fit dated fossil occurrences from before and after the boundary with a model that estimates speciation, extinction and preservation together, such as PyRate (Silvestro and colleagues, 2014). Release from competitors predicts a rise in speciation that starts at the extinction horizon, not before it. A tree of living mammals alone cannot settle this, because it cannot separate speciation from extinction.
2. *Check the niches.* If the new mammal types took on the ecological roles of groups that died out, that supports release from competitors. If the timing of diversification tracks an independently dated climate shift instead, that supports the alternative.
3. *Check the clocks.* Use fossil calibrations with an explicit justification of each fossil's place on the tree and the age of its rock (Parham and colleagues, 2012), and state the uncertainty of each divergence date.
4. *State the fossil biases.* Preservation is uneven, so a gap before the boundary is not proof of absence, and the Signor-Lipps effect makes extinctions look more gradual than they were.

</details>

## Going deeper

The numbers, methods and disputes behind the main line.

### The timeline in detail

Djokic and colleagues (2017) reported stromatolites, microbial palisade fabric and geyserite, a mineral deposit of hot springs, in the Dresser Formation rocks. Older claims need care: Allwood and colleagues (2018) reinterpreted supposed stromatolites in 3.7-billion-year-old rocks at Isua, Greenland, as more plausibly deformation structures.

Gumsley and colleagues (2017) bracketed the onset of the GOE using sequences of rock layers in southern Africa anchored by dated volcanic rocks. Their bracket is about 100 million years earlier than previous estimates.

Gibson and colleagues (2018) used rhenium-osmium dates of sedimentary rocks above and below *Bangiomorpha pubescens* in the Bylot Supergroup of Baffin Island to revise its age to 1.047 billion years (plus 0.013, minus 0.017). Using that age as a calibration, their molecular clocks put the origin of the primary plastid, the chloroplast that began as an engulfed cyanobacterium, at about 1.25 Ga. That figure is a model estimate conditional on the calibration, not an observed event and not a fossil floor.

The 558 Ma age for the *Dickinsonia* fossils comes from earlier dating that Bobrovskiy and colleagues cite; the Ediacara biota as a whole spans about 571 to 541 Ma.

### The Big Five in the original data

Raup and Sepkoski's 1982 analysis of fossil marine families found four extinction events statistically distinct from the background rate: the end of the Ordovician, the Late Permian, the Late Triassic and the end of the Cretaceous. They also named a fifth event, in the Devonian, that stood out from the background but was not statistically significant in those data. Marshall (2023), looking back forty years later, notes that later analyses found the Big Five not statistically distinct from background rates and that there is no general agreement on which intervals count as mass extinctions; the name has stayed in standard use.

### Extinction numbers

Alvarez and colleagues found iridium enriched about 30-fold in Italy, 160-fold in Denmark and 20-fold in New Zealand.

At Meishan, Burgess, Bowring and Shen dated zircons from five volcanic ash beds and placed the extinction between 251.941 ± 0.037 and 251.880 ± 0.031 Ma. Check their duration yourself: 251.941 − 251.880 = 0.061 million years, about 61,000 years, in line with the 60 ± 48 thousand years they estimated.

A figure of 96% "of all life" circulates for the end-Permian. It describes marine species, not all life, and Stanley (2016), inferring species losses from data on higher taxa, estimated about 81% and argued that larger figures merged separate Permian crises.

### More on the flask

Kassen, Llewellyn and Rainey (2004) then varied productivity and disturbance in the same system, and diversity peaked at intermediate levels of both.

### Why a tree of living species cannot settle rates

Louca and Pennell (2020) showed that, under a general birth-death model in which both rates may change through time, infinitely many histories are equally likely to have produced the same tree of living species, and that adding living species does not fix this even with unlimited data. The result concerns that general model: a constrained hypothesis can still be tested, but the constraint must be justified from outside the tree. PyRate lets the speciation and extinction rates vary independently through time; applied to fossil rhinoceroses, it inferred repeated shifts in both rates.

### A second design problem: a tree that rises toward the present

The living species of a clade form a time tree in which the number of lineages rises steeply toward the present. A student concludes that speciation sped up recently. What else could produce this pattern, and what data would decide between the explanations?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Alternative.** Constant speciation with high extinction gives the same shape, because old lineages have had longer to be removed. Under a general birth-death model infinitely many histories, including ones with no speed-up, fit an extant tree equally well (Louca and Pennell, 2020). The tree alone cannot decide.

**Data that would decide.**

1. *Add fossils.* Fit dated fossil occurrences with a model that estimates speciation, extinction and preservation together, such as PyRate (Silvestro and colleagues, 2014). Many extinct lineages and high extinction favour turnover; low extinction with recently rising speciation supports the student.
2. *Or justify a constraint.* Without fossils, test a specific model, such as a speed-up timed to an independently dated event, and say the conclusion depends on that constraint.
3. *State the fossil biases.* Preservation varies with rock type, environment and age, so let it vary through time in the model. First appearances are minimum ages, and the Signor-Lipps effect blurs last appearances.

</details>
