techniques:
  - name: "Radioactive hormone binding"
    blurb: >-
      Make the hormone radioactive without changing its chemistry (Jensen used tritium, a heavy
      form of hydrogen), give it to an animal or a tissue extract, and count where it stays.
      Binding that reaches a ceiling and is displaced by an excess of unlabelled hormone marks a
      limited number of specific sites, which is how a receptor behaves.
  - name: "cDNA cloning and sequence comparison"
    blurb: >-
      Copy a cell's messenger RNAs into DNA, find the copy that encodes your protein with an
      antibody or with a probe designed from a piece of its amino acid sequence, and read it.
      Lining up the sequences of different receptors showed the domains they share and turned up
      relatives with no known hormone.
  - name: "Domain exchange with a reporter gene"
    blurb: >-
      Join the hormone-binding part of one receptor to the DNA-binding part of another and put
      the hybrid into cells that carry a reporter, a gene whose product is easy to measure,
      placed behind a chosen response element. Which hormone turns on which reporter tells you
      which part of the protein does which job.
  - name: "X-ray crystallography of the hormone-binding domain"
    blurb: >-
      Crystallize the domain once with an agonist and once with an antagonist, and solve both
      structures. The difference between them shows what the hormone changes, which here is the
      position of helix 12.

**Inject a rat with radioactive estradiol, and almost every tissue lets the hormone go again—only the uterus and vagina keep it.** The first film introduced nuclear receptors as intracellular gene switches; here is the experiment that first pointed to a binding protein in target cells, and how its modules divide the work.

## Finding the receptor inside the cell

Estradiol, cortisol and testosterone are **steroids**: four fused carbon rings made from cholesterol ([lipid synthesis](/lessons/lipid-synthesis)). Because a steroid is hydrophobic, it crosses the lipid bilayer unaided and needs no cell-surface receptor. The [receptor field guide](/lessons/receptor-field-guide) linked receptor architecture to speed; this is the slow route.

In 1958, at the University of Chicago, Elwood Jensen tracked estradiol labelled with tritium (a radioactive heavy hydrogen). Many biochemists then expected estradiol to act as a catalyst, oxidized and reduced repeatedly to pass hydrogen between coenzymes.

**Predict.** If estradiol acted as a cycling catalyst across cells, would you expect target tissues to retain it chemically intact?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No—a hormone cycling between oxidized and reduced forms would be recovered partly changed, and nothing would need to hold it. Jensen recovered intact estradiol retained specifically by the uterus and vagina, pointing to an intracellular binding protein instead.

</details>

Purification and sequencing (see Going deeper) revealed one chain with two grips: one closes around the hormone, and another grips specific DNA. A **transcription factor** is a protein that binds DNA and controls whether a gene is copied into RNA. Unlike [TGF-β and Smads](/lessons/tgf-beta-and-smads), where the messenger was the transcription factor, here the receptor itself is—no second messenger is made and no kinase cascade is required to reach DNA.

<Support>**The one idea.** A nuclear receptor binds both ligand and DNA to control transcription; ligand shape and each cell's helpers decide whether genes turn on. **Scope:** This rule covers the canonical transcriptional route of steroid, thyroid and lipid-sensing nuclear receptors. It leaves aside rapid non-transcriptional steroid effects and cases where a receptor regulates genes by tethering to another transcription factor (such as NF-κB) without binding DNA itself.</Support>

## One modular plan, forty-eight receptors

The human genome holds 48 nuclear receptor genes, and most share one four-part plan:

- **Variable front end:** differs most between receptors and carries **AF-1**, a switching surface that can work without the hormone-binding domain.
- **DNA-binding domain:** about 70 amino acids folded around two zinc ions, four cysteines per zinc, setting one helix into the major groove to read six bases.
- **Hinge:** a flexible link between domains.
- **Hormone-binding domain:** about twelve helices around a hydrophobic pocket, plus the pairing surface and a second switching surface, **AF-2**.

**Predict.** Join the glucocorticoid receptor's hormone-binding domain to the estrogen receptor's DNA-binding domain, and add cortisol to cells carrying this hybrid. Which genes turn on—glucocorticoid targets, estrogen targets, or neither?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Estrogen targets: the hormone-binding domain chooses the hormone (cortisol) and the DNA-binding domain chooses the gene. In 1987 Stephen Green and Pierre Chambon showed this modularity with the reciprocal hybrid (estrogen hormone-binding domain plus glucocorticoid DNA-binding domain), where estradiol turned on a glucocorticoid-responsive gene.

</details>

Most of the 48 were found by sequence resemblance before anyone knew their **ligand** (the small molecule a receptor binds), and named **orphan receptors**. Several have since been matched to cellular lipids—**PPARs** bind fatty acids, **LXR** binds oxidized cholesterol, and **FXR** binds bile acids—so this family also reports on the cell's own lipids. Others still lack an agreed ligand.

## Two ways to wait—and how DNA spacing helps pick the pair

The first film showed the two waiting modes; here are their key nuances and how DNA geometry selects each receptor pair.

**Waiting off DNA with chaperones.** Glucocorticoid and androgen receptors wait mostly in the cytoplasm, held by **chaperones** (proteins that help other proteins hold their shape) built around **Hsp90**, which keep the pocket ready and keep the receptor off DNA. Hormone binding rearranges the complex; the receptor enters the nucleus, sheds its chaperones, pairs with an identical copy and binds DNA. The **estrogen receptor** uses the same Hsp90 chaperones, yet 1984 experiments (on enucleated cells and with antibody staining) placed most of it inside the nucleus even before hormone arrives. What unites these steroid receptors is waiting *off DNA* with chaperones.

**Waiting on DNA with RXR.** Receptors for thyroid hormone, retinoic acid, vitamin D and the PPARs stay in the nucleus on DNA, paired with **RXR**. Without hormone, thyroid hormone and retinoic acid receptors hold **co-repressors** (proteins that keep a gene off), creating an active "off"—quieter than no receptor at all; hormone swaps them for **co-activators** (proteins that help switch a gene on). This active repression is best documented for those two receptors, so do not assume it for every RXR partner.

Because most receptors act in pairs, their DNA site—a **response element**—has two six-base halves. For RXR pairs, the two halves point the same way, and the gap between them helps decide which pair fits:

| Receptor pair | Half-site layout | Example or spacer (`n` = any base) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steroid homodimers (e.g., estrogen receptor) | Inverted repeat (head-to-head palindrome: reads identically 5′→3′ on opposite strands) | `5′-AGGTCA nnn TGACCT-3′` / `3′-TCCAGT nnn ACTGGA-5′` (3-base gap) |
| PPAR + RXR | Direct repeat (halves point same way) | 1-base gap |
| Vitamin D receptor + RXR | Direct repeat | 3-base gap |
| Thyroid hormone receptor + RXR | Direct repeat | 4-base gap |
| Retinoic acid receptor + RXR | Direct repeat | 2- or 5-base gap |

Co-activators and co-repressors act largely on **chromatin**, DNA wound around **histone** proteins. Tightly packed chromatin resists transcription: co-activators bring enzymes that add acetyl groups to histones (loosening packing so RNA polymerase starts), while co-repressors bring enzymes that remove them.

**Predict.** Two cells receive a high dose of thyroid hormone: Cell A has a normal thyroid hormone receptor, while Cell B carries a mutant receptor that still binds DNA and co-repressors but cannot bind hormone. What happens to the target gene in each cell?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In Cell A the hormone swaps the co-repressor for a co-activator and the gene turns on. Cell B's mutant can never make that swap, so it holds the gene off whatever the hormone level—just as the chicken viral cancer gene **v-erbA** (a damaged thyroid hormone receptor that still binds DNA but no longer binds hormone) holds its target genes off.

</details>

Two scope refinements keep the model exact. First, thyroid hormone, retinoic acid (from vitamin A) and active vitamin D use nuclear receptors, but thyroid hormone carries charges and enters via transporter proteins—a transporter is a door, and the receptor stays inside. Second, some steroid effects appear within seconds to minutes without new RNA and are left aside here.

## What the hormone changes: helix 12 and helper proteins

The second film showed **helix 12**, the last helix of the hormone-binding domain, folding over an **agonist** (a molecule that switches the receptor on) or swinging into the co-activator groove with an **antagonist** (a molecule occupying the pocket without switching it on). Here are the sequence motif and structure behind that switch.

With estradiol in the pocket, closed helix 12 completes the shallow **AF-2** groove on the domain surface, fitting the short **LXXLL** helix (L = leucine, X = any amino acid) of co-activators. The liver converts **tamoxifen** into tighter-binding metabolites such as **4-hydroxytamoxifen**, whose long side chain projects where the lid should close. In a 1998 crystal structure from Geoffrey Greene's and David Agard's laboratories, helix 12 lies directly inside the AF-2 groove, excluding co-activators.

Yet recall our guiding rule—*ligand shape and each cell's helpers decide whether genes turn on.* A tamoxifen-bound receptor still pairs on DNA with **AF-1** intact at its front end.

### Design problem

Tamoxifen blocks estrogen in breast tissue yet imitates it in the uterus and, after menopause, in bone. Knowing that tamoxifen blocks AF-2 while leaving DNA binding and AF-1 intact, propose what differs between tissues and design a cell-line experiment to test it.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Proposal: tissues differ in helper proteins. A tissue rich in a co-activator usable through AF-1 reads tamoxifen as "on"; one with relatively more co-repressor reads it as "off."

Experiment: compare a breast cell line (tamoxifen keeps an estrogen-responsive gene off) with a uterine cell line (tamoxifen turns it on). Measure co-activators and co-repressors, then lower the candidate co-activator in uterine cells and raise it in breast cells. If right, tamoxifen loses activation in uterine cells and gains it in breast cells, while estradiol activates both; a receptor with AF-1 deleted should lose tamoxifen's activation in both.

In 2002 Yongfeng Shang and Myles Brown reported that tamoxifen brought co-activators to a subset of genes in a uterine cell line—requiring high **SRC-1** (steroid receptor co-activator 1, unrelated to the kinase Src)—and co-repressors in breast cells. Other differences are possible too: there are two estrogen receptor genes, and ERK ([Ras and MAPK](/lessons/ras-to-mapk)) can phosphorylate AF-1.

</details>

Such a drug is a **selective estrogen receptor modulator**: neither agonist nor antagonist until you name the tissue and gene. **Raloxifene** is a second example, acting like estrogen in bone without stimulating the uterus, and it is used against osteoporosis.

## Why it takes hours, and where it meets medicine

A signal that works through new RNA and new protein cannot be fast. As the second film showed, the first new messenger RNAs appear within tens of minutes of the hormone, and the change in the cell's proteins builds over hours—unlike adrenaline via cyclic AMP ([cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka)), which acts in seconds. In return, new proteins outlast the hormone, and one receptor resets many genes at once.

Three medical landmarks show the family at work:

**Glucocorticoids.** **Prednisone** and **dexamethasone** (synthetic cortisol relatives) calm inflammation. Stepping beyond our canonical DNA-binding rule, the hormone-bound receptor can tether directly to **NF-κB** ([Toll-like receptors and NF-κB](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb)) at inflammatory genes to blunt it without binding DNA itself, raise the inhibitor **IκB** in some cells, and switch on anti-inflammatory genes of its own. Because nearly every cell expresses this receptor, long treatment also raises blood glucose and thins bone.

**Predict.** If a synthetic glucocorticoid lets the receptor tether to NF-κB without forming DNA-binding dimers, which anti-inflammatory action could survive, and what must still be tested before claiming side-effect-free therapy?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Direct blunting of NF-κB at inflammatory genes could survive because tethering needs no DNA binding by the receptor. Because anti-inflammatory genes switched on directly by DNA-bound receptors (including IκB in some cells) also contribute, you must still test whether inflammation is controlled enough and whether glucose and bone side effects truly disappear.

</details>

**PPAR-γ.** Drives fat-cell formation. **Thiazolidinediones** such as **pioglitazone** are PPAR-γ agonists that raise insulin sensitivity; discovered by their effect in diabetic animals, their target was identified as PPAR-γ in 1995.

**Androgen insensitivity.** The androgen receptor gene sits on the X chromosome. An XY person whose sole copy cannot work has internal testes making testosterone that cells cannot read: the complete form produces female body form with blood testosterone in or above the usual male range—itself a clue in someone with a female body form. The fault is in the receptor, and in this pathway the receptor is nearly the whole pathway.

## Going deeper

### How the receptor was purified and cloned

Following Jensen's 1958 tritium study, David Toft and Jack Gorski (University of Illinois, 1966) extracted an estrogen-binding protein from rat uterus, and Jensen and Geoffrey Greene (1980) made **monoclonal antibodies** (antibodies of a single kind, each binding one spot on its target) against the human receptor. In 1985 Ronald Evans's laboratory published the human glucocorticoid receptor **cDNA** (a DNA copy of a messenger RNA). In March 1986 Pierre Chambon (Strasbourg) and Greene (Chicago) published the human estrogen receptor cDNA within weeks of each other, revealing close homology to the glucocorticoid receptor and to the chicken viral cancer gene **v-erbA**—whose normal cellular counterpart was identified by the end of 1986 as a thyroid hormone receptor.
