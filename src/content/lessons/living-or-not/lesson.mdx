    text: >-
      Researchers studied 15 adults with natural colds caused by rhinovirus. They tested hotel-room
      surfaces the adults had touched for rhinovirus genetic material. In a
      second part of the study, mucus containing the virus was dried onto light switches and telephone
      surfaces. Eighteen hours later, the virus’s genetic material was transferred to fingertips in 10 of 30 tries. A later study that tested for virus still able to infect found far less of it reached fingertips after a day than after an hour. The

    source: "Hershey and Chase, Journal of General Physiology, 1952 (doi 10.1085/jgp.36.1.39)."
    text: >-
      In 1952 Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase published a study of a phage, a virus that infects
      bacteria. They labeled the phage’s DNA and its protein coat in different ways, then let the
      phage infect bacteria. Most of the DNA entered the bacterial cells, while most of the
      protein-coat material stayed on the outside. The result showed that most of the phage DNA entered the cell and most of the coat stayed outside, which pointed to the DNA, not the coat, as what the host cell needs to build new viruses.

## Explain: what counts as alive

<WordBank terms={['virus', 'host cell', 'capsid', 'envelope', 'prokaryote', 'nucleoid', 'ribosome', 'cell wall', 'flagellum', 'heterotroph', 'photoautotroph', 'chemoautotroph']} />

The first film's characteristics of life make a checklist. Living things:

- are made of one or more cells
- take in and use energy (all the chemical reactions that do this are called **metabolism**)
- grow and develop
- respond to their surroundings
- keep their inside conditions steady
- reproduce
- change over many generations (evolve)

As the first film's red blood cell and crystal showed, no single box decides it. The key evidence here is different. A bacterium is a cell with its own ribosomes and metabolism. A virus is not a cell and must use a host cell's.

A **virus** is an infectious agent made of genetic material inside a protein coat. A **host cell** is the living cell a virus infects; the first film showed it supplying ribosomes, energy and building blocks. Picture a factory running someone else's blueprints. The picture has one limit. Depending on the virus, either the host's enzymes or enzymes made from the virus's own genes copy its genetic material.

<Support min={2}>

**The one idea.** Bacteria are living cells; viruses are not cells and are classed nonliving. A virus has no metabolism of its own, and new copies are built only inside a host cell.

**Scope:** this is the classification in Virginia's Biology standards (the SOL). Scientists still discuss definitions of life, and some living bacteria also depend on host cells, so needing a host does not decide the case by itself.

</Support>

Many scientists agree, though viruses carry genes and evolve, and some giant viruses carry more than a thousand genes. Under a definition based on cells and metabolism, they are nonliving.

If a virus can carry that many genes, genes cannot be what separates it from a bacterium. The second film opens both to find what does.

## Explain: parts reveal the difference

The second film opened both; this table lines up each part with its job. Bacteria are **prokaryotes**: one-celled living things with no nucleus and, typically, no membrane-covered organelles. Your cells are **eukaryotes**, which usually have a nucleus; mature red blood cells lose theirs. [One Cell or Many](/lessons/one-cell-or-many/) compares them.

| Part | Typical bacterium | Virus | What the part does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genetic material | DNA in the **nucleoid**, a region with no membrane around it | DNA or RNA, inside the capsid | Carries the instructions |
| Coat | Cell membrane around cytoplasm | **Capsid**, a protein coat | Membrane controls what enters and leaves; capsid protects the genes |
| **Ribosomes** | Yes | No | Build proteins; a virus uses the host cell’s |
| **Cell wall** | Often | No | Helps keep the cell from bursting when water rushes in |
| Outer layer | Some have a capsule | Some have an **envelope**, a fatty layer outside the capsid | Envelope helps some viruses enter cells |
| **Flagellum** (plural flagella) | Sometimes | No | A corkscrew-shaped tail that spins to push the cell through water |

Viruses infect all the main kinds of living thing: bacteria, archaea, plants, animals, fungi and protists. A virus that infects bacteria is called a phage. Each virus can enter only certain cells, because proteins on its surface must fit receptors on the host cell.

## Explain: three bacterial energy patterns

Every living cell needs energy to do work and carbon to build its parts. The table adds an example of each pattern from the second film.

| Group | Energy comes from | Carbon comes from | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Heterotroph** | Carbon compounds it takes in | The same compounds | E. coli in your gut; bacteria that decompose dead matter |
| **Photoautotroph** | Light | Carbon dioxide | Cyanobacteria in ponds and oceans |
| **Chemoautotroph** | Chemicals such as sulfur, iron or nitrogen compounds | Carbon dioxide | Bacteria near deep-sea vents and in soil |

## Elaborate: compare real sizes

A micrometer (μm) is a thousandth of a millimeter, and a nanometer (nm) is a thousandth of a micrometer.

| Object and what was measured | Reported size | Size in nanometers (nm) | Kind of sample |
|---|---|---|---|
| SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19 virus), spike on the surface | about 9 to 12 nm | 9 to 12 | Virus grown from patient samples, electron microscope |
| SARS-CoV-2, particle diameter | about 60 to 140 nm | 60 to 140 | Virus grown from patient samples, electron microscope |
| Influenza virus, diameter of round particles | 80 to 120 nm | 80 to 120 | Range from a review of studies |
| T4 phage head, after drying | about 75 × 60 nm | 75 × 60 | Atomic force microscope |
| T4 phage head, in liquid | about 100 × 85 nm | 100 × 85 | Atomic force microscope |
| T4 phage, whole particle length | about 200 nm | 200 | Earlier electron microscope work |
| Tupanvirus (a giant virus), average length | about 1.2 μm | 1,200 | Electron microscope |
| E. coli bacterium, length × width | about 2 × 0.5 μm | 2,000 × 500 | Reference size for normal cells |
| Human red blood cell, mean diameter | about 7.5 μm | 7,500 | Medical reference for normal cells |

These values come from different studies, not one experiment. The SARS-CoV-2 sample was virus grown from patients admitted to a Wuhan hospital in December 2019. The sources are Zhu and colleagues (2020), a 2006 influenza review by Machała and Brydak, Kuznetsov, Chang and McPherson (2011), Abrahão and colleagues (2018), Maki and colleagues (2000), and a 1990 medical textbook chapter by Lynch. The last column uses 1 μm = 1,000 nm.

Most viruses are about 20 to 300 nm across. Typical bacteria are about 1 to 5 μm (1,000 to 5,000 nm) long, so they are larger. The two groups overlap only at the extremes. A giant virus such as Tupanvirus is longer than the smallest typical bacteria, and a few bacteria are big enough to see without a microscope.

### Design problem

A jar of pond mud with a loose lid slowly grows dark specks of a mineral. Your partner says living bacteria are making the specks. Plan a test of that claim. Name your groups, what you keep the same, what you measure, how many jars you need, and how you stay safe.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Groups:** jars of fresh mud (living microbes present) and jars of the same mud heated in a pressure cooker by your teacher to kill the microbes.
- **Independent variable:** whether living microbes are present.
- **Dependent variable:** the area covered by dark specks, measured from photos each week.
- **Constants:** same amount of mud and water, same temperature, same light, same jar size and same number of weeks.
- **Repeated trials:** at least three jars in each group, so one odd jar does not decide the result.
- **Safety:** keep jars closed with a loose lid so gas can escape, wear gloves, never smell or taste the contents, and let your teacher dispose of them. Never grow microbes taken from people.
- **What would mean what:** specks only in jars with living microbes support the claim. Specks in the heated jars too would point to a chemical reaction that needs no life. Even then, the specks do not prove the bacteria use that chemical for energy. A 2020 study of manganese-forming bacteria answered that by tracing whether carbon from carbon dioxide ended up in new cells.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

Return to the switch. The virus there is not a cell and has no metabolism of its own. Only living host cells, such as the cells lining your nose, can build new copies of it. The course answer: bacteria are living cells; viruses are not cells and are classed nonliving.

**Science practices you used:** testing predictions in a model, converting units and comparing sizes in a data table, making a claim from evidence and naming its limits, and planning a fair test with a control group and repeated trials.
