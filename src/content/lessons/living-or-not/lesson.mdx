## Explain: what counts as alive

<WordBank terms={['virus', 'host cell', 'capsid', 'envelope', 'prokaryote', 'nucleoid', 'ribosome', 'cell wall', 'flagellum', 'heterotroph', 'photoautotroph', 'chemoautotroph']} />

Biologists use a group of characteristics to decide whether something is alive. Living things:

- are made of one or more cells
- take in and use energy (all the chemical reactions that do this are called **metabolism**)
- grow and develop
- respond to their surroundings
- keep their inside conditions steady
- reproduce
- change over many generations (evolve)

No single box decides it. A mature red blood cell cannot divide, but it is still a living cell in a living body. A crystal gets bigger, but it has no cells and no metabolism.

A **virus** is a tiny infectious agent made of genetic material inside a protein coat. It is not a cell. Outside a cell it does not take in food, use energy or grow. A **host cell** is the living cell a virus infects. Inside it, the virus’s genes get the cell to build new virus parts, which come together as new viruses. Picture a factory running someone else’s blueprints. The picture breaks down because nobody in the cell chooses to follow the plan; the cell’s machinery reads whatever genes are present.

Because viruses are not cells and have no metabolism of their own, Virginia’s Biology standards (the SOL) class them as nonliving. Many scientists agree, but the question is still discussed. Viruses carry genes and evolve, and some giant viruses carry more than a thousand genes. Under a definition based on cells and metabolism, they are nonliving.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** a bacterium is a living cell, while a virus is not a cell and can only be copied inside a host cell, so it is classed as nonliving.</Support>

## Explain: a bacterium and a virus, part by part

Bacteria are **prokaryotes**: living things made of one cell with no nucleus and, typically, no membrane-covered organelles. Your own cells are **eukaryotes**, which usually have a nucleus (mature red blood cells lose theirs). [One Cell or Many](/lessons/one-cell-or-many/) compares single cells with the cells of your body.

| Part | Typical bacterium | Virus | What the part does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genetic material | DNA in the **nucleoid**, a region with no membrane around it | DNA or RNA, inside the capsid | Carries the instructions |
| Coat | Cell membrane around cytoplasm | **Capsid**, a protein coat | Membrane controls what enters and leaves; capsid protects the genes |
| **Ribosomes** | Yes | No | Build proteins; a virus uses the host cell’s |
| **Cell wall** | Often | No | Helps keep the cell from bursting when water rushes in |
| Outer layer | Some have a capsule | Some have an **envelope**, a fatty layer outside the capsid | Envelope helps some viruses enter cells |
| **Flagellum** (plural flagella) | Sometimes | No | A whip-like tail that pushes the cell through water |

Sizes differ too. A micrometer (μm) is a thousandth of a millimeter, and a nanometer (nm) is a thousandth of a micrometer. Most viruses are about 20 to 300 nm across. Typical bacteria are about 1 to 5 μm long, roughly ten to a hundred times longer. There are exceptions on both sides, such as giant viruses and a few bacteria big enough to see without a microscope.

Viruses infect all the main kinds of living thing: bacteria, archaea, plants, animals, fungi and protists. A virus that infects bacteria is called a phage. Each virus can enter only certain cells, because proteins on its surface must fit receptors on the host cell.

## Explain: how bacteria get their energy

Every living cell needs energy and carbon to build its parts. Biology sorts bacteria into three main groups by how they get them.

| Group | Energy comes from | Carbon comes from | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Heterotroph** | Carbon compounds it takes in | The same compounds | E. coli in your gut; bacteria that decompose dead matter |
| **Photoautotroph** | Light | Carbon dioxide | Cyanobacteria in ponds and oceans |
| **Chemoautotroph** | Chemicals such as sulfur, iron or nitrogen compounds | Carbon dioxide | Bacteria near deep-sea vents and in soil |

A few bacteria mix these patterns, so the three groups are a useful simplification.

Most bacteria carry out every life process themselves when conditions suit them. Conditions matter: fully dried bacteria do not grow. A few bacteria, such as most of the Rickettsia that cause diseases like Rocky Mountain spotted fever, can grow only inside other cells. They are still living cells, with their own ribosomes and metabolism. [How Microbes Multiply](/lessons/how-microbes-multiply/) shows how bacteria divide.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** bacteria get energy from carbon compounds (heterotrophs), from light (photoautotrophs) or from chemicals (chemoautotrophs).</Support>

### Design problem

A sealed jar of pond mud slowly grows dark specks of a mineral. Your partner says living bacteria are making the specks. Plan a test of that claim. Name your groups, what you keep the same, what you measure, how many jars you need, and how you stay safe.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Groups:** jars of fresh mud (living microbes present) and jars of the same mud heated in a pressure cooker by your teacher to kill the microbes. A third group has only the water drained from the same mud, with no mud added at all.
- **Independent variable:** whether living microbes are present.
- **Dependent variable:** the area covered by dark specks, measured from photos each week.
- **Constants:** same amount of mud and water, same temperature, same light, same jar size and same number of weeks.
- **Repeated trials:** at least three jars in each group, so one odd jar does not decide the result.
- **Safety:** keep jars closed with a loose lid so gas can escape, wear gloves, never smell or taste the contents, and let your teacher dispose of them. Never grow microbes taken from people.
- **What would mean what:** specks only in jars with living microbes support the claim. Specks in the heated jars too would point to a chemical reaction that needs no life. Even then, the specks do not prove the bacteria use that chemical for energy. A 2020 study of manganese-forming bacteria answered that by tracing whether carbon from carbon dioxide ended up in new cells.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The cold virus on the light switch is not alive by the usual definition. It is not a cell, it does nothing on its own, and only living host cells, such as the cells lining your nose, can build new copies of it. The bacterium is a living cell that grows and divides by itself when conditions allow. Scientists still discuss where viruses fit, but the SOL answer is clear: bacteria are living, viruses are not.

**Science practices you used:** testing predictions in a model, converting units and comparing sizes in a data table, making a claim from evidence and naming its limits, and planning a fair test with a control group and repeated trials.
