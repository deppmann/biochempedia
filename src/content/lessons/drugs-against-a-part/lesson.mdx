techniques:
  - name: "Order of potency on isolated tissue"
    blurb: >-
      You apply a series of related compounds to a tissue or an isolated beating heart and record
      how strongly each one acts. If the order of potency differs between tissues, that points to different receptors, once differences in tissue response and drug handling have been allowed for. Ahlquist defined alpha and beta receptors this way, and Black's
      group tested their blockers on isolated hearts before animals and people.
  - name: "Kinase selectivity panel"
    blurb: >-
      You test one compound against many kinases, as purified enzymes or in cells that depend on
      different kinases, and compare the concentration needed to inhibit each. The result is a
      list of what the compound hits. Buchdunger's panel showed that the compound that became
      imatinib hit a short list.
  - name: "Sequencing the drug target at relapse"
    blurb: >-
      You read the gene for the drug's target from a patient's cells before treatment and again
      at relapse. A change that appears only at relapse, at a residue that touches the drug, is strong evidence that the drug was acting through that target, and rebuilding the mutant enzyme and testing it against the drug settles it. Gorre and Sawyers found T315I in BCR-ABL this
      way.
  - name: "Disulphide tethering read by mass spectrometry"
    blurb: >-
      You mix a protein that has a cysteine with a library of small fragments that each carry a
      disulphide, under conditions where disulphides can exchange. A fragment with some affinity
      for the surface near the cysteine stays attached, and a mass spectrometer detects the added
      mass. Shokat's group used 480 fragments to find binders beside cysteine 12 of K-Ras G12C.

  - stem: "Propranolol and adrenaline both bind the beta receptor. What makes propranolol an antagonist?"
    choices:
      - "It binds the receptor so weakly that the site stays empty most of the time"
      - "It breaks down adrenaline in the blood before the hormone can bind"
      - "It occupies the hormone's site and does not switch the receptor on"
      - "It bonds to the receptor for good and removes it from the cell surface"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      An antagonist has affinity without efficacy: it fits the site, so adrenaline cannot get in,
      and it does not cause the shape change that passes the signal on. A molecule that bound too
      weakly to occupy the site would block nothing. Propranolol acts on the receptor and not on
      the hormone, so breaking down adrenaline is not its mechanism. It binds reversibly and does
      not remove receptors from the surface; a permanent bond is how the K-Ras G12C drugs work,
      not how a beta-blocker works.
  - stem: "Around 1990, why did most pharmacologists expect a drug aimed at the ATP pocket of one kinase to inhibit many others?"
    choices:
      - "Kinases were known to hold ATP in a pocket that looks alike across the family"
      - "Kinases were thought to hold ATP through a permanent bond to a lysine side chain"
      - "Kinases were thought to sit in places inside a cell that no drug could ever reach"
      - "Kinases were thought to lack any pocket deep enough for a small molecule to fill"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Every protein kinase runs the same transfer from ATP, so the pocket that holds ATP is conserved
      across about five hundred human kinases, and a molecule shaped for one was expected to fit
      dozens. Kinases do have a pocket a small molecule can fill, since ATP is one; the missing
      pocket was the problem with Ras. ATP binds kinases reversibly and not through a permanent
      bond. Reaching kinases inside cells was not the objection; telling them apart was.
  - stem: "Imatinib inhibits ABL, the PDGF receptor and KIT, which normal cells also use. In culture it kills BCR-ABL leukaemia cells and leaves normal cells alive. Which explanation fits best?"
    choices:
      - "The leukaemia cells take up far more of the drug than the normal cells beside them"
      - "The normal cells destroy the drug before it can reach the ATP pocket of a kinase"
      - "The normal cells contain none of the three kinases that the drug is able to inhibit"
      - "The leukaemia cells need the fused kinase to survive and the normal cells do not"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The selectivity seen in cells comes from dependence: a dividing chronic myeloid leukaemia cell dies without BCR-ABL activity, while a normal cell gets through a period without ABL, PDGF receptor or KIT activity. Nothing in the result needs unequal uptake of the drug. Normal
      cells do contain ABL, the PDGF receptor and KIT, which is why effects on normal tissue are
      possible. Normal cells are not protected by destroying the drug; they are simply not killed
      by losing these kinases for a while.
  - stem: "At relapse, a patient's BCR-ABL has isoleucine in place of threonine 315. The mutant kinase phosphorylates its substrates at the normal rate, and imatinib no longer inhibits it. What does this say about residue 315?"
    choices:
      - "It is the point at which BCR is joined to ABL in the chain of the fusion protein"
      - "It makes a contact with the drug and is not needed for the kinase's reaction"
      - "It lies in the loop that every kinase needs in order to hold on to its ATP"
      - "It is the residue that hands the phosphate from ATP to the substrate protein"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The kinase still works at its normal rate, so residue 315 is unlikely to be the residue that transfers the phosphate or an essential part of what holds ATP; a change in either would be expected to slow the enzyme. Gatekeeper changes can even raise a kinase's activity.
      The drug no longer inhibits, so the residue must be one the drug depends on: threonine 315
      makes a contact with imatinib that the bulkier isoleucine cannot make. The join between BCR
      and ABL lies outside the drug's pocket in the kinase domain and is not what changed.
  - stem: "Sotorasib works on tumours with K-Ras G12C. A second patient's tumour has K-Ras G12D, with aspartate at position 12. Would you expect sotorasib to work, and why?"
    choices:

      - "Yes, because the drug competes with GTP at the nucleotide site of any Ras"
      - "No, because G12D Ras loses its lipid anchor and cannot reach the membrane"

    rationale: >-
      Sotorasib depends on a covalent bond to the sulphur of cysteine 12. Aspartate has a

    rationale: >-
      For GTP the ratio of concentration to dissociation constant is 0.5 mM over 10 pM, which is
      50,000,000. For the compound it is 1 µM over 1 nM, which is 1,000. The compound's share is

**In 2001 a capsule aimed at one enzyme, out of about five hundred human enzymes that share its pocket, was approved for a leukaemia. That same year a laboratory read, from the blood of patients who had relapsed, how the leukaemia was getting around it.**

Every pathway in this course is a chain of parts: a receptor, a switch, a kinase, a docking site. A targeted drug is a molecule made to act on one of those parts. This lesson sorts such drugs by where on the part they land, because the landing place shapes how selective a drug can be and how a disease will escape it. Dependence on the target, drug exposure and the rest of the network matter too.

## A decoy in the receptor: fit without the push

Some of the first drugs designed against a signalling part were aimed at something nobody had seen. In 1948 Raymond Ahlquist sorted the actions of adrenaline-like compounds into two families by their order of potency on tissues, and named two receptor types, alpha and beta. No hormone receptor had been purified. James Black joined ICI Pharmaceuticals in 1958 with a plan for angina, the chest pain of a heart whose arteries have narrowed: make the heart respond less to adrenaline, so that it asks for less oxygen.

A known compound, dichloroisoprenaline, showed the difficulty. In some of the group's tests it blocked the heart's response to adrenaline, and in others it acted as a stimulant itself, depending on the tissue. The chemist John Stephenson proposed swapping its ring for a naphthyl group. In the group's tests the new compound, pronethalol, blocked the receptor without stimulating it. A safer successor, propranolol, followed.

The result means that fitting a receptor and switching it on are separable properties. The lesson on binding and dose-response calls them affinity and efficacy, and calls a molecule with the first and without the second an antagonist. Picture a key that goes into a lock and will not turn: while it sits there, the right key cannot get in. Black later did the same at the histamine receptor of the stomach lining, which led to the ulcer drug cimetidine.

That is the first class: **a molecule that sits in a receptor's own site and does not switch it on.**

## One pocket, five hundred kinases: "Highly Conserved-- target for lots of drugs"

Many human kinases have been linked to disease. That is the case for kinase drugs. The case against was the first half of this section's heading. Every protein kinase runs the same reaction, moving the end phosphate of ATP onto a protein, so the pocket that holds ATP looks much alike across about five hundred human kinases. Around 1990 the reasonable expectation was that a molecule shaped to jam that pocket in one kinase would jam it in dozens.

The test case was chronic myeloid leukaemia, a cancer of white blood cells. Its cause is a fusion of two genes whose product, BCR-ABL, is a tyrosine kinase that is always on and that healthy blood cells do not normally have. At Ciba-Geigy in Basel a group that included the chemist Juerg Zimmermann was changing single groups on one core structure to shift its preference from kinase to kinase. One added methyl group removed its activity against protein kinase C while keeping it active against ABL. Elisabeth Buchdunger's pharmacology showed what the resulting compound hit: ABL and the PDGF receptor, and later KIT, with the EGF and insulin receptors barely touched. In Brian Druker's cultures in Oregon it blocked the growth of BCR-ABL leukaemia cells and left normal colonies alive. The compound became imatinib.

Two kinds of selectivity are in that story. One belongs to the molecule: a short list of kinases among those tested, measured on purified enzymes and in cells. The other belongs to the disease. Normal cells also use ABL, the PDGF receptor and KIT, and in these experiments they got through a period without them. In a patient, inhibiting them can still injure normal tissue. Most leukaemia cells do not get through a period without BCR-ABL. That second selectivity can only be measured in a living cell or a patient.

A structural account of the molecule's selectivity came after the fact. As the lesson on the phosphorylation switch shows, imatinib binds ABL in its switched-off shape, and switched-off shapes are thought to differ between kinases more than working shapes do, though how much of the selectivity this explains is still studied. That is how the second half of the heading came true. The second class is **a small molecule in the ATP pocket of a kinase**: imatinib (approved 2001), gefitinib (2003), erlotinib (2004), and the JAK inhibitors from the JAK-STAT lesson.

## When the drug stops working: one residue changed

Some patients in the early imatinib trials, mostly those with advanced disease, got well and then relapsed. In Charles Sawyers's laboratory at UCLA, Mercedes Gorre sequenced BCR-ABL from their blood. In several patients one DNA letter had changed, ACT to ATT, which replaces threonine 315 with isoleucine.

Threonine 315 is one of the contacts that hold imatinib in its pocket. Isoleucine is bulkier and cannot make that contact. The enzyme does not need this residue for the phosphate transfer itself, so the kinase keeps working while the drug fails to bind. The change is not neutral for the enzyme: mutating this gatekeeper shifts the kinase toward its active shape and can raise its activity.

A relapse looks like a failure of the idea. Read it as an experiment instead. The cells that survived were the ones already carrying a change at the one position the drug depended on, with the rest of the enzyme untouched; the drug selected them. That is strong evidence that the drug had been working through BCR-ABL, and that the leukaemia still needed BCR-ABL. It also wrote the specification for the next drug: grip ABL without needing residue 315. Dasatinib, a second-generation inhibitor, kills cells with other resistance mutations such as G250E, but T315I resists it too. Ponatinib was then built with a slim carbon-carbon triple bond where the isoleucine side chain sticks out, and it inhibits the T315I kinase.

### Design problem

A patient's leukaemia answers to a kinase inhibitor for a year and then returns. List three different ways the leukaemia cells could have escaped the drug while still depending on the same pathway. For each, say what you would measure to show it, and what kind of second drug you would design.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with one measurement. In the patient's cells, with the drug present, measure phosphorylation of a protein that the kinase phosphorylates directly. If it is phosphorylated again, the kinase itself is probably back on, which a target-specific test can confirm.

1. **The target changed.** A mutation alters a residue the drug touches. Sequence the kinase gene at relapse and compare it with the sample from before treatment, then show that the purified mutant enzyme needs far more drug. Design a drug that does not use that contact, or one that binds a different site on the same kinase, as asciminib does on ABL.
2. **There is more target.** Extra copies of the gene make more kinase than the dose can cover. Count gene copies and measure the protein. The target is unchanged, so a higher dose or a tighter-binding inhibitor of the same pocket may work, if that much inhibition can be reached safely.
3. **The pathway is fed from elsewhere.** The direct substrate stays unphosphorylated, so the kinase is still inhibited, but a step further down is active again because a second kinase drives it. Measure phosphorylation of downstream proteins with the drug present and find the kinase responsible. Design a drug against that kinase or the shared downstream step, and give it with the first.

Gorre and colleagues found the first two in patients: a mutation in some, extra copies of BCR-ABL in others.

## The target nobody could hit: a bond to the mutation itself

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Ras, the GTP switch from the lesson on GTPase switches, was the standard example of an undruggable target. Ras holds its nucleotide with picomolar affinity while the cell keeps GTP near the millimolar range, so a competitor for that site must beat a ligand that binds very tightly and is present in vast excess. And the known structures of Ras showed no obvious pocket for a drug-like molecule to fill.

A first plan went around the protein. Ras normally gets a farnesyl lipid to reach the membrane, as the lesson on lipid anchors explains, so chemists made inhibitors of the enzyme that adds it. In clinical trials these drugs did not control cancers in which K-Ras is commonly mutated. One reason is that when that enzyme is blocked, a related enzyme gives K-Ras a different lipid, geranylgeranyl, and K-Ras reaches the membrane anyway.

Kevan Shokat's laboratory went at the mutation itself. Glycine 12 has no side chain. One cancer mutation replaces it with cysteine, which puts a thiol, a reactive sulphur-hydrogen group, on the surface of the protein. The group held purified K-Ras G12C in its GDP-bound shape, passed 480 fragments over it that could form an exchangeable disulphide bond, and weighed the protein to see what had attached. The drugs that followed were built with chemistry that does not come undone. Two fragments attached to most of the protein. Normal K-Ras, which has three cysteines elsewhere, showed no detectable reaction.

The crystal structure showed something new. The compound lay in a pocket beneath the switch II loop that no earlier Ras structure had shown. Once bonded, it tilts Ras toward holding GDP and gets in the way of Raf. The compound sits beside the nucleotide site rather than in it, and its bond lasts for the life of that protein molecule, so it never has to out-compete GTP. In 2019 Canon and colleagues reported sotorasib, a drug for patients that bonds to the same cysteine. This is a class of its own: **a molecule that bonds to a residue only the mutant has.** Its limit is in its definition: a drug built around a thiol needs that thiol. Other mutations at position 12, such as aspartate or valine, leave no sulphur for this chemistry, so reaching them took other reactions, including one that bonds to aspartate 12 itself.

## Outside, inside, beside: the sorting, and what the network does next

You have three classes so far. Two more complete the sorting.

- **Outside.** An antibody is a large protein that does not cross the membrane, so it works on the outer face of a receptor. Trastuzumab (1998) and cetuximab (2004), from the lesson on receptor tyrosine kinases, are examples. The checkpoint antibodies in the lesson on antigen receptors land outside too.
- **Beside.** Some small molecules bind next to the active site and hold the enzyme in a shape that cannot work. The MEK inhibitors in the lesson on Ras and MAPK sit in a pocket beside the ATP site whose shape is particular to MEK.

The part need not be an enzyme. Venetoclax, from the lesson on cell death, fills the groove on BCL-2 where a partner protein would bind.

Wherever a drug lands, it lands in a network, and the lesson on signalling circuits tells you what to expect. ERK normally sends negative feedback to steps above it. Block MEK and that feedback is relieved, so the upstream steps push harder and the pathway can partly switch itself back on. A bypass is the other route. In some lung cancers treated with an EGF receptor inhibitor, extra copies of the gene for a different receptor, MET, switch the same downstream pathways on again.

For the K-Ras G12C drugs the feedback has a particular bite. Sotorasib and the other first-generation G12C drugs can bond only to Ras in its GDP-bound shape, and in cell experiments, signals from receptors that load Ras with GTP leave less of the protein in the shape those drugs need. Later compounds bond to the same cysteine on GTP-bound Ras instead, and are now in trials.
