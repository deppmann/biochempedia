  - name: "Charles Sawyers"
    contribution: >-
      His UCLA laboratory read the BCR-ABL gene in patients who relapsed on imatinib and found a
      single changed residue, threonine 315 to isoleucine, that keeps the drug out and leaves the

    profileSourceAnchor: "Gorre et al., Science 293:876 (2001); Sawyers, Nature Medicine 15:1158 (2009)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Sawyers trained as an oncology fellow in Owen Witte's laboratory at UCLA and later ran the
      Los Angeles arm of the first imatinib trial, which opened in June 1998. Patients with
      chronic myeloid leukaemia took a capsule and their blood counts returned to normal. Some of
      them, mostly patients with advanced disease, then relapsed. In his laboratory Mercedes Gorre sequenced BCR-ABL
      from the blood of relapsed patients, and in several she found one DNA letter changed, ACT to
      ATT, which swaps threonine 315 for isoleucine. The 2001 paper also reported patients whose
      cells carried extra copies of the BCR-ABL gene. In both cases the kinase was active again,
      so the leukaemia still depended on it. Sawyers later credited Gorre and Neil Shah with the
      resistance work. His laboratory went on to show that a second-generation compound,
      dasatinib, killed cells carrying another resistance mutation, G250E. The threonine 315 change turned a
      relapse into a specification for the next drug: one that does not need residue 315.

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Shokat came to biology as a chemist, with a degree from Reed College and a chemistry PhD
      from Berkeley in 1991. At UCSF his laboratory took on K-Ras, which holds its nucleotide too
      tightly to be out-competed and showed no groove for a drug. One cancer mutation, glycine 12
      to cysteine, puts a thiol on the protein's surface. The group held purified K-Ras G12C in
      its GDP-bound shape and passed 480 disulphide-containing fragments over it, weighing the
      protein by mass spectrometry to see what had attached. Two fragments modified most of the
      protein, and no reaction was detected with normal K-Ras, which has three cysteines of its
      own. A crystal structure showed the compound in a cavity beneath switch II. Bonded there, it
      shifted Ras toward GDP and impaired its binding to Raf. The paper, by Ostrem, Peters, Sos,
      Wells and Shokat, appeared in 2013. In 2019 Canon and colleagues reported AMG 510, later
      named sotorasib, which bonds to the same cysteine and was built for patients.

techniques:
  - name: "Order of potency on isolated tissue"
    blurb: >-
      You apply a series of related compounds to a tissue or an isolated beating heart and record
      how strongly each one acts. If the order of potency differs between tissues, that points to different receptors, once differences in tissue response and drug handling have been allowed for. Ahlquist defined alpha and beta receptors this way, and Black's
      group tested their blockers on isolated hearts before animals and people.
  - name: "Kinase selectivity panel"
    blurb: >-
      You test one compound against many kinases, as purified enzymes or in cells that depend on
      different kinases, and compare the concentration needed to inhibit each. The result is a
      list of what the compound hits. Buchdunger's panel showed that the compound that became
      imatinib hit a short list.
  - name: "Sequencing the drug target at relapse"
    blurb: >-
      You read the gene for the drug's target from a patient's cells before treatment and again
      at relapse. A change that appears only at relapse, at a residue that touches the drug, is strong evidence that the drug was acting through that target, and rebuilding the mutant enzyme and testing it against the drug settles it. Gorre and Sawyers found T315I in BCR-ABL this
      way.
  - name: "Disulphide tethering read by mass spectrometry"
    blurb: >-
      You mix a protein that has a cysteine with a library of small fragments that each carry a
      disulphide, under conditions where disulphides can exchange. A fragment with some affinity
      for the surface near the cysteine stays attached, and a mass spectrometer detects the added
      mass. Shokat's group used 480 fragments to find binders beside cysteine 12 of K-Ras G12C.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "Propranolol and adrenaline both bind the beta receptor. What makes propranolol an antagonist?"
    choices:
      - "It binds the receptor so weakly that the site stays empty most of the time"
      - "It breaks down adrenaline in the blood before the hormone can bind"
      - "It occupies the hormone's site and does not switch the receptor on"
      - "It bonds to the receptor for good and removes it from the cell surface"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      An antagonist has affinity without efficacy: it fits the site, so adrenaline cannot get in,
      and it does not cause the shape change that passes the signal on. A molecule that bound too
      weakly to occupy the site would block nothing. Propranolol acts on the receptor and not on
      the hormone, so breaking down adrenaline is not its mechanism. It binds reversibly and does
      not remove receptors from the surface; a permanent bond is how the K-Ras G12C drugs work,
      not how a beta-blocker works.
  - stem: "Around 1990, why did most pharmacologists expect a drug aimed at the ATP pocket of one kinase to inhibit many others?"
    choices:
      - "Kinases were known to hold ATP in a pocket that looks alike across the family"
      - "Kinases were thought to hold ATP through a permanent bond to a lysine side chain"
      - "Kinases were thought to sit in places inside a cell that no drug could ever reach"
      - "Kinases were thought to lack any pocket deep enough for a small molecule to fill"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Every protein kinase runs the same transfer from ATP, so the pocket that holds ATP is conserved
      across about five hundred human kinases, and a molecule shaped for one was expected to fit
      dozens. Kinases do have a pocket a small molecule can fill, since ATP is one; the missing
      pocket was the problem with Ras. ATP binds kinases reversibly and not through a permanent
      bond. Reaching kinases inside cells was not the objection; telling them apart was.
  - stem: "Imatinib inhibits ABL, the PDGF receptor and KIT, which normal cells also use. In culture it kills BCR-ABL leukaemia cells and leaves normal cells alive. Which explanation fits best?"
    choices:
      - "The leukaemia cells take up far more of the drug than the normal cells beside them"
      - "The normal cells destroy the drug before it can reach the ATP pocket of a kinase"
      - "The normal cells contain none of the three kinases that the drug is able to inhibit"
      - "The leukaemia cells need the fused kinase to survive and the normal cells do not"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The selectivity seen in cells comes from dependence: a dividing chronic myeloid leukaemia cell dies without BCR-ABL activity, while a normal cell gets through a period without ABL, PDGF receptor or KIT activity. Nothing in the result needs unequal uptake of the drug. Normal
      cells do contain ABL, the PDGF receptor and KIT, which is why effects on normal tissue are
      possible. Normal cells are not protected by destroying the drug; they are simply not killed
      by losing these kinases for a while.
  - stem: "At relapse, a patient's BCR-ABL has isoleucine in place of threonine 315. The mutant kinase phosphorylates its substrates at the normal rate, and imatinib no longer inhibits it. What does this say about residue 315?"
    choices:
      - "It is the point at which BCR is joined to ABL in the chain of the fusion protein"
      - "It makes a contact with the drug and is not needed for the kinase's reaction"
      - "It lies in the loop that every kinase needs in order to hold on to its ATP"
      - "It is the residue that hands the phosphate from ATP to the substrate protein"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The kinase still works at its normal rate, so residue 315 is unlikely to be the residue that transfers the phosphate or an essential part of what holds ATP; a change in either would be expected to slow the enzyme. Gatekeeper changes can even raise a kinase's activity.
      The drug no longer inhibits, so the residue must be one the drug depends on: threonine 315
      makes a contact with imatinib that the bulkier isoleucine cannot make. The join between BCR
      and ABL lies outside the drug's pocket in the kinase domain and is not what changed.
  - stem: "Sotorasib works on tumours with K-Ras G12C. A second patient's tumour has K-Ras G12D, with aspartate at position 12. Would you expect sotorasib to work, and why?"
    choices:
      - "No, because aspartate has no thiol for the drug to form its bond with"
      - "Yes, because the drug competes with GTP at the nucleotide site of any Ras"
      - "No, because G12D Ras loses its lipid anchor and cannot reach the membrane"
      - "Yes, because every position 12 mutant opens the same pocket for the drug to enter"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Sotorasib depends on a covalent bond to the sulphur of cysteine 12. Aspartate has a
      carboxylate and no thiol, so the bond cannot form and the drug is not held. Even if a
      similar pocket can open in other mutants, sotorasib's hold depends on the bond, so a pocket
      alone is not enough. The drug does not compete with GTP at the nucleotide site, which is the
      route that picomolar affinity closes. A mutation at position 12 changes GTP hydrolysis and
      not the lipid anchor at the other end of the protein, so G12D Ras reaches the membrane.

  - stem: "Assume K-Ras binds GTP with a dissociation constant of 10 pM and that a cell holds GTP at about 0.5 mM. A chemist makes a reversible compound that competes for the nucleotide site with a dissociation constant of 1 nM and reaches 1 µM inside cells. For each ligand, its share of the site scales with its concentration divided by its dissociation constant. About what fraction of Ras would hold the compound instead of GTP?"
    choices:
      - "About 1 molecule in every 100"
      - "About 1 molecule in every 50,000"
      - "About 999 molecules in every 1,000"
      - "About 1 molecule in every 500"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      For GTP the ratio of concentration to dissociation constant is 0.5 mM over 10 pM, which is
      50,000,000. For the compound it is 1 µM over 1 nM, which is 1,000. The compound's share is
      1,000 out of about 50,001,000, or about 1 in 50,000. The answer of 999 in 1,000 is what you
      get if you leave GTP out and treat the compound as the only ligand. One in 100 compares the
      two dissociation constants and ignores the concentrations. One in 500 compares the two
      concentrations and ignores the dissociation constants. This arithmetic is why a reversible
      competitor at the nucleotide site was judged hopeless.

    rationale: >-
      The substrate is phosphorylated despite the drug, so BCR-ABL is active again, and the only

    rationale: >-
      The protein gains mass only when cysteine 12 is present, and nineteen times more of it is

      A targeted drug acts on one named component of a signalling pathway, and the site it uses

  - level: "grad"

    text: >-
      Selectivity has two layers that are measured separately. Biochemical selectivity is the
      profile across the kinome: imatinib inhibits ABL, the PDGF receptor and KIT, and one account of why is that it binds
      an inactive conformation of the activation loop that few kinases adopt in the same way, with
      threonine 315, the gatekeeper residue, as one of its contacts. Therapeutic selectivity comes
      from oncogene dependence: most dividing BCR-ABL cells die without the kinase, while normal cells tolerate a period without ABL, PDGF receptor and KIT activity. The leukaemia stem cells are the exception: they survive full inhibition of the kinase, which is why the drug controls the disease without curing it. A resistance mutation is then genetic
      proof of target. T315I removes a contact and adds bulk without loss of catalytic activity,
      and ponatinib passes the isoleucine with an ethynyl linker.
      For K-Ras the competitive route is closed by arithmetic. With picomolar nucleotide affinity
      and GTP near half a millimolar, the ratio of concentration to dissociation constant favours GTP over a reversible competitor at reachable concentrations by many thousand-fold. **In 2001 a capsule aimed at one enzyme, out of about five hundred human enzymes that share its pocket, was approved for a leukaemia—and that same year a laboratory read, from relapsed patients' blood, how the leukaemia got around it.** Every pathway in this course is a chain of parts: a receptor, a switch, a kinase, a docking site. A **targeted drug** acts on one named part, and where it lands shapes both selectivity and escape.

## A decoy in the receptor: fit without the push

Some of the first designed signalling drugs aimed at a receptor nobody had purified. In 1948 Raymond Ahlquist sorted adrenaline-like compounds by their order of potency across tissues and defined two receptor types, alpha and beta. In 1958 James Black joined ICI Pharmaceuticals with a plan for angina, the chest pain of a heart with narrowed arteries: blunt the heart's response to adrenaline so it asks for less oxygen.

An existing compound, dichloroisoprenaline, showed the catch: in some tests it blocked adrenaline at the heart, yet in others it acted as a stimulant itself, depending on the tissue. John Stephenson proposed swapping its catechol ring for a naphthyl group. The new compound, **pronethalol**, blocked without stimulating; a safer successor, **propranolol**, followed. Black later applied the same idea to histamine receptors in the stomach lining, yielding the ulcer drug **cimetidine**.

That separated two properties from the lesson on binding and dose-response: **affinity** (how tightly a molecule fits the site) and **efficacy** (how strongly it switches the receptor on). A molecule with affinity and without agonist efficacy is an **antagonist**—a key that enters a lock and will not turn, keeping adrenaline out (see *Going deeper* for propranolol's resting-state activity).

> **The one idea.** Where a drug binds shapes selectivity, and escape guides the next drug; new sites and covalent bonds bypass competition, yet networks push back. **Scope:** Targeted drugs act on one named pathway part, but what happens in a patient also depends on target dependence, drug exposure, and the surrounding signalling network.

## One pocket, five hundred kinases: how imatinib became selective

The first film showed imatinib's two layers of selectivity at a shared ATP pocket; here are the chemistry and experiments behind them.

Around 1990 a selective kinase drug looked implausible because ~500 human protein kinases catalyse the same transfer of ATP's end phosphate onto a protein, so their active pockets fold into nearly the same shape. Chronic myeloid leukaemia offered a clean test: one fused, always-on tyrosine kinase, **BCR-ABL**, causes it, and healthy blood cells do not normally have it. At Ciba-Geigy in Basel, Juerg Zimmermann and colleagues altered single groups on a phenylaminopyrimidine core; one added methyl group removed activity against protein kinase C while keeping ABL inhibition. Elisabeth Buchdunger's pharmacology panel showed what the lead compound hit: ABL and the PDGF receptor, and later KIT, with the EGF and insulin receptors barely touched. In Brian Druker's cultures in Oregon, that compound—**imatinib**—blocked BCR-ABL leukaemia growth while sparing normal colonies.

Two distinct layers of selectivity are at work:
- **Biochemical selectivity** belongs to the molecule: its short target list on purified enzymes and in cells. As the lesson on the phosphorylation switch shows, imatinib binds ABL in an idle (switched-off) shape, and switched-off shapes are thought to diverge across kinases more than working shapes do, though how much of the selectivity this explains is still studied.
- **Cellular (therapeutic) selectivity** belongs to the disease: dividing leukaemia cells die without BCR-ABL, whereas normal cells get through a period without ABL, the PDGF receptor, and KIT. Normal cells do use all three, so in a patient inhibiting them can still injure normal tissue (see *Going deeper*); this layer can only be measured in a living cell or a patient.

That is the second class: **a small molecule in a kinase's ATP pocket**—imatinib (2001), gefitinib (2003), erlotinib (2004), and the JAK inhibitors from the JAK-STAT lesson.

## When the drug stops working: one residue changed

The first film traced how relapse wrote a blueprint; here is the molecular evidence. When patients in early imatinib trials—mostly with advanced disease—relapsed, Mercedes Gorre in Charles Sawyers's UCLA laboratory sequenced BCR-ABL from their blood. Several carried a single DNA change, `ACT` to `ATT`, swapping threonine 315 for isoleucine (**T315I**); others carried extra copies of BCR-ABL.

Position 315 is ABL's **gatekeeper residue**, guarding a deeper alcove beside the ATP pocket. Imatinib reaches into that alcove and hydrogen-bonds with threonine 315. Bulkier isoleucine breaks that contact and sterically blocks imatinib, yet ATP does not need the alcove, so the kinase keeps working—in fact, mutating the gatekeeper shifts ABL toward its active shape and can raise its activity.

Selecting a change at one drug contact while leaving catalysis intact is strong evidence that imatinib worked through BCR-ABL and that the tumour still depended on it. Second-generation **dasatinib** kills cells with other mutations such as G250E, yet T315I resists it too. **Ponatinib** passes isoleucine 315 with a slim carbon-carbon triple bond; **asciminib** bypasses the pocket by binding a separate site on ABL.

### Design problem

A patient's leukaemia answers to a kinase inhibitor for a year and then returns. List three different ways the leukaemia cells could have escaped the drug while still depending on the same pathway. For each, say what you would measure to show it, and what kind of second drug you would design.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with one measurement. In the patient's cells, with the drug present, measure phosphorylation of a protein that the kinase phosphorylates directly. If it is phosphorylated again, the kinase itself is probably back on, which a target-specific test can confirm.

1. **The target changed.** A mutation alters a residue the drug touches. Sequence the kinase gene at relapse and compare it with the sample from before treatment, then show that the purified mutant enzyme needs far more drug. Design a drug that does not use that contact, or one that binds a different site on the same kinase, as asciminib does on ABL.
2. **There is more target.** Extra copies of the gene make more kinase than the dose can cover. Count gene copies and measure the protein. The target is unchanged, so a higher dose or a tighter-binding inhibitor of the same pocket may work, if that much inhibition can be reached safely.
3. **The pathway is fed from elsewhere.** The direct substrate stays unphosphorylated, so the kinase is still inhibited, but a step further down is active again because a second kinase drives it. Measure phosphorylation of downstream proteins with the drug present and find the kinase responsible. Design a drug against that kinase or the shared downstream step, and give it with the first.

Gorre and colleagues found the first two in patients: a mutation in some, extra copies of BCR-ABL in others.

</details>

## The target nobody could hit: a bond to the mutation itself

The second film compared competitive binding in a kinase and in **Ras** (the GTPase switch from the lesson on GTPase switches). Side by side, the `[ligand] / Kd` ratio explains why cellular ATP is beatable while cellular GTP is not:

| Target & pocket | Natural ligand (`[L] / Kd` or `Km`) | Reversible drug (`[D] / Kd`) | Drug's share |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Typical kinase (ATP) | ~1 mM / ~10 µM = **100** | 1 µM / 1 nM = **1,000** | **~91%** |
| K-Ras (GTP) | ~0.5 mM / ~10 pM = **50,000,000** | 5 µM / 1 nM = **5,000** | **~1 in 10,000** |

That huge affinity gap—plus early structures showing no obvious groove—made Ras the standard "undruggable" target. An earlier attempt to block Ras's lipid anchor failed in K-Ras clinical trials via a backup lipid enzyme (see *Going deeper*).

Kevan Shokat's laboratory went at the mutation itself. Normal K-Ras has glycine (no side chain) at position 12; **G12C** puts a **cysteine** there, exposing a reactive sulphur-hydrogen group (**thiol**). Because K-Ras G12C still hydrolyses GTP slowly, it cycles briefly through its GDP-bound shape. Holding purified GDP-bound K-Ras G12C, the group passed 480 fragments carrying exchangeable disulphides over it and weighed the protein by mass spectrometry. Two fragments modified most of the mutant; normal K-Ras, with three cysteines elsewhere, showed no detectable reaction.

Crystal structures revealed a hidden pocket beneath the **switch II** loop, beside the nucleotide site. Clinical drugs—including **sotorasib** (Canon et al., 2019)—use permanent covalent chemistry. Bonded to cysteine 12, sotorasib tilts Ras toward GDP and impairs Raf binding for the protein's life, never fighting GTP.

**Predict.** A tumour carries K-Ras G12V, with valine at position 12, and the switch II pocket can still open. Would a clinical G12C drug gain its permanent bond?

This is a class of its own: **a molecule bonded covalently to a residue only the mutant has.** Its limit is its chemistry: G12D (aspartate) and G12V (valine) carry no thiol and require other strategies (see *Going deeper*).

## Outside, inside, beside: five landing places, and what the network does next

Two classes complete the sorting:
- **Outside.** An **antibody** cannot cross the membrane, so it binds a receptor's outer face—**trastuzumab** (1998) and **cetuximab** (2004) (receptor tyrosine kinases lesson), and checkpoint antibodies (antigen receptors lesson).
- **Beside.** An **allosteric inhibitor** binds next to the active site and locks the enzyme off—**MEK inhibitors** (Ras and MAPK lesson) fit a pocket particular to MEK.

A target need not be an enzyme: **venetoclax** (cell death lesson) fills BCL-2's protein-docking groove.

| Landing place | Mechanism | Selectivity lever | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Receptor's own site | Competitive decoy without push | Subtype binding pocket | Propranolol, cimetidine |
| 2. Receptor's outer face | Antibody on extracellular face | Surface epitope | Trastuzumab, cetuximab |
| 3. Kinase's ATP pocket | Small molecule in nucleotide cleft | Idle shape + target reliance | Imatinib, gefitinib, erlotinib, JAK inhibitors |
| 4. Beside the active site | Allosteric lock holding enzyme off | Adjacent non-conserved pocket | MEK inhibitors |
| 5. Mutant residue (covalent) | Permanent bond beside active site | Mutant-only residue | Sotorasib (K-Ras G12C) |

Even when a drug wins its target, remember our guiding rule: **new sites and covalent bonds bypass competition, yet networks push back.** As the lesson on signalling circuits shows, blocking one part alters the whole loop.

ERK normally sends negative feedback to the steps above it. Block MEK, and that feedback is relieved, so the upstream steps push harder and the pathway can partly switch itself back on.

A **bypass** is the other network answer: in some lung cancers treated with an EGF receptor inhibitor, extra copies of **MET** reactivate the same downstream pathways. And for GDP-state K-Ras G12C drugs, upstream receptor signals that reload Ras with GTP shrink the GDP-bound pool sotorasib needs; newer covalent compounds targeting **GTP-bound** K-Ras G12C are now in trials.

## Going deeper

### Inverse agonism at the beta receptor
Treating propranolol as a silent key is a clean first model; more precisely, unstimulated beta receptors retain low basal activity, and propranolol acts as an **inverse agonist**, lowering signalling below rest.

### Limits of kinase selectivity in patients
Imatinib's window has two clinical limits: inhibiting non-fused ABL, PDGFR, or KIT can occasionally injure normal tissue (heart-muscle ABL is a studied, though debated, case), and quiescent leukaemia stem cells survive full BCR-ABL inhibition, so the drug controls CML without curing it.

### Lipid-anchor bypass and newer Ras drugs
Earlier Ras drugs inhibited farnesyltransferase, which adds the farnesyl lipid Ras needs to reach the membrane (lesson on lipid anchors). They failed in K-Ras trials partly because a related enzyme attaches **geranylgeranyl** instead, restoring membrane delivery. Reaching mutants without cysteine 12 now relies on non-covalent switch II binders (such as K-Ras G12D inhibitors) and chemistry bonding directly to aspartate 12, alongside GTP-state G12C drugs—all still tested against feedback and bypass reactivation.
