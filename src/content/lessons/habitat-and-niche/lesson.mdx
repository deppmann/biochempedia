## Explain: a place to live and a way to live

<WordBank terms={['habitat', 'niche', 'species', 'resource', 'competition', 'biodiversity']} />

The first film named the warblers' spruce forest their **habitat**, the place where an organism lives. A pond, marsh or rotting log is a habitat too. A **niche** is an organism's role: how it lives and uses resources there. It includes:

- what it eats
- where and when it feeds
- the space it uses, such as treetops or soil
- what eats it
- what it does in the ecosystem, such as breaking down dead leaves

As a memory aid, think of the habitat as an address and the niche as a job. The comparison breaks down because a niche is more than one job.

**Predict.** A gray squirrel and an earthworm both live in one oak forest. Do they share a habitat, a niche, or both?

In a Virginia oak forest, they share a habitat. The squirrel eats acorns and nuts and buries some in the ground. Hawks and foxes hunt it. The earthworm eats dead leaves and bits of soil and helps break them down, as you saw in [Producers, Consumers and Decomposers](/lessons/producers-consumers-decomposers/). Their niches are very different.

## Explain: a niche helps an organism survive

The first film defined a **resource**: something a living thing needs and takes from its habitat, such as food, water, shelter or space. Part of a niche is how an organism gets them, and the amount of a resource can change a niche. When food is plentiful, an animal may feed in a few favourite spots. When food runs short, it may search more places or eat other things.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A niche includes how an organism gets needed resources, and it can shift when resources or conditions change.</Support>

## Explain: sharing a habitat

The first film called a **species** one kind of living thing. For animals such as birds, members of one species can breed with each other and have young that can also breed. It called **competition** a struggle for resources in short supply. So a deer and a warbler that share a forest but eat different foods do not compete for food just by living there.

When the shared resource is in short supply, heavy niche overlap creates more chances to compete for it. Species using different places, times or foods may compete less.

**Biodiversity** is the variety of living things in a place. Niche differences can help a habitat support more kinds of living things, but they do not guarantee it.

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The five warblers shared one habitat and used it in different but overlapping patterns. The table did not measure competition or food eaten, so it supports a careful explanation, not proof: different niches can reduce competition, helping species share a habitat.

**Science practices you used:** using a model to test a prediction, reading a data table and comparing percents, making a claim backed by evidence and reasoning, and planning a fair investigation.

### Design problem

Plan an investigation to find out whether two kinds of birds in your schoolyard feed at different heights. You will use two bird feeders and a timer, and you will not touch or catch any birds.

**Predict.** If both feeders stayed at the same height, could this investigation test whether feeder height changes visits? Explain.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do two kinds of birds, such as chickadees and mourning doves, visit a high feeder and a low feeder equally?
- **Independent variable:** feeder height, 0.5 m or 2.0 m above the ground.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of visits by each kind of bird in 15 minutes.
- **Constants:** the same tray feeder that both kinds can use, the same seed mix and amount, the same time of day, and feeders about 3 m apart.
- **Control:** after 5 of the 10 days, swap which feeder is high and which is low. This helps check that the spot is not causing the difference.
- **Repeated trials:** watch on 10 different days and find the mean visits for each kind at each height.
- **Safety:** an adult hangs the feeders. Watch quietly from at least 10 m away or from a window. Never touch birds, nests or droppings, and wash your hands after filling feeders.

If one kind visits the low feeder much more and the other visits the high feeder more, they may use different feeding heights here.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Notes on MacArthur’s data

MacArthur's height zones were 10 feet deep, so the metres in this lesson are converted and rounded. The branch base he mapped was bare or lichen-covered.

Weather could shift tree use. Most of the time, MacArthur's Cape May warblers fed near the treetops. In cold, wet, windy weather they fed low in willows and cherry trees instead, often among flowers.

### What else may help warblers share a forest

MacArthur thought other things mattered too. The birds also hunted in different ways. Each kind may be held back by food in different forests. Cape May and bay-breasted warblers became common mainly in years with outbreaks of spruce budworm caterpillars. Scientists still debate how much feeding zones explain why these warblers can share a forest. You will see more in [Competition and Cooperation](/lessons/competition-and-cooperation/).
