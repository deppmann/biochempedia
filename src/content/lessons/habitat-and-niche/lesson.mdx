## Explain: a place to live and a way to live

<WordBank terms={['habitat', 'niche', 'species', 'resource', 'competition', 'biodiversity']} />

A **habitat** is the place where an organism lives. A spruce forest is a habitat. So is a pond, a marsh or a rotting log.

A **niche** is an organism’s role in its habitat. It includes the jobs the organism does and the space it uses in the ecosystem. A niche includes:

- what it eats
- where and when it feeds
- the space it uses, such as treetops or soil
- what eats it
- what it does for the ecosystem, such as breaking down dead leaves

Think of the habitat as an address and the niche as a job. The comparison breaks down because a niche is more than one job. It is everything the organism does and uses.

Take a Virginia oak forest. A gray squirrel and an earthworm both live there. They share a habitat. The squirrel eats acorns and nuts and buries some in the ground. Hawks and foxes hunt it. The earthworm eats dead leaves and bits of soil and helps break them down, as you saw in [Producers, Consumers and Decomposers](/lessons/producers-consumers-decomposers/). Their niches are very different.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A habitat is where an organism lives, and a niche is its whole role there.</Support>

## Explain: a niche helps an organism survive

A **resource** is something a living thing needs and takes from its habitat, such as food, water, shelter or space. An organism’s niche is how it gets those resources. A warbler’s small, thin bill picks insects from among needles. Its niche gives it food, and food keeps it alive.

The amount of a resource can change a niche. When food is plentiful, an animal may feed in just a few favourite spots. When food runs short, it may have to search more places or eat other things. MacArthur saw Cape May warblers feed low in small trees during cold, wet, windy weather. Most of the time they stayed near the treetops.

## Explain: sharing a habitat

A **species** is one kind of living thing whose members can breed with each other and have young that can also breed. When two species need the same limited resource, they compete. **Competition** is when organisms struggle for the same resources that are in short supply. You will see more in [Competition and Cooperation](/lessons/competition-and-cooperation/).

If two species have niches that overlap a lot, they compete a lot. One may get most of the food, and the other may do poorly. If their niches are different, they compete less. Many species can then share one habitat.

**Biodiversity** is the variety of living things in a place. Different niches help a habitat hold more kinds of living things. But using a place differently does not remove all competition. How strongly each species competes, and the weather and food that year, also matter.

### Design problem

Plan an investigation to find out whether two kinds of birds in your schoolyard feed at different heights. You will use two bird feeders and a timer, and you will not touch or catch any birds.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do two kinds of birds, such as chickadees and mourning doves, visit a high feeder and a low feeder equally?
- **Independent variable:** feeder height, 0.5 m or 2.0 m above the ground.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of visits by each kind of bird in 15 minutes.
- **Constants:** the same tray feeder that both kinds can use, the same seed mix and amount, the same time of day, and feeders about 3 m apart.
- **Control:** halfway through, swap which feeder is high and which is low. This checks that the spot is not causing the difference.
- **Repeated trials:** watch on 10 different days and find the mean visits for each kind at each height.
- **Safety:** an adult hangs the feeders. Watch quietly from at least 10 m away or from a window. Never touch birds, nests or droppings, and wash your hands after filling feeders.

If one kind visits the low feeder much more and the other visits the high feeder more, they may use different feeding heights here.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The five warblers share one habitat, the spruce forest, but they do not live the same way. Each kind spent its time in its own pattern of zones, and they also hunted in different ways. Because their niches differ, they compete less for the same insects, even though their patterns still overlap.

**Science practices you used:** using a model to test a prediction, reading a data table and comparing percents, making a claim backed by evidence and reasoning, and planning a fair investigation.
