    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the 1970s it was known that LH in the blood of monkeys rises and falls in pulses, and the
      hypothalamic hormone that drives LH, GnRH, had just been isolated. Knobil's laboratory asked
      whether the pulses carried information. They worked with female rhesus monkeys in which
      lesions of the hypothalamus had abolished the secretion of LH and FSH. The pituitary itself
      was intact, so the experimenters could supply GnRH by intravenous infusion and read the
      pituitary's answer in blood samples. Brief infusions once an hour restored LH and FSH
      secretion. A continuous infusion did not sustain it, and the authors reported that returning
      to the hourly schedule brought secretion back. They concluded that the pattern of GnRH
      delivery, not the total amount, controlled the pituitary in these animals. The result
      explained why the hypothalamus releases GnRH in bursts, and it set the search, which still
      continues, for the neural pulse generator that times them.

    story: >-
      The idea that the brain controls the anterior pituitary by sending hormones down a short
      chain of blood vessels had wide support before anyone had purified one of those hormones. The
      problem was quantity. A hypothalamus is small, and each holds a tiny amount of each releasing
      hormone, so isolating one meant collecting and extracting hypothalami from slaughterhouse

      shared with Rosalyn Yalow for radioimmunoassay. Pure synthetic GnRH is what made experiments

    story: >-
      A section through a seminiferous tubule shows germ cells at several stages at once, from
      spermatogonia at the wall to spermatids near the centre, but a still picture cannot say how
      long one cell takes to travel that path. Heller and Clermont used a tracer. Thymidine is a

A rhesus monkey whose hypothalamus can no longer drive her pituitary gets the missing hormone, GnRH, by pump, as a steady drip or an hourly burst, the drip giving at least as much. Only one schedule works. The first film builds her loop; the second runs the test.

> **The one idea.** A mammal's **gonads** (testes or ovaries) run on a loop that the brain times. The hypothalamus sends **gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH)** to the pituitary in pulses, and the pituitary sends **luteinizing hormone (LH)** and **follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)** to the gonads. The gonads' hormones come back as a brake, except when **estradiol** from a growing ovarian follicle stays high for long enough: then it becomes an accelerator and sets off the LH surge that triggers ovulation. Whether the pulses themselves carry the message is what the second film tests.
>
> *Scope:* the brake is simplified for a first course. In the testis, testosterone brakes the hypothalamus and pituitary, and inhibin B brakes FSH; in the ovary, estradiol brakes through most of the follicular phase and progesterone after ovulation. The timing result comes from rhesus monkeys.

## Making sperm and eggs

**Gametes** are haploid sex cells, cells with one set of chromosomes. In **fertilization** two gametes, usually from two parents, join to give a diploid **zygote** with two sets, which is **sexual reproduction**. **Meiosis**, the division that halves the chromosome number and reshuffles the parents' **alleles** (versions of a gene), makes gametes; see [chromosomes and meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/).

**Spermatogenesis** happens in the **seminiferous tubules**, coiled tubes inside each testis. Germ cells mature as they move from the tubule wall toward its centre:

1. **Spermatogonia** (diploid germ cells, 46 chromosomes in humans, among them the stem cells of the line) divide by mitosis at the wall, renewing themselves through adult life and feeding cells into meiosis.
2. A **primary spermatocyte** (46 chromosomes) completes meiosis I, which separates the paired chromosomes, to give two **secondary spermatocytes**, 23 chromosomes each, each still made of two sister **chromatids**: copies made by DNA replication, though crossing over in meiosis I may have made them no longer identical.
3. Meiosis II separates the chromatids, giving four **spermatids**, each with 23 single chromosomes.
4. **Spermiogenesis** remodels each spermatid into a sperm with a head and a tail.

The first film's **Leydig** and **Sertoli cells** sit beside this path, and Sertoli support relies on testosterone's help. Sperm then mature in the **epididymis**, a coiled duct on the testis. Spermatogenesis in the testis takes roughly 64 to 74 days by different estimates (Going deeper).

**Oogenesis** in human females starts before birth:

1. In the fetal ovary, **oogonia** divide by mitosis and become **primary oocytes** (46 chromosomes), which enter meiosis I and arrest in prophase I.
2. Each waits, for years or decades, in a **follicle**, the oocyte plus the supporting cells around it.
3. After puberty, usually one follicle per cycle matures. Its oocyte finishes meiosis I shortly before ovulation, set going by the LH surge. The division is unequal: nearly all the cytoplasm goes to the **secondary oocyte**, and the other chromosome set leaves in a small **polar body**.
4. The secondary oocyte, the egg of everyday speech and of the first film, is released arrested in metaphase II. It finishes meiosis II only if fertilized, giving the egg proper and a second polar body.

Adult human ovaries are not known to make new oocytes, though negative evidence cannot prove absence (see the anecdote on egg stem cells). The testis renews its stock and makes four gametes per meiosis; the ovary spends a stock laid down before birth, one gamete per meiosis.

## The axis and its pulses

What the second film left out:

- **The doses.** The steady drip ran at one of four rates; at the two highest it delivered as much GnRH as the pulses or more, so too little hormone cannot explain the failure.
- **Where the pulses come from.** In mice, kisspeptin neurons in the hypothalamus drive them (Going deeper); in primates that is unsettled. Nor did the experiment show what pulse interval is normal in humans.
- **Why a steady supply fails.** The usual answer is **desensitization**: under constant GnRH, the pituitary cells that make LH and FSH turn their response down. The experiment did not identify the mechanism (Going deeper).

The whole loop is the **hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis** (portal route: [hypothalamus and autonomic control](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/)). LH and FSH, the **gonadotropins**, act through receptors of the family in [G-protein-coupled receptors](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/), and the first film's brakes are **negative feedback** ([the endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/)).

## The cycle and pregnancy

In human females the axis runs two linked cycles: the **ovarian cycle** (the follicles) and the **uterine cycle** (the uterine lining, or **endometrium**). The one idea again: the gonads' hormones brake the loop, except when estradiol stays high long enough.

In the **follicular phase**, FSH recruits a group of follicles and usually one becomes dominant. LH drives **theca cells** to make androgens, and FSH-supported **granulosa cells** convert them to estradiol. Rising estradiol rebuilds the endometrium after menstruation (the **proliferative phase**) and, for most of this phase, brakes the axis.

When estradiol stays high late in the phase, its effect switches to **positive feedback**, in which a signal boosts its own trigger. LH release rises steeply in the **LH surge**, which sets the oocyte finishing meiosis I and triggers **ovulation**, the release of the secondary oocyte. Here the timing that matters is how long estradiol stays high, a different signal from the GnRH pulses in the monkeys.

After ovulation, in the **luteal phase**, the emptied follicle becomes the **corpus luteum**, secreting progesterone and estradiol. Progesterone converts the thickened endometrium into a lining that can receive an embryo, the **secretory phase**, and it brakes LH and FSH again. Estradiol and progesterone act mainly through receptors inside the cell ([nuclear receptors](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/)). If no embryo implants, the corpus luteum regresses, progesterone falls, and the lining breaks down in **menstruation**, which begins the next cycle.

| One time axis | Follicular phase | Around ovulation | Luteal phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ovary | One follicle becomes dominant | LH surge; oocyte released | Corpus luteum, regresses without an embryo |
| Feedback | Estradiol brakes | Sustained high estradiol accelerates | Progesterone brakes |
| Uterus | Menstruation, then proliferative phase | Lining thick | Secretory phase, until progesterone falls |

The names pair up: the follicular phase spans menstruation and the proliferative phase, and the luteal phase is the secretory phase.

The textbook 28-day cycle with day-14 ovulation is a drawing convention: in a large, self-selected app-based study (Bull and colleagues), cycles averaged near 29 days, luteal phases near 12, and lengths varied widely (numbers in the anecdote).

If an embryo implants, its outer cell layer (the trophoblast, which forms much of the placenta) secretes **human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)**. Acting through the LH receptor, hCG keeps the corpus luteum making progesterone, so the lining is not shed. From about weeks 7 to 9 of gestation, the placenta itself becomes the main source of progesterone. Placental exchange of oxygen and antibodies is in [oxygen transport](/lessons/oxygen-transport/) and [adaptive immunity](/lessons/adaptive-immunity/).

## The same job across animals

**Asexual reproduction** makes offspring from one parent without fertilization, so the young carry that parent's genes. In **budding** a new individual grows out of the parent, as in Hydra; in **fission** the parent splits in two, as in some sea anemones and flatworms; in **fragmentation** a piece breaks off and regrows a whole body, as in some sea stars. In **parthenogenesis** an egg develops without being fertilized.

Sex carries what John Maynard Smith called the **twofold cost of males**. Suppose, in a made-up population, every female leaves two surviving young. An asexual female's two are both daughters, and each will bear young; a sexual female's are on average one daughter and one son, and the son bears no young himself. Counting only females and starting from one of each, asexual females go 1, 2, 4, 8 over three generations, while each sexual female leaves one daughter, so sexual females stay at 1.

Sex usually also means finding a mate, a cost asexual reproduction avoids; it offers new combinations of alleles. Why sex persists despite its costs is still argued; see [life histories](/lessons/life-histories/) and [selection in ecological context](/lessons/selection-in-ecological-context/).

Some animals use both: water fleas (Daphnia) and many aphids make generations of females by parthenogenesis, then males and sexual eggs, often as the season turns. **Hermaphrodites** make both eggs and sperm. Earthworms make both at the same time, and two worms usually exchange sperm. Some fish change sex: clownfish start as males and the largest can become female, while many wrasses change from female to male. How sex is determined is in [sex linkage and beyond](/lessons/sex-linkage-and-beyond/).

**External fertilization** happens outside the female's body, usually in water. Sea urchins **broadcast-spawn**, releasing eggs and sperm into the sea, where many gametes never meet and eggs and larvae are exposed to predators. In most frogs, the male clasps the female (**amplexus**) and releases sperm over her eggs as she lays them.

**Internal fertilization** happens inside the female's body. Sperm arrive close to the eggs, and on land the gametes are kept from drying out. Birds fertilize internally and then lay shelled eggs. Most mammals are **viviparous** (the young develop inside the mother and are born live), yet monotremes such as the platypus lay eggs, and some sharks and lizards bear live young.

Most mammals have **estrous cycles** instead of menstrual cycles: the endometrium is generally reabsorbed rather than shed, and females are usually receptive mainly around ovulation. Menstruation is known in humans, apes and Old World monkeys, and a few other mammals, such as some bats and the spiny mouse. The embryo's next steps are in [animal development](/lessons/animal-development/), and the plant counterpart is [plant reproduction](/lessons/plant-reproduction/).

### Design problem

Lutes and colleagues (Nature, 2010) stained oocyte nuclei with DAPI, a fluorescent dye that binds DNA: chromosomes of the parthenogenetic Aspidoscelis tesselata had about 2.2 times the volume of those of the sexual A. gularis, which the authors read as a doubling of DNA. In body cells (nucleated red blood cells), DNA content differed by less than 1 percent. In another parthenogen, A. neomexicana, FISH probes (fluorescent in situ hybridization: fluorescent probes that bind particular chromosomes) showed that the partners paired in meiosis were sister chromosomes (copies of the same chromosome), not the two different homologues (the maternal and paternal versions) a sexual animal pairs. The oocytes also appeared to show the structures of meiotic pairing and crossing over. How can these all-female lizards keep meiosis and still pass on the mother's genetic makeup, and what does each measurement rule out?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The doubled DNA suggests the chromosomes double once more before meiosis, so the oocytes that complete it carry a doubled set. Whether the doubling comes from extra DNA replication or from fusion of two germ cells is not settled, and a 2016 follow-up in A. neomexicana found that most oocytes enter meiosis without it and stall; only the rare doubled ones go on. The body-cell measurement rules out a simply bigger genome, so the doubling belongs to the germ line.

In a sexual animal crossing over mixes the paired maternal and paternal versions. If sister copies pair instead, crossing over swaps segments between identical copies and changes nothing, and the two divisions bring the doubled set back to the ordinary diploid number. The egg keeps both of the mother's different versions of each chromosome (her heterozygosity) while meiosis and crossing over still happen.

Limits: the evidence is from these Aspidoscelis species, sister pairing was shown only for chromosomes the probes could tell apart, and other parthenogens may differ.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Why a steady supply fails

In desensitization, sustained GnRH triggers adaptive changes in the gonadotrophs (the pituitary cells that make LH and FSH), in their signalling beyond the receptor as well as in the receptors themselves. Mammalian GnRH receptors lack the C-terminal tail that drives rapid desensitization in many related receptors, so this turning-down is slower. A response that merely saturated would not fall below the pulsed response at the same or a higher dose, so saturation alone is ruled out. Belchetz, Plant, Nakai, Keogh and Knobil described the failure as desensitization or down-regulation but did not test which mechanism was at work.

### The rest of the sperm route

From the epididymis, sperm pass on through the vas deferens, a duct that carries them onward; the seminal vesicles and prostate add fluid to make semen.

### How long does spermatogenesis take?

Heller and Clermont estimated that spermatogenesis in the testis takes about 64 days in men, not counting the time in the epididymis. Textbooks often give about 74 days, and the true value is still uncertain.

### Kisspeptin neurons in mice

In mice, Clarkson and colleagues (2017) recorded kisspeptin neurons in the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus; brief light-driven activation of those neurons evoked LH pulses, and silencing them suppressed the pulses.
