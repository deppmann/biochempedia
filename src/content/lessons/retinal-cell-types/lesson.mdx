summary: >-
  Ganglion cells are the retina's only output, and a mouse has more than 40 types of them. One
  idea carries the lesson: one type, one mosaic, one map. As a working picture, each type is a set
  of cells that share broadly the same filter on the scene and spread across the retina, usually in
  their own mosaic, so the optic nerve carries many overlapping maps of the scene, each filtered by
  retinal circuits before any spike leaves the eye. You watch one filter at work in the W3 cell, which fires for a small
  object moving over a still background and falls quiet when the whole scene moves, and meet a
  second in the lopsided JAM-B cell. Then the mosaic and its coverage factor become checks on
  whether a group of cells is one type, which explains why imaging, RNA sequencing, same-cell
  recording and electron microscopy gave different counts, and why the macaque fovea, dominated by
  midget cells, needs its own census. Going deeper holds the study details, the amacrine and
  layer-depth results, JAM-B labelling and colour, and density conservation.

techniques:
  - name: "Two-photon calcium imaging of the ganglion cell layer"
    blurb: >-
      A fluorescent calcium indicator reports activity in many cells at once while light stimuli
      are shown to an isolated retina. Cells are then grouped by response, which gives a
      functional census but includes displaced amacrine cells that must be sorted out.
  - name: "Droplet single-cell RNA sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      Each dissociated cell is captured in its own droplet with a barcode, so thousands of
      transcriptomes can be read and clustered. It separates cells by expression but does not by
      itself say what they compute.
  - name: "Patch-seq"
    blurb: >-
      A cell's light response is recorded with a pipette, and the same cell is then collected for RNA
      sequencing, with morphology in some cases. The response and the transcriptome belong to
      one cell, so the response and the transcriptome need no matching to each other, though placing the cell in a reference atlas still does.
  - name: "Serial block-face electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      A tissue block is imaged and a thin layer removed, over and over, giving a stack from which
      neurites can be traced. It gives exact shapes and contacts, but only for a small fixed
      piece of tissue.

practiceQuestions:
- stem: Every signal the brain receives from the eye leaves through ganglion cell axons in the optic nerve. Which statement best describes what those axons carry?
  choices:
  - One copy of the photoreceptor image, which the brain then splits into features such as motion and colour
  - Two channels only, ON and OFF, because every ganglion cell type is defined by the sign of its response
  - A single population code in which every ganglion cell reports every feature of the scene with equal weight
  - Dozens of parallel channels, each reporting the scene through its own filter, built by retinal circuits before any spike leaves
  answer: 3
  rationale: The optic nerve is not a camera cable. Distinct ganglion cell types each report the scene through their own filter, such as local change (W3) or a preferred direction of motion (JAM-B), and each filter is built by bipolar and amacrine circuits inside the retina. The cells of each type spread across the retina, usually in their own mosaic, so each channel is roughly a map of the visual field. The brain does not receive one raw image to split later, and information that no channel carries is gone before the brain has a chance. ON and OFF is only one way the types differ; W3 even pools excitation from both pathways. Different types respond to different features, so the cells do not all report everything equally, although the brain can build new features by combining channels.
- stem: In isolated mouse retina, a W3 cell fires when a small object moves against a still background but falls quiet when the whole scene moves. Which circuit property explains the silence?
  choices:
  - The cell receives input from the OFF pathway only, so whole-scene motion cannot excite it
  - Photoreceptors stop signalling during whole-scene motion, so no input reaches the bipolar cells
  - Motion in the distant surround recruits presynaptic suppression of its excitation and postsynaptic inhibition
  - Its dendritic arbor is too small to receive input from the bipolar cells under a moving scene
  answer: 2
  rationale: 'Motion in the distant surround recruits presynaptic suppression of the W3 cell''s excitation and postsynaptic inhibition, so the cell is silenced when everything moves together and responds when only a small object moves. The selectivity is built by inner-retina circuits: much of the excitation arrives through the VG3 amacrine cell, and wide-field amacrine cells were proposed as the source of suppression by global motion. A W3 cell pools rectified excitation from both ON and OFF pathways, so it is not OFF-only. The same photoreceptors and bipolar cells still drive the cell when a small object moves, so a loss of photoreceptor signal does not explain the silence. Arbor size alone cannot compare centre with distant surround.'
- stem: A group of cells has been treated as one ganglion cell type. Mapped across the retina, its cells include many pairs that sit unusually close together, its spacing looks closer to random, and its coverage factor is about twice that of similar types. What is the best reading?
  choices:
  - The cells must be displaced amacrine cells, because a high coverage factor rules out ganglion cells
  - The group may hold two types, each with its own mosaic, merged into one by the analysis
  - The type must be missing from part of the retina, because a gap would raise the coverage factor
  - The cells must share one light response, because irregular spacing comes from a single response class
  answer: 1
  rationale: Each real type tends to keep its own cells apart, but cells of two independently placed types can sit right beside each other, so merging two such types usually gives short neighbour distances and less regular spacing, and the coverage factor adds up across the two. An unusually high coverage factor can be a sign of a merge, but neither sign proves one, since some real types are only weakly regular, so same-cell or genetic evidence should follow. A high coverage factor says nothing about whether the cells are amacrine cells. A gap in the retina would lower the number of arbors over each point, not raise it. Irregular spacing does not show a shared light response; a merge of two types would, if anything, predict two response classes.
- stem: Tran and colleagues' atlas of adult mouse ganglion cells began with 45 clusters and ended with 46 types. What does that change show?
  choices:
  - Adding a second mouse strain raised the count, so strain differences created the extra type
  - Splitting one cluster into two known types raised the count, so clustering alone can miss real types
  - Dropping one marker gene raised the count, so the marker was hiding an unrelated cell type
  - Injury to the optic nerve raised the count, so damaged cells formed an additional type
  answer: 1
  rationale: One of the 45 clusters held two direction-selective types already known from function, and the authors split it with a semi-supervised analysis, which gave 46 types in a P56 atlas of uninjured cells. Expression clustering alone had under-resolved two known types, so the count depends on prior knowledge as well as on the data. No second strain was added to make the extra type, the atlas profiled uninjured cells, and the change did not come from removing a marker gene.
- stem: In the Goetz data, three sustained ON direction-selective types that differ in preferred direction aligned to one RNA cluster, C10. Which design would best test whether they are three types?
  choices:
  - Re-run the clustering with a lower resolution setting until the three types merge into one cluster
  - Record each cell's response, then collect its RNA and shape, and test for a separate mosaic
  - Count the genes expressed in C10 and compare the total with that of every other cluster
  - Compare the light responses in this data set with a second imaging data set from another lab
  answer: 1
  rationale: A same-cell measurement ties response, shape and RNA to one cell, and a mosaic test asks whether each candidate type forms its own regular mosaic. Lowering resolution would only hide the question by forcing the merge. A gene count says nothing about response or mosaic. Comparing across data sets leaves the match between cells open to the same errors that made the question hard.
- stem: In Peng and colleagues' adult macaque data, about 85% of sampled foveal ganglion cells were midget clusters and 11% parasol. What can you conclude?
  choices:
  - In this foveal macaque sample, midget and parasol clusters made up about 96% of the cells
  - People have 85% midget cells in the fovea, because macaque and human foveae are close relatives
  - Mouse ganglion cell types can be renamed midget and parasol using the macaque data as a key
  - Midget cells outnumber parasol cells by 85 to 11 in every region of the macaque retina
  answer: 0
  rationale: Adding the two percentages gives about 96% of this foveal macaque sample, leaving about 4% for all other clusters. The foveal percentages were measured in macaques, and the study's human data did not measure them, so the figures cannot be quoted for humans. Some mouse types do have primate counterparts (midget and parasol cells match rare mouse alpha cells), but those cells differ in size and frequency, and foveal percentages say nothing about which mouse type is which, so mouse types cannot be renamed from these data. The percentages come from the fovea, and the periphery was profiled separately with its own clusters.

- stem: In a made-up recording from isolated mouse retina, Type A ganglion cells fire 36 spikes to a small spot moving over a still background and 5 spikes when the whole background moves with the spot. Type B cells fire about 30 spikes in both conditions. Which conclusion is best supported?
  choices:
  - Type A is faulty, because a healthy ganglion cell should respond to all motion in its view
  - Type A carries a local-motion signal that separates object motion from whole-scene motion, and Type B does not
  - Types A and B must be one type, because both cells respond to the moving spot
  - Type B must be a displaced amacrine cell, because every ganglion cell suppresses whole-scene motion
  answer: 1
  rationale: Type A falls from 36 to 5 spikes when the background moves with the spot, so global motion suppresses it, like the W3 local-change cell. Type B responds to the spot whether or not the scene moves. The brain receives both signals in parallel, and a target that read only Type B could not tell an object moving from the whole scene moving. Selectivity is the point of a channel, so it is not a fault. Sharing one response does not make two groups one type, and calling A and B separate types would also want shape, expression and a mosaic. Many ganglion cells lack this suppression, so it cannot be used to sort out displaced amacrine cells.
- stem: A laboratory records light responses from 90 mouse ganglion cells and finds three response classes. RNA from the same cells falls into one cluster at default settings. At a finer clustering resolution the cluster splits into three, and each part matches one response class. What is the best reading?
  choices:
  - The first clustering was wrong, because a cluster that splits cannot have been a real group of cells
  - The three response classes are artefacts, because RNA clusters are the definition of a type
  - Cluster number depends on resolution, and the split agrees with function and supports three types
  - Response classes can never be told apart by RNA, so the finer split must come from noise
  answer: 2
  rationale: Cluster number is set partly by sequencing depth and clustering parameters, and here the finer split agrees with an independent, same-cell functional measure, which supports three types (a mosaic test or genetic access would strengthen it). A split cluster is not necessarily wrong, since a coarse cluster can contain several types. RNA clusters do not define a type on their own. Agreement with function makes noise an unlikely source of the split.
- stem: In a made-up data set, a type of mouse ganglion cell has a regional peak density of 200 cells/mm² and a mean dendritic field area of 20,000 µm². One mm² is 1,000,000 µm². Assuming uniform density, how many dendritic arbors cover an average point (the coverage factor)?
  choices:
  - About 4,000,000 arbors, from 200 cells times 20,000 µm²
  - About 0.4 arbors, from 200 cells/mm² times 0.002 mm²
  - About 40 arbors, from 200 cells/mm² times 0.2 mm²
  - About 4 arbors, from 200 cells/mm² times 0.02 mm²
  answer: 3
  rationale: 'Convert first: 20,000 µm² is 0.02 mm², so 200 cells/mm² times 0.02 mm² is 4. The 0.4 comes from a decimal slip the other way (0.002 mm²). A coverage of 4 means arbors overlap, which fits a single type whose regularly spaced cells have overlapping dendrites; by itself it does not show a merge.'

**A hawk's shadow slides across a meadow. What does a mouse's optic nerve tell its brain about it?**

Not a picture. Every signal the brain receives from the eye leaves through ganglion cells, the retina's only output neurons, whose axons make the optic nerve. A mouse has more than 40 types of ganglion cell, and each type reports the scene through its own filter. Some filters are simple: they signal local differences in brightness. Others are highly selective: they detect a small object moving, or motion in one direction. Bipolar and amacrine circuits inside the retina build each filter before any spike leaves the eye, so [the visual cortex](/lessons/visual-cortex/) can only work with what these channels deliver. Information that no channel carries is gone before the brain has a chance, though the brain can combine channels into new features.

[The retina](/lessons/retina/) followed the signal from photoreceptor to ganglion cell and built ON and OFF centre-surround fields; [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/) weighed labelled lines against population codes. This lesson watches one filter at work, then asks how anyone knows that a group of cells is one real type.

## One cell that ignores the world moving

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Start with a single channel and watch it work. Zhang and colleagues (2012) recorded from W3 cells in isolated mouse retina, using a mouse line, TYW3, that makes these cells fluorescent. (That line was later found to label several types; the brightly labelled local-change cell is now called W3B.)

First, a spot. A W3 cell fires when a small spot over it brightens, and fires again when the same spot dims; a plain ON cell would fire only to the brightening. The W3 cell pools excitation from both the ON pathway (driven when light goes up) and the OFF pathway (driven when light goes down). Each input is "rectified": it drives the cell for its own sign of change and does little for the other. So the two inputs add rather than cancel, and a W3 cell is excited by local change of either sign.

Now motion. Picture a small textured patch moving over the cell's centre. The "distant surround" in the table is a wide region far outside that centre.

| Stimulus (illustrative spike counts, not recorded data) | Centre | Distant surround | W3 spikes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small object, still background | moving | still | many (say 40) |
| Whole scene moves together | moving | moving with it | few (say 3) |

**Predict.** The centre gets the same stimulus in both rows. If the W3 cell listened only to its centre, what would the second row show? What must the surround be doing?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It would fire just as much. It does not, so motion far outside the centre must be silencing it. Zhang and colleagues found that motion in the distant surround acts in two places at once. Part of the silencing is presynaptic: the excitation is suppressed before it reaches the W3 cell, so less of it arrives (this lesson does not pin down at which input terminals). Part is postsynaptic: inhibition lands on the W3 cell itself. Older work in other species proposed wide-field amacrine cells, whose processes spread far across the retina, as the source of suppression by global motion.

</details>

So a W3 cell answers one question: *is something small moving differently from everything else?* The whole image slides together when the eye or head itself turns, so a cell like this can tell an object's own motion from the scene sliding past. The authors proposed that W3 cells may act as alarm neurons for overhead predators. That is a hypothesis: the experiments established selectivity and circuit properties ex vivo, and no behavioural test of necessity was done.

**Where the filter is built.** Nothing in the photoreceptors tells object motion from scene motion; the circuit between them and the W3 cell builds the difference. Much of the W3 excitation does not come straight from bipolar cells. It arrives through an excitatory amacrine cell, the VG3 amacrine cell (Krishnaswamy and colleagues, 2015). Keep the two amacrine cells apart: the VG3 cell brings excitation in, and wide-field amacrine cells are the proposed brake. The scene motion that W3 ignores still travels in other channels.

### JAM-B: a lopsided cell with a preferred direction

A second channel shows a different filter, tied to the cell's shape. Kim and colleagues (2008) used a genetically marked mouse line (JAM-B-CreER) to label a class of OFF ganglion cells; the label was strongly enriched for the class but may not be pure. About 85% of labelled cells had markedly asymmetric dendritic arbors, and in isolated retina these asymmetric cells preferred movement from soma toward dendrites. Because the lens inverts the image, that direction on the retina corresponds to upward motion in the visual field, so a statement about preferred direction needs its frame. Symmetric labelled cells in ventral retina showed no clear preferred direction, so a type's filter may itself change across the retina.

W3 reports local change, JAM-B prefers one direction, and other types report contrast, colour or light level.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson: one type, one mosaic, one map.** As a working picture, a ganglion cell type is a set of cells that share broadly the same filter on the scene and spread across the retina, usually spaced out in their own mosaic, so that together they sample the visual field. The mouse optic nerve therefore carries more than 40 overlapping maps of the scene, each filtered differently.

Read it as a working picture tested on mouse ganglion cells, not a definition. Density, arbor size and even response properties change across the retina, as W3 density and JAM-B direction preference do, and the brain need not read each map as a separate labelled line, since targets can combine channels.

## Testing a type: agreement and a mosaic

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Calling W3 or JAM-B a channel assumes that its cells form one real type. Three kinds of evidence can define a ganglion cell type:

- A **functional** type is a group of cells with the same light response.
- An **anatomical** type shares a dendritic shape and the depth in the inner plexiform layer (IPL, where bipolar, amacrine and ganglion cell processes meet) at which that shape sits.
- A **molecular** type shares an expression profile. A **cluster** is different: it is the group an algorithm draws around such profiles, and it may hold one type or several.

The usual standard for calling a group a type asks for agreement among them, plus a **mosaic**: cells of the type are spaced regularly across the retina. A mosaic describes the spacing of cell bodies; the dendrites of one type can still overlap a lot.

[The sensory neuron atlas](/lessons/sensory-neuron-atlas/) met the same problem in the dorsal root ganglion, and the answer is the same here: a transcriptomic or anatomical cluster becomes a type when response, shape and expression are tied to one another and, ideally, when genetic access lets you label or manipulate it.

### Coverage: how a type samples the scene

A useful measure of how a type samples the field is the **coverage factor**, the average number of dendritic arbors that cover a retinal location. A value above 1 is normal, because the arbors of one type overlap.

> **coverage factor = cell density (cells/mm²) × mean dendritic field area (mm²)**

**Predict.** The one idea suggests a check on whether a group is one type. Suppose an RNA cluster secretly holds two types, each with its own regular mosaic placed independently of the other, and you treat the cluster as one type. What happens to its spacing? What happens to its coverage factor?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The spacing usually becomes less regular. Each type keeps its own cells apart, but nothing stops a cell of one type from sitting right beside a cell of the other, so the merged group has some very short neighbour distances. The coverage adds up: the merged density is the sum of the two densities, so the coverage factor comes out higher than either type's own, and twice as high if the two types match in density and arbor size. Bae and colleagues (2018) warned that coverage alone can mislead, but an unusually high coverage factor can be a sign that two types have been merged. Neither sign proves a merge, since some real types are only weakly regular and some have high coverage, so the next step is to look for differences in response, shape or expression.

</details>

*Worked problem, coverage.* Zhang and colleagues (2012) reported, for W3 cells in mouse retina, a peak density of 440 cells/mm² in ventral retina and a mean dendritic field area of 10,300 µm². Step 1: convert units. 10,300 µm² × 10⁻⁶ mm²/µm² = 0.0103 mm². Step 2: multiply. 440 cells/mm² × 0.0103 mm² = 4.53. About 4.5 arbors cover an average point.

**Predict.** Does a coverage of about 4.5 show that the W3 count mixed several types together?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not by itself: a value above 1 is normal for one type. The figure also pairs a regional peak density with a mean area, so treat it as an estimate. And because TYW3 labels several types, if the density counted more than one of them, the density, and so the coverage, may be overestimated for W3B.

## Four ways to count

Recall the one idea: a real type shares one filter and, usually, its own mosaic. Each census method tests a different part of that claim, by one kind of evidence, so each gives its own number.

| Method | Groups cells by | Result | Study |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two-photon calcium imaging of the ganglion cell layer | light-response profile | at least 32 functional types | Baden and colleagues, 2016 |
| Droplet single-cell RNA sequencing | gene expression | 46 types (45 clusters, one split) | Tran and colleagues, 2019 |
| Same-cell recording, with RNA or shape from subsets | spike response, checked against RNA and shape | 42 functional types | Goetz and colleagues, 2022 |
| Electron microscopy of a patch whose light responses were imaged | arbor shape and IPL depth | 47 anatomical clusters, at least 35 types | Bae and colleagues, 2018 |

Imaging sees what responds differently under its stimuli, and the imaged layer also holds displaced amacrine cells, interneurons that had to be set aside before counting. Sequencing sees what is expressed differently under its clustering settings. Same-cell recording ties a response to RNA or shape within one cell. Anatomy sees shape exactly, but only in one small patch. Bae and colleagues drew 47 anatomical clusters. The clusters that held up to internal validation, together with types already securely known, gave a lower bound of 35 types. So 47 counts clusters, and 35 is a floor on types. Two of the four results are lower bounds, and the authors read the studies as converging on about 45 types. So the four numbers are not rival answers to one question. Each reflects a method, a preparation and a resolution, and the most trustworthy types are the ones that more than one method finds.

**Predict.** Tran and colleagues' RNA clustering ([droplet single-cell RNA sequencing](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/), uninjured adult mice) gave 45 clusters. One of them held two direction-selective types already known from function, ON-OFF cells preferring ventral or dorsal motion, which no marker had separated. What does that tell you about a type count from clustering alone?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Clustering alone can miss real types. The authors split that cluster with a semi-supervised analysis, giving 46 types, so the count depends on prior knowledge as well as on the data.

</details>

The same-cell census found a second mismatch of this kind. Goetz and colleagues recorded spikes from all the cells they sampled and, in subsets, collected the same cell for RNA (a Patch-seq variant) or recovered its morphology. Because response and RNA came from one cell, the alignment showed that the map between function and RNA is not always one to one: three sustained ON direction-selective types, which differ in preferred direction, aligned with a single cluster, C10.

## Mouse is not macaque: the primate fovea

Which types a retina has, and in what shares, differs between species. The retina of humans and macaques has a fovea, which a mouse retina lacks. The fovea packs cones and midget ganglion cells densely. There each red- or green-sensitive (L or M) cone feeds its own ON and OFF midget bipolar cells, which connect one to one with ON and OFF midget ganglion cells.

**Predict.** In this private line, the centre of a midget cell's receptive field takes its signal from one cone, not from a pooled group. What does that preserve for the brain?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The fine spatial detail of the cone array. That is why midget cells support high-acuity vision. Their surround still pools neighbouring cones, and in the fovea midget cells also carry red-green colour signals. Parasol cells respond transiently and are sensitive to motion and to low contrast, functionally like the transient alpha cells of rodents.

</details>

Peng and colleagues (2019) profiled cells from the fovea and the periphery of adult macaques, and checked the identities of the midget and parasol clusters with fluorescent in situ hybridization and viral labelling of cell shape. Midget clusters accounted for about 85% of sampled foveal ganglion cells and parasol clusters for 11%. When the group compared macaque and mouse, bipolar and amacrine types matched closely, but ganglion cell types diverged, and midget and parasol cells were taken to be primate specialities. A later atlas of 17 species (Hahn and colleagues, 2023) found their mouse counterparts.

Midget cells correspond to sustained alpha cells, which make up about 2% of mouse ganglion cells, and parasol cells to transient alpha cells. One of the mouse sustained alpha cells, the sustained ON alpha cell, is the M4 cell of [melanopsin cells](/lessons/iprgcs/). ON direction-selective cells and melanopsin cells are also conserved. A shared ancestry does not mean a shared size, frequency or job.

*Worked problem, composition.* In the foveal sample, midget plus parasol is 85% + 11% = 96%, leaving about 4% for every other cluster. In a made-up sample of 5,000 foveal cells that would be 0.04 × 5,000 = 200 cells. The 4% says how much of the sample is left over, not how many types share it.

The percentages are of sampled cells from the macaque fovea; they are not whole-retina figures and not human frequencies, and a mouse type cannot be renamed as a human type because the names look alike. The mouse census is a map of a mouse, and a primate needs its own.

Where next: how the direction-selective circuits are built is the subject of [the next lesson](/lessons/direction-selectivity/), non-image-forming channels appear in [melanopsin cells](/lessons/iprgcs/), and [photoreceptor adaptation and degeneration](/lessons/photoreceptor-adaptation-and-degeneration/) asks what changes when the input fails. Developmental lessons on [spontaneous activity](/lessons/spontaneous-activity/) and [topographic maps](/lessons/topographic-maps/) cover how the output layer is wired, and [microscopy and images](/lessons/microscopy-and-images/) covers the reconstruction methods.

### Design problem

Return to the three Goetz types that share cluster C10. Does the retina send three direction channels here, or one type with several response modes? Given responses for all the cells and RNA or shape for subsets, say what each measurement can decide, and propose what to add.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with what each measurement is. A cluster is separable expression, and its number depends on sequencing depth and clustering settings. A functional type is a shared response, and a separate mosaic is independent evidence for it, so sharing one cluster does not make the three the same.

The data as given support three functional types that RNA does not separate at this resolution, as happened with two direction-selective types in the Tran atlas. Goetz and colleagues separated them by preferred direction, as is usual for direction-selective cells, and treat them as three types.

A better experiment avoids comparing data sets and extends the same-cell approach. Record the light response of many more cells in each class, then recover RNA and shape from those same cells. If the three response classes still share their expression, test whether each one has its own mosaic, with its own regular spacing and coverage. Ask for genetic access to one of the three, say a driver line that labels one class, so the class can be marked and manipulated. If a finer clustering later splits C10 into three along function, that supports three types. If the three classes together form one regular mosaic and none forms its own, that points toward one type with several response modes, though spatial tests alone cannot settle it.

</details>

## Going deeper

The study details behind the main line (who measured what, in which animals, with how many cells) and side results it leaves out.

### The three functional and molecular censuses

**Imaging.** Baden and colleagues imaged isolated ventral retina from mice aged four to eight weeks, using two-photon calcium imaging of the ganglion cell layer. The data set held 11,210 cells, not 11,210 ganglion cells: 14 groups of displaced amacrine cells were separated from the ganglion cell groups before counting. The study distinguished a minimum of 32 functional types, four of them from clusters the authors labelled uncertain, and concluded that some unusually abundant types probably hide further subdivisions. The imaging was acquired at 7.8 Hz, so it follows a response profile, not individual spikes.

**Sequencing.** In droplet sequencing each dissociated cell is captured in its own droplet with a barcode. Tran and colleagues profiled 35,699 high-quality ganglion cell transcriptomes from mice at postnatal day 56, flow-sorted from uninjured retina. Types ranged from 0.15% to 8.4% of the cells profiled, and most clusters needed a combination of two marker genes for a unique identity. These are shares of flow-sorted, dissociated cells, which need not match the true frequencies of the types in the retina.

*Worked problem, rare types.* How many cells does a 0.15% type contribute? 0.0015 × 35,699 = 53.5, so about 54 cells. The largest type, 8.4%, gives 0.084 × 35,699 = 2,998.7, about 3,000 cells. The smallest type therefore rests on roughly fifty cells, and a different depth or clustering setting could merge it or split it. A type this rare is also hard to test for a mosaic, since a small patch of retina may hold few of its cells.

**Same-cell recording.** Goetz and colleagues recorded light-evoked spikes in the cell-attached configuration from 1,859 ganglion cells in isolated retina from mice 4 to 12 weeks old. They estimated that their 42 types matched 89% of the types in the Eyewire anatomical catalogue (Bae 2018), which is a share of types, not of all ganglion cells.

### Dense reconstruction and the anatomical count

Helmstaedter and colleagues (2013) used serial block-face electron microscopy, in which the face of a tissue block is imaged and shaved away repeatedly, with manual tracing and machine-learning segmentation, to reconstruct 950 neurons and their mutual contacts in the mouse IPL. The authors cautioned that not all contacts are synapses, so a contact map proposes partners and does not prove them.

Bae and colleagues (2018) worked in one ex vivo mouse retinal patch, called e2198, in which light responses had been imaged and the same tissue then imaged by electron microscopy, so each reconstructed arbor could be set beside its own response. They extracted calcium signals for 326 cells and kept 381 reconstructed ganglion cell arbors, which formed the 47 anatomical clusters.

### Density conservation

The **density-conservation hypothesis** says that the aggregate dendritic density of a type (its total dendrite per unit area of retina) should be about uniform across the retina, even though cell density and arbor size vary across the retina. The coverage factor in the main line is a related measure that counts arbors and not total dendrite. Bae and colleagues (2018) proposed density conservation to replace the older demand that a type's arbors tile with little overlap.

### Inside the W3 circuit: amacrine diversity and layer depth

Amacrine cells are themselves diverse. Yan and colleagues (2020) sequenced 32,523 amacrine cells from mice at postnatal days 18 to 19 and reached 63 putative amacrine types, which they treated as a lower limit, so this repertoire is part of the circuit behind each channel.

Where a ganglion cell's dendrites sit in the IPL helps set which inputs it receives: in one reconstructed patch, cells arborising in the inner margin of the IPL had on average more sustained responses.

### W3 and JAM-B: labelling, history and colour

**W3 arbor size.** The mean dendritic field area in the coverage problem was 10,300 ± 500 µm² (n = 25).

**Earlier object-motion cells.** Older recordings in isolated salamander and rabbit retina (Ölveczky, Baccus and Meister, 2003) had already found a subset of ganglion cells that preferred differential motion of centre and surround, and proposed wide-field amacrine cells as the source of suppression by global motion. Zhang's group concluded that W3 corresponds to the rabbit local edge detector, which does not make every object-motion cell in every species a W3 cell.

**JAM-B labelling.** CreER is a recombinase switched on by tamoxifen, so a reporter then labels cells that expressed the marker. A few hundred ganglion cells per retina were marked, at least 90% of them expressing JAM-B.

**JAM-B colour.** Later work found that JAM-B cells also report a UV-green colour contrast.

### Where the channels go

Anterograde tracing shows that mouse ganglion cells reach more than 50 areas of the brain, but maps of which type goes to which area are still rudimentary.

### Abundance is not importance

A type's share of the census says little about its job. Melanopsin cells are sparse, yet they are crucial for circadian entrainment and the pupillary light reflex.

### The macaque sample

Peng and colleagues profiled approximately 165,000 cells from adult male macaques (*Macaca fascicularis*, aged three to nine years), and found 16 foveal and 18 peripheral ganglion cell clusters.
