    blurb: >-

  - name: "Droplet single-cell RNA sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      Each dissociated cell is captured in its own droplet with a barcode, so thousands of
      transcriptomes can be read and clustered. It separates cells by expression but does not by
      itself say what they compute.
  - name: "Patch-seq"
    blurb: >-
      A cell's light response is recorded with a pipette, and the same cell is then collected for RNA
      sequencing, with morphology in some cases. The response and the transcriptome belong to
      one cell, so the response and the transcriptome need no matching to each other, though placing the cell in a reference atlas still does.
  - name: "Serial block-face electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      A tissue block is imaged and a thin layer removed, over and over, giving a stack from which

  - Type A carries a local-motion signal that separates object motion from whole-scene motion, and Type B does not
  - Types A and B must be one type, because both cells respond to the moving spot

  answer: 1
  rationale: Type A falls from 40 to 3 spikes when the background moves with the spot, so global motion suppresses it, like the W3 local-change cell. Type B responds to the spot whether or not the scene moves. The brain receives both signals in parallel, and a target that read only Type B could not tell an object moving from the whole scene moving. Selectivity is the point of a channel, so it is not a fault. Sharing one response does not make two groups one type, and calling A and B separate types would also want shape, expression and a mosaic. Many ganglion cells lack this suppression, so it cannot be used to sort out displaced amacrine cells.

**Ganglion cells are the retina's only output, and they do not send the brain a picture.** Their axons make the optic nerve, so every signal the brain receives from the eye has passed through them. A mouse has more than 40 types of ganglion cell, and each type sends the brain a different feature of the scene. Some simply signal local differences in brightness; others detect a small object moving or motion in one direction. Each feature is built by bipolar and amacrine circuits inside the retina before any spike leaves the eye, so [the visual cortex](/lessons/visual-cortex/) can only work with what these channels deliver.

[The retina](/lessons/retina/) followed the signal from photoreceptor to ganglion cell and built ON and OFF centre-surround fields; [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/) weighed labelled lines against population codes. This lesson starts at the output layer. It looks first at two channels and at how a retinal circuit builds one of them. It then asks how anyone knows that a channel is a real cell type: censuses made by imaging, RNA sequencing, same-cell recording and electron microscopy gave different counts, which their authors read as converging on about 45 types. It ends with the primate retina, whose fovea needs its own census.

## The optic nerve carries parallel feature channels

The optic nerve is not a camera cable. It carries dozens of filtered versions of the scene, each assembled by a distinct circuit before any spike leaves the eye. Information that no channel carries is gone before the brain has a chance, though the brain can build new features by combining channels. Anterograde tracing shows that mouse ganglion cells reach more than 50 areas of the brain, but maps of which type goes to which area are still rudimentary. Two channels show what a feature is and how a circuit makes it.

**W3 cells: local change.** In a mouse line that makes W3 cells fluorescent (the brightly labelled local-change cell is now called W3B; see Going deeper), Zhang and colleagues (2012) recorded from isolated retina. A W3 cell pools rectified excitation from ON and OFF pathways, which means it is excited by local change of either sign. Motion in the distant surround recruits presynaptic suppression of that excitation and postsynaptic inhibition, so a W3 cell responds to a small object moving against a still background and falls quiet when the whole scene moves. The authors proposed that W3 cells may act as alarm neurons for overhead predators; the experiments established selectivity and circuit properties ex vivo, and no behavioural test of necessity was done.

**How the circuit builds the channel.** The feature is made inside the retina, by the bipolar and amacrine cells that feed the ganglion cell. Much of the W3 excitation reaches the cell indirectly, through an excitatory amacrine cell (the VG3 amacrine cell), and not straight from bipolar cells (Krishnaswamy and colleagues, 2015). Older recordings had already proposed wide-field amacrine cells as the source of suppression by global motion. Amacrine cells are themselves diverse, with at least 63 putative types in the mouse, so this repertoire is part of the circuit behind each channel. Where a ganglion cell's dendrites sit in the inner plexiform layer (IPL, where bipolar, amacrine and ganglion cell processes meet) helps set which inputs it receives: in one reconstructed patch, cells arborising in the inner margin of the IPL had on average more sustained responses.

**JAM-B cells: one direction.** Kim and colleagues (2008) used a genetically marked mouse line (JAM-B-CreER) to label a class of OFF ganglion cells; the label was strongly enriched for the class but may not be pure. Most labelled cells had markedly asymmetric dendritic arbors, and in isolated retina these asymmetric cells preferred movement from soma toward dendrites. The same preference is described as upward motion in the visual field. Keep the frames apart: the lens inverts the image, so what is "up" in the world runs in the opposite sense across the retina, and a statement about the preferred direction needs its frame. Later work found that JAM-B cells also report a UV-green colour contrast.

W3 reports local change, JAM-B reports one direction, and others report contrast, colour or light level. How the direction-selective circuits are built is the subject of [the next lesson](/lessons/direction-selectivity/), non-image-forming channels appear in [melanopsin cells](/lessons/iprgcs/), and [photoreceptor adaptation and degeneration](/lessons/photoreceptor-adaptation-and-degeneration/) asks what changes when the input fails. Developmental lessons on [spontaneous activity](/lessons/spontaneous-activity/) and [topographic maps](/lessons/topographic-maps/) cover how the output layer is wired, and [microscopy and images](/lessons/microscopy-and-images/) covers the reconstruction methods.

## What makes a type a type, not a cluster

Calling W3 or JAM-B a channel assumes that its cells form one real type. Three kinds of evidence can define a ganglion cell type. A **functional** type is a group of cells with the same light response. An **anatomical** type shares a dendritic shape and the depth in the IPL at which that shape sits. A **molecular** type shares an expression profile, and a **cluster** is the group an algorithm draws around such profiles. The usual standard for calling a group a type asks for agreement among them, plus a **mosaic**: cells of the type are spaced regularly across the retina. A mosaic describes the spacing of cell bodies; the dendrites of one type can still overlap a lot. Some real types are only weakly regular, so a mosaic is strong support, not a strict rule.

A cluster is separable expression, not yet a function. [The sensory neuron atlas](/lessons/sensory-neuron-atlas/) met the same problem in the dorsal root ganglion, and the answer is the same here. A transcriptomic or anatomical cluster becomes a type when response, shape and expression are tied to one another and, ideally, when genetic access lets you label or manipulate it.

## Four censuses, one convergence

Each method counts by one kind of evidence, so each gives its own number. Two of the four results are lower bounds, and the authors read the studies as converging on about 45 types.

| Method | Groups cells by | Result | Study |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two-photon calcium imaging of the ganglion cell layer | light-response profile | at least 32 functional types | Baden and colleagues, 2016 |
| Droplet single-cell RNA sequencing | gene expression | 46 types (45 clusters, one split) | Tran and colleagues, 2019 |
| Same-cell recording, with RNA or shape from subsets | spike response, checked against RNA and shape | 42 functional types | Goetz and colleagues, 2022 |

**Imaging.** Baden and colleagues used two-photon calcium imaging of the ganglion cell layer in isolated mouse retina. The layer also holds displaced amacrine cells, interneurons whose cell bodies sit among the ganglion cells, and these had to be separated from the ganglion cell groups before counting. The study distinguished a minimum of 32 functional types, four of them from clusters the authors labelled uncertain, and concluded that some unusually abundant types probably hide further subdivisions.

**Sequencing.** Tran and colleagues used [droplet single-cell RNA sequencing](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/), in which each dissociated cell is captured in its own droplet with a barcode, on ganglion cells from uninjured adult mice. There were 45 clusters. One of them held two known direction-selective types (ON-OFF cells preferring ventral or dorsal motion) that no marker had separated, and the authors split it with a semi-supervised analysis, giving 46 types. Clustering alone had missed a known difference. Rare types rest on few cells, so a different depth or clustering setting could merge or split them.

**Same-cell recording.** Goetz and colleagues recorded light-evoked spikes from ganglion cells in isolated mouse retina and sorted them into 42 functional types. In subsets they also collected the cell for RNA (a Patch-seq variant) or recovered its morphology. The same-cell alignment showed that the map between function and RNA is not always one to one: three sustained ON direction-selective types, which differ in preferred direction, aligned with a single cluster, C10.

**Anatomy.** Bae and colleagues used a mouse retinal patch in which light responses had been imaged and the same tissue then imaged by electron microscopy, so each reconstructed arbor could be set beside its own response. The ganglion cells formed 47 anatomical clusters, and the union of internally validated clusters and securely known types set a lower bound of 35 types. Forty-seven is a count of clusters, and 35 is the number they could support.

So the four numbers are not rival answers to one question. Each reflects a method, a preparation and a resolution. Imaging sees what responds differently under its stimuli, sequencing sees what is expressed differently under its clustering settings, and the most trustworthy types are the ones that more than one method finds.

## Mouse is not macaque: the primate fovea

The retina of humans and macaques has a fovea, which a mouse retina lacks. The fovea packs cones and midget ganglion cells densely. There each cone feeds its own ON and OFF midget bipolar cells, which connect one to one with ON and OFF midget ganglion cells. This private line keeps the fine spatial detail of the cone array, so midget cells support high-acuity vision. Parasol cells are motion-sensitive, functionally like the transient alpha cells of rodents.

Peng and colleagues (2019) profiled cells from the fovea and the periphery of adult macaques, and checked the identities of the midget and parasol clusters with fluorescent in situ hybridization and viral labelling of cell shape. Midget clusters accounted for about 85% of sampled foveal ganglion cells and parasol clusters for 11%. When the group compared macaque and mouse, bipolar and amacrine types matched closely, but ganglion cell types diverged, and midget and parasol cells were taken to be primate specialities. A later atlas of 17 species (Hahn and colleagues, 2023) found their mouse counterparts: midget cells correspond to sustained alpha cells, which make up about 2% of mouse ganglion cells, and parasol cells to transient alpha cells. ON direction-selective cells and melanopsin cells are also conserved. A shared ancestry does not mean a shared size, frequency or job. Abundance is not importance either: melanopsin cells are sparse, yet they are crucial for circadian entrainment and the pupillary light reflex.

*Worked problem, composition.* In the foveal sample, midget plus parasol is 85% + 11% = 96%, leaving about 4% for every other cluster. In a made-up sample of 5,000 foveal cells that would be 0.04 × 5,000 = 200 cells. The 4% says how much of the sample is left over, not how many types share it.

The percentages are of sampled cells from the macaque fovea; they are not whole-retina figures and not human frequencies, and a mouse type cannot be renamed as a human type because the names look alike. The mouse census is a map of a mouse, and a primate needs its own.

## Mosaics and coverage: how a type samples the scene

Each channel has to report its feature across the visual field, so the cells of a type are spread across the retina. A useful measure is the **coverage factor**, the average number of dendritic arbors that cover a retinal location. It equals the density of cells times the area of one arbor.

> **coverage factor = cell density (cells/mm²) × mean dendritic field area (mm²)**

The **density-conservation hypothesis** says that the aggregate dendritic density of a type (its total dendrite per unit area of retina) should be about uniform across the retina, even though cell density and arbor size vary across the retina. The coverage factor below is a related measure that counts arbors and not total dendrite. Bae and colleagues (2018) proposed density conservation to replace the older demand that a type's arbors tile with little overlap, and warned that coverage alone can mislead; an unusually high coverage factor can be a sign that two types have been merged.

*Worked problem, coverage.* Zhang and colleagues (2012) reported, for W3 cells in mouse retina, a peak density of 440 cells/mm² in ventral retina and a mean dendritic field area of 10,300 µm². Step 1: convert units. 10,300 µm² × 10⁻⁶ mm²/µm² = 0.0103 mm². Step 2: multiply. 440 cells/mm² × 0.0103 mm² = 4.53. About 4.5 arbors cover an average point. This does not by itself show that several types were mixed together: aggregate density can be conserved while individual arbors overlap. ### Design problem

In the Goetz data, three sustained ON direction-selective types, told apart by their preferred direction of motion, mapped onto one transcriptomic cluster (C10). Does the retina send three direction channels here, or one type with several response modes? Say what each measurement can decide, and propose an experiment.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with what each measurement is. A cluster is separable expression, and its number depends on sequencing depth and clustering settings. A functional type is a shared response pattern, and a separate mosaic is independent evidence for it, so sharing one cluster does not make the three the same.

The data as given support three functional types that are not separated by RNA at this resolution. Goetz and colleagues separated them by preferred direction, as is usual for direction-selective cells, and treat them as three types. RNA at this resolution simply does not split them, as happened with two direction-selective types in the Tran atlas.

A better experiment avoids comparing data sets. Record the light response of a cell, then recover its RNA and shape from that same cell. If the three response classes still share their expression, test whether each one has its own mosaic, with its own regular spacing and coverage. Ask for genetic access to one of the three, say a driver line that labels one class, so the class can be marked and manipulated. If a finer clustering later splits C10 into three along function, that supports three types. If the three classes together form one regular mosaic and none forms its own, that points toward one type with several response modes, though spatial tests alone cannot settle it.

## Going deeper

These are the study details behind the main line: who measured what, in which animals, and with how many cells.

### The three functional and molecular censuses

**Imaging.** Baden and colleagues imaged isolated ventral retina from mice aged four to eight weeks. The data set held 11,210 cells, not 11,210 ganglion cells: 14 groups of displaced amacrine cells were separated from the ganglion cell groups. Four of the 32 groups came from clusters the authors labelled uncertain. The imaging was acquired at 7.8 Hz, so it follows a response profile, not individual spikes.

**Sequencing.** Tran and colleagues profiled 35,699 high-quality ganglion cell transcriptomes from mice at postnatal day 56, flow-sorted from uninjured retina. Types ranged from 0.15% to 8.4% of the cells profiled, and most clusters needed a combination of two marker genes for a unique identity.

*Worked problem, rare types.* How many cells does a 0.15% type contribute? 0.0015 × 35,699 = 53.5, so about 54 cells. The largest type, 8.4%, gives 0.084 × 35,699 = 2,998.7, about 3,000 cells. The smallest type therefore rests on roughly fifty cells, and a different depth or clustering setting could merge it or split it.

**Same-cell recording.** Goetz and colleagues recorded in the cell-attached configuration from 1,859 ganglion cells in mice 4 to 12 weeks old. They estimated that their 42 types matched 89% of the types in the Eyewire anatomical catalogue (Bae 2018), which is a share of types, not of all ganglion cells.

### Dense reconstruction and the anatomical count

Helmstaedter and colleagues (2013) used serial block-face electron microscopy, in which the face of a tissue block is imaged and shaved away repeatedly, with manual tracing and machine-learning segmentation, to reconstruct 950 neurons and their mutual contacts in the mouse IPL. The authors cautioned that not all contacts are synapses, so a contact map proposes partners and does not prove them.

Yan and colleagues (2020) sequenced 32,523 amacrine cells from mice at postnatal days 18 to 19 and reached 63 putative amacrine types, which they treated as a lower limit.

Bae and colleagues (2018) worked in one ex vivo mouse retinal patch, called e2198. They extracted calcium signals for 326 cells and kept 381 reconstructed ganglion cell arbors, which formed the 47 anatomical clusters.

### The macaque sample

Peng and colleagues profiled approximately 165,000 cells from adult male macaques (*Macaca fascicularis*, aged three to nine years), and found 16 foveal and 18 peripheral ganglion cell clusters. The periphery has its own list of clusters, so the foveal percentages do not describe it.

### W3 and JAM-B: labelling, history and tallies

**W3 labelling.** The W3 cells were made fluorescent in the TYW3 transgenic line. Later work showed this line labels several types, and the brightly labelled local-change cell is now called W3B. The mean dendritic field area in the coverage problem was 10,300 ± 500 µm² (n = 25).

**Earlier object-motion cells.** Older recordings in isolated salamander and rabbit retina (Ölveczky, Baccus and Meister, 2003) had already found a subset of ganglion cells that preferred differential motion of centre and surround, and proposed wide-field amacrine cells as the source of suppression by global motion. Zhang's group concluded that W3 corresponds to the rabbit local edge detector, which does not make every object-motion cell in every species a W3 cell.

**JAM-B labelling.** CreER is a recombinase switched on by tamoxifen, so a reporter then labels cells that expressed the marker. A few hundred ganglion cells per retina were marked, at least 90% of them expressing JAM-B. About 85% of the labelled cells had markedly asymmetric dendritic arbors, while symmetric cells in ventral retina showed no clear preferred direction.
