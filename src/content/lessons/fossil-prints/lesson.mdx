## The big idea

Set aside about 30 minutes. Your child makes hollow prints in clay. When you trade prints, you can use ridges and veins to guess what made each one. Fossils can preserve clues about living things and the places they lived.

**Say it together** (read aloud to start):

> "A fossil is a print or part of a living thing from long ago, saved in rock. A hollow print is a mold, and a filled-in copy is a cast. Prints give us clues about what lived there and what the place was like."

In nature, mud or sand buries a shell. Long after, water can dissolve the shell and leave a hollow **mold** in rock. If plaster here—or mud and minerals in nature—fills that hollow, it forms a **cast**.

Fossils are clues to both life and place. Virginia's state fossil, a scallop called *Chesapecten jeffersonius*, is found in cliffs along rivers in eastern Virginia such as the James River. Scallops live in the sea, so these fossils show a shallow sea once covered that land millions of years ago.

<SafetyNote>
- **Supervision:** Stay with your child the whole time. An adult does all plaster work (measuring, mixing, pouring) and handles glass or sharp edges.
- **Choking.** Keep small shells, toy pieces, loose bits of clay or plaster, and any other small objects, including loose pinecone pieces, away from children under 4. **Call 911 if choking.**
- **Plaster heat:** Plaster of Paris gets warm, sometimes hot, as it hardens. Never press a hand, finger, or body part into plaster, even for a moment—bad burns happen that way. Make handprints in modelling clay instead.
- **Clay and allergies.** Skip homemade flour or salt dough. Most flour is raw and can carry *E. coli* and *Salmonella*. The CDC and FDA advise against letting children play with raw flour dough, even for crafts, because children put their hands in their mouths. Many store-bought play doughs are made with wheat flour. If anyone has a wheat allergy or dye sensitivity, check the label before using play dough. Keep clay out of mouths.
- **Plants, shells, and tools.** Pick leaves only from plants you know. Never pick poison ivy (leaves of three, let it be) or taste leaves, pinecones, berries, or mushrooms. Use empty, whole, clean shells with smooth edges. An adult checks the shells and discards cracked ones because broken shells are sharp. Use a rolling pin or a smooth plastic jar.
- **Handwashing:** Wash hands with soap and water after handling leaves, shells, clay, plaster, or sand.

<details>
<summary>More safety (optional plaster dust, emergencies, sand dig, and outdoors)</summary>

- **Plaster dust and PPE:** Plaster powder is not food, and its dust can irritate eyes and lungs; some brands list a little quartz (crystalline silica) and warn not to breathe dust. Only the adult handles powder: outdoors or at a wide-open window, wearing a NIOSH-approved dust mask, eye protection, and gloves (use latex-free gloves if anyone has a latex allergy), with children and pets well away. Mix as directed, wipe spills with a damp cloth, and keep the bag closed and out of reach. Scrape leftover plaster into the trash, not the sink, where it hardens in pipes.
- **Eye or swallowing emergencies.** If plaster gets in an eye, rinse it right away with lots of clean water for at least 15 minutes, then get medical help. If a child swallows plaster, do not make them vomit. Get medical help right away. For expert advice on either exposure, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222.
- **Sand dig:** Use natural river or beach play sand, not crushed stone, outdoors or on a sheet. Keep sand lightly damp so dry dust is not breathed in; never throw, blow, or pour it. Let it dry afterward and cover the tub so pets do not use it as a litter box.
- **Outdoors:** When gathering leaves or digging outside, stay in your own yard or a place with permission, stay away from roads, dress for sun and weather, and check for ticks afterward.

## What to do

Focus on **molds** first so hollow prints feel clear before you ever introduce a cast.

1. **Soften and flatten.** Your child kneads a ball of modelling clay about the size of an orange until soft, then flattens it to about 2 cm thick on a paper plate with a rolling pin or plastic jar. Make one disc per object.
2. **Work one practice mold.** Pick a ridged shell. Before pressing, ask: *"What do you think the clay will look like when we lift this off?"* Press firmly straight down, then lift straight up. **What they notice:** sharp ridges leave crisp grooves that dip inward. Say: *"The shell is gone, but its hollow shape stayed. That hollow print is a mold."*
3. **Press the rest.** Press leaves with thick veins, a pinecone, and toy feet with clear toes. Notice which objects leave the sharpest clues.
4. **Trade, predict, and check.** Make prints while your child looks away, then swap plates. Ask: *"What made this print? What clue tells you? Where did it live: in the sea, on land, or in a tree?"* Check each guess against the real objects. A ridged scallop suggests the sea; a leaf suggests land with plants.
5. **Make a cast (optional, adult only).** Build a clay wall about 2 cm high around one mold. Following the plaster safety notes, the adult stirs plaster into water in a disposable cup as directed until like thick pancake batter, then pours gently into the mold until full.
6. **Wait and predict.** Plaster hardens in about 30 to 60 minutes, depending on mix and room. It can get hot, so keep hands off and children back until cool. Leave overnight to dry. Before peeling the clay, ask: *"Will the plaster be hollow like the mold, or stick up like the shell?"*
7. **Compare if you made a cast.** Have the adult peel away the clay. Set the mold and cast side by side. Ask, "Which one dips in, and which one sticks up like the shell?"

- *"If you found a seashell print on top of a mountain, what would you think?"*

## Why it works

Many fossils preserve shapes like your clay print. Most living things never become fossils. They rot, get eaten, or break apart. Fossils are more likely to form when hard parts like shells, bones, or teeth are buried quickly in mud or sand. Over a very long time, the layers above press the mud into rock. Water moving through the rock can dissolve the shell and leave a hollow mold. Minerals or sediment can later fill that hollow and form a natural cast.

A small inset shows a clay disc with a hollow print and a wall of clay built up around it." caption="A mold is the hollow shape a shell leaves in clay, and a cast is a copy that fills the mold, so it sticks up like the real shell. Plaster casts are optional and an adult job." />

Fossils show what lived in a place and what the place was like. Virginia's state fossil scallop lived about 4.5 to 4.3 million years ago in a shallow sea over what is now the Coastal Plain. A drawing of a Virginia fossil scallop, printed in England in 1687, is thought to be the first published picture of a fossil from North America. The scallop is now **extinct**, meaning none are alive anywhere. Fossils of extinct animals are part of the evidence that living things have changed over long spans of time.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Skip the plaster. Make three or four prints and play "guess what made it" with familiar objects: a shell, a leaf, a pinecone, or their own fingertip. Sort the prints into "lived in water" and "lived on land."

### Ages 8 to 10
Make a fossil dig. Bury hardened casts, shells, and leaf prints in layers in a tub of play sand: sea things at the bottom, land things on top. Your child digs gently with a paintbrush and spoon and tallies each layer. Ask what the layers say about how the place changed. Keep sand lightly damp and below face height.

## If it doesn't go as planned

- **Blurry prints:** Clay may be too soft or the object slid. Let warm clay cool a few minutes, then press straight down.
- **Cracked or soft plaster:** Too much water or old, damp powder. Follow package amounts and use fresh powder next time.
- **Stuck cast.** After the cast has cooled and dried overnight, have an adult peel the clay away slowly. The adult can pick out the last bits with a blunt wooden stick.
