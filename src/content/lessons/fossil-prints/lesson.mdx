## The big idea

When your child presses a shell into soft clay and lifts it out, the shell is gone but its shape stays. That is how many fossils form. A **fossil** is the remains or the print of a living thing from long ago, saved in rock.

There are two kinds of print to make. A **mold** is the hollow shape left behind. A **cast** is a copy made when something fills the mold, like plaster in this activity or mud and minerals in nature.

Fossils are clues. A shell fossil says what animal lived there and also what the place was like. Virginia's state fossil is a scallop shell called *Chesapecten jeffersonius*. It is found in cliffs along rivers in eastern Virginia, such as the James River. Scallops live in the sea, so these fossils show that a shallow sea once covered that land, millions of years ago. Virginia's grade 3 standard asks children to use fossils this way, as evidence of what lived long ago and what the place was like.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time. An adult does all the plaster work: measuring, mixing and pouring.
- Plaster of Paris gets warm, and sometimes hot, as it hardens. Never press a hand, finger or any body part into plaster, even for a moment; people have been badly burned that way. Make handprints in modelling clay instead.
- Plaster of Paris powder is not food, and its dust can irritate eyes and lungs; some brands list a little quartz (crystalline silica) and say not to breathe the dust. Only the adult handles the powder: outdoors or at a wide-open window, wearing a NIOSH-approved dust mask, eye protection and gloves, with children and pets well away. Add the powder as the package directs, wipe spills with a damp cloth so dust is not stirred up, and keep the bag closed and out of reach of children. In a classroom, skip the plaster or have the teacher make casts with the children out of the room. Scrape leftover plaster into the trash, not the sink, where it can harden in the pipes.
- Do not make homemade flour or salt dough for this activity. Most flour is raw and can carry germs such as E. coli and Salmonella, and the CDC and FDA advise not letting children play with raw flour dough, even for crafts, because children put their hands in their mouths.
- Some store-bought play doughs contain wheat, so check the label if anyone has a wheat allergy. Keep small shells and toy pieces away from children under 4.
- Wash hands with soap and water after handling clay, plaster or sand.
- Pick leaves only from plants you know, never poison ivy (leaves of three, let it be). Use whole, clean shells with smooth edges and throw away any that are cracked, because broken shell is sharp.
- For the sand dig, use natural river or beach play sand, not crushed stone, and dig outdoors or on a sheet. Keep the sand lightly damp while you dig, because dry sand makes dust that can be breathed in; do not throw, blow or pour it. Let the sand dry afterwards, then cover the tub so pets do not use it as a litter box.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Soften the clay.** Your child squeezes and kneads the modelling clay in their hands until it is soft enough to shape.
2. **Flatten.** Your child rolls a ball of clay about the size of an orange, then presses it flat to about 2 cm thick on a paper plate. Make one for each object.
3. **Press.** Press each object firmly and straight down, then lift it straight up. Shells with ridges, leaves with thick veins and toy feet with clear toes make the sharpest prints. Each print is a mold.
4. **Trade and guess.** Make some prints while your child looks away. Then swap. Ask, "What made this print? Where did it live: in the sea, on land, or in a tree?" A ridged scallop shell suggests the sea. A leaf suggests land with plants.
5. **Make a cast (optional, adult only).** Use extra clay to build a wall about 2 cm high around the print. In a disposable cup, the adult stirs plaster into water as the package directs, until it is like thick pancake batter. Pour it gently into the mold until full.
6. **Wait.** The plaster usually hardens in about 30 to 60 minutes, depending on the mix and the room. It can get hot as it sets, so keep hands off it and children back until it has cooled. Leave it overnight to dry before you peel off the clay.
7. **Compare.** Put the mold and the cast side by side. Ask, "Which one is hollow? Which one sticks up, like the real shell?"

With a class, give each table its own set of objects and let groups trade prints and guess.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "What do you think made this print? How can you tell?"
- "I wonder where this animal lived. What clues does the print give?"
- "What do you think the cast will look like when we peel off the clay?"
- "If you found a shell print on top of a mountain, what would you think?"
- "What parts of an animal would make a good fossil? What parts would rot away?"

## Why it works

Most living things never become fossils. They rot, get eaten or break apart. A fossil is more likely when hard parts, like shells, bones or teeth, are buried quickly in mud or sand. Over a very long time, the layers above press the mud into rock. Water moving through the rock can dissolve the shell and leave a hollow mold. If minerals or sediment later fill that hollow, they form a natural cast.

Fossils show what lived in a place and what the place was like. Virginia's state fossil scallop lived about 4.5 to 4.3 million years ago in a shallow sea over what is now the Coastal Plain. A drawing of a Virginia fossil scallop, printed in England in 1687, is thought to be the first published picture of a fossil from North America. The scallop is now **extinct**, which means none are alive anywhere. Fossils of extinct animals are part of the evidence that living things have changed over long spans of time.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Skip the plaster. Make three or four prints and play "guess what made it." Use objects your child knows well: a shell, a leaf, a pinecone, their own fingertip. Then sort the prints into "lived in water" and "lived on land."

### Ages 8 to 10
Make a fossil dig. Bury your hardened casts and some shells and leaf prints in layers in a tub of play sand: sea things at the bottom, land things on top. Your child digs with a paintbrush and spoon and keeps a tally of what was in each layer. Ask what the layers say about how the place changed. Brush gently, keep the sand lightly damp, and keep it below face height.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If prints are blurry, the clay may be too soft or the object slid. Let warm clay cool for a few minutes, and press straight down. If the plaster cracks or stays soft, it had too much water, so follow the package amounts next time. If the cast sticks, peel the clay away slowly and pick out the last bits with a wooden stick.
