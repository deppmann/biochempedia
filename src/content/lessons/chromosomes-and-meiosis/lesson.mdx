      took up Thomas Montgomery's view that one member of each pair comes from each parent, and argued in 1903 that the separation of these pairs, each
      pair orienting independently of the others, would produce Mendel's ratios.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Sutton, Biol. Bull. 4:24–39 (1902); Sutton, \"The chromosomes in heredity\", Biol. Bull. 4:231–251 (1903)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Sutton studied the germ cells of Brachystola magna, a large grasshopper whose chromosomes
      differ enough in size to be told apart. In a 1902 paper in the Biological Bulletin he
      described the chromosome set in detail and noted that the chromosomes fall into pairs whose
      members match in size. Following Thomas Montgomery, he interpreted pairing at synapsis as one member of each size class
      from the father meeting its partner from the mother. By then he was a graduate student with
      E. B. Wilson at Columbia University. In "The chromosomes in heredity", printed in the same
      journal in 1903, he asked what that picture predicts. If each pair separates at the
      reduction division, and each pair orients without regard to the others, a gamete receives a
      mixture of maternal and paternal chromosomes. For a model animal with four pairs he counted
      16 possible kinds of gamete and 256 combinations in the offspring. He stated plainly that in
      his pure-bred material he could not tell a maternal chromosome from a paternal one. The paper
      turned Mendel's abstract factors into a set of predictions that a microscope could test.
  - name: "Theodor Boveri"
    contribution: >-
      Used sea-urchin eggs fertilized by two sperm to show that normal development needs the right
      combination of chromosomes, not merely the right number, so that individual chromosomes
      differ in what they carry.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Boveri, Verh. Phys.-Med. Ges. Würzburg N.F. 35:67–90 (1902), English translation by S. Gluecksohn-Waelsch (1964)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Boveri, a zoologist at Würzburg, had already studied the chromosomes of the roundworm
      Ascaris and argued that chromosomes keep their individual identity from one division to the
      next. In 1902 he turned to an accident of fertilization. When two sperm enter a sea-urchin
      egg, the egg can form a spindle with three or four poles, and its chromosomes are shared out
      unevenly among the first cells. Boveri compared such eggs, and the cells isolated from them,
      with normal eggs. As a rule, cells from the same abnormal egg developed differently from one
      another, and most such embryos developed abnormally. He concluded that what mattered was
      which chromosomes a cell received, not just how many. That meant each chromosome carries
      something the others lack, which is what the chromosome theory needs. Sutton and Boveri are
      generally given shared credit for the theory, and the joined name for it came later. The
      work also led Boveri to suggest that abnormal chromosome sets might underlie the growth of
      tumours.
  - name: "Eleanor Carothers"
    contribution: >-
      Followed a visibly unequal pair of homologs and an unpaired X chromosome through meiosis in
      grasshopper males, and found that the X went with either member of the pair about equally
      often, a direct cytological test of independent assortment.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Carothers, \"The Mendelian ratio in relation to certain orthopteran chromosomes\", J. Morphol. 24:487–511 (1913)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Sutton's theory had a weak point that he named himself: he could not tell the two members of
      a pair apart, so he could not watch them assort. Carothers, a student of the cytologist
      Clarence McClung, found grasshoppers in which the two members of a pair could be told apart. In some individuals one homolog pair
      was heteromorphic, meaning that one member was visibly larger than the other. In males the
      X chromosome has no partner, so at the first meiotic division it goes to one pole or the
      other. That gave her two markers she could see. If chromosome pairs orient independently,
      the X should go with the larger homolog in about half of the cells and with the smaller in
      the other half. In her 1913 paper in the Journal of Morphology she reported that it did. She
      continued to study heteromorphic homologs in other grasshoppers in later papers. Her counts
      made independent assortment of non-homologous chromosomes something seen in cells, rather
      than inferred from the ratios of crosses.

      Testes or ovaries are fixed, sectioned or squashed, and stained so that chromosomes can be
      counted and their shapes compared at each stage of meiosis. Sutton and Carothers drew
      their conclusions from preparations of this kind; Boveri used such preparations too, alongside watching living sea-urchin embryos develop.
  - name: "Heteromorphic marker chromosomes"
    blurb: >-
      A chromosome pair whose two members differ visibly, in size or in a knob at one end, lets
      you tell the homologs apart under the microscope. You can then follow each member into the
      daughter cells and ask whether its behaviour matches the inheritance of genes.
  - name: "Chromosome spreads for counting"
    blurb: >-
      Dividing cells are arrested in metaphase, swollen in a dilute (hypotonic) solution so that
      their chromosomes separate, and spread on a slide for counting. Applied to cultured human
      cells, this approach gave the human diploid number of 46 reported by Tjio and Levan in 1956.

**A fruit fly cell holds eight chromosomes. Its eggs and sperm hold four, one from each of four pairs. Which four, crossing over aside, is settled at one moment of one division.**

In [the previous lesson](/lessons/mendels-peas/) you met Mendel's two laws: the two alleles of a gene separate into different gametes, and alleles of different genes enter gametes independently. Mendel inferred both from counts of peas but never saw what did the separating. The first film, at the top of this page, showed it: **meiosis**, the cell division that makes eggs and sperm, dealing out chromosomes that come in pairs, one member of each pair, a **homolog**, from each parent. What the film left out: the counts exams ask for, the egg's lopsided division, what crossing over changes, and how the idea was proved.

> **The one idea.** In a normal meiosis, meiosis I deals one homolog of every pair to each daughter cell, and each pair is dealt by its own fair coin toss. Meiosis II then splits sisters, the two copies made of each chromosome before meiosis began. The deal gives the law of segregation: each gamete gets one allele of each gene, so an Aa cell gives two A and two a. The coin tosses give independent assortment, which shows only over many meioses.
>
> *Scope:* a cell with two sets of chromosomes, and genes on different chromosomes. An ordinary crossover can change when A and a part, not the two-and-two count; you will predict it below. It applies wherever the rule recurs. Exceptions: gene conversion, which can give three A and one a, and meiotic drive (both in Going deeper), errors that leave gametes with too many or too few chromosomes, and genes close together on one chromosome, which tend to travel together ([linkage and mapping](/lessons/linkage-and-mapping/)).

## Count before you divide: homologs, sisters and ploidy

The first film counted centromeres; here are the terms behind that count, plus one it left out, DNA content. Chromosomes live in the nucleus, the membrane-bound compartment that holds a eukaryotic cell's DNA ([cell compartments](/lessons/intro-biochemistry-cell-biology/) has more).

Think of a pair of shoes. The left and right shoes are homologs: same job, built separately, one from each parent. Now photocopy each shoe and staple the copy to it. Each stapled bundle is one chromosome made of two sister chromatids, joined at the **centromere**. The rule that saves most students on exams: **count centromeres, not arms.** A replicated chromosome is still one chromosome.

The four terms:

- **Homologous chromosomes (homologs)** are the left and right shoe: the two members of a pair, one inherited from each parent. They carry the same genes at the same loci (a locus is a gene's place on the chromosome), but can carry different alleles.
- **Sister chromatids** are a shoe and its stapled copy: the two identical copies of one chromosome made in S phase, the stage before division when DNA is copied, still joined at the centromere.
- **Ploidy** is the number of complete chromosome sets in a cell. Count centromeres to get the chromosome number, then compare it with one set: a fly cell with 8 chromosomes holds two sets of 4 and is **diploid** (2n); a cell with 4, one set, is **haploid** (n).
- The **C value** counts DNA instead of centromeres. One unreplicated haploid set, one shoe of each pair with nothing stapled on, is 1C. A diploid cell is 2C in G1, the growth stage before S phase, and 4C after S phase.

Ploidy counts sets of chromosomes; C counts DNA. They can disagree: after S phase a fly cell holds 16 chromatids and 4C of DNA, and is still diploid.

## Meiosis I separates homologs; meiosis II separates sisters

Meiosis is one round of DNA replication followed by two divisions.

**Before meiosis: S phase.** Every chromosome is copied into two sister chromatids. The chromosome number does not change; chromatids and DNA double.

**Meiosis I: homologs pair, then part.** In **prophase I**, early in the first division, each chromosome finds its homolog and the two line up side by side along their length. That pairing is **synapsis**, and a synapsed pair is a **bivalent**: two homologs, four chromatids (exams also call it a **tetrad**). While paired, homologs can exchange segments between **non-sister chromatids** (chromatids of different homologs), which is **crossing over**; it has its own section after the second film. At **metaphase I** the bivalents sit across the middle of the cell on the **spindle**, the array of protein fibres that pulls chromosomes to the poles, with the two homologs facing opposite poles. At **anaphase I** the homologs are pulled apart, and each daughter cell receives one member of every pair.

**Meiosis II: sisters part.** No DNA is copied in between. Each cell lines its chromosomes up again, and **anaphase II** pulls the sister chromatids apart, much as a mitosis would.

Mitosis differs at the very first step: homologs do not synapse or form bivalents. Each chromosome lines up alone and its sisters part, so both daughter cells keep the parent's two sets. Meiosis halves the sets and shuffles them.

**Worked problem.** *Drosophila melanogaster* has 2n = 8, so n = 4. Assume normal divisions and follow one cell. The same pattern holds for any diploid.

| Prophase I, homologs paired | 8 | 16 | 4 | 4C |

**Step 1.** S phase doubles chromatids and DNA but not chromosomes: 8 chromosomes, 8 × 2 = 16 chromatids, 4C.
**Step 2.** Eight chromosomes in pairs make 8/2 = 4 bivalents.

**Predict.** Anaphase I sends one homolog of each pair to each pole. Fill in the next two rows, for one cell after meiosis I and after meiosis II: chromosomes, chromatids, C value. Is the cell after meiosis I haploid or diploid, and does its DNA content match any cell you have already met?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

| Stage | Chromosomes | Chromatids | Bivalents | C value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Each cell after meiosis I | 4 | 8 | 0 | 2C |
| Each cell after meiosis II | 4 | 4 | 0 | 1C |

Four centromeres, one from each pair: the cell is **haploid**, one set. Each chromosome still has two chromatids, so its DNA content is 2C, the same as a diploid cell in G1. Ploidy and C disagree again. Anaphase II then splits the sisters: 4 chromosomes, 4 chromatids, 1C.

</details>

The commonest exam error is to say that meiosis I separates sisters. If you call a cell after meiosis I diploid, count its centromeres again.

(Male fruit flies pair their homologs in an unusual way; see Going deeper.)

## Where Mendel's laws live in the cell

Take a fly heterozygous Aa at one locus. A sits on one homolog, a on the other. After S phase one homolog carries A and A on its sisters, the other a and a. Anaphase I separates the homologs, so A and a go to different cells; anaphase II separates identical sisters. Two of the four products carry A and two carry a. That is the law of segregation, seen as chromosome movement. Crossing over can change when A and a part (a Predict after the second film), but each final product still gets one allele.

**Predict.** Suppose a cell skipped meiosis I and ran only meiosis II. What would the products of an Aa cell carry?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Anaphase II pulls sisters apart, so each product would get one chromatid of every chromosome, from both homologs: one A chromatid and one a chromatid. Each product would be Aa, with two sets, like its parent. That is a mitosis-like division, not a reduction. Segregation needs meiosis I, the step that deals homologs apart.

</details>

Independent assortment comes from metaphase I. Each bivalent can face the spindle two ways, like a coin toss, independently of the other pairs.

**Worked problem, continued.** Ignoring crossing over, the fly's 4 bivalents give

> **2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 2^4 = 16 possible chromosome combinations in a gamete**

For humans, 2n = 46 and n = 23:

> **2^23 = 8,388,608, about 8.4 million**

That counts combinations across many meioses; because it ignores crossing over, it understates real variety.

Now put alleles on it. A fly is Aa Bb, with the two loci on different chromosomes, and no crossing over. In any one meiosis the bivalents take one orientation, so the four products are either 2 AB + 2 ab or 2 Ab + 2 aB: only **two kinds** of gamete. The two orientations are equally likely, so across many meioses each of AB, Ab, aB and ab makes up 1/4 of the gametes. Mendel's four equal classes belong to the gametes of many meioses. For loci on different chromosomes that holds with or without crossing over; what a crossover between a locus and its centromere does to one meiosis comes after the second film.

<MeiosisAlleleTracker />

Independent assortment holds only for loci on different chromosomes, or far apart on one, where frequent crossing over gives similar ratios. Loci close together on one chromosome tend to travel together: [linkage and mapping](/lessons/linkage-and-mapping/).

## Four sperm, one egg

In sperm production, one meiosis gives four haploid cells and all four become sperm. In egg production in humans and other animals, the divisions are unequal: one large egg and small polar bodies that do not become gametes. The first polar body often does not divide, and in mammals the egg finishes meiosis II only after a sperm enters.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/chromosomes-and-meiosis/sperm-and-egg-products.webp" width={1200} height={832} alt="AI-generated illustration: two flow diagrams of one meiosis. In sperm production four equal cells result and all become gametes; in egg production the divisions are unequal, giving one large egg and small polar bodies that do not become gametes." caption="One meiosis makes four sperm but only one egg: the egg keeps nearly all the cytoplasm, and the polar bodies carry off the spare chromosome sets." />

**Predict.** A woman is Aa. One of her eggs carries A. Assuming no crossover between the locus and its centromere, what did the first polar body receive?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The a homolog, as two a chromatids. Anaphase I kept the A homolog, with its two A sisters, in the egg and sent the a homolog into the first polar body. The egg's second, unequal division then put one of its two A chromatids out into another small polar body.

</details>

That wait raises a problem. In a mammalian egg the sisters must stay joined from the end of meiosis I until anaphase II, and before that the homologs had to stay joined until anaphase I. Something holds each pair, then lets go in the right order. For sisters it is a ring-shaped protein complex, **cohesin**, which links them from S phase. In yeast and mammals as studied, cohesin on the chromosome arms is cut at anaphase I, while cohesin around the centromeres is protected by a protein called **shugoshin** until anaphase II. What holds the homologs, and why cutting the arms is enough to let them go, is the second film.

## Crossing over: what holds the pair, and what it does to alleles

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The second film showed what a crossover does to the link between homologs, but not what it does to alleles.

**Predict.** Go back to the Aa Bb genotype, with A and B on different chromosomes, now in a female fly (male flies do not cross over). This time one crossover falls between A and its centromere, and none near B. At which division do A and a part? How many kinds of gamete can this one meiosis give?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

**Anaphase II.**

1. The crossover swaps the segment carrying the A locus between one chromatid of each homolog.
2. So each homolog leaves meiosis I carrying one A chromatid and one a chromatid. Its sisters are no longer identical at A.
3. A and a part at anaphase II instead. Each final product still gets one allele, and two of the four carry A.

**Four kinds, one of each.**

1. Whichever way the bivalents faced, B goes to one cell and b to the other at anaphase I.
2. Each of those cells then splits A from a at anaphase II.
3. So one meiosis gives AB, aB, Ab and ab. The pooled count over many meioses is still 1:1:1:1.

</details>

> **The one idea, again.** A crossover does not change the deal: each gamete still gets one allele, and an ordinary crossover leaves two A and two a. It can change when A and a part, and then one meiosis can give one of each of the four kinds. That the four kinds are equally common is a statement about many meioses.

The second film ran the long prophase I as one scene; cytologists divide it into substages by how the chromosomes look:

- **Leptotene:** the replicated chromosomes begin to condense into thin threads.
- **Zygotene:** homologs pair.
- **Pachytene:** homologs are fully synapsed, held by the **synaptonemal complex**, a protein structure that zips them together along their length. Crossing over is completed here, when there are four chromatids per bivalent.
- **Diplotene:** the complex comes apart, homologs pull slightly away from each other, and they stay joined at **chiasmata** (singular chiasma), the visible sites of earlier crossovers.
- **Diakinesis:** chromosomes condense further, still joined at chiasmata, as the cell moves toward metaphase I.

By metaphase I crossing over is over, and it is not part of a normal mitosis.

Two caveats on the second film. Its link, a crossover plus the cohesion beyond it holding homologs together until anaphase I so the spindle can pull them to opposite poles, holds in most organisms studied but not all (male fruit flies manage without; see Going deeper). And the molecular steps of the exchange, which begin with a deliberate break in the DNA of one chromatid, repaired by copying from a chromatid of the homolog, are taught in [DNA repair and recombination](/lessons/dna-repair-and-recombination/). The 1931 maize experiment that tied a visible exchange to genetic crossing over is in Going deeper.

## Sutton, Boveri and the proof

By 1902 cytologists knew that chromosomes pair and are halved in number before gametes form. Walter Sutton, a graduate student with E. B. Wilson at Columbia, worked on the grasshopper *Brachystola magna*, whose chromosomes differ in size. He saw that they came in matched pairs, and in 1903 argued that maternal and paternal members pair and then separate, each pair orienting independently. That predicts both of Mendel's laws. He wrote that in his material he could not tell maternal from paternal members, so the idea remained a hypothesis.

Theodor Boveri came at it from sea urchins. In eggs fertilized by two sperm, chromosomes were distributed unevenly among three or four poles, and as a rule the resulting cells developed differently and abnormally. He concluded that development needs a particular combination of chromosomes, not merely a number, so different chromosomes carry different things. Neither man proved the theory alone. Sutton and Boveri are generally credited together, and the joined name came later.

The test of Sutton's weak point came from Eleanor Carothers in 1913. In grasshopper males with one heteromorphic pair, meaning one member visibly larger, and an unpaired X, she could see which homolog the X travelled with. It went with each about equally often. The strongest proof came in 1916, when Calvin Bridges showed in *Drosophila* that rare flies with exceptional X-linked inheritance had the exceptional chromosome sets his explanation predicted, a story for [sex linkage](/lessons/sex-linkage-and-beyond/) and [chromosome changes](/lessons/chromosome-changes/).

### Design problem

You repeat Carothers' kind of study in grasshopper males with a heteromorphic pair and an unpaired X. You score 300 first meiotic divisions: the X went to the pole with the larger homolog in 154 and with the smaller in 146. These are the counts Carothers reported in 1913. Does this support independent assortment, and what would dependence look like?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Expectation.** If the X and the heteromorphic pair orient independently, the X goes with either homolog with probability 1/2: expected 150 and 150.

**Test.** Use the chi-square statistic, sum of (observed − expected)^2 / expected, taught fully in [probability and crosses](/lessons/probability-and-crosses/):

> **χ² = (154 − 150)^2/150 + (146 − 150)^2/150 = 16/150 + 16/150 = 32/150 = 0.21**

With two classes there is 1 degree of freedom, and the critical value at P = 0.05 is 3.84. 0.21 is far below it, so the counts are consistent with random, independent orientation.

**What dependence would look like.** A large excess in one direction, for example 220 to 80 (χ² = 65.3), that recurs in other males and preparations. A single small deviation is what sampling noise would produce, though it cannot rule out a small real bias.

**What this tests.** Chromosomes, by sight, not alleles: two non-homologous chromosomes behave as independent assortment predicts, which Mendel's second law needs if genes sit on chromosomes. It does not by itself show that any gene is on these chromosomes.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Male fruit flies pair without a zipper

The worked-problem fly is a fair general model, but males of *Drosophila melanogaster* are a special case: their homologs pair without a synaptonemal complex and without crossing over.

### When a crossover does not move the split

One crossover between a locus and its centromere moves the A/a split to anaphase II. Two crossovers in that interval can go either way. A two-strand double, two exchanges between the same two chromatids, swaps the segment back. A four-strand double, in which every chromatid takes part once, swaps both chromatids of each homolog. Either way the sisters match again, and A and a part at anaphase I. A three-strand double leaves the sisters different, so A and a still part at anaphase II.

### When the count is not two and two

The two-and-two count is the normal outcome, not an exceptionless law. The break repair that makes crossovers can also copy a short stretch of one homolog onto a chromatid of the other, with or without a crossover; this is gene conversion. At a locus inside such a stretch, one meiosis can give three A and one a.

### Crossover numbers, and a zipper that flows

Crossover numbers vary even within a species. In single-sperm sequencing of 31,228 gametes from 20 men, the average number of crossovers per sperm ranged from 22.2 to 28.1 between donors. And the synaptonemal complex is less rigid than the zipper image suggests: in living *C. elegans*, its components behave like a liquid crystal, ordered yet able to flow.

### Crossing over seen in maize

In maize, Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock tied the exchange of visibly marked chromosome segments to genetic crossing over in 1931; you will use that link to build maps in [linkage and mapping](/lessons/linkage-and-mapping/).

### Not always a fair coin

Fair segregation is itself a model with exceptions: in hybrid mouse oocytes, spindle asymmetry lets stronger centromeres orient preferentially toward the egg side of the first division.

### Forty-six, not forty-eight

Even the human count needed work. The accepted diploid number was 48 until Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan reported 46 in 1956.
