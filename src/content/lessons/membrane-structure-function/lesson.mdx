  - stem: "Phospholipids form a bilayer spontaneously in water, with no ATP required. The thermodynamic driving force is best described as:"
    choices:
      - "an increase in entropy as ordered water around the tails is released"
      - "the formation of covalent bonds between adjacent lipid tails"
      - "a decrease in the enthalpy of the lipid heads as they bond to one another"
      - "the active pumping of lipids into position by membrane proteins"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Self-assembly is driven by the hydrophobic effect, which is entropic: clustering
      the nonpolar tails frees the water that had been ordered around them, raising
      entropy. No covalent bonds form between the tails — the membrane is held by
      noncovalent forces — the process is not primarily enthalpy-driven at the heads,
      and it requires no ATP or pumping, which is the whole point.
  - stem: "Indole and tryptophan share nearly the same aromatic ring, yet indole crosses a lipid bilayer far more readily than tryptophan. This comparison best illustrates that membrane permeability is governed primarily by:"
    choices:
      - "molecular weight"
      - "aromatic ring size"
      - "charge and the desolvation cost it imposes"
      - "the presence of double bonds"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Tryptophan carries charged groups that bring a large desolvation penalty (stripping the
      hydration shell from charged groups is very costly), so it is essentially blocked despite
      its near-identical ring. The pair is built to isolate charge from size: the ring is
      shared, the weight difference is too modest to explain orders of magnitude, and double bonds are not the variable
      being tested.
  - stem: "A cell membrane contains saturated lipids, cis-unsaturated lipids, and trans-unsaturated lipids. Which lipid would pack most like a fully saturated lipid and therefore raise the membrane's melting temperature?"
    choices:
      - "the cis-unsaturated lipid, because its double bond adds rigidity"
      - "the trans-unsaturated lipid, because its double bond leaves the chain straight"
      - "the cis-unsaturated lipid, because the kink helps stacking"
      - "none of them; only chain length affects packing"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A trans double bond keeps the acyl chain straight, so it stacks neatly like a
      saturated chain and raises Tm. A cis double bond introduces a roughly 30-degree
      kink that prevents tight packing and lowers Tm, so the cis options are wrong. Chain
      length matters too, but it is not the only factor, so the claim that only chain
      length affects packing is also wrong.

  - stem: "A cell has K⁺ at 4 mM outside and 140 mM inside, at 37 °C, so E_K is about −95 mV. An experimenter holds the membrane at −100 mV and K⁺ channels open. Which way does K⁺ flow?"
    choices:
      - "outward, because K⁺ is far more concentrated inside"
      - "inward, because −100 mV is more negative than E_K, so the voltage term outweighs the concentration term"
      - "no net flow, because the concentration and voltage pushes always cancel when channels are open"
      - "outward, because a negative inside pushes positive ions out"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The concentration term for moving K⁺ in is 2.58 × ln(140 / 4) = +9.17 kJ/mol, and the
      voltage term is 96.5 × (−0.100) = −9.65 kJ/mol, so ΔG for moving K⁺ in is about −0.5

      at E_K itself, not whenever channels open; and a negative inside pulls positive ions
      in, not out.

    choices:
      - "Na⁺ is too large to fit through the narrow filter"
      - "Na⁺ is repelled by a fixed negative charge unique to the filter"
      - "Na⁺ cannot contact all 8 oxygens at once, so the filter fails to offset its dehydration energy"
      - "Na⁺ binds the oxygens too tightly and becomes stuck"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Selectivity is thermodynamic, not steric. In the textbook snug-fit model, the 8 oxygens closely mimic the cage of
      water K⁺ carries, so K⁺ pays little net energy to enter; the smaller Na⁺ cannot
      reach all 8 simultaneously, so the filter does not compensate Na⁺'s larger
      dehydration cost and it is excluded. Na⁺ is smaller, not larger, ruling out a size
      barrier; the mechanism is carbonyl coordination, not a single fixed charge; and in this model Na⁺
      is excluded because the filter coordinates it poorly, not because it binds too tightly. Simulations suggest the filter's flexibility and carbonyl chemistry also contribute.

**A sodium ion is smaller than a water molecule, yet water crosses a bare lipid bilayer about ten billion times faster.**

A sodium ion is about 1.0 Å in radius. A water molecule is about 1.4 Å. If a membrane worked like a sieve, sodium would win. It loses by ten orders of magnitude, and the reason explains most of what a membrane does. In most cystic fibrosis, one chloride channel, CFTR, mostly never reaches the cell surface, and salt and water stop moving as they should.

## The membrane and its puzzles

A cell membrane is a **lipid bilayer**: two sheets of lipid molecules, back to back. Each lipid has a polar **head** that sits comfortably in water and two greasy hydrocarbon **tails** that do not. The two sheets are called **leaflets**. Their tails meet in the middle of the bilayer, and their heads face the water on both sides. Proteins sit in this sheet and do most of the membrane's work.

That simple sheet raises five puzzles.

1. **Self-assembly.** Why does the sheet build itself, with no ATP and no covalent bonds holding it together? And why does it reseal when you puncture it?
2. **The blocked ion.** Why does the small sodium ion barely cross, while the larger water molecule crosses easily?
3. **Moving ions anyway.** How do cells move ions across at all, and what decides which way they go?
4. **The picky channel.** How does a potassium channel reject sodium, the smaller ion?
5. **The missing channel.** What goes wrong in cystic fibrosis, and how do drugs repair part of it?

One idea answers puzzles 2 to 4 and frames puzzle 5. It needs one term. A solute's **electrochemical gradient** is the combined push it feels from two differences across the membrane: a difference in its concentration and, if it is charged, a difference in voltage.

Every crossing answers two separate questions: how fast, set by the toll for leaving water, and which way, set for a solute moving on its own only by its electrochemical gradient. The film above separates them: a route around the toll changes speed, while only coupled energy drives an uphill move. The text below supplies the measurements, mechanisms and cases the film skips.

## A sheet that builds itself

Phospholipids form bilayers on their own. Drop lipids in water and the tails cluster to escape it, the heads face out, and a bilayer forms. Closed vesicles form on their own as well.

The driving force is the **hydrophobic effect**, and it is entropic. Water molecules next to an oily tail are held in an ordered cage around it. When the tails huddle together, that caged water is released into disorder, and the entropy of the system rises. The energy comes from water: once the lipids are made, the cell spends no ATP to assemble them, though making the lipids and inserting proteins does cost energy.

The same force sets the sheet's shape. A bilayer always forms closed compartments with no free edges, because an edge would leave tails exposed to water. Noncovalent forces hold it together, not covalent bonds. The lipid bilayer itself is about 5 nanometers (50 Å) thick overall (about 4 nm between the phosphate groups of the two leaflets), with an oily core near 3 nm. A plasma membrane looks thicker in electron micrographs, around 7 to 8 nm, partly because the stain and its proteins and attached sugars add to its apparent width. For scale, a ribosome is about 25 nm across, so a membrane as it appears in micrographs is roughly a quarter of that.

## The toll for leaving water

Now the second puzzle. If size does not decide, something else must.

The film above shows a charged solute's water shell meeting the bilayer's oily core. Water molecules are **polar**: each has a slightly negative oxygen end and slightly positive hydrogen ends. An ion's charge orients and holds those ends in a tight shell around it. That attraction makes salts dissolve, and pulling the shell off costs energy. Because hydrocarbon tails offer no partial charges in return, this trade carries the **toll for leaving water**, also called the desolvation penalty.

An inset shows a protein pore whose walls are lined with polar groups that take the place of the water, so an ion crosses without paying the toll." caption="The toll for leaving water. A protein speeds the crossing by offering a path lined with polar groups that stand in for the lost water; a path alone changes how fast, not which way." />

How large is the toll for an ion? The energy released when an ion gains its water shell, its hydration energy, is a few hundred kilojoules per mole: roughly 300 to 400 kJ/mol for K⁺ and Na⁺, with about 70 kJ/mol more for the smaller Na⁺, whose charge is packed more tightly. The exact values depend on the convention used (Going deeper has a table), but the roughly 70 kJ/mol difference in hydration free energy does not, and only that difference matters later, at the potassium channel. For comparison, a hydrogen bond is about 20 kJ/mol. A toll that large means an ion essentially never dissolves in the oil and crosses directly. The few ions that do leak through probably take a cheaper route: they drag some water into the bilayer with them, or slip through rare, short-lived water-filled defects. Even that cheapest route is extremely slow.

To feel the gap between water and sodium, scale it in time. If a water molecule took one millisecond to cross, a sodium ion would take about four months. Chemists measure how readily a solute crosses with a **permeability coefficient**, in centimeters per second. Across the solutes a cell cares about, it spans about twelve orders of magnitude. Small gases such as O₂ and CO₂ sit at the top, at about 1 cm/s and above. Water comes next, then small polar molecules such as urea and glycerol, then glucose. Ions sit at the bottom, near 10⁻¹² cm/s for Na⁺.

A natural pair isolates the variable. Indole is the aromatic ring of tryptophan on its own. Tryptophan carries the same ring plus an amino group and a carboxyl group, which at pH 7 are both charged. Indole crosses many orders of magnitude faster. The ring is the same and the weight differs by less than twofold, so neither size nor weight can explain the gap. Each charged group on tryptophan pays the toll. The ring crosses fine; the charge is what costs.

## A separate question: keeping the sheet fluid

This is the one topic outside the guiding idea: it is about how tightly lipid tails pack, not about crossing.

A membrane has to stay fluid, and the cell tunes that fluidity with chemistry. Below a transition temperature, Tm, the tails pack into an ordered, nearly frozen **gel**. Above it they are disordered and the sheet is fluid. Tm is the midpoint of that change. Two features of the tails set it. Longer fatty-acid tails pack more snugly and raise Tm. A cis double bond puts a roughly 30-degree kink in the tail that prevents tight packing and lowers Tm. Trans fats straighten the tail back out, so they pack like a saturated fat despite being technically unsaturated.

Cholesterol is the membrane's thermostat, and it works in both directions. When the cell gets cold, its rigid four-ring body jams between lipids and stops them from packing into a gel, so the membrane does not freeze solid. When the cell warms up, the same flat rings press against the upper stretch of neighboring tails and restrain their motion, so the membrane does not get too runny. The thermostat is a teaching image: cholesterol does not hold the membrane at a set temperature, but it broadens and flattens the gel-to-fluid change, so fluidity changes less as temperature changes.

### What moves, and how fast

Membrane proteins and lipids move, and FRAP lets us watch them do it. In fluorescence recovery after photobleaching, tag the membrane with a fluorescent dye, bleach a small spot dark with a laser, and watch unbleached molecules diffuse back in. How fast the spot recovers gives the diffusion coefficient. How completely it recovers gives the mobile fraction. If recovery stalls at 50%, half the labeled molecules cannot move, often because they are anchored to the cytoskeleton.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/membrane-structure-function/frap-bleach-and-recover.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: three membrane patches show fluorescent labeling, laser bleaching of a circular spot, and recovery as unbleached molecules diffuse into the spot. Incomplete fluorescence recovery indicates a fraction that does not exchange during the observation period; attachment to the cytoskeleton is one possible explanation." />

The two kinds of motion are not equal. Lateral diffusion, within one leaflet, is fast: a lipid can travel the length of a bacterium in about a second. Transverse motion, a lipid flipping from one leaflet to the other, is glacially slow. Here the toll returns. To flip, the polar head would have to cross the oily core. Flip-flop happens at a useful rate only with dedicated enzymes: flippases move lipids to the inner leaflet, floppases to the outer, and scramblases either way.

That difference in rates is what keeps the two leaflets different. The membrane is **asymmetric**: the outer leaflet differs from the inner one. Phosphatidylserine, for instance, stays on the inside of a resting cell, because flip-flop is slow on its own and ATP-driven flippases keep returning it to the inner leaflet. When a cell dies, phosphatidylserine flips outward, and that becomes an "eat me" signal for the immune system.

## Proteins in the sheet

Most of what a membrane does, it does through protein. Most membrane proteins are **integral**, embedded in the bilayer; the rest are **peripheral**, perched on the surface. The test for telling them apart is operational. Peripheral proteins come off with high salt or a pH change. Integral proteins release only when detergent dissolves the membrane around them.

Proteins that cross the bilayer face a toll of the same kind as ions, though far smaller per group. Every peptide bond in a protein's backbone carries a polar N–H and a polar C=O. Burying those groups in oil without a partner would be costly. An **alpha-helix** solves this: its backbone groups hydrogen-bond to each other along the helix, so the backbone pays its own toll, and its hydrophobic side chains face the oil. A helix rises 1.5 Å per residue, so about 20 hydrophobic residues span the 3 nm oily core once, and multi-pass proteins string several together. A **beta-barrel** rolls a beta sheet into a tube, in which each strand hydrogen-bonds to its neighbors. Beta-barrels are found in the outer membranes of bacteria, mitochondria and chloroplasts.

Some proteins skip spanning entirely and attach by a lipid anchor: a GPI anchor on the outside, a prenyl or fatty-acyl group on the inside. The signaling protein Ras, for example, is prenylated so it can dock at the inner membrane. Other enzymes, such as aspirin's target COX-1, sit in only one leaflet.

## Which way: the electrochemical gradient

So far the toll has told us how fast things cross. Now the second question, which way. The electrochemical gradient has two parts.

The first part is concentration. A solute tends to move from where it is more concentrated to where it is less. The second part is voltage, and it applies only to charged solutes. The **membrane potential**, Vm, is the voltage inside the cell relative to outside. In a typical neuron the inside sits around 60 to 70 millivolts negative. Most cells fall between about −35 and −80 mV, and some sit far shallower: red blood cells are about −10 mV. A negative inside pulls positive ions in and pushes negative ions out.

## Moving things across: channels, carriers and paying for uphill

Charged or large solutes use transport proteins, whose mechanism and energy source are separate. The film above animates that split. A **channel** is a pore open to both sides when its gate opens. Channels pass typically millions of ions per second, up to about 10⁸ per second for the fastest potassium channels; voltage (voltage-gated channels) or a bound molecule (ligand-gated channels) gates many of them. A **carrier** uses **alternating access**: it binds cargo on one side, changes shape and releases it on the other. It is never open to both sides at once, and it is slower than a channel. Separately, the solute moves either downhill with its electrochemical gradient or uphill against it.

| | **Downhill**: speeds the move toward equilibrium | **Uphill**: against the gradient |
|---|---|---|
| **Channel** (a pore, open to both sides when its gate is open) | Potassium channels, aquaporins, CFTR | None. A pore open to both sides only lets solutes run downhill. |
| **Carrier** (alternating access, never open to both sides at once) | Uniporters, which carry one solute down its gradient | **Pumps** spend ATP (the Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase). **Coupled transporters** spend another solute's downhill run (SGLT1, the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger). |

### The pump that charges the battery

Pumps are slow, around a hundred cycles per second, but they push solutes uphill. The flagship pump is the Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase, a P-type ATPase that ejects 3 sodium ions and imports 2 potassium ions per ATP. The "P" marks its trick: it phosphorylates one of its own aspartate residues, and that phosphoaspartate intermediate drives the shape change from inward-facing to outward-facing.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/membrane-structure-function/sodium-potassium-pump-two-states.webp" width={1200} height={564} alt="AI-generated illustration: two schematic states of the Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase, with extracellular space above and cytoplasm below. The inward-facing state binds three sodium ions, and ATP transfers phosphate to a separate cytosolic aspartate site. The phosphorylated outward-facing state releases three sodium ions and binds two potassium ions from outside; potassium delivery into the cytoplasm occurs later in the cycle." caption="The Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase phosphorylates one of its own aspartates, and that phosphoaspartate intermediate drives the flip from inward-facing to outward-facing, ejecting 3 sodium ions and importing 2 potassium ions per ATP." />

Can one ATP pay for that? Use the numbers from the last section. Pushing one mole of sodium out against its gradient costs about +13.2 kJ, the reverse of the −13.2 kJ/mol for moving it in, so three cost about 39.6 kJ. Bringing one mole of potassium in costs about 2.4 kJ, so two cost about 4.8 kJ. One cycle costs about 44 kJ/mol. ATP hydrolysis under cell conditions releases roughly 50 to 57 kJ/mol. The pump fits inside one ATP, with a modest margin.

It runs constantly and burns a large fraction of your resting ATP, on the order of a quarter, and up to half in neurons, to maintain the gradients that almost everything else depends on.

### Spending the battery: coupled transport

The film above shows a sodium gradient acting as a battery. Spending one solute's downhill run to drag another uphill, with no ATP spent at that step, is **secondary active transport**. Symporters move both solutes in the same direction: SGLT1 hauls glucose into intestinal cells on the back of sodium. Antiporters move them in opposite directions: the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger uses sodium flowing in to push calcium out.

The coupling ratio matters. SGLT1 carries two sodium ions with each glucose. Two sodiums running in release about 2 × 13.2 = 26.4 kJ/mol. Glucose is uncharged, so only its concentration term counts, and 26.4 kJ/mol is enough in principle to hold glucose about 28,000-fold higher inside than outside. (26.4 / 2.58 is about 10.2, and e raised to 10.2 is about 28,000.) One sodium per glucose would allow only about 170-fold. Carrying two sodiums is what lets the transporter pull glucose so far uphill.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/membrane-structure-function/sodium-battery-sglt1.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: on the left, the sodium-potassium pump uses ATP to push three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in, leaving sodium crowded outside the cell. In animal cells, these sodium-coupled transporters depend on the gradient the Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase builds. Other secondary transporters, such as those in plant cells and lysosomes, spend proton gradients made by proton pumps.

Two drugs test this logic. The diabetes drugs called gliflozins block SGLT2, the kidney's sodium-glucose symporter that normally reclaims filtered glucose, so glucose spills into the urine and blood sugar falls. Digitalis, from the foxglove plant, inhibits the Na⁺-K⁺ ATPase. Sodium then backs up inside the heart cell, the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger slows because the sodium gradient it spends has shrunk, calcium accumulates, and each heartbeat hits harder. It is a treatment for failing hearts with a narrow therapeutic window, since too much triggers calcium-overload arrhythmias.

## Lowering the toll exactly: the potassium channel filter

Now the fourth puzzle. A potassium channel passes K⁺ more than a thousand times more readily than Na⁺, and Na⁺ is the smaller ion.

The part of the channel that chooses is the **selectivity filter**, the narrowest stretch of the pore. It is a short tube lined mostly with backbone carbonyl oxygens, turned inward by the signature sequence Thr-Val-Gly-Tyr-Gly (TVGYG). Along the tube sit four binding sites in a row, called S1 to S4. Each site is a cage of eight oxygens; in the innermost site, S4, four of them come from threonine side chains.

**Predict.** Na⁺ is smaller than K⁺. If the filter were a sieve, which ion would pass more easily? Since K⁺ wins instead, what must the filter be doing? Use the toll.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A sieve would pass Na⁺, so the filter is not a sieve. It works on the toll. A K⁺ ion entering the filter sheds its water, and the eight oxygens of a site take the water's place closely, so K⁺ pays little net energy to enter. In the classic snug-fit picture, Na⁺ is too small to touch all eight oxygens at once. It cannot be paid back the energy it spent shedding its own water, so it stalls. The filter repays nearly all of K⁺'s toll and falls short on Na⁺'s.

</details>

How exact must the fit be? Less exact than it sounds. A preference of 1000 to 1 corresponds to a free-energy difference of RT × ln(1000), only about 17.8 kJ/mol at 37 °C. The hydration energies of Na⁺ and K⁺ differ by about 70 kJ/mol. So the filter does not have to be a perfect stand-in for water. It can even give Na⁺ more energy back than K⁺, as much as about 52 kJ/mol more, and still pass K⁺ a thousand times better, because that falls about 18 kJ/mol short of Na⁺'s extra 70. Small differences in a huge toll are enough. Simulations refine the snug-fit picture: they suggest the oxygens do give Na⁺ more energy than K⁺, just not enough more. The filter's carbonyls are flexible, and their number and electrical properties matter as much as the fit, a point still being worked out.

That raises one more puzzle. If the filter binds K⁺ well enough to be that selective, why does K⁺ ever leave? The answer is probably crowding. Several K⁺ ions sit in the filter at once, and like charges repel. When a second ion enters, the repulsion between the ions lowers the barrier for the leading ion to leave. This is the **knock-on** mechanism. A Newton's cradle is a fair image of it, but only an image: the energy is not passed along like a collision. Whether water molecules separate the ions in the filter is still argued, though recent evidence favors direct ion-to-ion contact. The point to keep: repulsion among the crowded ions lifts each ion out of the deep well it would sit in if it were alone, so K⁺ leaves fast. K⁺ moves at near the diffusion limit, as fast as ions can diffuse to the pore, around 10⁸ ions per second, selective and fast at the same time. Roderick MacKinnon solved this structure and won the 2003 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it.

Aquaporins give water its own cheap path in the same spirit, single file, while excluding protons. How voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels use the gradients from this lesson to fire a nerve impulse is the subject of the [Action Potential](/lessons/action-potential/) lesson.

## CFTR: a channel built from a pump family

Now the last puzzle. CFTR belongs to the **ABC transporter** family, and its family is mostly pumps. ABC transporters share an architecture: two transmembrane domains that form the path across, and two nucleotide-binding domains that act as the ATP-powered engine. When ATP binds, the two engine domains clamp together and flip the protein from inward-facing to outward-facing. Most family members use that flip to pump solutes; P-glycoprotein, which throws drugs out of cells, is one.

CFTR is the family's odd member. It has the same core domains plus a regulatory domain, and it works as a chloride channel. Once protein kinase A phosphorylates the regulatory domain, ATP binding gates its pore open.

CFTR uses ATP, so why is it a channel and not a pump? Apply the "which way" question. Once the pore is open, chloride flows down its own electrochemical gradient, the way it would through any channel. ATP opens the gate. It does not pay for the move.

The disease comes from a missing protein. In ΔF508, the most common mutation, one phenylalanine is deleted. The protein misfolds, and most of it is destroyed in the endoplasmic reticulum, so in most cystic fibrosis CFTR mostly fails to reach the membranes of cells lining the lungs and gut. The main defect is not a broken channel at the membrane. Most of it never arrives, and the little that does opens poorly. Everything downstream, the thick mucus, the chronic infections, the shortened life, follows from that one missing protein.

## Going deeper

### Hydration energies depend on the convention

The toll for an ion is often quoted as a single number, but tables differ, because absolute hydration energies depend on a reference convention. All values below are magnitudes, in kJ/mol.

| Quantity | K⁺ | Na⁺ | Na⁺ minus K⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydration free energy, Marcus scale | 295 | 365 | 70 |
| Hydration free energy, absolute scale (Tissandier) | 352 | 424 | 72 |
| Values often quoted in teaching (about 330 and 400), between the free-energy and enthalpy figures | 330 | 400 | 70 |

The difference on the free-energy scales is the number the filter section uses. Tabulated hydration enthalpies are a different quantity; they differ by somewhat more, about 80 kJ/mol.

### How ions actually leak through a bare bilayer

If an ion had to pay its whole toll to cross, it would never cross. The chance that a molecule has enough energy to cross a barrier is about e^(−ΔG/RT), the Boltzmann factor. For a toll of 330 to 400 kJ/mol that is 10⁻⁵⁶ to 10⁻⁶⁷. Even if the oil's slight polarizability is counted (the Born model with a dielectric constant of 2, and effective cavity radii of 1.68 Å for Na⁺ and 2.17 Å for K⁺, larger than the ionic radii in the opening), the cost of moving the ion into the core is about 201 and 156 kJ/mol, giving factors of 10⁻³⁴ and 10⁻²⁶. The observed water-to-sodium ratio of 10¹⁰ corresponds to an extra barrier for Na⁺, over and above water's own, of only about 59 kJ/mol (RT × ln 10¹⁰). So real ions must take the much cheaper water-dragging or defect route described in the main text, and which picture fits best depends on how thick the bilayer is. That route sets the measured leak.

### Liposomes as drug carriers

Liposomes, closed lipid vesicles, carry drugs. Load a water-soluble drug in the aqueous core, a fat-soluble one in the bilayer, and you have a delivery vehicle. Doxil packages the chemotherapy drug doxorubicin in liposomes and spares the heart much of its toxicity. The COVID-19 mRNA vaccines used lipid nanoparticles to protect fragile mRNA long enough to deliver it. Closed vesicles form on their own, but making small, uniform liposomes usually needs extra steps such as sonication or extrusion.

### Lipid rafts

Cholesterol and sphingolipids are thought to cluster into lipid rafts, small, short-lived patches roughly 10 to 200 nm across. Their size and roles in living cells are still debated. They may act as gathering points where signaling proteins find their partners, and HIV and influenza appear to use them to assemble and bud.

### Statins and muscle pain

The pathway that makes cholesterol may also explain why statins can cause muscle pain in some people. Statins block the mevalonate pathway, which makes not only cholesterol but also isoprenoids and coenzyme Q10 that muscle cells depend on. The mechanism is still debated.

### COX-1, aspirin and ibuprofen

The enzyme COX-1 is a monotopic membrane protein: it sits in one leaflet of the endoplasmic reticulum and nuclear membranes, without spanning the bilayer. It pulls arachidonic acid in through a hydrophobic channel to make prostaglandins. Aspirin works by irreversibly acetylating a serine (Ser530) in that substrate channel, which blocks arachidonic acid from reaching the catalytic site. Because the block is permanent, it lasts until the cell makes fresh enzyme. Platelets have no nucleus and cannot make new COX-1, so a blocked platelet stays blocked for its whole life, which is the basis of low-dose aspirin's lasting antiplatelet effect. Ibuprofen competes for the same site reversibly, so taking ibuprofen before aspirin can shield the site and blunt aspirin's antiplatelet effect; how much heart protection is lost is still unclear.

### Toxins and sensor channels

Two famous toxins, tetrodotoxin from pufferfish and saxitoxin from the dinoflagellates behind paralytic shellfish poisoning, both plug the outer mouth of voltage-gated sodium channels, so nerves cannot depolarize and the victim is paralyzed. TRP channels sense the environment: TRPV1 opens to heat and to capsaicin, and TRPM8 opens to cold and to menthol. Capsaicin activates the heat-sensing channel directly, which is why chili registers as heat and mint as cold even though nothing has changed temperature.

### From gradients to a nerve impulse

The action potential is where all of this comes together: voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels firing in sequence, the Na⁺-K⁺ pump maintaining the gradients, an inactivation gate that closes the sodium channel shortly after it opens, and a refractory period that keeps the signal traveling in one direction. The region the impulse just passed cannot fire again yet, so the signal does not run backward. The membrane, the impermeable sheet that builds itself, turns out to be the substrate on which a nerve fires.

### P-glycoprotein and multidrug resistance

P-glycoprotein is the ABC family's promiscuous pump, accepting structurally diverse drugs. Tumor cells that overexpress it can become multidrug-resistant, pumping chemotherapy out before it can work.

### How an aquaporin passes water but not protons

Aquaporins are channels that pass about 3 billion (3 × 10⁹) water molecules per second through a pore just 2.8 Angstroms wide, single file. Protons normally race through water by hopping along hydrogen bonds, but at the pore's center two asparagines, helped by the electric field of two short pore helices, reorient the water and break that chain, and a positively charged arginine at the narrowest point repels protons. The channel passes water in a torrent and still excludes the proton. In the kidney, the hormone vasopressin inserts aquaporin-2 into the collecting-duct membrane; when that regulation fails, the kidneys cannot concentrate urine and a person can pass up to about 20 liters of dilute urine a day, the condition called diabetes insipidus.

### Trikafta and the clinical picture for CFTR

The drug Trikafta, approved in 2019, combines two correctors (elexacaftor and tezacaftor) with a potentiator (ivacaftor). It is approved for people with at least one copy of ΔF508, and for some other mutations. The projection of decades of added expected survival is for people who carry two copies of ΔF508.
