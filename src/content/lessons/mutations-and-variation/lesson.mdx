## Mutation adds a new genetic difference

A **mutation** is a change in the DNA sequence, the order of the bases. Reshuffling mixes alleles the parents already have. A mutation can make a new allele. Recall from [DNA: The Code of Life](/lessons/dna-the-code-of-life/) that many genes carry the code for a protein. So a mutation can change that protein or how much of it a cell makes. Sometimes the protein works the same as before.

Causes include copying mistakes before a cell divides, radiation such as ultraviolet light from the Sun, and some chemicals.

Mutations are **random**: they happen by chance, not because a living thing needs their effect.

## In animals, location controls inheritance

As the first film showed, in people and other animals a body-cell mutation stays in that one body, but a sex-cell mutation can be passed to offspring. **Body cells** are all the cells of the body except eggs, sperm and the cells that make them, such as skin, muscle and bone cells. **Sex cells** are eggs and sperm. Bacteria and plants use other routes (see Going deeper).

## Helpful, harmful or neither: test the claim

A mutation is like a misprint in a recipe. Many misprints change little, some spoil the dish, and a few make it better, perhaps only in one kitchen. The comparison stops there: a recipe has a writer, but no one chooses a mutation.

Mutations are the source of new genetic variation. Heredity passes it from parents to offspring, so offspring resemble their parents. That variation may or may not help an individual survive and reproduce. Individuals that happen to carry a helpful variation are more likely to survive and pass it on ([Natural Selection in Action](/lessons/natural-selection-in-action/)). Over many generations, natural selection and chance act on that variation, and this helps produce the diversity of life.

<Support min={2}>**Reminder:** A mutation can help, harm or make no difference. Its effect depends on the change and often on its environment.</Support>

### Design problem

Plan a study of how much white oak leaves vary. Do leaves from the sunny side of one tree differ in length from leaves on the shady side?

**Predict 5.** Before collecting data, can you know whether the sunny-side leaves will be longer or shorter?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The environment may affect growth, but measurements must show the direction.

</details>

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Are oak leaves from the sunny side of a tree longer or shorter than leaves from the shady side?
- **Independent variable:** the side of the tree, sunny or shady.
- **Dependent variable:** leaf length in centimetres, measured from where the flat part of the leaf meets the stalk to the tip.
- **Constants:** one tree, leaves collected on the same day, the same ruler and the same start and end points.
- **Control:** comparing the two sides of the same tree, so the tree's genes are the same.
- **Repeated trials:** pick 20 leaves from low branches on each side, then repeat with a second tree.
- **Safety:** pick leaves only from branches you can reach from the ground, wash hands afterwards, and never climb the tree.

If one side's leaves are clearly longer, the environment affects leaf length. The spread within each side shows variation even with the same genes.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

Leaves on one oak share nearly the same inherited DNA, so their differences come from growth conditions and chance during growth. Height is shaped by both inheritance and environment. New genetic variation starts with mutations, which arise by chance. Many make little difference, some harm and a few help, and in animals only those in sex cells can reach offspring.

## Going deeper

### Other inheritance routes

The body-cell and sex-cell rule is for people and other animals that reproduce with eggs and sperm. Some animals can also make young by budding or from a broken-off piece, and then a body-cell change can reach the young. A bacterium can pass a DNA change to descendant cells when it splits. In a plant, a change near a growing tip can reach cells that later make flowers.

### Where the height numbers come from

The heights come from the CDC 2000 growth charts (file statage.csv). The CDC built them mostly from U.S. surveys taken from 1963 to 1994.
