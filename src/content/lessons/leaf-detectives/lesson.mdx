## The big idea

Hand a child a pile of leaves and they start sorting: big ones, red ones, pointy ones. This activity turns that habit into the way scientists sort living things, one feature at a time, using features that stay the same.

Leaves differ in shape and in their edges, which can be smooth, toothed or prickly. Some have **lobes**, parts that stick out like fingers. Some are one piece, and some are made of many small **leaflets** on one stalk. These features help name a tree. Colour is a poor feature, because the same leaf can be green in summer and red or yellow in fall.

A **dichotomous key** is a list of yes-or-no questions. Each answer splits the group in two until one leaf is left. Virginia's grade 1 standard asks children to sort plants by their features, grade 4 asks them to use a simple key, and kindergarten connects leaves to the change of seasons.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child outdoors, within sight at all times and within arm's reach near roads, parking lots and water.
- Pick up fallen leaves only, from lawns, paths and places you have permission to use. Don't pull leaves off shrubs or vines.
- Don't dig into leaf piles or reach under logs, rocks or brush. Snakes can rest there, and Virginia has venomous copperheads and timber rattlesnakes, so look at any snake from several feet away and never touch it. Wet, rotting leaves hold mould too: pick up dry leaves, and a child with asthma or a mould allergy should skip leaf piles or let someone else collect.
- Poison ivy grows across Virginia, as a low plant or a hairy vine climbing trees. Its leaves come in groups of three and turn red or orange in fall, and even dead, fallen leaves can cause a rash. Poison oak, less common in Virginia than poison ivy, also has leaves of three. Do not touch any leaf made of three leaflets: leave it on the ground, and if you are not sure what a leaf is, leave it. If skin touches one, wash with soap and cool water soon, and wash the clothes too.
- American holly leaves have sharp spines, and sweetgum balls are prickly; pick them up by the stem. Holly berries, wild berries and mushrooms are not for eating. If a child is choking or struggling to breathe, call 911. If a child swallows any berry, mushroom or plant part, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 right away.
- Wash hands with soap and water after collecting. Stay on paths and out of tall grass and thick leaf litter, where ticks wait. Afterward check skin, hair and clothes for ticks, and shower soon, within two hours if you can.
- Keep crayon pieces away from children under 4, who can choke on them.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Collect.** On a 10 to 15-minute walk, pick up 8 to 12 fallen leaves that look different from each other. Choose whole, fairly flat leaves. Add a bundle of pine needles if you find one. With a class, give each group of four a bag and ask for at least six kinds.
2. **Look closely.** Lay the leaves out. Run a finger along each edge. Look at the veins, the stalk and the tip. Use the magnifying glass if you have one.
3. **First sort.** Ask your child to split the leaves into two groups any way they like. Then ask, "What was your rule?" Write it down in their words.
4. **One feature at a time.** Sort again, using one feature each round: needle or flat? Smooth edge or toothed? Lobes or no lobes? One piece or many leaflets? Before each round, ask your child to guess which group will be bigger.
5. **Test colour.** Ask, "Would colour make a good rule?" Find two leaves from the same tree in different colours if you can. A red maple leaf can be green, red, orange or yellow depending on the date.
6. **Rubbings.** Put a leaf vein side up under thin paper. Rub the side of a peeled crayon over it. The veins and edges show up. Make rubbings of three very different leaves.
7. **Build a key.** Together, write yes-or-no questions that lead to each of your leaves. The example key below works for only six kinds of tree: pine, American holly, tulip poplar, white or red oak, sweetgum and red maple. Other leaves will not fit, such as the smooth, unlobed leaf of a willow oak.
   - Is it a needle? Yes: pine. Count the needles in a bundle: 2, 3 or 5 point to different pines. No: next question.
   - Are there sharp spines along the edge? Yes: American holly. No: next question.
   - Does it have 4 lobes and a flat, squared-off tip? Yes: tulip poplar. No: next question.
   - Does it have 5 or more lobes spaced along both sides of a long middle vein, like a feather? Yes: an oak with many lobes, such as white oak or red oak. No: next question.
   - Is it a star with 5 (sometimes 7) long, even points? Yes: sweetgum. No: maple, with 3 to 5 lobes and often pale underneath.
8. **Test the key.** Give a friend or family member one leaf and the key. Does the key lead them to the right name?

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder if all the leaves from one tree look exactly alike. How could we check?"
- "Which feature do you think will split our pile closest to half and half?"
- "Is a pine needle a leaf? What does a leaf do?"
- "What do you think this leaf looked like in summer?"
- "If we found a new leaf, what question would you ask first?"
- "What do you think will show up in the rubbing?"

## Why it works

Scientists sort living things by features that are steady from one plant to the next and through the year. For leaves, these include whether the leaf is a needle or flat, simple (one piece) or **compound** (made of leaflets), lobed or not, the kind of edge, the pattern of veins, and whether leaves sit in pairs or one at a time along a twig. Size varies, so it helps only a little.

Colour changes because of the seasons. In fall, a tree stops making **chlorophyll**, the green colouring, and the green fades. Yellows and oranges that were there all along show, and some trees make new reds. A key built on colour would give a different answer in July and in October.

A dichotomous key can only choose among the kinds it was built for. Field guides, such as the Virginia Department of Forestry's guide to native trees, use longer keys of the same type to cover many more kinds; that guide covers about 80 native trees.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use six leaves that are very different. Sort by one feature at a time using two hoops on the floor: "pointy edges" and "smooth edges", then "fingers" and "no fingers". Skip the written key. Make rubbings and let your child match each rubbing to its leaf. Ask them to find two leaves that are alike in one way and different in another.

A finished leaf rubbing lies beside it." caption="A leaf rubbing. Put a leaf vein side up under thin paper and rub the side of a peeled crayon over it. The veins and edges show up." />

### Ages 8 to 10
Let your child write the key from scratch for their own leaves, with each question answerable by yes or no. Test it on someone else and fix any question that confused them. Add tree names from a field guide or app. Measure each leaf's length in centimetres and see whether size is a reliable feature for one kind of tree.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If you can only find leaves from one or two kinds of tree, use printed photos, or visit a park with labelled trees. Crumbly leaves tear during rubbings; press them flat inside a heavy book for a day first. If the key leads to a wrong answer, look at which question failed and rewrite it. Fixing a key is what scientists do too.
