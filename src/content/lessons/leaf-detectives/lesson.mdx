## The big idea

Hand a child a pile of leaves and they start sorting: big ones, red ones, pointy ones. In 30 minutes, your child practises looking closely, sorting by one rule at a time, and checking which clues stay steady from summer to fall. Older children turn those clues into a working yes-or-no key.

> **Say it together**
> "A leaf’s shape and edge give us clues about its tree. Colour changes with the seasons. Let’s look closely and sort by one clue at a time."

Leaves differ in shape and in their edges, which can be smooth, toothed or prickly. Some have **lobes**, parts that stick out like fingers. Some are one piece, and some are made of many small **leaflets** on one stalk. Colour is a poor clue, because the same leaf can be green in summer and red or yellow in fall. A **dichotomous key** is a list of yes-or-no questions that splits a group in two until only one kind of leaf is left.

<SafetyNote>
- **Outdoors and weather:** Stay with your child and keep them in sight. Stay within arm’s reach near roads, parking lots and water. Never leave a child alone near water. Dress for the weather, use a hat, shade or sunscreen, and skip walks under trees in storms or high winds.
- **Fallen leaves only:** Collect fallen leaves from lawns, paths and places you have permission to use. Don't pull leaves off shrubs or vines, and check each leaf before it goes in the bag.
- **Skip leaves of three:** Poison ivy grows across Virginia as a low plant or a hairy vine climbing trees. Its leaves have three leaflets and can turn red or orange in fall. Poison oak is less common in Virginia and also has three leaflets. Dead or fallen poison ivy is still a rash hazard. Leave any leaf with three leaflets on the ground. Leave unfamiliar leaves where they are. If skin touches suspected poison ivy or poison oak, wash with soap and cool water soon, and wash the clothes too.
- **Never taste. Choking and Poison Help.** Never taste any leaf, berry, mushroom or plant part. Keep crayon pieces and any small items you find, including leaves, leaflets, pine needles and berries, away from children under 4. Call 911 if a child is choking or struggling to breathe. If a child swallows a berry, mushroom or plant part, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222 right away.
- **Glass, sharp edges and sun heat:** An adult handles any glass magnifying glass (or use plastic), scissors for cutting string, and spiny American holly leaves or prickly sweetgum balls (pick up by the stem). Never look at the sun through a magnifying glass or hold it in sunshine over dry leaves or skin—it can burn skin or start a fire.
- **Wash hands:** Wash hands with soap and water after collecting.

<details>
<summary>More safety: snakes, stings, ticks and mould</summary>

- **Snakes and leaf piles:** Don’t dig into leaf piles or reach under logs, rocks or brush. Snakes can rest there. Virginia has venomous copperheads and timber rattlesnakes. Look at any snake from several feet away and never touch it. Leave insects where you find them. If you find one on a leaf you picked up, set the leaf back where you found it.
- **Mould and asthma:** Wet, rotting leaves hold mould. Pick dry leaves; a child with asthma or a mould allergy should skip leaf piles or let someone else collect.
- **Bees and wasps:** Watch for bees and wasps. Yellow jackets nest in the ground or under leaf litter in fall; move away calmly if insects fly in and out of a hole. Bring an epinephrine auto-injector for sting allergies, and call 911 for trouble breathing or face swelling after a sting.
- **Ticks:** Stay on paths and out of tall grass and thick leaf litter, where ticks wait. Afterward check skin, hair and clothes for ticks. Shower soon, within two hours if you can.

## What to do

1. **Collect.** On a 10 to 15-minute walk, pick up 8 to 12 different fallen leaves, checking each together first. Choose whole, fairly flat leaves, plus a bundle of pine needles if found. With a class, give each group of four a bag and ask for at least six kinds.
2. **Look closely.** Spread the leaves out. Ask, *"Is this edge smooth, toothed or prickly? What do you notice on the back?"* Look at the veins, stalk and tip. Let your child feel the edge of a leaf you have checked for spines. Use a magnifier if you have one, away from direct sun. The adult handles any glass magnifier and any spiny leaves.
3. **First sort.** Lay out the two hoops or string loops. Say, *"Split these into two piles any way you like."* Then ask, *"What was your rule?"* Write it down in their words.
4. **One feature at a time.** Sort one feature per round: needle or flat; smooth edge or toothed; lobes or no lobes; one piece or many leaflets. Try one leaf together first: *"Lobes like fingers, or no lobes? Which hoop?"* Before sorting the rest, **predict, then check**: *"Which hoop do you think will hold more? Let's test it."*
5. **Test colour (predict, then check).** Hold up a bright fall leaf and ask, *"What colour was this leaf in July? Would colour give the same answer all year?"* Find two leaves from the same tree in different colours if you can. A red maple leaf can be green, red, orange or yellow depending on the date.
6. **Rubbings.** Put a leaf vein side up under thin paper and rub the side of a peeled crayon over it. Ask, *"What do you think will show up first?"* Your child sees the veins and edges pop out. Make rubbings of three very different leaves.
7. **Build a key.** Try one leaf together with the example below. It has six outcomes: pine, American holly, tulip poplar, a lobed oak such as white or red oak, sweetgum, and red maple. Other leaves may go to the wrong answer, such as the smooth, unlobed leaf of a willow oak. If your child is ready to make a key, write a set of yes-or-no questions together that leads to each of the leaves you collected.
   - Is it a needle? Yes: pine (2, 3 or 5 needles per bundle point to different pines). No: next question.
   - Is it a stiff, shiny leaf with no lobes and sharp spines along the edge? Yes: American holly. No: next question.
   - Does it have 4 lobes (sometimes 6) and a flat or notched tip? Yes: tulip poplar. No: next question.
   - Does it have 5 or more lobes spaced along both sides of a long middle vein, like a feather? Yes: an oak with many lobes, such as white oak or red oak. No: next question.
   - Is it a star with 5 (sometimes 7) long, even points? Yes: sweetgum. No: maple, with 3 to 5 lobes and often pale underneath.
8. **Test the key.** Give a friend or family member one leaf and the key. Does it lead them to the right name?
9. **Wrap up together.** Ask these two questions:
   - *"Which clue told two leaves apart best today—shape, edge or colour?"*
   - *"If we visited the same tree in July, would that clue still work?"*

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder if all the leaves from one tree look exactly alike. How could we check?"
- "Which feature do you think will split our pile closest to half and half?"
- "Is a pine needle a leaf? What does a leaf do?"
- "What do you think this leaf looked like in summer?"
- "If we found a new leaf, what question would you ask first?"
- "What do you think will show up in the rubbing?"

## Why it works

To name a living thing, scientists use features that stay steady from one plant to the next and through the year. For leaves, these include needle or flat, simple (one piece) or **compound** (made of leaflets), lobed or not, edge type, vein pattern, and whether leaves sit in pairs or one at a time along a twig. Size varies, so it helps only a little.

Colour changes with the seasons. In fall, a tree stops making **chlorophyll**, the green colouring, and the green fades. Yellows and oranges that were there all along show, and some trees make new reds. A key built on colour would give a different answer in July and in October.

A dichotomous key only chooses among the kinds it was built for. Field guides, such as the Virginia Department of Forestry's guide to native trees, use longer keys of the same type; that guide covers about 80 native trees. A key is a tool for naming, while scientists group living things into families by how they are related—so two trees with look-alike leaves, such as sweetgum and maple, can belong to different families.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use six very different leaves. Sort one feature at a time in two hoops on the floor: "pointy edges" and "smooth edges", then "fingers" and "no fingers". Skip the written key. Make rubbings and let your child match each rubbing to its leaf. Ask them to find two leaves that are alike in one way and different in another.

A finished leaf rubbing lies beside it." caption="A leaf rubbing. Put a leaf vein side up under thin paper and rub the side of a peeled crayon over it. The veins and edges show up." />

### For older children (8 to 10)
Let your child write a yes-or-no key from scratch for their own leaves. Test it on someone else and fix any question that confused them. Add tree names from a field guide or app. Measure each leaf's length in centimetres and check whether size is a steady feature for one kind of tree.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If you only find leaves from one or two kinds of tree, use printed photos or visit a park with labelled trees. Crumbly leaves tear during rubbings; pick fresher fallen leaves that still bend, and press curled ones flat inside a heavy book for a day first. If a key leads to a wrong answer, find the question that failed and rewrite it. Fixing a key is what scientists do too.
