techniques:
  - name: "Thoracic duct cannulation"
    blurb: >-
      A fine tube is placed in the thoracic duct of an anaesthetized animal and left in place, so
      lymph can be collected for hours or days. Counting the cells in it gives lymphocyte output
      per hour; in rats the duct drains the mesenteric nodes but only part of the subcutaneous
      node output (a fitted model assumes about half), so it samples part of the pool, not all of it.
  - name: "Adoptive transfer of labelled lymphocytes"
    blurb: >-
      Lymphocytes are collected, tagged with a radioactive label such as chromium-51 or a
      fluorescent dye, and injected by vein. Measuring the label in blood, lymph and each organ
      over time gives entry and exit rates, and fitting a compartment model turns them into
      residence times.
  - name: "Knockout mice and haematopoietic chimeras"
    blurb: >-
      A gene is deleted in every cell (L-selectin, CCR7) or only in blood-forming cells, by
      transplanting mutant fetal liver or marrow into irradiated hosts (S1P1). Comparing the two
      designs, or transferring mutant cells into normal mice, tells you whether a molecule is
      needed in the lymphocyte or in its surroundings.
  - name: "Frozen-section adhesion assay"
    blurb: >-
      Lymphocytes are laid over thin frozen sections of lymph node, washed, and counted where they
      stick. Stamper and Woodruff found that they stick selectively to high endothelial venules,
      which made homing a measurable binding event that antibodies could block.

**Drain the thoracic duct of a rat for a few days and the lymph flowing out of it runs short of lymphocytes.**

If lymph nodes were making those cells, drainage should not have used them up. Most were not made there; they were passing through. A naive lymphocyte (one that has not yet met the antigen it recognises) spends its life on a loop: blood, lymph node, lymph, blood again. It never settles in one place, so how many lymphocytes a node holds is a question about traffic.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Every secondary lymphoid organ is like a bathtub with the tap and the drain both running. At steady state (entry equals exit, so the count holds still), **the number of lymphocytes an organ holds = the number entering per hour × the average time each one stays.** The stay is set by the way out, not the way in: if cells leave through a single exit step at a fixed rate (for slow exits, the same small fraction of those present per minute), the average stay is 1 / that rate. Entry has its own molecular gate, and so does exit. Open or shut either gate and the cells move.
>
> **Where the rule holds.** Naive lymphocytes at steady state, with no cell division or death; it is false in the first hours after something changes. Holding an organ's entry fixed while changing it assumes it is a small part of the pool, such as one node among hundreds (last section).

One real number: in a fitted rat model, about 0.7% of the lymphocytes in the spleen leave per minute, so the average cell stays 1 / 0.007 per min = 143 minutes, about 2.4 hours.

## Lymphocytes go around a loop, not out of a factory

Lymphocytes are made in the primary lymphoid organs. In humans and mice, B cells develop in the bone marrow from blood-forming stem cells ([stem cells](/lessons/stem-cells/)), and T cells mature in the thymus from marrow-derived precursors. The secondary lymphoid organs (lymph nodes, the spleen, and mucosal tissue such as the tonsils and the Peyer's patches of the gut wall) host mature lymphocytes and are where those cells meet antigen.
In the 1950s the usual view was that the lymphocytes in lymph were newly made in the nodes upstream. James Gowans tested it in rats fitted with a chronic cannula in the thoracic duct, the large lymph vessel that empties into the veins at the base of the neck ([capillary exchange and lymph](/lessons/capillary-exchange-and-lymph/) covers how lymph forms and moves). The cannula let him collect lymph for days.

If nodes made the cells and released them once, duct output should have held roughly steady while production continued. If the same cells were going around, removing them should empty the loop. Output fell progressively.

**Predict.** Gowans then returned the collected cells into a vein. What should happen to duct output if the nodes are a factory? What if the cells go around a loop?

A factory would not care: cells put into blood have no way back into its production line, so output would stay low. A loop would refill: cells put into blood re-enter the nodes and come out in lymph again. Output recovered, and the returned cells were later found in duct lymph.

**The cells go around**, and the nodes are not the main source of duct lymphocytes in the rat. This is rat data, from a duct that samples only part of the pool (Going deeper, "What Gowans's experiment did not show").

Gowans's 1964 papers asked where the cells leave the blood. Radioactively labelled lymphocytes injected by vein into rats moved quickly into lymph nodes across the walls of high endothelial venules (HEVs), small venules lined by plump, almost cuboidal endothelial cells instead of flat ones. Gowans concluded that HEVs are the site of large-scale migration of lymphocytes from blood into the secondary organs.

## A node samples lymph and blood; the spleen samples blood alone

Why tour at all? Only a tiny fraction of T cells, of the order of one naive T cell in a hundred thousand to a million, can recognise any one antigen, so the antigen has to be brought to a place they all pass through. Dendritic cells carry it there: they pick up antigen in skin or gut and travel in afferent lymph (lymph flowing into the node) to its T-cell zone, a trip that needs the chemokine receptor CCR7 (in CCR7-deficient mice, activated skin dendritic cells fail to reach the nodes). So the lymphocytes touring the node have something to find.

A lymph node is a bean-shaped filter on a lymph vessel. Afferent lymphatics enter its convex surface and empty into the subcapsular sinus, a space under the capsule. Inside lie B-cell follicles in the outer cortex, a T-cell zone (the paracortex) where the HEVs sit, and medullary cords and sinuses. One efferent lymphatic, sometimes two, leaves at the hilum, beside the artery and vein.

Think of the node as a hotel with two entrances, afferent lymph from tissue and HEVs from blood, and one exit, the efferent lymphatic. The blood entrance is much the busier. At rest, afferent lymph has only about 5 to 10 percent of the cell concentration of efferent lymph, and cannulation studies in large animals found that more than 90 percent of the lymphocytes in efferent lymph of resting nodes had entered through HEVs.

The hotel also carries the guiding idea: 100 check-ins a night, each staying 3 nights, fills 300 rooms.

The spleen, the largest secondary lymphoid organ, has no afferent lymphatics and no HEVs, so it samples blood, not lymph. Arterial blood brings both antigen and lymphocytes. Its white pulp holds T-cell and B-cell zones around arterioles, and its red pulp removes old red cells ([blood cells and hemostasis](/lessons/blood-cells-and-hemostasis/)).

## Getting in on chemokines, getting out on S1P

### The way in: three steps at the high endothelial venule

In a peripheral lymph node:

1. **Tether and roll.** L-selectin (CD62L) on the lymphocyte binds sulfated mucin-like glycoproteins on the HEV, which tethers a fast-moving cell and sets it rolling. L-selectin needs no signal before it binds, though its grip is stable only above a minimum shear from flowing blood.
2. **Switch on the integrin.** The integrin LFA-1 stays in a low-affinity shape until a chemokine (CCL21, CCL19 or CXCL13) signals through its receptor, CCR7 for CCL21 and CCL19. In mice the HEVs make CCL21 themselves; human HEVs carry it but do not make it.
3. **Arrest and cross.** The signal changes LFA-1's shape so it binds ICAM-1 and ICAM-2 on the endothelium, and the cell stops, then crosses the wall.

**Predict.** L-selectin is already sticky. Why would a lymphocyte need a second adhesion molecule that only grips after a chemokine signal?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

L-selectin catches and lets go, so the cell rolls but cannot stop. Requiring a chemokine signal before the firm grip means the cell commits to stopping only where that signal is presented, on the HEVs of a lymphoid organ.

</details>

### The way out: S1P1 reads the S1P in lymph

Exit was a later question. The lipid sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) is high in blood and lymph and low inside lymphoid tissue, and lymphocytes carry a G protein-coupled receptor for it, S1P1. In 2004 Mehrdad Matloubian, Charles Lo, Guy Cinamon and colleagues in Jason Cyster's laboratory asked whether lymphocytes need it to leave. Mice lacking S1P1 in every cell do not survive embryonic development, so they gave irradiated mice blood-forming cells from S1P1-deficient fetal liver. If S1P1 is needed for exit, cells should enter lymphoid organs and pile up there.

They did. Mature T cells could not leave the thymus, so no T cells reached the periphery. S1P1-deficient T and B cells transferred into normal mice could not leave lymphoid organs, so the requirement lies in the lymphocyte, not its surroundings. This is mouse work: the study gave no human values, and did not show how the S1P gradient is kept or that every subset depends on S1P1 equally.

The exit gate can also be closed from inside. The same study found that S1P1 falls when lymphocytes are activated, which goes with retention. CD69, a surface protein that appears early after activation, binds S1P1 and opposes its function, and the pair is internalized and degraded, so T cells expressing CD69 stay in the node.

## Why a cell's stay is set by the way out

With both gates in hand, ask **how long a lymphocyte stays**, starting with the spleen. A lymphocyte there has no scheduled departure. At each moment it either happens to reach a place where it can sense the S1P of blood and leave, or it does not. So the fitted model treats leaving as a matter of chance: the same *fraction* of the cells present leaves each minute, not the same *number*: the rat spleen's 0.7% per minute holds whether it contains a thousand cells or a billion. A process like that is called **first-order**, and the 0.007 per min is its **exit rate constant**. (A lymph node adds a delay before exit becomes possible, so its fit needs two such steps in series; see Going deeper.)

That one property fixes the average stay. If 10% left each hour, the average cell would stay about 10 hours; at 0.007 per min it stays the opening's 143 minutes. The entry rate is not in that calculation: how fast cells arrive has no effect on how long any one of them stays.

At steady state, as many cells leave per hour as arrive, so the two rules combine into the guiding idea:

## A draining node holds on to its visitors

A node that drains a site of immune challenge changes its traffic. Migrating dendritic cells arrive in afferent lymph and settle in the T-cell zone. More lymphocytes enter from blood through the HEVs, and for a time fewer leave: in mouse studies, lymphocytes in the node express CD69, which removes S1P1 from their surface. The node enlarges and holds more lymphocytes than its partner on the other side of the body.

**Predict.** A draining node comes to hold twice as many lymphocytes as before. Using the guiding idea, give two different ways that could happen.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Double the entry (each cell staying as long as before), or halve the exit rate constant (the same entry, each cell staying twice as long), or any mix of the two. The rule cannot tell you which happened; only measuring entry and exit separately can. How much of the enlargement comes from more entry and how much from less exit is still debated. A model fitted to rat data (Ganusov and Auerbach, 2014) put it down mainly to increased entry.

</details>

**When halving exit doubles the holding.** The second answer kept entry fixed. That is fair for one draining node out of hundreds: slowing its exit barely changes the blood count, so entry keeps its old rate and halving exit doubles both the stay and the number held. It is not fair if *every* node slows at once, as with fingolimod. Then blood is drained, entry into each node falls with it, and the holding rises by much less than the stay (Going deeper, "When every node slows at once"). The film's line about entry fixed and exit halved is this one-node case.

If entry also rises, the node holds more again. The longer stay gives the rare cells that recognise the antigen more time to find it. The shutdown is transient, and exit resumes afterwards.

### Design problem (extension)

You want to measure how long naive T cells stay in a mouse lymph node and test whether S1P1 sets that time. Design an adoptive-transfer experiment, say what each control rules out, and interpret these made-up results: at the start, one node holds 10,000 labelled normal T cells and 10,000 labelled S1P1-deficient T cells; 6, 12 and 24 h later it holds 6,100, 3,700 and 1,400 normal cells, and 9,800, 9,500 and 9,100 S1P1-deficient cells.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Take naive T cells from normal mice and from mice in which S1P1 can be deleted in T cells after they have left the thymus (for example with an inducible Cre); fetal liver chimeras will not do, because their mature T cells never reach the periphery. Give each population a different congenic surface marker, and both a photoconvertible fluorescent protein that turns from green to red under violet light. Inject them together by vein into one normal host, so differences cannot come from the host. Prepare both donor populations the same way, and swap the two markers between repeats so the labels themselves are not the cause.

A day later, expose one peripheral node to violet light under anaesthesia. Transferred cells inside it turn red; cells arriving later stay green, so new arrivals do not blur the cohort. Harvest that node from separate groups of mice at 0, 6, 12 and 24 h and count red cells of each type by flow cytometry.

Controls: equal time-zero counts show both populations start from the same cohort, so later differences reflect exit or death, and checking the viability of each population in the node helps tell them apart; illuminating cells and checking their viability tests whether the light harms them; counting red cells in blood and in an unexposed node shows where leavers go.

Interpretation: for first-order exit, cells remaining in the node = N0 x exp(-k t). For normal cells, ln(10,000 / 3,700) = 0.99 over 12 h, so k is about 0.083 per h and mean residence about 12 h; the 6 h and 24 h points fit (10,000 x exp(-0.5) = 6,070; 10,000 x exp(-2) = 1,350). For S1P1-deficient cells, ln(10,000 / 9,100) = 0.094 over 24 h, about 0.004 per h, or roughly 250 h: exit is nearly abolished, and the small loss could be cell death. Limits: one species and one node type, a few red cells may re-enter from blood, and the result shows S1P1 is needed for exit, not that it alone sets the normal residence time.

</details>

## Going deeper

The main line above does not depend on these details.

### Miller's controls

How the thymus's role was found, by Miller's neonatal thymectomy in mice and by Good's laboratory in rabbits, belongs to [adaptive immunity](/lessons/adaptive-immunity/). Two of Miller's controls carry the logic: sham-operated mice had the same surgery without losing the thymus, and adult mice thymectomized, irradiated and given marrow could not recover immune function, unlike sham-operated controls given the same irradiation and marrow (Cross, Leuchars and Miller, 1964).

### What Gowans's experiment did not show

It did not show which lymphocytes recirculate, how fast each kind does, or what molecules let them cross a vessel wall, and it gave no human values. In the rat model below, the duct is assumed to collect lymph from the mesenteric nodes and about half of the output of the subcutaneous nodes.

### How the entry molecules were found

Thome had described the shape of HEVs in 1898; for decades their role went unrecognised, until Gowans's 1964 work. Stamper and Woodruff in 1976 laid rat and mouse lymphocytes over frozen sections of lymph node and found that they stuck to HEVs and not to other vessels. In 1983 Gallatin, Weissman and Butcher described MEL-14, an antibody to a mouse lymphocyte molecule that appeared to be required for homing to nodes; the molecule is L-selectin. In 1994 mice lacking L-selectin had a severe reduction in lymphocytes in their peripheral nodes.

### Other routes in

HEVs in the Peyer's patches of the gut also use a second pair: the integrin alpha4beta7 on the lymphocyte binds MAdCAM-1 on the venule. The HEVs depend on lymph: tying off a node's afferent lymphatics makes them lose their specialised features. L-selectin is shed from the cell surface on activation. The figure of more than 90 percent entry through HEVs is for resting peripheral nodes; afferent lymph contributes more during inflammation.

### How antigen gets into a node

Antigen arrives in afferent lymph, free or carried by cells. Small soluble molecules travel from the subcapsular sinus toward the HEVs through conduits, fine tubes of reticular fibres wrapped in fibroblastic reticular cells; in mice, resident dendritic cells in the T-cell zone pick up soluble antigen this way before migrating dendritic cells arrive from the tissue. Steinman and Cohn described and named dendritic cells in 1973, in mouse spleen, lymph node and Peyer's patch, where they were only 0.1 to 1.6 percent of nucleated cells.

The spleen has its own route. Intravital imaging in mouse spleen showed T cells leaving arterial blood into the red pulp, latching onto perivascular stromal cells without needing CCR7, then moving into the T-cell zones, which does need CCR7.

### The rat model in full

This is where the film's figures come from (about 26 seconds per pass in blood; the spleen holding eight times as many cells as blood and the subcutaneous nodes about fifteen times), and the sandbox's starting values.

Ganusov and Auerbach (2014) fitted a compartment model to data from chromium-51-labelled thoracic duct lymphocytes injected into rats. It assumes first-order entry and exit, steady state, no cell death or division, and that labelled cells behave like the rest of the pool.

(a) Spleen: entry from blood 0.056 per min, exit 0.007 per min. Mean residence = 1 / 0.007 per min = 142.9 min, about 143 min or 2.4 h. The fitted table gives 144 min (115 to 173).

(b) In a made-up cohort of 1,000,000 labelled cells in blood, the initial rate of entry into the spleen is 0.056 per min × 1,000,000 = 56,000 cells per min.

(c) Time in blood. The model's entry rates from blood are 1.83, 0.41, 0.056, 0.026, 0.0106 and 0.0053 per min; the two largest are into the vessels of lung and liver, from which cells return quickly. The sum is 2.338 per min, so the mean time in blood per pass is 1 / 2.338 per min = 0.428 min = 25.7 s, about 26 s, as the model reports. Of cells leaving blood, 0.056 / 2.338 = 2.4 percent go to the spleen and 0.026 / 2.338 = 1.1 percent to the subcutaneous nodes.

(d) Where the pool sits. Keep only blood (B), spleen (S) and subcutaneous nodes (N). At steady state entry equals exit for each, so S / B = 0.056 / 0.007 = 8.0. The model treats the subcutaneous nodes as two sub-compartments in series, each with exit 0.0034 per min. This is a mathematical device for the delay before cells leave, not two regions of a node, and it gives a peaked spread of stays rather than the simple exponential of a one-step compartment. N1 / B = 0.026 / 0.0034 = 7.65, the second holds the same, so N / B = 15.3. With two exit steps in series, node residence = 2 / 0.0034 per min = 588 min, about 9.8 h (table: 593 min), not 1 / 0.0034. Blood's share is 1 / (1 + 8.0 + 15.3) = 1 / 24.3 = 4.1 percent.

Checks: 2.338 × 0.428 = 1.00; 0.024 × 2.338 = 0.056; 24.3 × 0.041 = 1.00. The blood share is a teaching value, since lung, liver, other organs and cell loss are left out.

### When every node slows at once

Take the three-compartment version in (d) and halve the exit rate constant of all the subcutaneous nodes together. The pool is closed, so blood's share falls from 4.12% to 2.53% (× 0.61). Entry into the nodes is proportional to the cells in blood, so it falls by the same factor, and the nodes' share rises from 62.95% to 77.27%: × 1.23, not × 2. Cut exit tenfold from both spleen and nodes, the direction a drug like fingolimod pushes, and in this model blood's share falls about tenfold, from 4.12% to 0.43%, while the nodes' share rises only from 62.95% to 65.38%: the drop in the blood count is the whole-pool effect, not a single-node one. The tenfold cut is made up; fingolimod itself lowers the blood lymphocyte count by about 70 percent, and spares cells that do not need CCR7 to re-enter nodes.
