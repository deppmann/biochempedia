techniques:
  - name: "Thoracic duct cannulation"
    blurb: >-
      A fine tube is placed in the thoracic duct of an anaesthetized animal and left in place, so
      lymph can be collected for hours or days. Counting the cells in it gives lymphocyte output
      per hour; in rats the duct drains the mesenteric nodes but only part of the subcutaneous
      node output (a fitted model assumes about half), so it samples part of the pool, not all of it.
  - name: "Adoptive transfer of labelled lymphocytes"
    blurb: >-
      Lymphocytes are collected, tagged with a radioactive label such as chromium-51 or a
      fluorescent dye, and injected by vein. Measuring the label in blood, lymph and each organ
      over time gives entry and exit rates, and fitting a compartment model turns them into
      residence times.
  - name: "Knockout mice and haematopoietic chimeras"
    blurb: >-
      A gene is deleted in every cell (L-selectin, CCR7) or only in blood-forming cells, by
      transplanting mutant fetal liver or marrow into irradiated hosts (S1P1). Comparing the two
      designs, or transferring mutant cells into normal mice, tells you whether a molecule is
      needed in the lymphocyte or in its surroundings.
  - name: "Frozen-section adhesion assay"
    blurb: >-
      Lymphocytes are laid over thin frozen sections of lymph node, washed, and counted where they
      stick. Stamper and Woodruff found that they stick selectively to high endothelial venules,
      which made homing a measurable binding event that antibodies could block.

**Drain the thoracic duct of a rat for a few days and the lymph flowing out of it runs short of lymphocytes.** If lymph nodes were making those cells, drainage should not have used them up. This lesson follows a naive lymphocyte (one that has not yet met the antigen it recognises) around the loop that explains the result, and puts numbers on how long it stays at each stop.

## Lymphocytes go around a loop, not out of a factory

Lymphocytes are made in the primary lymphoid organs. In humans and mice, B cells develop in the bone marrow from blood-forming stem cells ([stem cells](/lessons/stem-cells/)), and T cells mature in the thymus from marrow-derived precursors. The secondary lymphoid organs (lymph nodes, the spleen, and mucosal tissue such as the tonsils and the Peyer's patches of the gut wall) host mature lymphocytes and are where those cells meet antigen. How the thymus's role was found, by Miller's neonatal thymectomy in mice and by Good's laboratory in rabbits, belongs to [adaptive immunity](/lessons/adaptive-immunity/). Two of Miller's controls carry the logic: sham-operated mice had the same surgery without losing the thymus, and adult mice thymectomized and then irradiated could not recover immune function, unlike irradiated sham-operated controls (Cross, Leuchars and Miller, 1964). Newborn mice have true lymphocytes only in the thymus.

In the 1950s the usual view was that the lymphocytes in lymph were newly made in the nodes upstream. James Gowans tested it in rats fitted with a chronic cannula in the thoracic duct, the large lymph vessel that empties into the veins at the base of the neck ([capillary exchange and lymph](/lessons/capillary-exchange-and-lymph/) covers how lymph forms and moves). The cannula let him collect lymph for days.

The two views made different predictions. If nodes made the cells and released them once, duct output should have held roughly steady while production continued. If the same cells were going around, removing them should empty the loop. Output fell progressively. When the collected cells were returned by vein, output recovered, and the returned cells were later recovered in duct lymph.

That ruled in recirculation from blood, through lymphoid tissue, into lymph and back to blood, and ruled out the nodes as the main source of duct lymphocytes in the rat. It did not show which lymphocytes recirculate, how fast each kind does, or what molecules let them cross a vessel wall, and it gave no human values. The duct also samples only part of the pool: in the rat model fitted below it is assumed to collect lymph from the mesenteric nodes and about half of the output of the subcutaneous nodes.

Gowans's 1964 papers asked where the cells leave the blood. Radioactively labelled lymphocytes injected by vein into rats moved quickly into lymph nodes across the walls of high endothelial venules (HEVs), small venules lined by plump, almost cuboidal endothelial cells instead of flat ones. Thome had described their shape in 1898; for decades their role went unrecognised. Gowans concluded that HEVs are the site of large-scale migration of lymphocytes from blood into the secondary organs.

## A node samples lymph and blood; the spleen samples blood alone

A lymph node is a bean-shaped filter on a lymph vessel. Afferent lymphatics enter its convex surface and empty into the subcapsular sinus, a space under the capsule. Inside lie B-cell follicles in the outer cortex, a T-cell zone (the paracortex) where the HEVs sit, and medullary cords and sinuses. One efferent lymphatic, sometimes two, leaves at the hilum, beside the artery and vein.

Think of the node as a hotel with two entrances, afferent lymph from tissue and HEVs from blood, and one exit, the efferent lymphatic. The blood entrance is much the busier. At rest, afferent lymph has only about 5 to 10 percent of the cell concentration of efferent lymph, and cannulation studies in large animals found that more than 90 percent of the lymphocytes in efferent lymph had entered through HEVs.

Antigen arrives by two routes. Small soluble molecules travel from the subcapsular sinus toward the HEVs through conduits, fine tubes of reticular fibres wrapped in fibroblastic reticular cells; in mice, resident dendritic cells in the T-cell zone pick up soluble antigen this way before migrating dendritic cells arrive from the tissue. Steinman and Cohn described and named dendritic cells in 1973, in mouse spleen, lymph node and Peyer's patch, where they were only 0.1 to 1.6 percent of nucleated cells. Their trip from skin to the draining node depends on the chemokine receptor CCR7: in CCR7-deficient mice, activated skin dendritic cells fail to reach the nodes.

The spleen, the largest secondary lymphoid organ, has no afferent lymphatics and no HEVs, so it samples blood, not lymph. Arterial blood brings both antigen and lymphocytes. Its white pulp holds T-cell and B-cell zones around arterioles, and its red pulp removes old red cells ([blood cells and hemostasis](/lessons/blood-cells-and-hemostasis/)). Intravital imaging in mouse spleen showed T cells leaving arterial blood into the red pulp, latching onto perivascular stromal cells without needing CCR7, then moving into the T-cell zones, which does need CCR7.

{/* DIAGRAM: Lymph node in section: afferent lymphatics, subcapsular sinus, follicles, paracortex with HEVs and conduits, medulla, efferent lymphatic at the hilum */}

## Getting in on chemokines, getting out on S1P

Entry through an HEV is a sequence. L-selectin (CD62L) on the lymphocyte binds sulfated mucin-like glycoproteins on the HEV, which tethers a fast-moving cell and sets it rolling. L-selectin is active all the time. The integrin LFA-1 is not: it stays in a low-affinity shape until a chemokine (CCL21, CCL19 or CXCL13) signals through its receptor, CCR7 for CCL21 and CCL19. The signal changes LFA-1's shape so it binds ICAM-1 and ICAM-2 on the endothelium, and the cell stops, then crosses the wall. In mice the HEVs make CCL21 themselves; in humans they do not. The HEVs also depend on lymph: tying off a node's afferent lymphatics makes them lose their specialised features.

The molecules were found in steps. Stamper and Woodruff in 1976 laid rat and mouse lymphocytes over frozen sections of lymph node and found that they stuck to HEVs and not to other vessels. In 1983 Gallatin, Weissman and Butcher described MEL-14, an antibody to a mouse lymphocyte molecule that appeared to be required for homing to nodes; the molecule is L-selectin. In 1994 mice lacking L-selectin had a severe reduction in lymphocytes in their peripheral nodes. Cells do not all stay equally long once inside: in mice, naive CD4 T cells cross nodes about twice as fast as naive CD8 T cells.

Exit was a later question. The lipid sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) is high in blood and lymph and low inside lymphoid tissue, and lymphocytes carry a G protein-coupled receptor for it, S1P1. In 2004 Mehrdad Matloubian, Charles Lo, Guy Cinamon and colleagues in Jason Cyster's laboratory asked whether lymphocytes need it to leave. Mice lacking S1P1 in every cell do not survive embryonic development, so they gave irradiated mice blood-forming cells from S1P1-deficient fetal liver. If S1P1 is needed for exit, cells should enter lymphoid organs and pile up there.

They did. Mature T cells could not leave the thymus, so no T cells reached the periphery. S1P1-deficient T and B cells transferred into normal mice could not leave lymphoid organs, so the requirement lies in the lymphocyte, not its surroundings. The study did not give human values, show how the S1P gradient is kept, or show that every subset depends on S1P1 equally. It also found that S1P1 falls when lymphocytes are activated, which goes with retention. CD69, a surface protein that appears early after activation, causes S1P1 to be internalized and degraded, and T cells expressing it stay in the node.

## Residence time is set by the exit rate

Ganusov and Auerbach (2014) fitted a compartment model to data from chromium-51-labelled thoracic duct lymphocytes injected into rats. Assume first-order (constant-fraction) entry and exit, steady state, no cell death or division, and that labelled cells behave like the rest of the pool.

> **mean residence time = 1 / exit rate constant**
>
> **number held at steady state = cells entering per unit time x mean residence time (Little's law)**

(a) Spleen: entry from blood 0.056 per min, exit 0.007 per min. Mean residence = 1 / 0.007 per min = 142.9 min, about 143 min or 2.4 h. The fitted table gives 144 min (115 to 173).

(b) In a made-up cohort of 1,000,000 labelled cells in blood, the initial rate of entry into the spleen is 0.056 per min x 1,000,000 = 56,000 cells per min.

(c) Time in blood. The model's entry rates from blood are 1.83, 0.41, 0.056, 0.026, 0.0106 and 0.0053 per min; the two largest are into the vessels of lung and liver, from which cells return quickly. The sum is 2.338 per min, so the mean time in blood per pass is 1 / 2.338 per min = 0.428 min = 25.7 s, about 26 s, as the model reports. Of cells leaving blood, 0.056 / 2.338 = 2.4 percent go to the spleen and 0.026 / 2.338 = 1.1 percent to the subcutaneous nodes.

(d) Where the pool sits. Keep only blood (B), spleen (S) and subcutaneous nodes (N). At steady state entry equals exit for each, so S / B = 0.056 / 0.007 = 8.0. The model treats a node as two sub-compartments in series, each with exit 0.0034 per min: N1 / B = 0.026 / 0.0034 = 7.65, the second holds the same, so N / B = 15.3. Node residence = 2 / 0.0034 per min = 588 min, about 9.8 h (table: 593 min). Blood's share is 1 / (1 + 8.0 + 15.3) = 1 / 24.3 = 4.1 percent. The spleen receives twice the node's share of cells leaving blood but holds about half as many, because cells leave it four times faster.

(e) Mouse nodes, made-up entry: 1,000 naive CD4 and 1,000 naive CD8 T cells enter one peripheral node per hour. With residence times of 12.2 h and 21.2 h, the node holds 1,000 per h x 12.2 h = 12,200 CD4 cells and 21,200 CD8 cells, a ratio of 1.74 from residence time alone.

Answers: 143 min; 56,000 cells per min; 26 s, 2.4 and 1.1 percent; about 4 percent in blood; 12,200 and 21,200 cells. Checks: 2.338 x 0.428 = 1.00; 0.024 x 2.338 = 0.056; 24.3 x 0.041 = 1.00. The blood share is a teaching value, since lung, liver, other organs and cell loss are left out.

## A draining node holds on to its visitors

A node that drains a site of immune challenge changes its traffic. Migrating dendritic cells arrive in afferent lymph and settle in the T-cell zone. Lymphocytes keep entering from blood through the HEVs, but fewer leave: in mouse studies, lymphocytes in the node express CD69, CD69 removes S1P1 from their surface, and exit slows. The node enlarges and holds more lymphocytes than its partner on the other side of the body.

Little's law gives the direction. If entry is unchanged and the exit rate constant halves, residence time doubles and so does the number held. If entry also rises, the node holds more again. The longer stay gives the rare cells that recognise the antigen more time to find it. The shutdown is transient, and exit resumes afterwards.

### Design problem

You want to measure how long naive T cells stay in a mouse lymph node and test whether S1P1 sets that time. Design an adoptive-transfer experiment, say what each control rules out, and interpret these made-up results: at the start, one node holds 10,000 labelled normal T cells and 10,000 labelled S1P1-deficient T cells; 6, 12 and 24 h later it holds 6,100, 3,700 and 1,400 normal cells, and 9,800, 9,500 and 9,100 S1P1-deficient cells.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Take naive T cells from normal mice and from mice in which S1P1 can be deleted in T cells after they have left the thymus (for example with an inducible Cre); fetal liver chimeras will not do, because their mature T cells never reach the periphery. Give each population a different congenic surface marker, and both a photoconvertible fluorescent protein that turns from green to red under violet light. Inject them together by vein into one normal host, so any difference belongs to the cells, not to the mouse or the handling.

A day later, expose one peripheral node to violet light under anaesthesia. Transferred cells inside it turn red; cells arriving later stay green, so new arrivals do not blur the cohort. Count red cells of each type in that node at 6, 12 and 24 h by flow cytometry.

Controls: equal time-zero counts show both populations entered equally, so later differences reflect exit; illuminating cells and checking their viability tests whether the light harms them; counting red cells in blood and in an unexposed node shows where leavers go.

Interpretation: for first-order exit, cells remaining in the node = N0 x exp(-k t). For normal cells, ln(10,000 / 3,700) = 0.99 over 12 h, so k is about 0.083 per h and mean residence about 12 h; the 6 h and 24 h points fit (10,000 x exp(-0.5) = 6,070; 10,000 x exp(-2) = 1,350). For S1P1-deficient cells, ln(10,000 / 9,100) = 0.094 over 24 h, about 0.004 per h, or roughly 250 h: exit is nearly abolished, and the small loss could be cell death. Limits: one species and one node type, a few red cells may re-enter from blood, and the result shows S1P1 is needed for exit, not that it alone sets the normal residence time.

</details>
