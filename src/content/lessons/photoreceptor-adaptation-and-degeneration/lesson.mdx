- stem: Light closes the CNG channels of a photoreceptor outer segment. What happens to calcium next?
  choices:
  - Entry and extrusion both stop, so calcium stays level
  - Entry continues but extrusion stops, so calcium rises
  - Entry speeds up to match extrusion, so calcium stays level
  - Entry stops but extrusion continues, so calcium falls
  answer: 3

Entry speeding up would need open channels, the opposite of what light does.

  choices:
  - They pump calcium out of the outer segment in the light
  - They slow cGMP synthesis, which makes each response larger
  - Falling calcium lets them speed cGMP synthesis, curbing responses
  - They are the proteins that close CNG channels in the light

- stem: Deficient cones have I0 = 12,100 photons per square micrometre per second and n = 1.5. What is their relative sensitivity at a background of 24,200 photons per square micrometre per second?
  choices:
  - '0.111'
  - '0.261'
  - '0.500'
  - '0.333'

**A photoreceptor keeps working as background light changes enormously because it turns its own gain down as the light brightens. Light lowers calcium in the outer segment, and falling calcium feeds back on phototransduction: the GCAPs speed cGMP synthesis, and recoverin lets the pigment be shut off sooner. That feedback keeps the cell responding to changes in light instead of saturating.**

The [retina](/lessons/retina/) lesson gave you phototransduction and the calcium loop, and [senses and cGMP](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/) gave you the cascade and its shutoff. [Sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/) introduced adaptation as a property of any sense. This lesson restates the loop briefly, shows what knockout mice revealed about it, and reads its effect on an adaptation curve. It then compares how rods and cones divide the range of light, and ends with what happens when photoreceptors die: in typical retinitis pigmentosa rods die first and cones follow, and gene therapy has begun to improve vision in specific genetic forms.

## Calcium feedback sets the gain

The loop runs like this. Light closes cyclic-nucleotide-gated (CNG) channels in the outer segment. Calcium entry through them falls, while a sodium-calcium-potassium exchanger (NCKX) keeps pumping calcium out, so outer-segment calcium falls. At low calcium, calcium-sensing proteins change state. GCAP1 and GCAP2, the guanylyl cyclase activating proteins, speed cGMP synthesis, which reopens channels; the GCAPs can change the cyclase's activity up to about 20-fold, and this cGMP route is the strongest of the calcium feedbacks. Recoverin lets go of GRK1, the kinase that switches off activated visual pigment, so the pigment is shut off faster. A third feedback acts on the CNG channel itself: calcium-bound calmodulin, and in cones a protein called CNG-modulin, lower the channel's sensitivity to cGMP, so lower calcium helps channels reopen. In mouse rods this channel effect is small. These effects push the cell back toward its dark state while the light is still on, and that push is gain control. Reviews of the field credit this feedback with most of the shift in sensitivity that lets rods and cones work over a wide range of light.

The test of a loop is to cut it. In mouse rods lacking both GCAP1 and GCAP2 (Mendez and colleagues, 2001), the calcium feedback on cGMP synthesis is gone. Responses recover far more slowly, single-photon responses become about five times larger than in controls, and adaptation to background light is severely compromised. Cones lacking the GCAPs show largely the same picture (Sakurai and colleagues, 2011). In people, mutations in the GCAPs, particularly GCAP1, are linked to severe inherited blinding diseases, among them Leber congenital amaurosis and cone-rod dystrophies. So the GCAPs normally limit the size of each response and shorten it, and without them the cell cannot adapt properly. Deleting recoverin also changes the response: in both rods and cones it speeds recovery in darkness and dim backgrounds, as expected if recoverin normally slows pigment shutoff.

One loose end is minor for this lesson. When GCAP1, GCAP2 and recoverin are all deleted, mouse rods and cones still show some adaptation, so other mechanisms add to this loop. What supplies the remainder is unresolved; the experiments and their limits are in Going deeper.

## Reading an adaptation curve: half-sensitivity background and slope

Sensitivity here means response amplitude divided by the strength of a dim flash. As the background brightens, sensitivity falls. A mouse cone study (Vinberg and Kefalov, 2018) fitted this with a modified Weber-Fechner relation, where S is the flash sensitivity on a background of intensity I, and Sdark is the sensitivity in darkness:

> **S(I) / Sdark = 1 / (1 + (I / I0)^n)**

I0 is the half-sensitivity background: when I equals I0, the bracket is 1 + 1 and sensitivity is 0.5 of its dark value. The exponent n sets the slope. On a log-log plot, at backgrounds well above I0, the curve falls with slope minus n. With n = 1 and backgrounds well above I0, sensitivity is roughly proportional to 1/I, so response size on a background stays about constant for a fixed contrast, which is Weber behaviour. With n above 1, sensitivity falls faster than Weber's rule says.

**Worked problem: two kinds of mouse cone at one background.** The fit values come from Vinberg and Kefalov (2018), recorded ex vivo from isolated mouse retinas. Control cones had I0 = 48,800 photons per square micrometre per second and n = 1.0. Cones lacking GCAPs and recoverin had I0 = 12,100 photons per square micrometre per second and n = 1.5. Take a background of I = 48,800 photons per square micrometre per second.

1. Control: I/I0 = 1, so S/Sdark = 1/(1 + 1) = 0.500.
2. Deficient: I/I0 = 48,800/12,100 = 4.03. Raise it to the power 1.5: 4.03^1.5 = 8.10. So S/Sdark = 1/(1 + 8.10) = 0.110.
3. For a response of fixed size, each preparation measured against its own dark sensitivity, the deficient cones need a flash 0.500/0.110 = 4.55 times stronger.

Each sensitivity is normalized to its own genotype's dark sensitivity, so 4.55 is not a ratio of absolute thresholds between genotypes. The inputs are central fit values with no uncertainty carried through (the paper gives them as means with standard errors), and both the ratio and the sensitivities are dimensionless.

Read the two fits together and the contribution of the GCAPs and recoverin is visible. Without them, the cones reach half sensitivity at about a quarter of the control background, and beyond it their sensitivity falls faster than Weber's rule. The same study reported dim-flash time to peak of 56 ms in control cones and 142 ms in the deficient cones (means, standard errors in the paper), so the deficient cones were also slower, consistent with weaker feedback.

## Rods and cones divide the range, and rods in daylight

Rods and cones split the range of light. Rods are the more sensitive cells: one absorbed photon gives a rod a clear electrical response. Hecht, Shlaer and Pirenne inferred this in 1942 from human threshold flashes: because so few absorptions were spread over about 500 rods, they concluded that a single quantum is enough to excite a rod. Recordings from single toad rods later showed this directly (Baylor, Lamb and Yau, 1979). Cones are less sensitive but faster. A biochemical comparison of each phototransduction reaction in purified carp rods and cones (reviewed by Kawamura and Tachibanaki, 2022) found that the reactions that generate a response are somewhat less effective in cones, while the reactions that end and recover a response are much more effective. That combination gives cones lower sensitivity and briefer responses. Rods serve dim light; cones serve daylight, colour and sharp vision.

A standard story says rods saturate once background light is bright enough, and cones then carry vision. Earlier in this course, [retinal cell types](/lessons/retinal-cell-types/) and [intrinsically photosensitive ganglion cells](/lessons/iprgcs/) described other routes for bright-light information; here the question is whether the rod route really closes.

Tikidji-Hamburyan, Reinhard, Storchi and colleagues (2017) tested it in cone-deficient mice, recording ganglion cells in isolated retina and neurons of the visual thalamus (the dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus) in anaesthetized mice, with intact controls as well. Rod-driven responses to moderate-contrast stimuli were present at backgrounds up to 10^7 dark-adapted-equivalent activated rhodopsins (R*) per rod per second in isolated retina, and up to 10^6.35 in the thalamus in vivo. Contrast sensitivity fell when a bright background came on and then recovered with continued exposure, and recovery was faster at higher backgrounds. A phototransduction model suggested that changes in gain and bleaching adaptation, the loss of sensitivity as pigment is bleached until it is regenerated through the visual cycle, contribute at high backgrounds.

So the rule needs revising: in these mice, rod saturation depends on how long the retina has been exposed, not on irradiance alone. A recent review of this work (Reinhard and colleagues) proposes that rods are better thought of as tuned to low contrast than to low light. The evidence here is from mouse retina and thalamus, so it sets no daylight cutoff for human rods.

A rod responding to one photon is not the same as a person seeing one. Hecht's threshold flashes did not show that a person detects one delivered photon; their inference was statistical, about a handful of absorptions. A 2016 study that delivered photons one at a time found a pooled result close to chance and a higher one in a high-confidence subset, so its strength depends on how trials were selected and analysed. Both experiments are taken apart in Going deeper.

## When photoreceptors die: rods first, then cones

In typical retinitis pigmentosa (RP), a heterogeneous group of progressive dystrophies, rod degeneration precedes cone loss. Patients typically lose night vision first, often in adolescence, then peripheral visual field; central vision is lost later, when cones fail (Verbakel and colleagues, 2018). In many cases the cones do not express the disease gene, yet they die too, typically after most of the rods around them have gone (Xue and Cepko, 2024). Because cones carry daylight, colour and sharp central vision, keeping cones alive is a major goal of research and therapy.

Why cones die is not settled. One candidate is lost support from rods. Léveillard and colleagues (Nature Genetics 36:755-759, 2004) screened for factors that keep cones alive and found RdCVF, the rod-derived cone viability factor, described as a truncated, thioredoxin-like protein. Aït-Ali and colleagues (Cell 161:817-832, 2015), using embryonic chicken cone cultures and the rd1 mouse, found that RdCVF binds basigin-1 on the photoreceptor surface, that basigin-1 associates with GLUT1, and that the pathway increases cone glucose entry and aerobic glycolysis. A missense mutant failed to bind basigin-1, stimulate glucose uptake or prevent secondary cone death in the model. This is experimental work, not a human trial, and it is one proposed cause among several: Punzo and colleagues (2009) found signs that cones starve in mouse models of retinitis pigmentosa, and oxidative damage is another candidate. A gene therapy that delivers both RdCVF and its long, antioxidant form is being developed to preserve cones whatever the causative mutation; in animal models, including pigs carrying a rhodopsin mutation, it helped maintain cone structure and function. Compare the metabolic coupling in [glia and metabolic partners](/lessons/glia-metabolic-partners/) and the wider picture in [neurodegeneration](/lessons/neurodegeneration/) and [cell death](/lessons/cell-death/).

## Rescuing vision: what two gene-therapy trials can claim

Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA) typically causes profound visual impairment beginning in the first months of life, and it covers defects in phototransduction, the retinoid cycle (the pigment epithelium's recycling of retinoids) and ciliary transport. Biallelic RPE65 disease is one retinoid-cycle form. Russell and colleagues (Lancet, 2017) ran a randomized, controlled, open-label phase 3 trial of bilateral subretinal AAV2-RPE65 gene augmentation in people with biallelic RPE65 disease (age 3 years and over) who had enough viable retina; outcome grading was masked. The outcome was a multi-luminance mobility test (MLMT), a navigation course scored by light level. At one year the between-group difference in mean bilateral change was 1.6 light levels (95% CI 0.72 to 2.41), and 13 of 20 treated participants passed at 1 lux. The US FDA approved the product, voretigene neparvovec, in December 2017, the first approved gene therapy for an inherited retinal disease. It is a genotype-specific proof that adding a working gene can improve vision. A 2026 meta-analysis of post-approval studies found improvements in full-field light sensitivity consistent with the trials, and also chorioretinal atrophy, a complication not seen in the trials, whose clinical importance is not yet clear.

Some retinal diseases are not suited to adding a gene copy, and genome editing inside the eye is being tested instead. Pierce and colleagues (New England Journal of Medicine 390:1972-1984, 2024) delivered CRISPR editing machinery for CEP290 IVS26 to the worse eye of 14 participants (see [CRISPR and functional genomics](/lessons/crispr-and-functional-genomics/) for the tool, and [sequencing and genomes](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/) for finding such variants). The study was open-label and single-arm, with no sham control. It recorded no dose-limiting toxic effects and reported signs of possible benefit; the design problem below asks what each design can claim. Retinal implants and optogenetic vision restoration are for [sensory prostheses](/lessons/sensory-prostheses/).

### Design problem

The RPE65 augmentation trial was randomized and controlled, open-label, with masked outcome grading and a one-year between-group difference of 1.6 light levels (95% CI 0.72 to 2.41), with 13 of 20 treated participants passing at 1 lux. In the CEP290 editing study, 6 of 14 participants improved on cone-mediated sensitivity by full-field stimulus testing and 11 of 14 improved on at least one of four outcomes, in an open-label, single-ascending-dose study with no sham control, with counts pooled across three dose levels. Interpret these data: which claims does each design support?

<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with what randomization buys. It adds a control group that goes through the same period of practice and repeated testing, and masked grading limits grader bias. The trial was open-label, so participants knew their group and their own expectations were not equalized. That supports the RPE65 claim that treated participants did better on mobility than controls at one year, by 1.6 light levels. That mobility finding is limited to participants with biallelic RPE65 disease and viable retina, to the MLMT as an endpoint, and to the follow-up of the trial. The trial's secondary outcomes, such as full-field light sensitivity, need their own reading.

Now take what a single-arm study cannot control. Participants may improve with practice on the task, outcomes fluctuate naturally, and people who know they were treated may try harder or report more. Without a comparison group those effects cannot be separated from treatment. The untreated fellow eye is a useful within-participant comparison, because it shares the person and the testing days, but it is not a sham: it does not have the same procedure, and the two eyes may differ in disease stage. So the CEP290 numbers support a statement of safety (no dose-limiting toxic effects were recorded) and a preliminary signal of possible benefit, no more.

Next, separate the endpoints. Mobility, retinal sensitivity, acuity and patient-reported outcomes are different measurements. That 11 of 14 improved on at least one of four outcomes is a weaker statement than that 11 improved on all four, and a count of "at least one" rises with the number of outcomes. The 6 of 14 on cone-mediated sensitivity is a specific, more informative figure.

Neither study gives a prognosis, a claim of general benefit, or any advice about treatment for an individual.

## Going deeper

### When the sensors are deleted, some adaptation remains

Deleting GCAP1, GCAP2 and recoverin does not abolish all adaptation. Vinberg and colleagues (2015) showed this first in mouse rods, recording ex vivo from isolated retina at 37 degrees Celsius with a transretinal electroretinogram (ERG), in which downstream synaptic responses are blocked so the voltage across the retina reports the photoreceptors. They compared wild-type rods with rods lacking the GCAPs, rods lacking recoverin, and rods lacking all three proteins. Calcium-dependent light adaptation persisted in the rods lacking both sensors.

Vinberg and Kefalov (2018) repeated the logic in cones. The mice also lacked rod transducin (a Gnat1 knockout), so rod phototransduction was gone and the recorded signal came from cones. All mice were 2 to 3 months old, and the flashes for the fits in the worked problem were 505-nm light. They compared control cones with cones lacking GCAP1, GCAP2 and recoverin, and they repeated the measurement with extracellular calcium lowered from 1.2 mM to an estimated 30 nM. The knockout cones still adapted to background light. The authors interpreted this as a sign of a calcium-independent component, which they did not find in mouse rods, and also reported a further calcium-dependent mechanism in the same cones.

Hold that conclusion at its real strength. The low-calcium bath is a change in the solution around the retina, not a direct clamp of outer-segment calcium, and the inference rests partly on fitting a model. The simplest calcium-independent explanation is that a steady background keeps PDE active, so cGMP turns over faster and each flash changes it more easily, with no calcium involved. The authors modelled this effect and argued it does not account for all of the residual adaptation. No molecule has been identified for what remains. A direct test would hold outer-segment calcium fixed while the background series is run in cells lacking each candidate in turn. Calcium clamps of this kind have been used in amphibian photoreceptors, but the authors note they have not yet worked in mouse rods or cones. Until then the honest statement is that GCAPs and recoverin contribute and do not account for all of the adaptation.

A review by Vinberg and colleagues (2018) adds context. GCAP2 is also expressed in mouse cones and can drive cGMP synthesis when GCAP1 is missing, though GCAP1 dominates in normal cones. And, surprisingly given that cones adapt over a wider range than rods, calcium control of the cyclase contributes less to the sensitivity of cones than of rods, both in darkness and during light adaptation.

### Rods in bright light: the recordings

The cone-deficient mice in the Tikidji-Hamburyan study were Cnga3 knockouts. Ganglion cells were recorded with a multi-electrode array in isolated retinas of mice 4.5 to 6 weeks old, ERGs were measured at 8 weeks, and thalamic recordings were made in mice about 6 to 8 weeks old. Two limits follow from the design. Photoisomerization rates cannot be turned into lux without assumptions this study does not supply. And whether human rods contribute to conscious vision in bright light would need a human test with rod-isolating conditions.

### One photon: what 1942 showed and what 2016 added

Hecht, Shlaer and Pirenne (Journal of General Physiology 25:819-840, 1942) measured the least blue-green light that dark-adapted human observers could detect. Threshold flashes held 54 to 148 photons at the cornea. After correcting for losses in the eye, they estimated an upper limit of 5 to 14 photons absorbed by rods, and their frequency-of-seeing analysis gave 5 to 8 critical events at threshold. The arithmetic gives a feel for the losses: 5/54 and 14/148 are both near 0.09 to 0.10, so at most about one corneal photon in ten ended up absorbed by a rod, on their upper-limit estimates. Hecht did not show that a person detects one delivered photon. The inference was statistical, about a handful of absorptions.

Tinsley, Molodtsov, Prevedel and colleagues (Nature Communications 7:12172, 2016) delivered photons one at a time. Alipasha Vaziri conceived and supervised the study. Three trained men in their twenties, dark-adapted, chose which of two time intervals contained a heralded light event. Among 30,767 trials, 2,420 were post-selected as single-photon events. The pooled probability of a correct choice was 0.516 plus or minus 0.010, against 0.5 for chance, with P = 0.0545. A high-confidence subset gave 0.60 plus or minus 0.03. Combining the single-photon and attenuated-light data with Fisher's method gave P = 0.014. A rough check: if the pooled figure rests on those 2,420 events, the binomial standard error is the square root of 0.25/2,420, about 0.010, and 0.516 is about 1.6 standard errors above chance. The authors also interpreted a dependence of detection on recently seen photons as a possible priming process lasting seconds, and that is an interpretation.

So the evidence for single-photon perception is real but analysis-dependent: it comes from three men, it depends on how trials were selected, and it does not show reliable awareness of every photon that reaches the cornea. It also separates steps that are easy to merge: a photon delivered, a rod absorbing it, the observer's confidence, and the forced choice.

### RdCVF: one gene, two forms

Léveillard's screen was expression cloning: a library of mouse retinal cDNAs was tested on cone-enriched cultures from embryonic chicken retina, and the clone that supported cone survival was RdCVF. This short RdCVF is enzymatically inactive. The same gene, Nxnl1, also encodes a long form, RdCVFL, a thioredoxin-fold protein that helps protect photoreceptors against oxidative stress; it is the long, antioxidant form included in the gene therapy described above.

### Trial details

In the retinoid cycle that RPE65 disease breaks, RPE65 in the pigment epithelium converts all-trans retinyl ester to 11-cis retinol. The US FDA approved the RPE65 product on 19 December 2017 according to its records.
