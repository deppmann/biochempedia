      disease.

techniques:
  - name: "Pedigree linkage and cosegregation"
    blurb: >-
      You follow genetic markers and candidate variants through a family with an inherited
      disease and ask whether one variant travels with affected relatives and not with unaffected
      ones. Replication in an unrelated family strengthens the case that the variant causes disease
      in those families, not in every patient.
  - name: "Immunoelectron microscopy of extracted filaments"
    blurb: >-
      Insoluble filaments are extracted from postmortem brain, labelled with antibodies carrying
      gold particles, and imaged in the electron microscope. Gold on the filaments identifies a
      component. It tells you composition, not whether the filaments cause cell death.
  - name: "Axotomy with genetic loss of function"
    blurb: >-
      You cut an identified nerve in flies or mice lacking a gene and compare the distal axon with
      that of controls over days to weeks. Surviving axons show that the gene is needed for
      degeneration after injury, in that preparation.
  - name: "Randomized placebo-controlled trial"
    blurb: >-
      Participants are assigned at random to drug or placebo, usually with neither they nor the assessors knowing which, and followed for a fixed
      time on a prespecified clinical endpoint, with biomarkers and safety measured alongside. The
      difference between arms estimates the drug's effect in the enrolled population.

**In a large trial, people with early Alzheimer disease received either lecanemab, an antibody that clears amyloid plaques from the brain, or a placebo for 18 months. Both groups got worse. The treated group got worse more slowly.**

Amyloid plaques are deposits of a small peptide, amyloid-β (Aβ), that build up between neurons in Alzheimer disease. The gap between the two groups is some of the strongest evidence that a drug that clears amyloid can change the course of the disease. It is also evidence that the drug did not stop it. Reading that result well means knowing what trials, autopsies and family genetics can each show.

## Four questions and one idea

This lesson compares three diseases: Alzheimer disease (AD), Parkinson disease (PD) and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). For each one it asks four questions.

1. **Which neurons die?**
2. **What builds up in or around the affected neurons?**
3. **What can start the disease?**
4. **Does removing what builds up help?**

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Each kind of evidence supports some claims and not others. A deposit seen at autopsy shows what builds up, not whether it kills. A disease-causing variant found in families shows what can start the disease, not whether removing its product later helps. Whether changing the target helps once the disease is under way is tested most directly by randomized trials in people, judged on clinical outcomes such as thinking, daily function or survival, which also measure the harms. For amyloid in early Alzheimer disease, the answer so far is that decline slows but does not stop.

The rule concerns evidence for cause and treatment. How a committed neuron dies is in [death machinery in neurons](/lessons/death-machinery-in-neurons/), and the cell's protein disposal systems are in [quality control and autophagy](/lessons/quality-control-and-autophagy/). After the four questions, the lesson asks why these neurons fail first, which is still open.

## Which neurons die: three diseases, three populations

A **neurodegenerative disease** is a progressive loss of particular neurons. The three diseases here usually begin in adult life. The important word is particular. **Selective vulnerability** means that the pathology strikes some neuronal populations and spares others, often close neighbours (reviewed by Fu, Hardy and Duff, 2018). Any account of these diseases has to fit this pattern.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/neurodegeneration/vulnerable-populations.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: A medial view of the brain and upper spinal cord with gold marking the neurons lost: entorhinal cortex and hippocampal CA1 early in Alzheimer disease, substantia nigra dopamine neurons in Parkinson disease, and motor cortex upper motor neurons with their descending axons in ALS. An inset of the spinal cord marks the ventral-horn lower motor neurons also lost in ALS." caption="Each disease strikes particular neurons and spares others: entorhinal and CA1 neurons early in Alzheimer disease, nigral dopamine neurons in Parkinson disease, and upper and lower motor neurons in ALS." />

In **Alzheimer disease**, neurons lost early include neurons of layer II of the **entorhinal cortex**, the main gateway between neocortex and hippocampus, and pyramidal neurons of hippocampal field CA1. Both belong to the circuit for new declarative memories ([memory systems](/lessons/memory-systems/)).

In **Parkinson disease**, the defining loss is of dopamine neurons in the **substantia nigra pars compacta** (SNpc), the midbrain nucleus whose axons supply the striatum. Their loss unbalances the loops described in [the basal ganglia](/lessons/basal-ganglia/). Even "dopamine neurons" is too broad. When the dopamine neurons of postmortem human substantia nigra were sorted into ten populations by the genes they express, one population, marked by expression of the gene AGTR1, was highly susceptible to loss in PD (Kamath and colleagues, 2022).

In **ALS**, the targets are motor neurons. **Upper motor neurons** have their cell bodies in motor cortex and send axons down the corticospinal tract to the spinal cord and the corticobulbar projection to brainstem motor nuclei. **Lower motor neurons** sit in the brainstem and the ventral horn of the spinal cord and send axons to muscle. Both the cell bodies and their axons degenerate. As lower motor neurons die, whole **motor units** (one motor neuron and all the muscle fibres it supplies; see [motor units and reflexes](/lessons/motor-units-reflexes/)) drop out.

## What builds up: proteins that clump

Each disease has a protein that misfolds and gathers into insoluble deposits seen at autopsy.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/neurodegeneration/disease-deposits.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: Three neurons compared: in Alzheimer disease an amyloid plaque lies outside the cell and a tau tangle inside; in Parkinson disease a Lewy body made of alpha-synuclein filaments sits in the cell body and a Lewy neurite in a process; in ALS, TDP-43 has left the nucleus and gathered in a cytoplasmic inclusion." caption="Each disease has its own deposit at autopsy: amyloid plaques outside and tau tangles inside neurons in Alzheimer disease, alpha-synuclein Lewy bodies and neurites in Parkinson disease, and TDP-43 that has left the nucleus for a cytoplasmic inclusion in most ALS." />

- **AD** has two. **Amyloid plaques** lie outside cells and contain **Aβ**, peptides cut from amyloid precursor protein (APP) by two protein-cutting enzymes, β-secretase and then γ-secretase. **Neurofibrillary tangles** lie inside neurons and contain misfolded, hyperphosphorylated **tau** (carrying extra phosphate groups), a protein that normally binds microtubules.
- **PD** has **Lewy bodies** and Lewy neurites, inclusions in neuronal cell bodies and processes. In 1998 Spillantini, Goedert and colleagues found **α-synuclein** to be the major component of the filaments extracted from Lewy bodies.
- **ALS** in most cases has inclusions of **TDP-43**, an RNA-binding protein that leaves the nucleus and gathers in the cytoplasm. Neumann and colleagues identified it in 2006. There are exceptions, and the next section uses them.

**Predict.** Suppose a protein turns up in every lesion of a disease. Does it follow that the protein kills the neuron?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. A deposit tells you what a lesion is made of, not that it kills the cell. Small soluble aggregates, loss of the protein's normal job, or the cell's response could do the damage. For Lewy bodies, for example, whether the inclusions themselves kill neurons is still argued.

</details>

You may also read that misfolded seeds pass from cell to cell within one nervous system and template more misfolding ("prion-like spread"). That is a hypothesis, tested mostly in animal and cell models. These diseases do not pass between people through ordinary contact, although rare transmission of Aβ pathology through contaminated medical material, such as growth hormone taken from cadaver pituitaries, has been reported (Banerjee and colleagues, 2024).

## What can start it: genes that cause and genes that raise risk

To ask what *starts* the disease, look at families. Two kinds of gene variant matter here, and the difference between them carries the rest of the lesson. The background is in [pedigrees](/lessons/pedigrees/) and [complex traits and GWAS](/lessons/complex-traits-and-gwas/).

- A **Mendelian variant** can cause the disease in the families that carry it. In dominant disease one altered copy can be enough; in recessive disease, such as parkin parkinsonism, both copies of the gene are altered.
- A **risk allele** only shifts the odds. Many carriers never get the disease, and many patients do not carry it. It is neither necessary nor sufficient.

In a pedigree, a Mendelian disease genotype and the illness travel together: affected relatives carry it, and relatives without it usually stay free of the family's disease. This travelling together is called **cosegregation**. It is evidence for a cause, not proof by itself, because a nearby harmless variant can travel with the true one. Finding the same pattern in a second, unrelated family, and showing what the variant does, makes the case much stronger.

Even so, not everyone with the disease genotype falls ill by a given age. The share who do by that age is the **penetrance**, and it can be incomplete. For LRRK2 G2019S, one consortium estimate was 74% by age 79, and other studies report lower figures.

Mendelian variants are rare, and most people with AD, PD or ALS carry no known Mendelian variant. They matter because each established one names something that can start the disease.

**Predict.** A family's ALS is caused by a mutation in SOD1, an antioxidant enzyme. Most people with ALS have TDP-43 inclusions in their motor neurons. Would you expect this family's motor neurons to have them?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. In a 2007 postmortem series, Mackenzie and colleagues found that ALS cases with SOD1 mutations lacked TDP-43 inclusions, yet their motor neurons died. In FUS-linked familial ALS, mutant FUS accumulates in neuronal cytoplasm, and TDP-43 inclusions are also absent (Vance and colleagues, 2009). |---|---|---|---|
| AD | APP (dominant) | amyloid precursor protein | Encodes the precursor from which plaque Aβ is cut |
| AD | PSEN1, PSEN2 (dominant) | presenilin, the catalytic core of γ-secretase, the enzyme whose cut releases Aβ | Influences how Aβ is processed |

Read down the last column. In Alzheimer disease all three dominant genes act on Aβ. In Parkinson disease and ALS, gene and deposit sometimes part company, so the gene that can start a disease and the protein that builds up need not be the same.

The best-known risk allele is **APOE ε4**. It raises the risk of late-onset AD, yet even in old age it is not sufficient to produce clinical disease, and many patients carry no ε4.

The AD rows have a pattern: all three dominant genes act on how much Aβ is made or which lengths. Presenilin mutations, for example, shift the cut toward longer forms such as Aβ42, which clump more readily, sometimes while lowering the total. In 1992, building on the APP mutations, Hardy and Higgins proposed the **amyloid cascade hypothesis**: Aβ accumulation starts a process that leads to tangles, neuron loss and dementia. The presenilin genes, found in 1995, later fit the same pattern. Mendelian genetics supports an initiating role for Aβ in familial disease. It does not show that Aβ alone explains the common late-onset form, and it does not show that removing Aβ from a patient will help. That needs direct evidence of treatment benefit, ideally from randomized trials.

## Does removing it help: testing amyloid in trials

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

If Aβ starts the disease, removing it might change the course, though a process already under way may no longer need Aβ to keep going. Antibodies against Aβ have now tested that.

**Worked problem: what does "27% slowing" mean?** Clarity AD (van Dyck and colleagues, 2023) randomized 1,795 people with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to AD, with amyloid confirmed, to lecanemab or placebo for 18 months. The primary outcome was the change in **CDR-SB** (Clinical Dementia Rating, Sum of Boxes), a clinician's rating of cognition and daily function that runs from 0 to 18, higher meaning worse. Adjusted mean changes at 18 months were +1.21 points with lecanemab and +1.66 with placebo. Here we work with these group averages.

*Step 1. Absolute difference.* 1.66 − 1.21 = 0.45 points less worsening. (The paper subtracts the other way, reporting −0.45 points, 95% confidence interval −0.67 to −0.23.)

*Step 2. Relative slowing,* using the placebo change as the base:

> **relative slowing = (placebo change − drug change) / placebo change = 0.45 / 1.66 = 0.271, about 27%**

Check: 1.66 × (1 − 0.271) = 1.66 × 0.729 = 1.21.

*Step 3, predict first.* A headline says the drug "slowed decline by 27%". Did the treated group still get worse, and what is the 27% a share of?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes. Its mean change was +1.21, a worsening. The 27% is a share of the placebo group's decline. It is not a 27% gain in memory, not a fraction of patients helped, and not anyone's prognosis. Slower worsening is still worsening. When the placebo group worsens on average, any relative slowing below 100% still leaves the treated group worsening on average.

</details>

### What it costs

Safety is part of the result. In TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 (Sims and colleagues, 2023), another antibody, donanemab, was tested in 1,736 people with early symptomatic AD and PET evidence of amyloid and tau. **ARIA with edema or effusion**, amyloid-related imaging abnormalities seen on MRI as fluid or swelling in the brain, occurred in 24.0% of donanemab recipients and 2.1% on placebo.

Report that two ways. As a ratio, 24.0 / 2.1 ≈ 11.4: it was about eleven times as common with the drug. As a gap, it is 21.9 percentage points, about one extra case for every 4.6 people treated over the trial's 76 weeks: that says how many people it touches. Three deaths with donanemab and one with placebo were considered treatment related.

Donanemab also slowed decline, by 35.1% in people with low or medium tau and by 22.3% in the combined population, but on a different scale (iADRS, the integrated Alzheimer's Disease Rating Scale) over 76 weeks. Setting 27% beside 35.1% does not rank the drugs: the scales, populations and durations differ.

### A biomarker is not a benefit

A slower rise in a biomarker, a lab or imaging measure of the disease process, is not the same as slower decline in the person. In the A4 study (Sperling and colleagues, 2023), cognitively unimpaired adults aged 65 to 85 with elevated amyloid received solanezumab, an antibody to the single-molecule (monomeric) form of Aβ, or placebo for 240 weeks. Amyloid PET is measured in **centiloids**, a standard scale on which 0 is the average of young adults without plaques and 100 is the average in typical Alzheimer dementia. It rose in both arms, by 11.6 centiloids with drug and 19.3 with placebo. So amyloid built up about 40% more slowly with the drug: (19.3 − 11.6) / 19.3 ≈ 0.40.

**Predict.** Did these people's thinking decline more slowly too?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not as far as the trial could show. Thinking declined in both groups, and the trial did not demonstrate slower decline with the drug. A biomarker effect alone does not establish clinical benefit.

</details>

ALS gives the same lesson from another angle. In a 28-week randomized trial in SOD1-mutant ALS, tofersen, an antisense drug that lowers SOD1 production, reduced SOD1 in cerebrospinal fluid and neurofilament light (a protein released from damaged axons) in plasma more than placebo, but clinical endpoints did not improve over those 28 weeks (Miller and colleagues, 2022). Going deeper gives the longer follow-up and the approval that rested on the neurofilament result.

### What none of the trials showed

None of these trials showed reversal of decline, return of lost neurons, or benefit beyond the populations and durations studied, and the Clarity AD authors called for longer trials. Many earlier anti-amyloid drugs did not meet their endpoints (reviewed by Selkoe and Hardy, 2016), and verubecestat, which blocks Aβ production, did not help people with early symptoms and left some scores worse than placebo (Egan and colleagues, 2019). Together the results support a contribution of amyloid that treatment can reach, without establishing amyloid as the sole cause.

## Why these neurons

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

No one yet knows why an entorhinal layer II neuron or an SNpc dopamine neuron fails before its neighbours. (In Parkinson disease, some brainstem neurons are affected even earlier than nigral ones.) The rule still applies: ask what each kind of evidence can support. A feature that goes with vulnerability is a **correlate**. Changing something in an animal model and seeing the disease course change is stronger, but it holds for that model. A mechanism needed after injury may or may not be needed in disease.

*A correlate.* Nigral dopamine neurons have very large axonal arbors and high energy demand, which may contribute to their vulnerability compared with neighbouring dopamine neurons of the ventral tegmental area.

*A manipulation in a model.* Selectivity also works within a cell class. **Gamma motor neurons**, small motor neurons that set the sensitivity of muscle spindles, were completely spared while alpha motor neurons degenerated in SOD1, TDP-43 and FUS mutant mouse models (Lalancette-Hebert and colleagues, 2016).

In the same study, genetically reducing the number of gamma motor neurons, and so the activation of spindle afferents (the sensory fibres from muscle spindles), delayed onset and prolonged lifespan in SOD1G93A mice, which overexpress a mutant human SOD1. So circuit input can change how fast a vulnerable neuron dies, at least in that model.

*A mechanism needed after injury.* Axons and synapses degenerate in injury and in these diseases alike, and injury offers a well-defined mechanism to compare. After a cut, the part of the axon cut off from the cell body (the distal axon) fragments in **Wallerian degeneration**, an active program that requires SARM1 in flies and mice (Osterloh and colleagues, 2012); the purified TIR domain of SARM1 has intrinsic NAD+-cleaving activity. The full story is in [injury and regeneration](/lessons/injury-and-regeneration/). Whether that program drives chronic disease is a separate question.

### Design problem

When Peters and colleagues (2018) crossed Sarm1-null mice (mice lacking the gene) with SOD1G93A mice, Sarm1 deletion did not improve survival or the behavioural, electrophysiological or histological outcomes they measured. A colleague concludes that axon degeneration does not matter in ALS. Is that justified, and what would you do next? Predict your answer before you open the box.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The conclusion goes too far. The 2012 result concerns a cut; it does not show that chronic disease uses the same program. Motor neurons in SOD1G93A mice may die by another route, such as an apoptotic pathway in the cell body, or axons might be saved in part without any gain in survival.

Design: measure separate endpoints in the same mice, with genotype hidden from the scorers. Count axons in ventral roots and peripheral nerve, score neuromuscular junction innervation, estimate motor units electrophysiologically (motor unit number estimation), count spinal motor neuron cell bodies, and record onset and survival.

Include a positive control, a check that the method can detect an effect, in a separate, matched cohort: cut a nerve and confirm that Sarm1-null axons resist degeneration, so a negative result in disease cannot be blamed on a failed knockout. Leave the disease-outcome mice uninjured, because a cut nerve would itself change motor and survival measures. Then test a second model, such as a TDP-43 model, because one SOD1 overexpression line is not human ALS. A clean answer would read: "Sarm1 loss protects cut axons in these mice but does not change the measured course of disease in this model."

</details>

## Going deeper

### How the dopamine subtypes were found

Kamath and colleagues (2022) sequenced RNA from single nuclei of postmortem human substantia nigra to sort the dopamine neurons, then mapped the populations within the substantia nigra with Slide-seq (a method that reads out RNA at known positions in a tissue section) in macaque midbrain, with human tissue used to confirm the locations.

### The founding families

The families behind the table: an APP variant cosegregating in a family (Goate and colleagues, 1991); SNCA in Italian and Greek-origin kindreds (1997); parkin deletions in Japanese juvenile parkinsonism (1998); 11 SOD1 missense mutations in 13 families (Rosen and colleagues, 1993); and a GGGGCC repeat expansion in C9ORF72 (2011).

### A protective variant: what genetics can say about lifelong change

A rare APP variant, A673T, reduces Aβ production and was found to protect carriers against Alzheimer disease (Jonsson and colleagues, 2012). That is human genetic evidence that supports the idea that lifelong lower Aβ production protects. It does not show that lowering Aβ in someone who is already ill will help, which is the question the trials ask.

### Is a 0.45-point gap meaningful?

Whether a gap of this size matters in daily life is openly debated. Andrews and colleagues (2019) estimated the smallest meaningful worsening in one person on this scale at about 1 point in mild cognitive impairment and about 1.6 in mild dementia. Those are thresholds for an individual's change, not for the gap between two group averages, so setting 0.45 beside them does not settle the question.

### A what-if: time saved

This is a what-if, not a trial result. If decline in each arm were a straight line over the 18 months, placebo would worsen by about 0.092 points a month and lecanemab by about 0.067. A drug that changes the *rate* of decline should then open a gap that grows with time: about 0.15 points at 6 months, 0.30 at 12 and 0.45 at 18. On the same assumption, the 0.45-point gap equals about 4.9 months of placebo decline (0.45 / (1.66 / 18)). Real decline need not be linear, so both figures depend on that assumption.

### Tofersen after 28 weeks

In the open-label extension, people who had started tofersen at entry had declined less at 52 weeks than those who started 28 weeks later, a hint that needs confirmation. In 2023 the US Food and Drug Administration granted tofersen accelerated approval on the basis of the fall in plasma neurofilament light, as a marker judged reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit. That is the treatment question in practice: a regulator accepted a biomarker while the clinical answer is still being sought.
