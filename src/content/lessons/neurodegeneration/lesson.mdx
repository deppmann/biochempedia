techniques:
  - name: "Pedigree linkage and cosegregation"
    blurb: >-
      You follow genetic markers and candidate variants through a family with an inherited
      disease and ask whether one variant travels with affected relatives and not with unaffected
      ones. Replication in an unrelated family strengthens the case that the variant causes disease
      in those families, not in every patient.
  - name: "Immunoelectron microscopy of extracted filaments"
    blurb: >-
      Insoluble filaments are extracted from postmortem brain, labelled with antibodies carrying
      gold particles, and imaged in the electron microscope. Gold on the filaments identifies a
      component. It tells you composition, not whether the filaments cause cell death.
  - name: "Axotomy with genetic loss of function"
    blurb: >-
      You cut an identified nerve in flies or mice lacking a gene and compare the distal axon with
      that of controls over days to weeks. Surviving axons show that the gene is needed for
      degeneration after injury, in that preparation.
  - name: "Randomized placebo-controlled trial"
    blurb: >-
      Participants are assigned at random, and blind, to drug or placebo and followed for a fixed
      time on a prespecified clinical endpoint, with biomarkers and safety measured alongside. The
      difference between arms estimates the drug's effect in the enrolled population.

**In the Clarity AD trial, people with early Alzheimer disease who received lecanemab for 18 months worsened by 1.21 points on a dementia rating scale. People on placebo worsened by 1.66. That gap is some of the strongest evidence that removing amyloid changes the disease. It is also evidence that the drug did not stop it.**

To read that result you need to know which neurons die, what clumps in them, and which genes cause disease or only raise risk. This lesson compares three diseases: Alzheimer disease (AD), Parkinson disease (PD) and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Then it tests the amyloid hypothesis against the trials and asks why some neurons fail while their neighbours survive. How a committed neuron dies is in [death machinery in neurons](/lessons/death-machinery-in-neurons/), and the cell's protein disposal systems are in [quality control and autophagy](/lessons/quality-control-and-autophagy/).

## Three diseases, three neuron populations

A **neurodegenerative disease** is a progressive loss of particular neurons in the adult nervous system. The important word is particular. **Selective vulnerability** means that the pathology strikes some neuronal populations and spares others, often close neighbours (reviewed by Fu, Hardy and Duff, 2018).

In **Alzheimer disease**, neurons lost early include the pyramidal neurons of layer II of the **entorhinal cortex**, the main gateway between neocortex and hippocampus, and pyramidal neurons of hippocampal field CA1. Both belong to the circuit for new declarative memories ([memory systems](/lessons/memory-systems/)).

In **Parkinson disease**, the defining loss is of dopamine neurons in the **substantia nigra pars compacta** (SNpc), the midbrain nucleus whose axons supply the striatum. Their loss unbalances the loops described in [the basal ganglia](/lessons/basal-ganglia/). Even "dopamine neurons" is too broad. Kamath and colleagues (2022) sequenced RNA from single nuclei of postmortem human substantia nigra, sorted the dopamine neurons into ten populations, and mapped them within the substantia nigra with Slide-seq in macaque midbrain, with human tissue used to confirm the locations. One population, marked by expression of the gene AGTR1, was highly susceptible to loss in PD.

In **ALS**, the targets are motor neurons. **Upper motor neurons** have their cell bodies in motor cortex and send axons down the corticospinal tract to the spinal cord and the corticobulbar projection to brainstem motor nuclei. **Lower motor neurons** sit in the brainstem and the ventral horn of the spinal cord and send axons to muscle. Both the cell bodies and their axons degenerate. As lower motor neurons die, whole **motor units** (one motor neuron and all the muscle fibres it supplies; see [motor units and reflexes](/lessons/motor-units-reflexes/)) drop out.

## Proteins that clump

Each disease has a protein that misfolds and gathers into insoluble deposits seen at autopsy.

- **AD** has two. **Amyloid plaques** lie outside cells and contain **amyloid-β (Aβ)**, peptides cut from amyloid precursor protein (APP) by β-secretase and then γ-secretase. **Neurofibrillary tangles** lie inside neurons and contain misfolded, hyperphosphorylated **tau**, a protein that normally binds microtubules.
- **PD** has **Lewy bodies** and Lewy neurites, inclusions in neuronal cell bodies and processes. In 1998 Spillantini, Goedert and colleagues extracted Lewy body filaments from postmortem brain, labelled them with anti-α-synuclein antibodies under the electron microscope, and found **α-synuclein** to be the major filament component.
- **ALS** in most cases has inclusions of **TDP-43**, an RNA-binding protein that leaves the nucleus and gathers in the cytoplasm. Neumann and colleagues identified it in 2006. There are exceptions: in a 2007 postmortem series, Mackenzie and colleagues found that ALS cases with SOD1 mutations lacked TDP-43 inclusions. In FUS-linked familial ALS, mutant FUS accumulates in neuronal cytoplasm.

A deposit tells you what a lesion is made of. It does not tell you the deposit kills the cell. Small soluble aggregates, loss of the protein's normal job, or the cell's response could do the damage. The idea that misfolded seeds pass from cell to cell and template more misfolding ("prion-like spread") is a hypothesis, tested mostly in animal and cell models. It concerns spread within one nervous system, not catching the disease from another person.

## Genes that cause and genes that raise risk

Genetics separates two kinds of evidence (see [pedigrees](/lessons/pedigrees/) and [complex traits and GWAS](/lessons/complex-traits-and-gwas/)). A **Mendelian** variant causes disease in most or all of those who carry it, though penetrance can be incomplete (for LRRK2 G2019S, one consortium estimate was 74% by age 79), and it segregates with disease through a family. A **risk allele** shifts the odds and is neither necessary nor sufficient.

| Disease | Mendelian genes (examples) | What the families showed |
|---|---|---|
| AD | APP, PSEN1, PSEN2 (dominant) | APP variant cosegregating in a family (Goate and colleagues, 1991) |
| PD | SNCA, LRRK2 (dominant); parkin (recessive, juvenile) | SNCA in Italian and Greek-origin kindreds (1997); parkin deletions in Japanese juvenile parkinsonism (1998) |
| ALS | SOD1, TARDBP, FUS; C9ORF72 repeat | 11 SOD1 missense mutations in 13 families (Rosen and colleagues, 1993); a GGGGCC repeat expansion in C9ORF72 (2011) |

**APOE ε4** is the best-known risk allele: it raises the risk of late-onset AD, yet even in old age it is not sufficient to produce clinical disease. The C9ORF72 change is an expanded stretch of noncoding DNA in an intron, not a change in a protein's sequence. Percentages need denominators: the often-quoted 46.0% for C9ORF72 refers to familial ALS in a Finnish study population.

The AD row has a pattern. Presenilin, the product of PSEN1 and PSEN2, is the catalytic core of γ-secretase, so all three dominant AD genes act on making Aβ. In 1992 Hardy and Higgins turned that into the **amyloid cascade hypothesis**: Aβ accumulation starts a process that leads to tangles, neuron loss and dementia. Mendelian genetics supports an initiating role for Aβ in familial disease. It does not show that Aβ alone explains the common late-onset form.

## Testing amyloid in trials

If Aβ starts the disease, removing it should change the course. Antibodies against Aβ have now tested that.

**Worked problem: what does "27% slowing" mean?** Clarity AD (van Dyck and colleagues, 2023) randomized 1,795 people with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to AD, with amyloid confirmed, to lecanemab or placebo for 18 months. The primary outcome was the change in **CDR-SB** (Clinical Dementia Rating, Sum of Boxes), a clinician's rating of cognition and daily function that runs from 0 to 18, higher meaning worse. Adjusted mean changes at 18 months were +1.21 points with lecanemab and +1.66 with placebo. Assume these group means are the quantities of interest.

*Step 1. Absolute difference.* 1.66 − 1.21 = 0.45 points less worsening. The paper subtracts the other way (lecanemab minus placebo), so it reports a difference of −0.45 points, 95% confidence interval −0.67 to −0.23.

*Step 2. Relative slowing,* using the placebo change as the base:

> **relative slowing = (placebo change − drug change) / placebo change = 0.45 / 1.66 = 0.271, about 27%**

Check: 1.66 × (1 − 0.271) = 1.66 × 0.729 = 1.21.

*Step 3. Did the treated group improve?* No. Its mean change was +1.21, a worsening. The 27% compares two amounts of decline. It is not a 27% gain in memory, not a fraction of patients helped, and not anyone's prognosis.

*Step 4. Safety is part of the result.* In TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 (Sims and colleagues, 2023), donanemab was tested in 1,736 people with early symptomatic AD and PET evidence of amyloid and tau. **ARIA with edema or effusion**, amyloid-related imaging abnormalities seen on MRI as fluid or swelling in the brain, occurred in 24.0% of donanemab recipients and 2.1% on placebo: 21.9 percentage points apart, a ratio of 24.0 / 2.1 ≈ 11.4. Three deaths with donanemab and one with placebo were considered treatment related. Donanemab slowed decline by 35.1% in people with low or medium tau and by 22.3% in the combined population, on a different scale (iADRS) over 76 weeks. Setting 27% beside 35.1% does not rank the drugs: the scales, populations and durations differ.

A negative result marks the limit. In the A4 study (Sperling and colleagues, 2023), cognitively unimpaired adults aged 65 to 85 with elevated amyloid received solanezumab, an antibody to the single-molecule (monomeric) form of Aβ, or placebo for 240 weeks. Cognitive decline was not slowed. Amyloid PET rose in both arms, by 11.6 centiloids with drug and 19.3 with placebo. A slower rise in a biomarker did not bring clinical benefit.

ALS gives the same lesson from another angle. In a 28-week randomized trial in SOD1-mutant ALS, tofersen, an antisense drug that lowers SOD1 production, reduced SOD1 in cerebrospinal fluid and neurofilament light (a protein released from damaged axons) in plasma more than placebo, but clinical endpoints did not improve over those 28 weeks (Miller and colleagues, 2022).

None of these trials showed reversal of decline, return of lost neurons, or benefit beyond the populations and durations studied, and the Clarity AD authors called for longer trials. Together the results support a contribution of amyloid that treatment can reach, without establishing amyloid as the sole cause.

## Why these neurons

No one yet knows why an entorhinal layer II neuron or an SNpc dopamine neuron fails first. Nigral dopamine neurons have very large axonal arbors and high energy demand, which may contribute to their vulnerability compared with neighbouring dopamine neurons of the ventral tegmental area.

Selectivity also works within a cell class. **Gamma motor neurons**, small motor neurons that set the sensitivity of muscle spindles, were completely spared while alpha motor neurons degenerated in SOD1, TDP-43 and FUS mutant mouse models (Lalancette-Hebert and colleagues, 2016). In the same study, genetically reducing the number of gamma motor neurons, and so the activation of spindle afferents, delayed onset and prolonged lifespan in SOD1G93A mice, which overexpress a mutant human SOD1. So circuit input can change how fast a vulnerable neuron dies, at least in that model.

Axons and synapses degenerate in injury and in these diseases alike, and injury offers a well-defined mechanism to compare. After a cut, the distal axon fragments in **Wallerian degeneration**, an active program that requires SARM1 in flies and mice; the purified TIR domain of SARM1 has intrinsic NAD+-cleaving activity. The full story is in [injury and regeneration](/lessons/injury-and-regeneration/). Whether that program drives chronic disease is a separate question, and one test came out negative.

### Design problem

Loss of Sarm1 preserves cut axons in flies and mice (Osterloh and colleagues, 2012). Yet when Peters and colleagues (2018) crossed Sarm1-null mice with SOD1G93A mice, Sarm1 deletion did not improve survival or the behavioural, electrophysiological or histological outcomes they measured. A colleague concludes that axon degeneration does not matter in ALS. Is that justified, and what would you do next?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The conclusion goes too far. The 2012 result shows Sarm1 is needed for Wallerian degeneration after a cut. It does not show that chronic disease uses the same program. Motor neurons in SOD1G93A mice may die by another route, such as an apoptotic pathway in the cell body, or axons might be saved in part without any gain in survival.

Design: measure separate endpoints in the same mice, with genotype hidden from the scorers. Count axons in ventral roots and peripheral nerve, score neuromuscular junction innervation, estimate motor units electrophysiologically (motor unit number estimation), count spinal motor neuron cell bodies, and record onset and survival. Include a positive control in the same animals: cut a nerve on one side and confirm that Sarm1-null axons resist degeneration there, so a negative result in disease cannot be blamed on a failed knockout. Then test a second model, such as a TDP-43 model, because one SOD1 overexpression line is not human ALS. A clean answer would read: "Sarm1 loss protects cut axons in these mice but does not change the measured course of disease in this model."
