summary: >-
  A developing neuron survives when the neurotrophin its target supplies reaches a Trk receptor, a receptor tyrosine kinase that starts survival signals. A neuron reads a neurotrophin through two kinds of receptor: three Trk kinases, each preferring particular factors, and one shared receptor, p75, from the TNF-receptor family. This lesson matches the four mammalian neurotrophins to their receptors, uses mouse knockouts to show which neurons need which pair, traces how the receptors were found, and explains how the uncut precursor of NGF can kill a cell that the trimmed protein would keep alive. Afterwards you can predict how a neuron is likely to respond from the form of the factor and the receptors it carries.
hook: "Many developing neurons live only if their target supplies enough of the right neurotrophin. How does the neuron read that factor, and how can the same factor sometimes kill?"

    story: >-
      Hempstead's name runs through both halves of the receptor story. In 1991 she was an author
      on the Science paper from Kaplan, Parada and colleagues showing that NGF
      binds the Trk protein and switches on its kinase, and first author on a Nature paper from the
      same collaboration, with Moses Chao, reporting that high-affinity NGF binding needed Trk and

      an author, with Anders Nykjær and Claus Munck Petersen, on the paper showing that the
      receptor sortilin is needed for that death. The work made the processing of a growth factor
      a step to be studied in its own right.
  - name: "Mariano Barbacid"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory found trk as part of an oncogene in a human colon carcinoma, cloned its

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Barbacid came to neurotrophins from cancer. In 1982 his group, with Prem Reddy, reported that
      a human bladder carcinoma oncogene, HRAS, differs from the normal gene by a single amino acid
      change. In 1986, with Dionisio Martin-Zanca and Stephen Hughes, he described an oncogene from
      a human colon carcinoma in which part of a tropomyosin gene had fused to a gene for a
      tyrosine kinase. The normal version, named trk, encoded a membrane receptor with no known
      ligand. In 1989 Ruediger Klein, working in the laboratory, reported a relative, trkB. In
      1991 the group published papers showing that Trk is a receptor for NGF, that TrkB answers
      BDNF and NT-3, and, with Fabienne Lamballe, that a third relative, TrkC, prefers NT-3. Between
      1993 and 1994 the laboratory reported mice lacking TrkB, TrkA and TrkC in turn. Those mice
      turned a set of binding preferences into a map of which neurons need which receptor.
  - name: "Yves-Alain Barde"
    contribution: >-
      With Deborah Edgar and Hans Thoenen purified brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) from pig
      brain in 1982, the second neurotrophin, and later helped show by cloning that BDNF and NGF
      belong to one family.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Barde, Edgar & Thoenen, EMBO J. 1:549 (1982); Leibrock et al., Nature 341:149 (1989)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      For three decades NGF was the only molecule of its kind. Many neurons, including some
      sensory neurons, could not be kept alive by it, which suggested other factors existed.
      Working with Deborah Edgar and Hans Thoenen, Barde purified a protein from pig brain that
      supported the survival of cultured sensory neurons from chick embryos, and the three
      reported it in 1982 as a new neurotrophic factor from mammalian brain. Brain tissue holds
      very little of it, and its sequence came only in 1989, when Barde was an author on the
      paper that cloned BDNF and showed that it resembles NGF. That resemblance meant NGF was the
      first member of a family. Within two years, searches for genes resembling both NGF and BDNF
      had found neurotrophin-3 and neurotrophin-4, and the question became which receptor
      recognised which factor. Barde's group later reported, in chick retina, that NGF acting
      through p75 can kill cells, an early sign that the shared receptor carries a death signal.
  - name: "Moses Chao"
    contribution: >-
      Cloned the human low-affinity NGF receptor, p75, in 1986, was an author on the 1991 papers
      that linked TrkA and p75 in high-affinity binding, and reported that NGF kills cultured
      oligodendrocytes through p75.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Johnson et al., Cell 47:545 (1986); Casaccia-Bonnefil et al., Nature 383:716 (1996); Chao, Nat. Rev. Neurosci. 4:299 (2003)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The first neurotrophin receptor to be cloned was the one that could not explain survival
      by itself. In 1986 Chao and colleagues transferred human DNA into mouse cells, found cells that
      made the human receptor, and isolated its gene; a second paper that year reported its sequence, with cysteine-rich repeats outside the cell and no
      kinase domain inside. Eric Shooter's laboratory reported the rat receptor the following
      year. This protein, p75, bound NGF with low affinity, and neurons were known to have
      higher-affinity sites as well. Chao was an author on the 1991 papers that showed how the
      p75 and Trk proteins relate in binding NGF. In 1996, with Patrizia Casaccia-Bonnefil and
      colleagues, he reported that NGF kills cultured rat oligodendrocytes, which lack TrkA,
      through p75. His 2003 review in Nature Reviews Neuroscience presents the system as one
      shared receptor and three kinases that converge on many signaling pathways.

techniques:
  - name: "Radioligand binding"
    blurb: >-
      Cells are incubated with a radioactively labelled factor at a range of concentrations, and
      the bound label is counted. Plotting bound against free ligand gives the number of binding
      sites and their dissociation constant, which is how neurons were shown to carry two classes
      of NGF site.
  - name: "Expressing a receptor in cells that lack it"
    blurb: >-
      The gene for one receptor is put into fibroblasts that do not normally respond to
      neurotrophins. If a factor now binds, phosphorylates the receptor and changes the cell's
      behaviour, that receptor is sufficient for the response in those cells.
  - name: "Gene knockout mice"
    blurb: >-
      A gene is disrupted in mouse embryonic stem cells, and the change is bred into a line of mice
      lacking it. Counting neurons in each ganglion of the mutant at several ages, alongside counts of dying and dividing cells, shows which populations need that
      factor or receptor, and at what stage.
  - name: "Cleavage-resistant precursor"
    blurb: >-
      The cut site between the pro-domain and the mature protein, and the other cut sites in the pro-domain, are mutated so that proteases
      cannot trim it. Comparing this form with mature protein shows what the precursor does on its
      own.

    choices:
      - "About 50% of the 10 pM sites and about 1% of the 1 nM sites"
      - "About 1% of the 10 pM sites and about 50% of the 1 nM sites"
      - "About 99% of the 10 pM sites and about 10% of the 1 nM sites"
      - "About 50% of the 10 pM sites and about 50% of the 1 nM sites"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      For the 10 pM sites, 10 / (10 + 10) is 0.5. For the 1 nM sites, 10 / (10 + 1,000) is about
      0.01. So at a low free concentration such as 10 pM, the high-affinity sites are about fifty times more fully
      occupied, as a fraction of sites. Swapping the two values reverses which constant is lower. Half of each
      ignores the hundredfold difference in Kd. Ninety-nine percent would need NGF far above the
      Kd, and 10% misreads 10 pM as a tenth of 1 nM rather than a hundredth.

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm five"
    text: >-
      Nerve cells need little packets of food from the places they connect to, or they die. There
      are four kinds of packet, and each nerve cell has its own kind of mouth for the kind it eats.
      Many nerve cells also have one more mouth that can taste all the packets. If a packet arrives
      before it has been unwrapped, that extra mouth can tell the cell to die instead.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Neurotrophins are proteins that keep particular nerve cells alive. Mammals make four: NGF,
      BDNF, NT-3 and NT-4. A nerve cell senses them with receptors on its surface. Three receptors,
      called TrkA, TrkB and TrkC, each prefer certain neurotrophins and send a survival signal
      when they bind them. A fourth receptor, p75, binds all of them. When scientists removed one
      neurotrophin or one Trk receptor from mice, particular groups of nerve cells were lost, and
      different factor-receptor pairs mattered for different groups. Neurotrophins are made first as longer precursors. If the precursor of NGF is not cut,
      it binds p75 and its partner sortilin tightly and, in cells with little TrkA, can tell the cell to die, while the cut form binds TrkA and keeps a TrkA-carrying cell alive.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      The four mammalian neurotrophins are dimeric proteins with a shared fold. NGF prefers TrkA,
      BDNF and NT-4 prefer TrkB, and NT-3 prefers TrkC while also activating TrkA and TrkB more
      weakly. Trk receptors are receptor tyrosine kinases: ligand-induced dimerization leads to
      autophosphorylation and engagement of Ras-ERK, PI3K-Akt and PLC-gamma pathways that support
      survival and growth. p75, a TNF-receptor family member with a death domain, binds all four
      neurotrophins with lower affinity and can modify Trk binding. Mouse knockouts show
      population-specific losses: sympathetic and nociceptive neurons without NGF or TrkA,
      vestibular and nodose-petrosal neurons without BDNF or TrkB, proprioceptors without NT-3 or
      TrkC. Neurotrophins are secreted partly as precursors; Lee and colleagues reported in 2001
      that proNGF is cleaved outside the cell by plasmin and matrix metalloproteinases, and that
      uncleaved proNGF binds p75 with high affinity and, with the co-receptor sortilin, triggers
      apoptosis of cultured sympathetic neurons.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      Equilibrium binding on sensory neurons and PC12 cells revealed NGF sites with Kd near
      10^-11 M and near 10^-9 M. p75 alone gives low-affinity sites, and in 1991 the
      high-affinity class was attributed either to TrkA with p75 (Hempstead et al.) or to TrkA
      itself, which Klein et al. reported could bind NGF with high affinity in nonneural cells without p75
      and which was sufficient for NGF-driven mitogenesis in fibroblasts (Cordon-Cardo et al.). Structures show that NGF binds the second immunoglobulin-like domain
      of TrkA and the cysteine-rich domains of p75 at different surfaces, and the stoichiometry and
      geometry of any TrkA-p75 complex in neurons remain unsettled. Selectivity among Trk
      receptors is set partly by contacts at the neurotrophin amino terminus and partly by receptor
      isoforms; neuron-specific TrkA and TrkB splice forms, and kinase-truncated TrkB and TrkC,
      tune responses.


      Precursor signaling adds a ligand-processing variable. Uncleaved proNGF and proBDNF bind a
      p75-sortilin complex in which sortilin engages the pro-domain and p75 the mature domain, and
      this complex can drive JNK activation and apoptosis in cultured neurons and in some injury
      models. An open question is how much uncut precursor reaches receptors in a developing animal,
      at which stages and in which tissues, and whether extracellular processing is regulated
      enough to act as a developmental switch rather than a background rate.

anecdotes:
  - title: "A tumour gene named for a muscle protein"
    source: "Martin-Zanca, Hughes & Barbacid, Nature 319:743 (1986)."
    text: >-
      The first Trk gene was found in a colon carcinoma, not a neuron. Dionisio Martin-Zanca,
      Stephen Hughes and Mariano Barbacid reported in 1986 that the tumour's oncogene was a fusion:
      the front of a gene for a non-muscle tropomyosin joined to the kinase part of an unknown
      receptor. The fusion kept the kinase on without any ligand. The name trk comes from that
      fusion, for tropomyosin receptor kinase. Five years passed before NGF was shown to be the
      ligand for the normal receptor, which is why a growth factor for nerve cells has a receptor
      named after a tumour.
  - title: "Looking for one gene, finding its relatives"
    source: "Klein et al., EMBO J. 8:3701 (1989); Lamballe, Klein & Barbacid, Cell 66:967 (1991); Sedwick, J. Cell Biol. 194:162 (2011)."
    text: >-
      In a 2011 interview, Ruediger Klein recalled screening a mouse brain library for trk and
      recovering a related gene instead. That gene, trkB, was reported in 1989 by Klein, Luis
      Parada, François Coulier and Barbacid. A later search, with Fabienne Lamballe, used both
      known sequences and found a third relative, trkC, reported in 1991. In Klein's account the
      original trk was hard to find in brain because its message is scarce there and more abundant
      in peripheral sensory neurons. A search for one receptor had turned up a family of three.

**Many developing neurons live only if their target supplies enough of the right neurotrophin, and each neuron reads that factor with a Trk receptor, a kinase that switches on survival signals.**

The [last lesson](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/) left you with a rule: a target makes a limited amount of a survival factor, and neurons that fail to get enough die. This lesson follows the factor to the receptors that read it.

## The layout

Most of this lesson happens at one cell. Picture a sympathetic neuron in the superior cervical ganglion, a cluster of nerve cell bodies in the neck. Its axon runs out to a target, such as a blood vessel or a gland, that makes only a small amount of nerve growth factor (NGF). Many neurons send axons to that same target.

NGF is one of four mammalian **neurotrophins**: NGF, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), neurotrophin-3 (NT-3) and neurotrophin-4 (NT-4). Each is a **dimer**, two identical chains held together, and all four share one fold.

A neuron reads them with two kinds of receptor.

- **Three Trk receptors** (pronounced "track"): TrkA, TrkB and TrkC. Each is a receptor tyrosine kinase, and each prefers particular factors.
- **One shared receptor, p75** (also written p75NTR). It binds all four factors and has no kinase. It belongs to the family of the tumour necrosis factor (TNF) receptor, and it is a relative of Fas, the death receptor from [that lesson](/lessons/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases/). Like Fas, it carries a **death domain**, a stretch inside the cell that can bind proteins that lead toward apoptosis, though not the same proteins Fas uses.

| Neurotrophin | The Trk receptor it prefers |
|---|---|
| NGF | TrkA |
| BDNF | TrkB |
| NT-4 | TrkB |
| NT-3 | TrkC, and more weakly TrkA and TrkB |

All four also bind p75. NGF is made first as a longer precursor, proNGF, with an extra stretch at its front, the pro-domain, which proteases cut off to leave the mature protein. What p75 does depends on its partners: with TrkA it can work alongside the kinase, and with sortilin, a membrane protein that grips the pro-domain, it can kill.

The map raises two puzzles.

1. **How does a neuron know which factor is its own?**
2. **How can one factor keep one cell alive and kill another?** Add mature NGF to cultured sympathetic neurons and they survive. Add the same protein before its pro-domain has been cut off, and in Barbara Hempstead's 2001 experiments the neurons died.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** The target supplies the factor; the neuron's receptors decide what it means. A Trk receptor with its ligand usually reads a neurotrophin as "stay alive". p75 can read it as "die" in some cells, and with its partner sortilin it reads the uncut precursor that way. Cutting the precursor removes the stretch sortilin grips, so the cut changes which receptors the factor works through best.

**What the rule covers.** The survival and death of developing neurons, and of neurons and glia in culture, in the systems used here: sympathetic and sensory ganglia in mice, cultured sympathetic neurons, oligodendrocytes and the chick retina. It has three limits. "Decides" means the whole set of receptors and how much of each, not one receptor alone. It does not say how much uncut precursor reaches neurons in a normal embryo, which is still unsettled. And it leaves out the neurotrophins' other jobs, such as axon growth, BDNF acting through TrkB at adult synapses and p75's roles in axon growth and pruning, as well as reports that TrkA and TrkC without ligand can signal death in some neurons.

## How a Trk receptor turns on

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The factor dimer has two binding faces, so it can hold two Trk receptors at once and bring them together. Their kinase domains, now side by side, phosphorylate each other on tyrosines, as in [the lesson on receptor tyrosine kinases](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/). The phosphorylated tyrosines become docking sites that start two survival branches you have met: the Ras to ERK cascade ([that lesson](/lessons/ras-to-mapk/)) and the PI3K to Akt route ([that lesson](/lessons/pi3k-akt-insulin/)).

That answers the first puzzle in outline: a neuron answers the factor whose Trk receptor it makes. A neuron that makes TrkA is switched on by NGF, one that makes TrkC by NT-3. The factor does not know which neuron it is for; the receptor sorts it.

## Where the receptors came from

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

**The first neurotrophin receptor cloned could not explain survival.** In 1986 Moses Chao and colleagues isolated the gene for the human NGF receptor by transferring human DNA into mouse cells, finding cells that made the human receptor, and cloning the gene. Eric Shooter's laboratory reported the rat receptor in 1987. This protein, p75, bound NGF with low affinity, and it had no kinase. Neurons were known to have higher-affinity sites as well, and on its own p75 could not account for them. Which protein makes those sites was the puzzle of the 1980s.

**The kinase came from a tumour.** In 1986 Mariano Barbacid's laboratory described an oncogene from a human colon carcinoma in which part of a tropomyosin gene had fused to the gene for a tyrosine kinase ([that lesson](/lessons/oncogenes/)). The fusion kept the kinase on without any ligand. The normal gene, *trk*, encoded a membrane receptor with no known ligand. If DCC from [the netrin lesson](/lessons/netrin-and-dcc/) comes to mind, it should: here again a gene first studied in cancer turned out to have a job in wiring the nervous system.

**Predict.** A fibroblast makes no Trk receptor and does not respond to NGF. Give it the gene for the normal Trk protein, then add NGF. What should NGF do to the Trk protein, and what would that tell you about what Trk is sufficient for?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In spring 1991 two groups showed that NGF binds the Trk protein and causes its tyrosine phosphorylation: David Kaplan, Barbara Hempstead, Dionisio Martin-Zanca, Moses Chao and Luis Parada, and, separately, Ruediger Klein, Barbacid and colleagues. Barbacid's group also found that fibroblasts carrying Trk divided in response to NGF without needing p75. The orphan kinase from a tumour was a receptor for NGF, and in those cells it was enough for the factor to be read.

</details>

The original Trk protein is now called TrkA, because two relatives, TrkB and TrkC, were found soon after. How p75 and TrkA together give high-affinity binding was disputed and is still not settled; Going deeper follows the dispute.

## Three deletions, three populations

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

A knockout mouse is one in which a chosen gene, or part of it, has been disrupted so that it makes no working protein, or no working form of one protein. Deleting each neurotrophin or Trk gene in mice, one at a time, sorts the family by which neurons are lost. Start with the best-followed case.

**Predict.** In mice lacking TrkA, Anne Fagan and colleagues followed the superior cervical ganglion through development. At embryonic day 13.5 it had a normal number of cells. If TrkA is needed for the neurons to survive, rather than to be made, what should the neuron counts, the dividing cells and the dying cells do from then on?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The mutant ganglion fell steadily behind: about 15% fewer cells at day 15.5, 35% fewer at day 17.5 and about half as many neurons as control ganglia at birth. Over those embryonic ages, dividing cells (mitotic figures) were no fewer than in controls, while dying cells, with shrunken, condensed (pyknotic) nuclei, increased. By postnatal day 9 the ganglion had lost nearly all of its principal neurons. The neurons were made and then died.

</details>

The other pairs give a map. "Lost" means fewer neurons: usually part of a ganglion survives, but some defined groups, like the TrkA-null sympathetic neurons above, are lost almost entirely.

| Mice lacking | Neurons lost |
|---|---|
| NGF or TrkA | sympathetic neurons; many small sensory neurons that sense pain and heat |
| BDNF | vestibular neurons, which sense balance and head movement; many nodose-petrosal neurons, which carry signals from the heart, lungs and gut |
| NT-4 | nodose-petrosal neurons |
| the kinase-containing form of TrkB | neurons in both the central and peripheral nervous systems |
| NT-3 or TrkC | proprioceptive afferents, the sensory neurons that report where a limb is; the mice move and hold their limbs abnormally |

One result marks the edge of the map. In NGF-null mice the basal forebrain cholinergic neurons still develop.

Each factor-receptor pair has its own pattern of loss, though deleting a factor and its main receptor removes many of the same neurons. The four factors share a fold and a co-receptor, but to a neuron they are separate signals.

**Predict.** NT-3 prefers TrkC, but it can also activate TrkA and TrkB. Should a mouse lacking NT-3 lose more neurons or fewer than a mouse lacking TrkC?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Expect more. Removing TrkC takes away only NT-3's action through TrkC, while removing NT-3 also takes away its weaker help through TrkA and TrkB. In many ganglia compared at the same age, NT-3-null mice do lose more neurons than TrkC-null mice, though the size of the gap depends on the ganglion, the age and which form of TrkC was deleted. The map has overlaps like this, and some populations need more than one factor at different stages.

</details>

## The receptor that can kill

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

Every factor binds p75, yet nothing so far says what p75 does on its own. In the mid-1990s two groups found that it can kill. Barde's group reported that NGF kills cells in the developing chick retina through p75, and Chao's group reported that NGF kills cultured rat oligodendrocytes, the myelin-making cells of the brain and spinal cord, which lack TrkA. A cell with p75 and no Trk to read the factor as "stay alive" can read it as "die". In the retina the NGF was the tissue's own: an antibody to NGF reduced the death. The oligodendrocytes were given NGF in culture, where doses are often nanomolar; the last section shows why that matters.

### The uncut precursor

Like most secreted proteins, NGF starts as a longer precursor, and the accepted view had been that its pro-domain is trimmed inside the cell. In 2001 Ramee Lee, Pouneh Kermani, Kenneth Teng and Barbara Hempstead reported that cells secrete proNGF uncut, and that it can be cut outside the cell by plasmin, a protease made from the blood protein plasminogen, and by particular matrix metalloproteinases.

To ask what the precursor does on its own, mutate its cut sites so that proteases cannot trim it, and compare it with mature NGF at the same concentration.

Keep the scope the papers give. Precursor killing was shown in cultured neurons, in cells of the oligodendrocyte lineage and in rodent injury models. How much it contributes to the normal deaths of development is less clear. Fahnestock and colleagues found that uncleavable proNGF can keep sympathetic neurons alive through TrkA, though about fivefold less well than mature NGF, so the outcome depends on the cell's balance of TrkA, p75 and sortilin. What happens after the death signal starts is the subject of [that lesson](/lessons/death-machinery-in-neurons/).

## Reading one factor two ways

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

The second puzzle now has two halves that seem to clash: mature NGF keeps sympathetic neurons alive yet killed TrkA-lacking cells through p75, and proNGF killed sympathetic neurons in one lab but kept them weakly alive in another. Binding arithmetic helps with the first clash. The second turns on the pro-domain, and why the labs disagreed is still open (Going deeper).

Binding studies on chick embryo sensory neurons found two classes of NGF site, with dissociation constants (Kd) of about 20 picomolar and 2 nanomolar. Treat these as orders of magnitude: about 10 pM (10⁻¹¹ M) for the high-affinity sites and about 1 nM (10⁻⁹ M) for p75 alone, a hundredfold apart. The model treats the high-affinity sites as Trk-linked, setting aside whether p75 helps make them. Kd is the free concentration at which half the sites are occupied. For simple binding:

> fraction of sites occupied = [NGF] / ([NGF] + Kd)

**Predict.** Give mature NGF to a cell at 10 pM, a low concentration chosen for illustration. About what fraction of the Trk-linked sites fill, and what fraction of the p75 sites? Now raise NGF to about 2 nM. Which sites gain the most?

</details>

Targets supply small amounts of factor, so the high-affinity sites are likely the ones that matter most for survival. Culture experiments often add NGF at around 50 nanograms per millilitre, which for a dimer of about 26.5 kilodaltons is about 2 nM. At that level many p75-alone sites are occupied, which fits mature NGF acting through p75 in TrkA-lacking cells in a dish.

Keep the model's limits in view. The percentages are fractions of each class of site, not counts of receptors or strengths of signal, and the model has no threshold for survival or death. Nor can it explain the precursor: in the worked example, at a dose where mature NGF added no death, the uncleavable precursor killed. What the precursor adds is the pro-domain, which sortilin grips. This lesson puts no binding numbers on proNGF; its effects are compared by experiment instead.

Put the pieces together and three variables go a long way toward deciding what one neurotrophin does at one cell.

1. **Whether the ligand has been cut.** Uncut proNGF can kill through p75 with sortilin; mature NGF works best through Trk. Neither form is confined to one receptor, as both clashes showed. Enzymes inside and outside the cell decide which form arrives.
2. **Which receptors the cell makes.** Its Trk receptor picks the factor. p75 binds every factor, and what it does with them depends on partners such as sortilin and TrkA.
3. **How much of each it makes.** The ratio of p75 and sortilin to TrkA changes what the same ligand does. With TrkA, p75 may also raise NGF binding in some cells, a point still argued.

### Design problem

Your purified "NGF" kills cultured cells that express p75 but little TrkA, while a second batch keeps sympathetic neurons alive. Design the experiments that find out why.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with the protein itself. Run both batches on a gel and probe a western blot with an antibody to NGF, and with one to the pro-domain. If the killing batch carries a larger band that the pro-domain antibody recognises, it contains uncut proNGF.

Next, separate the two forms. Make a proNGF with its cleavage sites mutated so that proteases cannot trim it, and test it beside pure mature NGF at the same concentration on both kinds of cell. Prediction: the uncleavable precursor kills the cells with p75 but little TrkA, while mature NGF does not. On sympathetic neurons mature NGF should support survival; the precursor may kill them or support them weakly, since groups have found both.

Then test the receptors. Block p75 with an antibody, or use cells from p75-null mice, and block sortilin, for example with an inhibitor that blocks its binding to the pro-domain or by knocking it down. If killing by the precursor disappears when either partner is gone, the precursor acts through p75 with sortilin.

Last, ask how the first batch came to contain precursor. Add plasmin to it; if the killing activity falls as the precursor band is converted to mature NGF, the difference between the batches was processing, not two different genes.

</details>

## Putting it together

Both puzzles have one answer: the target supplies the factor, and the receptors give it its meaning. Knockouts show each Trk pair keeping its own populations alive, and p75, with sortilin for the uncut precursor, lets the same factor kill.

One step remains. How competing neurons use p75 against each other is the subject of [competition for targets](/lessons/competition-for-targets/).

## Going deeper

### The dispute over high-affinity NGF binding

The 1991 papers did not end the argument. Hempstead and colleagues reported that high-affinity NGF binding required TrkA and p75 expressed together. Barbacid's group, besides the fibroblast result above, reported high-affinity NGF binding to TrkA in nonneural cells that lacked p75. So the groups disagreed about binding itself, and the debate ran for years. A 2022 review concludes that p75 and TrkA form a complex that gives high-affinity NGF binding. A 2007 structural study found no direct contact between the two receptors on cells and proposed that they cooperate through shared signaling partners instead.

### How BDNF, NT-3, NT-4, TrkB and TrkC were found

**The other factors came from brain.** In 1982 Yves-Alain Barde, Deborah Edgar and Hans Thoenen purified BDNF from pig brain as a protein that kept cultured chick sensory neurons alive. Its cloning in 1989 showed that it resembles NGF, and searches for genes resembling both found NT-3 in 1990 and NT-4 in 1991. Klein, in Barbacid's laboratory, had already cloned a relative of *trk*, *trkB*, in 1989. In 1991 three groups showed that TrkB is a receptor for BDNF and NT-3, and Fabienne Lamballe, Klein and Barbacid reported a third relative, TrkC, that prefers NT-3. NT-4 was shown to act through TrkB soon after.

### Names and counts

NT-4 is called NT-4/5 in older papers. Some fish have extra neurotrophins (NT-6 and NT-7), which is why the count of four is given for mammals.

### p75 and Fas: a family resemblance

p75's death domain is a family resemblance to Fas, not the same machinery. Fas kills through the adaptor FADD and caspase-8. p75's death signalling, including that of the p75-sortilin complex bound to a precursor, runs through other adaptors and activation of JNK.

### Gene dosage matters

Many mice with one copy of the BDNF gene eat more and become obese, and mice with one copy of the NGF gene show shrinkage of basal forebrain cholinergic neurons and poorer memory. Half the factor is not always enough. The obesity is an adult phenotype of the hypothalamus, outside the developmental rule of this lesson.

### Unsettled details

Mice lacking TrkB. Most mice lacking the kinase-containing form of TrkB die within a day of birth. A study of these mice also reported fewer motor neurons, but mice lacking both TrkB ligands, BDNF and NT-4, kept their facial motor neurons, so whether motor neurons need TrkB during development is unsettled.

Why groups disagreed about proNGF. Possible reasons include the dose, the cells' balance of receptors, how the precursor was prepared and how much of it was cut during the experiment. A 2008 study by Boutilier and colleagues found that TrkA activation by proNGF needed the precursor to be processed.
