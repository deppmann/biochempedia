techniques:
  - name: "Reporter lines and intersectional silencing"
    blurb: >-
      Mouse lines in which a genetic driver switches on a fluorescent reporter label cells that
      express a marker gene. Combining two drivers restricts the label, or a silencing tool such
      as tetanus toxin, to cells in the spinal cord. The result tells you which cells have the
      marker and, for silencing, which behaviours need that lineage, not that the marked cells are
      one pure cell type.
  - name: "Whole-cell patch clamp in spinal slices"
    blurb: >-
      A glass pipette records voltage or current from a single labelled neuron in a thin slice of
      mouse spinal cord. Injecting current shows the cell's firing pattern, such as an initial
      burst, delayed spiking, tonic firing or gaps. The preparation is from young animals and
      cut off from most of its inputs, although some slices keep dorsal roots attached so afferents can be stimulated.
  - name: "In vivo multielectrode recording with antidromic identification"
    blurb: >-
      Electrode arrays record spikes in anaesthetized mice while a motorized probe indents or
      vibrates the skin. Stimulating a neuron's output axon at a candidate target and checking that the evoked spike
      collides with a spontaneous one proves that the neuron projects to that target. This labels neurons by target,
      such as thalamus or inferior colliculus.
  - name: "Reversible input silencing"
    blurb: >-
      A light-gated silencer (ACR1) in afferent terminals, or a glutamate receptor antagonist
      applied to the lumbar cord, removes one route's contribution to a recorded neuron while
      leaving the other route in place. Each tool has limits of efficacy and specificity that must
      be checked before reading the result.

      A PSDC neuron is a spinal neuron, so it receives synaptic input in the dorsal horn and then

      relay neuron lies beyond the brainstem and is not in the dorsal column at all.
  - stem: "In anaesthetized adult mice, the same afferent input that excites a postsynaptic dorsal column neuron also excites an interneuron that then inhibits it. Which motif is this, and which interneurons supplied it in Chirila and colleagues' study?"
    choices:
      - "Presynaptic inhibition, supplied by PVi neurons"
      - "Feedforward inhibition, supplied by glycinergic Rorβ interneurons"
      - "Feedforward inhibition, supplied by Cdh3 neurons on afferent terminals"
      - "Presynaptic inhibition, supplied by glycinergic Rorβ interneurons"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      When the input that excites a neuron also excites an interneuron that inhibits it, excitation is followed by inhibition: that is feedforward inhibition, and Rorβ interneurons supplied glycinergic feedforward inhibition onto PSDC neurons. Presynaptic inhibition is applied to the afferent terminal and scales down the transmitter it releases, which is a different motif. PVi neurons form axo-axonic contacts on afferent terminals and take part in presynaptic inhibition, but they are not its only source. Cdh3 neurons form axo-axonic contacts as well, and that is a presynaptic arrangement, not feedforward.

**When the skin of a mouse's hindpaw is pressed, the brainstem can hear about it twice.** Once from the touch afferent's own axon, which climbs the spinal cord without a synapse. And once from a neuron in the spinal cord that has already worked on the signal.

What does the second report add? Three mouse studies, from 2017 and 2022, answer the question.

## Two routes on one drawing

[Touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) draws the dorsal column-medial lemniscus pathway as a blue line. The first-order axon ascends on the same side and synapses in the gracile or cuneate nucleus (together, the dorsal column nuclei, DCN). The second-order axon crosses in the medulla and reaches the ventral posterolateral thalamus (VPL). That route is correct. This lesson starts below the drawing, in the cord, and follows touch from the hindlimb to the gracile nucleus.

Picture the cross-section of the cord again: the grey butterfly, with the dorsal horn at the top and the white-matter dorsal column above it. The touch afferents that matter here are **Aβ low-threshold mechanoreceptors** (Aβ-LTMRs; the LTMR classes are sorted in [the LTMR lesson](/lessons/ltmr-classes/)). As each one enters, it does two things.

- **The direct route.** Its main branch climbs the dorsal column to the gracile nucleus with no synapse on the way. Keep drawing this in blue.
- **The indirect route.** Other branches end in the dorsal horn, a layered region where what happens is a circuit, not a handoff. There the afferent drives local interneurons and, through them and in places directly, a spinal neuron whose own axon climbs the same dorsal column to the same nucleus. Draw this in gold.

Other LTMR classes have no direct branch to the gracile nucleus; they can contribute through spinal circuits.

That spinal neuron is a **postsynaptic dorsal column (PSDC) neuron**: a spinal neuron that receives input in the dorsal horn and sends an axon up the dorsal column to the DCN. PSDC neurons are not a new discovery. Recordings in cats described their complex receptive fields by the early 1980s. What the mouse work adds is genetic access, and a test of what each route carries.

In Touch and Pain the blue line was one axon to the medulla. Below that drawing there are two.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Two routes, one patch of skin. A touch reaches the gracile nucleus by two routes. The direct route is the afferent's own axon, and it keeps precise timing. The indirect route runs through the dorsal horn, where local circuits rebuild the signal; in the illustrative model below (equal cord-to-brainstem paths, matched final synapses, a PSDC axon no faster than the afferent) it arrives a few milliseconds later, and it reports sustained pressure, up to high forces. The two reports describe the same patch of skin and meet on single brainstem neurons.

The lesson builds it in three steps, then marks its limits: the experiment that showed what each route carries, the arithmetic of what a slower route costs, and what the dorsal horn does to the signal on the way.

**Predict.** You can record from one gracile neuron while you touch the paw, and both routes feed it. How would you find out what each route contributes?

## The key experiment: silence one route at a time

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Turecek, Lehnert and Ginty (Nature, 2022) recorded single neurons in the hindlimb part of the gracile nucleus of adult, urethane-anaesthetized mice. They picked out the neurons that project to the thalamus (VPL) by **antidromic activation**: stimulating their axons there and testing for collision with a spontaneous spike, which proves the projection.

Then they took one route away at a time.

- **To remove the direct route,** they silenced afferent terminals reversibly with a light-gated chloride channel, ACR1. The light was aimed at the gracile nucleus, sparing the afferents' branches in the cord.
- **To remove the indirect route,** they applied glutamate receptor antagonists to the lumbar cord. That blocks the spinal synapses that drive PSDC neurons, while the afferents' ascending branches, which have no synapse on their way up the dorsal column, keep conducting.

They tested three kinds of stimulus on the hindpaw: vibration, gentle displacement of the skin, and sustained indentation with a blunt probe 1 mm across, at forces from 1 to 300 mN.

**Predict.** For each stimulus, which route do you expect to carry it? Use what you know: the direct route has no synapse before the brainstem, and the indirect route passes at least one synapse in the cord (two, through an interneuron).

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

| Stimulus | Direct route (afferent axon) | Indirect route (through PSDC neurons) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vibration | Carried, with high temporal precision; it underlies the high-frequency tuning | Can contribute to low-frequency (10 Hz) vibration responses |
| Gentle displacement | Contributes | Contributes |
| Sustained indentation, up to high force | Not enough on its own (see the next column) | Carried: with the lumbar synapses blocked, leaving only the direct route, sustained-indentation firing almost disappeared in most units |

If you gave fast vibration to the direct route, you were right, and the next section shows why a relay could struggle to keep that timing. If you gave all light touch to the direct route, the data say otherwise: both routes contributed to gentle displacement, so "direct means vibration and indirect means light touch" is too simple. Blocking the cord also raised the onset threshold, the force needed to make the neuron fire at all, so the indirect route is not only a late add-on.

</details>

Now the result that gives the lesson its idea. On single VPL-projecting neurons, the direct and indirect inputs had **highly aligned receptive fields** (a receptive field is the patch of skin whose touch drives the neuron). These neurons had a small excitatory field surrounded by a larger suppressive region, where touch reduced their firing. So the two routes are not two maps but two reports on one spot, meeting on one neuron.

One limit, said once. ACR1 silencing was validated only for gentle stimuli, about 20 to 30 mN or less, so the high-force conclusion rests on the lumbar drug, and that drug is not specific to PSDC neurons.

## What a slower route costs

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

What does a later report cost? Start by estimating how much later.

**Worked problem: how much later?** Suppose a gracile neuron receives both inputs from a mouse hindpaw. These are illustrative numbers, not measurements. The model assumes constant velocity, conduction plus synaptic delay only (see [the action potential lesson](/lessons/action-potential/)), one shared start at the paw, equal path lengths from cord to brainstem, and the same delay at each route's final synapse in the gracile nucleus, which then cancels.

> **t = L / v**

- Direct: 0.08 m at 16 m/s = 0.005 s = 5.0 ms.
- Indirect, paw to lumbar cord: 0.04 m at 16 m/s = 2.5 ms.
- Indirect, two spinal synapses at an assumed 1 ms each = 2.0 ms.
- Indirect, PSDC axon: 0.04 m at an assumed 8 m/s = 5.0 ms.
- Indirect total: 2.5 + 2.0 + 5.0 = 9.5 ms. Difference: 9.5 − 5.0 = 4.5 ms.

The indirect input arrives about 4.5 ms later in this model. Its size is not a measurement: other plausible path lengths, speeds and delays give lags from under 1 ms to about 12 ms (Going deeper). Its sign is firmer: the two routes make separate synapses onto the same gracile neuron, and under the stated assumptions, with a PSDC axon no faster than the afferent, the spinal synapses alone make the indirect input later. Whether two components that far apart can be told apart in a real recording depends on how much their timing varies and overlaps.

**Predict.** Take the 4.5 ms lag. Does it matter more for a 300 ms press, or for a 500 Hz vibration?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

For a readout of average firing during the plateau of a 300 ms press, probably little: 4.5 ms is 1.5 percent of the press, though that fraction says nothing about onset detection, integration with other inputs or perception. For vibration at 500 Hz, the highest frequency tested, one cycle lasts 1/500 s = 2.0 ms, so the lag spans more than two whole cycles (2.25).

A fixed delay does not blur one route's own locking: if every spike is late by the same amount, the spikes still share one phase. That phase moves, though. At 500 Hz, 2.25 cycles shifts the locked phase by a quarter cycle (90°), which matters where the two routes meet on one neuron. What blurs locking is variability: in the model below, each synapse adds a little random jitter to when the next spike fires.

</details>

A toy model shows how much jitter could matter. **Vector strength** is a locking score: how tightly spikes lock to one phase of a vibration cycle, from 1 (every spike at the same phase) to 0 (no net phase preference, as when spikes spread evenly over the cycle, or split equally between opposite phases). If spike times scatter around one phase with a normal spread σ, vector strength = exp(−(2πfσ)²/2) at frequency f (f in Hz, σ in seconds). Assume the direct route has σ = 0.2 ms, and that each of two spinal synapses adds independent random jitter of σ = 0.5 ms. Independent spreads add as squares, so the indirect route has σ = √(0.2² + 0.5² + 0.5²) ≈ 0.73 ms.

| Vibration | One cycle | Vector strength, direct (σ 0.2 ms) | Vector strength, indirect (σ 0.73 ms) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 50 Hz | 20 ms | 1.00 | 0.97 |
| 300 Hz | 3.3 ms | 0.93 | 0.38 |
| 500 Hz | 2.0 ms | 0.82 | 0.07 |

The jitter values are assumptions, not Turecek and colleagues' measurements, and relays need not add this much, so the model shows a possibility, not the paper's explanation: if each spinal synapse adds even half a millisecond of independent jitter, locking at hundreds of hertz would be mostly lost on the relayed route, while locking at 50 Hz survives. It also fits some gracile neurons better than others. Most thalamus-projecting neurons entrained to vibration (fired in time with each cycle) only below 150 Hz, where the model predicts little cost; it was most inferior colliculus-projecting neurons, identified the same antidromic way, that phase-locked from 10 to 500 Hz.

Against a 300 ms press, the same 0.73 ms of scatter is small (0.25 percent), though small is not proven harmless. So for a plateau-rate readout the lag is probably cheap, and what the indirect route adds is what it carries. Whether onset timing or the relative arrival times matter is open.

## What the dorsal horn does to the signal

Start with one anatomical fact from the atlas in the next section: PSDC neurons received their excitatory input mainly from local interneurons of the zone where touch afferents end, rather than straight from the afferents. So most of what a PSDC neuron sends up is the output of a circuit, rebuilt rather than passed on.

Chirila and colleagues (Cell, 2022) asked what that circuit does to a touch signal. They recorded from the dorsal horn of adult, urethane-anaesthetized mice while the skin was indented, and sorted the responses into six functional profiles. Those are response clusters, not six cell types. They found **convergence**, one neuron receiving input from more than one kind of afferent: individual neurons received input from both low-threshold and high-threshold mechanoreceptors. High-threshold mechanoreceptors are afferents that respond only to stronger, often damaging, stimuli.

Two inhibitory motifs shaped the output of PSDC neurons.

- **Feedforward inhibition.** The same afferent input that excites a PSDC neuron also excites an interneuron that inhibits it, so excitation is followed by inhibition. Rorβ interneurons supplied glycinergic feedforward inhibition onto PSDC neurons.
- **Presynaptic inhibition.** Inhibition is applied to the afferent terminal, scaling down the transmitter it releases. PVi neurons, the parvalbumin-expressing inhibitory population, form axo-axonic contacts on afferent terminals and take part in this GABA-A receptor dependent inhibition, but they are not its only source. Chirila and colleagues removed it in all sensory neurons, not by targeting PVi neurons.

**Predict.** Remove either kind of inhibition. Will the PSDC neuron's receptive field shrink or grow? And what might happen at the end of a step indentation?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It grows. Disrupting either motif enlarged PSDC receptive fields: a larger field means touches farther from its centre now drive the neuron. Loss of presynaptic inhibition also produced **after-discharge**, continued firing after the end of a step indentation.

</details>

Disrupting feedforward inhibition also changed touch responses in primary somatosensory cortex (S1) of awake mice, and those mice showed tactile over-reactivity and a texture discrimination deficit. The study did not show that the dorsal column carried the cortical change. The limits are plain: the dorsal horn and PSDC recordings were made in anaesthetized mice, and both disruptions were chronic conditional knockouts (Vgat deleted in Rorβ neurons for feedforward inhibition, and the GABA-A β3 subunit gene deleted in sensory neurons for presynaptic inhibition), which cannot be assumed to isolate acute circuit function. The dorsal horn is therefore not a simple relay on the way to the dorsal column.

## Who is in the zone: an atlas

Abraira and colleagues (Cell, 2017) asked an earlier question: which cells sit where touch afferents end? In the mouse lumbar cord, LTMRs end in a band in the middle of the dorsal horn, the **LTMR recipient zone (LTMR-RZ)**. It begins just below the band labelled by IB4, a marker of a set of nociceptor terminals.

They described seven excitatory and four inhibitory interneuron populations there. A **reporter-defined** population is one marked in a mouse line by a gene it expresses, which is a molecular label, not proof of a single cell type (genetic access is covered in [tools for development](/lessons/tools-for-development/)). Two have already appeared, Rorβ and PVi; CCK and Rorβ return in the texture test below.

Three facts set the scale. About 70 percent of the neurons intrinsic to the zone were excitatory and about 30 percent inhibitory. The reporter lines together covered an estimated 71 to 82 percent of LTMR-RZ neurons, so the atlas is incomplete. And the populations were not private lines. By counting pre- and postsynaptic marker apposition, each LTMR subtype assayed contacted four to eleven interneuron subtypes, and each interneuron subtype sampled one to three of the LTMR classes assayed (which does not exclude classes not assayed), so the zone mixes its inputs. The interneurons also received descending corticospinal input.

PVi and a second inhibitory population, Cdh3, formed **axo-axonic** contacts (synapses onto another neuron's axon terminal) on sensory terminals, consistent with presynaptic inhibition of the afferents (see [synaptic integration](/lessons/synaptic-integration/)).

Did any of it matter for behaviour? The authors silenced spinal CCK or Rorβ lineages and tested a texture version of the novel object recognition test, in which a mouse that remembers an object explores a new one more. Both silenced groups lost the preference for the newly textured object.

**Predict.** A mouse that no longer prefers a novel texture might have lost touch. What else could it have lost, and what control would rule that out?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It might have stopped exploring, or lost memory, motivation or movement. The control is the same recognition task with objects that differ in colour and shape rather than texture. The silenced mice kept the preference, so the deficit is not a general failure to explore or recognize objects.

</details>

One limit to keep in mind: a lineage includes every cell that ever expressed the marker, so the silencing is not acute and not one pure type. It shows these lineages contribute to the task, not that each subtype is needed.

## When inhibition fails

If inhibition holds back touch from other circuits, loss of inhibition could let it reach them. Duan and colleagues (Cell, 2014) tested that idea for spinal pain circuits in mice and found that dynorphin-lineage inhibitory neurons gated Aβ input away from somatostatin-lineage neurons needed for mechanical pain behaviours; [spinal circuits that gate pain](/lessons/spinal-pain-circuits/) covers the work in full. This is a separate circuit from the PSDC experiments. An enlarged PSDC receptive field is not allodynia (pain from normally non-painful touch), and the blunt 300 mN indentations did not evoke pain behaviour in awake mice. A later lesson takes touch over-reactivity in [autism models](/lessons/touch-in-autism-models/) further.

## Where the idea stops

Here is how far "two routes, one patch of skin" holds.

- **Mouse hindlimb only.** The evidence is from the mouse lumbar dorsal horn and gracile nucleus, recorded mostly in urethane-anaesthetized adults. There are no human data.
- **The hindlimb and gracile example only.** The cuneate nucleus also belongs to the dorsal column pathway but is not analysed here; the anterolateral and trigeminal systems are separate pathways.
- **Rebuilt is not copied.** The indirect route is not a delayed duplicate: it supports sustained-intensity coding into the high-force range that direct input alone did not reproduce: blocking lumbar transmission nearly abolished sustained responses in most recorded units. Both routes contribute to gentle displacement, so neither is a single-submodality channel.
- **The direct route skips the dorsal horn, not processing.** It still ends at a synapse in the gracile nucleus, and the thalamus-projecting neurons there have suppressive surrounds. Silencing the direct afferent terminals reduced that surround suppression, which points to direct input acting mainly through local inhibitory neurons of the nucleus; the full microcircuit was not resolved.
- **What reaches the brainstem, not what the animal perceives.** No study here recorded PSDC or gracile neurons in awake animals.

## Design problem

Chirila and colleagues found that PSDC receptive fields grow when presynaptic inhibition is lost. Design an experiment to show that this matters for perception, and say what would be needed in people.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Make the change acute.** Remove presynaptic inhibition of the afferents acutely in adult mice, not by a developmental or germline deletion, which can change the cord's wiring. Silencing PVi neurons alone would not do this, because they are one of several sources of axo-axonic contacts and also make ordinary synapses.
- **Tie it to PSDC output.** Add a perturbation or rescue restricted to PSDC output. A spinal drug blockade is not PSDC-specific, so a drug result must not be read as one.
- **Record awake.** Record PSDC neurons in awake mice, not under urethane, to check that the field enlargement survives without anaesthesia.
- **Measure perception with a control.** Measure tactile acuity behaviourally, for example discrimination of closely spaced textures, paired with a control that does not depend on texture, like Abraira's colour and shape object test, so a general change in exploration is not mistaken for a tactile one.
- **People.** Mouse data do not transfer by default. In people, you would need psychophysical acuity measures and a way to change spinal inhibition safely, and a result would still be limited to that preparation.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Ages and preparations

The 2017 study used fixed tissue mostly from mice aged 30 to 35 days, spinal slices from mice aged 13 to 21 days, and behaviour in males aged 7 to 12 weeks. In the two 2022 studies the dorsal horn and gracile nucleus were recorded in adult mice under urethane anaesthesia: older than 6 weeks for the dorsal horn recordings of Chirila and colleagues, and older than 5 weeks, of both sexes, for the gracile recordings of Turecek and colleagues.

### Where touch afferents end

LTMR terminals overlap in laminae IIiv to IV of the dorsal horn (laminae are the layers numbered from the surface; IIiv is the inner, ventral part of lamina II). In the mouse lumbar cord, the LTMR-RZ was sampled from the IB4 border to about 250 µm below it.

### The interneuron populations and their firing patterns

The excitatory populations were PKCγ, Cbln2, NeuroD4, PVe, CCK, 5HTr6 and Igfbp5. The inhibitory ones were PVi, Kcnip2, Rorβ and Cdh3. Parvalbumin is not an inhibitory marker by default here: PVe is excitatory and PVi is inhibitory.

In spinal slices, the populations fired differently when current was injected. Cbln2 neurons burst at the start and PKCγ neurons spiked after a delay. Among inhibitory neurons, PVi cells fired tonically, Kcnip2 and Rorβ cells after a delay, and Cdh3 cells with gaps.

### Counting inhibitory contacts

Abraira counted 2.9 ± 0.1 (mean ± SEM) axo-axonic inhibitory contacts per sensory terminal in fixed mouse tissue, of which 1.5 ± 0.1 were from PVi neurons. 1.5 / 2.9 = 0.52, so about 52 percent of the contacts. These are contact counts, not conductances, so they do not say PVi neurons supply half of the inhibition.

### No core-shell map

Turecek and colleagues could not detect a clear core-shell organization in the mouse gracile recordings, so the split in this lesson is direct versus indirect route, not a map of submodalities across the nucleus.

### How big is the lag?

Under the worked problem's assumptions, the lag is L(cord to DCN) × (1/v(PSDC) − 1/v(afferent)) plus the number of spinal synapses times the synaptic delay. A sweep over illustrative mouse ranges (paw to cord 3 to 5 cm, cord to gracile nucleus 2.5 to 4.5 cm, afferent 10 to 25 m/s, PSDC axon 5 to 16 m/s and never faster than the afferent, one to three spinal synapses at 0.5 to 1.5 ms each), sampled on the grid paw to cord {3, 4, 5} cm, cord to gracile {2.5, 3.5, 4.5} cm, afferent {10, 16, 25} m/s, PSDC {5, 8, 12, 16} m/s, synapses {1, 2, 3} at {0.5, 1, 1.5} ms, with every admissible combination weighted equally (810 combinations), gave lags from 0.5 to 11.7 ms, with a median of about 4.0 ms. The median is a property of that grid, not a biological estimate. Every lag was positive. These ranges are assumptions for illustration, not measurements.

### The Duan experiment

Ablating somatostatin-lineage excitatory spinal neurons impaired mechanical pain behaviours, and dynorphin-lineage inhibitory neurons gated Aβ input from activating the somatostatin neurons (ablation in vivo; slice recordings from mice aged 23 to 30 days).
