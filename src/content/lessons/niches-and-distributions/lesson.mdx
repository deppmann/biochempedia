    story: >-
      Hutchinson taught at Yale for more than four decades and worked on lakes, on the chemistry
      of natural waters and on the theory of how many species can live together. He did not
      coin the word niche. Joseph Grinnell had used it for the habitat and habits of a bird, and
      Charles Elton for the place of an animal in its food web. In 1957, closing a Cold Spring
      Harbor symposium on population studies, Hutchinson offered a more abstract version. Take
      every environmental variable that matters to a species, such as temperature, moisture or
      the size of food items, and treat each as an axis. The set of points in that space where
      the species can persist forms a region with n dimensions, a hypervolume. He called it the
      fundamental niche, and the part a species holds when competitors are present the realized
      niche. The construction made the niche something you could measure axis by axis, compare
      between species and test, which is how ecologists still use it.

techniques:
  - name: "Transplants beyond the range, with within-range controls"
    blurb: >-
      Individuals or seeds are planted inside the range, at the edge and beyond it, with the same
      handling at every site. If only the beyond-range plantings fail, conditions there are
      implicated; if all fail, the test cannot single out the edge, and handling, planting material or a bad year may be to blame.
  - name: "Neighbour removal and exclosure experiments"
    blurb: >-
      Competitors are cleared, or herbivores fenced out, on randomly chosen plots and left on
      others. Comparing the two estimates the net effect of those species on performance at a site,
      including any effect they have on physical conditions such as shade or soil moisture.
  - name: "Thermal performance assays"
    blurb: >-
      Individuals are reared at several constant temperatures and a named response, such as
      development rate or survival, is measured at each. The curve that results shows where
      that response peaks and where it falls to zero.
  - name: "Stage-structured matrix population models"
    blurb: >-
      Survival, growth and reproduction measured at each life stage are combined into a matrix
      that steps the number in each stage forward one year. If the same matrix holds every year,
      its dominant eigenvalue is the long-run lambda, the finite rate of increase per year; when
      years differ, long-run lambda is estimated from the sequence of yearly matrices. A long-run
      lambda of 1 or more, without immigrants, means the population can replace itself under
      those conditions.

A range map blurs three things: the conditions a species can live in, the other species it meets, and the places it has reached.

> **The one idea.** Count a local population once a year. Next year's count is this year's multiplied by **λ** (lambda), the factor its own births and deaths produce that year, plus any **immigrants**, I: N_next = λ·N + I. A species' **niche** is the set of conditions in which the **long-run λ**, taken across good and bad years with no immigrants, is at least 1. A map adds two mismatches: suitable places never reached, and places outside the niche where immigrants keep a population going. Other species move λ, down (competitors, enemies) or up (helpers).
>
> *Scope, for every λ in this lesson:* one species at one site, counted as a single number and closed except for immigrants (a stage-structured model, used in the transplant studies, does the same bookkeeping stage by stage); λ is judged while the population is sparse; chance births and deaths in very small populations, which can end one even at λ = 1, are set aside, as is evolution at the edge over the length of an experiment.

## The bookkeeping behind a map

The first film drew the niche; here is its bookkeeping. λ, the **finite rate of increase**, sums up survival, growth and reproduction across the whole life cycle, the vital rates from [life histories](/lessons/life-histories/).

G. Evelyn Hutchinson gave the niche this form in 1957: with n axes (temperature, soil moisture, salt, the size of food items) it is an **n-dimensional hypervolume** in **niche space**, while **geographic space** is the map. Conditions vary with latitude, altitude and microclimate ([climate and biomes](/lessons/climate-and-biomes/)), so one patch of niche space can map onto scattered places, and one place can hold several microclimates.

The card's two cases are the first film's two mismatches: N = 0 with I = 0 is a place never reached, and the steady 100 plants at λ = 0.8 are a **sink population**.

## Fundamental and realized niches

Hutchinson split the niche in two. The **fundamental niche** is the set of conditions in which a species can persist with no competitors or enemies present (fine print in Going deeper). The **realized niche** is the set of conditions it occupies when other species are present. Competitors and enemies usually make it a smaller part of the fundamental niche; helpers can extend it past it.

Joseph Connell's classic field case, published in 1961, comes from the rocky shore at Millport on the Isle of Cumbrae, Scotland. Adults of the barnacle *Chthamalus stellatus* lived in a band high on the rocks, above the larger *Balanus balanoides*. Yet *Chthamalus* larvae settled lower.

**Predict.** Something removes the *Chthamalus* that settle below the adult band. Name two things that could do it, and an experiment that would tell them apart.

Helpers work the other way. A shrub that shades seedlings, or a mussel bed that holds water at low tide, raises λ and can let a species persist in conditions it cannot tolerate alone. If the fundamental niche is defined before any interaction, the realized niche then reaches past the fundamental one, so "the realized niche is always smaller" has exceptions (a framing some ecologists dispute).

**Species distribution models**, fitted to the climates of places where a species has been recorded, describe the map, sinks and gaps included: occupied conditions plus accessibility, closer to the realized niche filtered by dispersal than to the fundamental niche.

## Tolerance: one piece of λ at a time

Rearing individuals in the laboratory at several constant temperatures and recording a named response gives a **thermal performance curve**. For a rate such as development, it rises from zero at **Tmin**, peaks at **Topt** and falls to zero at **Tmax**; survival curves are often flat across middle temperatures. The fall at high temperature has several causes, among them proteins losing their folded, active shape, as in [enzyme action](/lessons/enzyme-action-basics/).

Every value belongs to its response: for development, Tmin and Tmax are where *that rate* reaches zero, not necessarily lethal temperatures. If a step every individual must pass through, such as development to adulthood, stops at some temperature, the life cycle cannot be completed there and λ is below 1 whatever the other rates do. Short of that, λ has to be worked out from the whole life cycle.

**Predict.** Development rate and survival both fall at extreme temperatures. Across moths and butterflies, which would you expect to limit performance at the cold end, and which at the hot end?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In a 2024 synthesis of laboratory data sets for Lepidoptera, Mariana Abarca and colleagues found that development rate generally limited performance at the cold end and survival at the hot end. So different rates set the two ends; Going deeper works a breadth from both.

</details>

A laboratory breadth is not a range boundary: a moth in the field can shelter in shade, and its distribution also depends on host plants, enemies and dispersal. A chamber measures one piece of λ; to learn whether a whole population can replace itself past an edge, you have to plant it there, as the second film does.

## Testing a range edge

Amy Angert and Douglas Schemske grew two California monkeyflowers, *Mimulus cardinalis* of low elevations and *M. lewisii* of high ones, in gardens inside and beyond each species' elevation range. Each had zero or near-zero fitness beyond its limits, which fits niche limits, but elevation changes many conditions at once, so temperature cannot be separated from the rest.

How often is a range edge a niche edge? In 2014 Anna Hargreaves, Karen Samis and Christopher Eckert meta-analysed 111 transplant tests beyond the range; in three quarters, performance fell beyond the edge.

**Predict.** N_next = λ·N + I allows one kind of edge the second film did not show. What is it?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A range that runs past the niche limit, its edge populations sinks (λ below 1, I above 0). In a transplant, plants fail even just inside the edge. The review found all three. In 46% of tests the range limit sat at a niche limit. In 26%, mostly latitudinal or longitudinal limits, transplants sustained themselves beyond the range, consistent with dispersal limitation (λ at least 1, N = 0). In 23%, all at elevation limits, the range ran past the niche limit. The shares do not add to 100%.

</details>

The opening dune plant, *Camissoniopsis cheiranthifolia*, is the second film's first result; Regan Cross and Christopher Eckert revisited it 12 to 14 years after planting. Exactly tripling in that time means multiplying by about 1.08 to 1.10 a year on average, more than tripling a little more: a species can sit just above replacement and still be missing (a rough illustration, not a measured long-run λ). They inferred that limits on local dispersal had likely contributed to the edge. Later surveys by Eckert's group found that colonisation of empty dune patches falls toward the limit, where suitable habitat is scarcer and fewer plants grow nearby, so the edge may be held by a balance of colonisation and local extinction (empty patches colonised more slowly than occupied ones die out) rather than by seeds never arriving. One site shows the edge is not where the species fails there, not that every dune farther north is suitable. See [spatial populations](/lessons/spatial-populations/) for movement between populations and [global change](/lessons/global-change/) for edges shifting under warming.

### Design problem

Take the second film's test of a range edge at a headland. What would you add before trusting its answer?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Keep the film's plan and add:

- Seed from several source populations, so no one population decides the result, plus seed and sites from the range edge itself, as the 2014 review stressed.
- Several years of survival, growth and seed production, combined in a **stage-structured model** (one that tracks seedlings, juveniles and adults separately) to estimate long-run λ for each site and treatment.
- The landowner's permission and often agency permits, sites that can be monitored, and removal of plants at the end if required, because a species that thrives there could spread as a new invader.

Read λ as the film does. Where it falls below 1 beyond the edge with and without neighbours, climate is a strong candidate, but herbivores, pathogens or missing soil partners need their own tests before the limit is called abiotic.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Why λ has to be a long-run rate

**Predict.** At one site λ alternates between 1.5 in good years and 0.6 in bad ones, an average of 1.05. Does the population grow?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Each pair of years multiplies it by 1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9, so for a single count like this the long-run λ is the geometric mean, √0.9 ≈ 0.95, and 100 plants fall to about 12 in 40 years. One rare bad year does the same: λ = 1.1 for nine years and 0.3 in the tenth averages 1.02, but the long-run λ is about 0.97. Most transplants run a few years at a few sites, so rare lethal years and long-term adaptation at the edge are hard to capture.

</details>

### Fine print on the fundamental niche

In the fundamental niche, the species' food and any partner it cannot live without are assumed present. Hutchinson framed it in terms of competitors. Some treatments add biotic axes, such as the density of a predator, but the fundamental niche is usually drawn on physical conditions and resources.

### Worked problem: a performance breadth from development and survival

The 2024 synthesis by Mariana Abarca and colleagues used 46 laboratory data sets from 26 species of Lepidoptera. They combined the two rates:

> **P = D x S**   (D = development rate, S = survival, each scaled to its own maximum)

The numbers are invented for teaching. A moth is reared at three temperatures. Survival peaked at a cooler temperature not shown, so S is below 1 at all three.

| Temperature | D | S |
|---|---|---|
| 20 degrees C | 0.50 | 0.80 |
| 25 degrees C | 1.00 | 0.60 |
| 30 degrees C | 0.80 | 0.20 |

Assume P changes in a straight line between treatments, do not extrapolate beyond 20 or 30 degrees C, and call the acceptable range P of at least 0.50 (the threshold Abarca's group used, an operational choice).

1. *Compute P.* 0.50 x 0.80 = 0.40 at 20 degrees C; 1.00 x 0.60 = 0.60 at 25; 0.80 x 0.20 = 0.16 at 30. The best tested temperature is 25 degrees C. The true peak could lie between treatments.
2. *Lower crossing.* P must rise 0.10 of the 0.20 it gains from 20 to 25, half the way: 20 + (0.50 - 0.40)/(0.60 - 0.40) x 5 = 22.5 degrees C.
3. *Upper crossing.* 25 + (0.60 - 0.50)/(0.60 - 0.16) x 5 = 25 + 1.14 = 26.14 degrees C.
4. *Breadth.* 26.14 - 22.5 = 3.64 degrees C.
5. *Which rate limits each end?* At 20 degrees C survival is high and development slow, so development limits the cold end. At 30 degrees C development is still fairly fast but survival has collapsed, so survival limits the hot end.

P leaves out fecundity, so it is not lambda. The crossings are not lethal limits, and they are not a range boundary.

### Field experiment on competition: the Welk study

Welk and colleagues grew 14 perennial grassland species in nine German botanical gardens, with a sown competitor grass, *Festuca rubra*, present or absent and molluscs excluded or not, in a fully crossed design. In their model, competition reduced the perennials' aboveground biomass by 40.8% overall. Survival also declined as precipitation fell across the gardens, but climate varied among gardens rather than being assigned at random, so that part is an association.

### Names and word history

Hutchinson did not coin the word *niche*: Joseph Grinnell used it in 1917 for a bird's habitat and habits, and Charles Elton in 1927 for an animal's place in a food web. The Millport barnacles are now identified as *C. montagui*, a separate species split from *C. stellatus* after Connell's work, and *Balanus balanoides* is now *Semibalanus balanoides*.
