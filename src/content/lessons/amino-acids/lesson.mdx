techniques:
  - name: "Titration to find pKa and pI"
    blurb: >-
      Add base in measured steps and track pH. Each plateau is a buffering region
      centered on a pKa; the steep jumps mark the equivalence points. The
      half-equivalence point reads the pKa directly, and pI sits at the pH where net
      charge is zero — the two numbers that fingerprint an amino acid's acid-base
      behavior.
  - name: "Assigning chirality (the CORN rule)"
    blurb: >-
      View the alpha-carbon with the H pointing toward you and trace CO → R → N
      around the carbon. Clockwise reads L, counterclockwise reads D. Commit to one
      camera angle: look from the opposite side and the handedness flips. Ribosomes
      build proteins from L-amino acids (plus achiral glycine); D residues in some
      peptides are made by later enzymes.
  - name: "Detecting amino acids (ninhydrin and chromatography)"
    blurb: >-
      Ninhydrin reacts with free alpha-amino groups to produce a purple color
      (yellow for proline), so a separated mixture lights up spot by spot. Paper or
      ion-exchange chromatography sorts amino acids by charge and hydrophobicity —
      the same two side-chain properties this lesson is built on.

  - stem: "Glycine is the only achiral amino acid. What structural feature accounts for this?"
    choices:
      - "Its R group is a single hydrogen, so the alpha-carbon bears two identical hydrogen substituents"
      - "Its R group forms a covalent ring with the backbone nitrogen, locking the configuration"
      - "Its side chain contains a sulfur atom that prevents mirror-image formation"
      - "Its carboxylate group is replaced by an amide, eliminating one stereocenter"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Chirality requires four different groups on the alpha-carbon. For glycine, R =
      H, so the carbon carries two hydrogens plus the amino and carboxylate groups;
      with two identical substituents the molecule is superimposable on its mirror
      image and therefore achiral. The covalent-ring option describes proline's
      cyclic side chain (proline is still chiral); the sulfur and amide claims are
      simply false.
  - stem: "A student titrates glycine and identifies pKa₁ = 2.34 (alpha-carboxyl) and pKa₂ = 9.60 (alpha-amino). Which statement correctly describes the predominant form and how to obtain the isoelectric point (pI)?"
    choices:
      - "The fully protonated +1 form predominates between the two pKa values; pI = 2.34"
      - "The net-zero form predominates only above pKa₂; pI = 9.60"
      - "The pI equals the higher pKa because the amino group deprotonates last; pI = 9.60"
      - "The net-zero zwitterion predominates between the two pKa values; pI = (2.34 + 9.60)/2 = 5.97"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Between pKa₁ and pKa₂ the carboxyl is deprotonated (−COO⁻) and the amino group
      is still protonated (−NH₃⁺), giving the net-zero zwitterion. The pI is the
      average of the two pKa values that bracket this neutral form: (2.34 + 9.60)/2 =
      5.97. The +1 form predominates only below pKa₁; above pKa₂ the molecule is net
      −1; and pI is the average of the flanking pKa values, not simply the higher one.
  - stem: "Histidine has three ionizable groups: alpha-carboxyl (pKa₁ = 1.82), the imidazole side chain (pKa₂ = 6.00), and alpha-amino (pKa₃ = 9.17). At physiological pH (7.4), what is the net charge on free histidine, and why?"
    choices:
      - "+1 — the imidazole stays protonated and adds a positive charge at pH 7.4"
      - "0 — the carboxyl is deprotonated (−1), the imidazole is mostly deprotonated/neutral (0), and the alpha-amino is protonated (+1)"
      - "−1 — both the carboxyl and the imidazole carry negative charges at pH 7.4"
      - "+2 — both the imidazole and the alpha-amino are protonated at pH 7.4"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Apply pH > pKa → deprotonated. At pH 7.4: carboxyl (pKa 1.82) is deprotonated,
      −1; imidazole (pKa 6.00) is below pH 7.4 so mostly neutral, 0; alpha-amino (pKa
      9.17) is above pH 7.4 so still protonated, +1. Sum = 0. The imidazole, more than
      a full unit below the pH, is predominantly neutral — not still positive.
  - stem: "An enzyme uses a single ionizable side chain as a proton-shuttle group, and the designer wants it to buffer most effectively near physiological pH (~7.4) while toggling between charged and neutral states. Which amino acid fits, and why?"
    choices:
      - "Aspartate — its carboxyl pKa (~4) makes it the strongest acid and therefore the best buffer at pH 7.4"
      - "Lysine — its high side-chain pKa (~10.5) keeps it fully protonated and locked in one state at pH 7.4"
      - "Glycine — its small size lets it move protons quickly regardless of pKa"
      - "Histidine — a group buffers best when the ambient pH is near its pKa, and of these options the imidazole pKa (~6.0) is by far the closest to physiological pH"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      A group buffers best when the solution pH is within about ±1 unit of its pKa.
      Free histidine's imidazole pKa (~6.0) sits just outside that window, but it is by far the
      closest of these options, and inside proteins it is often shifted nearer to 7, which is why it can both buffer and toggle between charged and neutral — the
      "Swiss Army knife of catalysis." Aspartate buffers near pH 4; free lysine stays almost fully
      protonated and rarely toggles near 7.4; glycine's size has nothing to do with
      buffering, which depends on pKa.

mcatQuestions:
  - stem: "An ionizable group with a pKa of 4.34 sits in a solution buffered at pH 6.34. By the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, what is the approximate ratio of deprotonated to protonated form, [A⁻]/[HA]?"
    choices:
      - "1 : 100"
      - "100 : 1"
      - "10 : 1"
      - "1 : 1"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]), so log([A⁻]/[HA]) = 6.34 − 4.34 = 2.0 and [A⁻]/[HA] =
      10² = 100:1. Because pH is above pKa, the deprotonated form dominates (ruling out
      1:100). A ratio of 10:1 would need a difference of only 1.0; 1:1 would need pH =
      pKa.

  - stem: "Sickle-cell disease arises when glutamate at position 6 of beta-globin (E6) is replaced by valine. A different, real variant, hemoglobin C, instead substitutes that same surface glutamate with lysine. Which variant best reproduces the sticky-patch aggregation of sickle cell, and on what basis?"
    choices:
      - "The Glu→Lys variant — Lys is larger than Glu, and steric bulk alone causes aggregation"
      - "The Glu→Val variant — it converts a charged/hydrophilic surface residue into a hydrophobic one, creating a sticky patch that drives aggregation"
      - "The Glu→Lys variant — replacing a negative with a positive charge removes all surface charge and exposes the core"
      - "Both equally — any substitution at position 6 produces identical aggregation regardless of the new residue"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The pathology is a property switch: a charged, hydrophilic surface Glu becomes a
      hydrophobic Val, and the new greasy patch drives deoxyhemoglobin to polymerize.

      depends specifically on the property of the new residue.

    label: "Like I'm 5"
    text: |
      Think of twenty LEGO people who all have the same body, but each one holds a
      different tool. The body is the same for everybody. The tool in their hand is
      the only thing that's different — and the tool decides what that person can do.
      Some tools are oily and don't like water, some have a plus or a minus charge
      like a magnet, some can grab and hold things. In your body these little people
      are called amino acids, and the tool is called the side chain. Change one
      person's tool and you can change what the whole team builds. That's how
      swapping just one amino acid can change a whole protein.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: |
      Every amino acid is built around one central carbon with four things attached:
      an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen, and a side chain called the R
      group. The first three are essentially the same in all twenty. The R group is
      the part that changes, and it carries all the differences — whether the amino
      acid is greasy (hydrophobic) or likes water, whether it has a charge, whether
      it can grab a proton.

      Charge depends on the surrounding acidity. At blood pH (about 7.4) the carboxyl
      group has lost a proton (−COO⁻) and the amino group has gained one (−NH₃⁺), so
      the amino acid carries both a plus and a minus at once — a zwitterion. Each group has
      a pKa, the pH at which half of those groups hold their proton. The rule that runs
      everything: if the pH is above a group's pKa, the group has lost its proton; if
      below, it keeps it. Because the R group sets so much of this, changing
      one amino acid for another — like the glutamate-to-valine swap in sickle cell —
      can flip a protein's behavior.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: |
      All twenty standard amino acids share an alpha-carbon bearing four
      substituents: −NH₃⁺ (in proline, a ring −NH₂⁺−), −COO⁻, −H, and a side chain (R). With four different
      groups, the alpha-carbon is a stereocenter, giving L and D enantiomers;
      proteins are built almost exclusively from L. Glycine (R = H) is the sole exception — two
      identical substituents make it achiral.

      The R group sets most of what distinguishes one amino acid from another, and the cleanest sorting is by
      polarity and charge: hydrophobic, polar-neutral, basic (Lys and Arg, positive at
      pH 7.4; His, only about 4% protonated in free solution), and acidic (Asp, Glu). At physiological pH the molecule is a
      zwitterion, and the protonation state of every ionizable group follows from the
      Henderson–Hasselbalch logic: pH > pKa favors the deprotonated form, pH < pKa the
      protonated form.

      Seven side chains are ionizable (Asp, Glu, His, Cys, Tyr, Lys, Arg). A titration
      curve plots pH against equivalents of base; each plateau is a buffering region
      whose midpoint equals a pKa, and the isoelectric point pI is the average of the
      two pKa values bracketing the net-neutral species. For glycine, pI = (2.34 +
      9.60)/2 = 5.97; for histidine the imidazole (pKa ≈ 6.0) adds a third inflection
      and shifts pI to 7.59. Histidine's near-physiological pKa is a main reason it is
      the most common catalytic residue in enzymes, though Asp, Glu, Lys, Cys and
      others also do acid-base work.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: |
      The amino-acid alpha-carbon is a stereocenter for nineteen of the twenty
      residues; the L configuration (S at Cα for all but cysteine, where CIP priority
      of the sulfur-bearing side chain inverts the descriptor to R) is enforced
      biosynthetically. Side-chain identity governs partitioning, electrostatics,
      hydrogen-bonding, and reactivity — the variables that downstream folding and
      catalysis read out.

      Protonation states are set by local pKa, which the protein environment can shift
      substantially from solution values: a buried Asp or a Lys in a hydrophobic
      pocket can move several pH units, and these perturbations underlie pH-dependent
      catalysis and conformational switching. Henderson–Hasselbalch (pH = pKa +
      log[A⁻]/[HA]) gives the fractional deprotonation f = 1/(1 + 10^(pKa − pH)) for
      each group; for independent groups, summing fractional deprotonation reconstructs the
      titration curve (interacting groups in proteins need coupled models), and net charge is the signed sum over groups. The isoelectric
      point is the pH at which net charge is zero — for a simple diprotic case the mean
      of the flanking pKa values, but in general the root of the net-charge function,
      which is how pI shifts when a side-chain pKa is perturbed.

      Histidine's imidazole (solution pKa ≈ 6.0) sits near physiological pH, so modest
      electrostatic tuning lets it act as either a general acid or a general base
      within a single catalytic cycle — the rationale for its prevalence in catalytic
      triads, metalloenzyme coordination, and the hemoglobin Bohr effect. The
      sickle-cell E6V substitution is the canonical illustration that a single
      side-chain property change (anionic/hydrophilic → hydrophobic) can drive
      pathological self-association: deoxy-HbS polymerization nucleated by the new
      hydrophobic contact.

**In 1949 Linus Pauling, Harvey Itano, S. J. Singer and Ibert Wells put hemoglobin from healthy people and from people with sickle-cell anemia in an electric field, and the two moved differently: at the same pH they carried different net charges.**

For two molecules of the same size and shape, only a charge difference explains that. The difference turned out to be one amino acid: at position 6 of each beta chain, a valine where a glutamate belongs. Why does that swap change the charge, and why does it make red cells sickle? The answers differ, and by the end you can work out both.

*The film above builds the charge half of the answer: proton switches, pKa and pI.* This page adds the numbers behind them and a second property, polarity, which the sickle story also needs.

## One backbone, twenty side chains

Every amino acid is a central carbon, the **alpha-carbon**, carrying four groups: an amino group (written −NH₂), a carboxyl group (written −COOH), a hydrogen, and a side chain, the **R group**. That is how it is drawn on paper; in water near neutral pH the ends are −NH₃⁺ and −COO⁻ (the next section shows why). Three groups are shared (proline's side chain loops back onto its backbone nitrogen, making a secondary amine). The R group carries the differences: size, charge, comfort in water, reactivity.

With four different groups, the alpha-carbon is **chiral**: it has two mirror images, L and D, that cannot be superimposed, like your two hands. Proteins are built almost exclusively from the **L form**. The exception is **glycine**, whose R group is just another hydrogen, so it is the only achiral amino acid. That tiny side chain lets it take backbone angles other residues cannot, so it turns up in tight turns.

## Proton switches: the one rule for charge

Both ends of the backbone are **proton switches**, as the film shows: groups that hold a hydrogen ion, H⁺ (**protonated**), or let it go (**deprotonated**), depending on how acidic the surroundings are. Each has a set point, its **pKa**, where half of those groups hold a proton.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson** (the film's, in three lines).
>
> 1. Below a group's pKa it mostly holds its proton; above it, it mostly lets go. Each pH unit from the pKa changes the ratio of the two forms tenfold.
> 2. Holding a proton always makes a group one charge more positive.
> 3. Net charge is the sum of the switches: two on the backbone, one or none on the side chain. The pI is where they sum to zero.

This holds for free amino acids in water; inside a protein, neighbors can shift a pKa (Going deeper). Point 2 matters most, because switches come in two kinds:

| Kind of switch | Holding H⁺ | Without H⁺ | Examples (pKa) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acid type** | 0 (−COOH) | −1 (−COO⁻) | backbone carboxyl (about 2), Asp and Glu side chains (about 4) |
| **Base type** | +1 (−NH₃⁺; His and Arg have a protonated ring or guanidinium) | 0 (−NH₂; the neutral ring or guanidine) | backbone amino (about 9.5), His (about 6), Lys (about 10.5), Arg (about 12.5) |

So "deprotonated" does not mean "negative": a lysine side chain that has lost its proton is neutral.

Glycine has just the two backbone switches: carboxyl pKa₁ = 2.34 and amino pKa₂ = 9.60.

**Predict.** What is glycine's net charge at pH 1, at pH 6 and at pH 12?

- **pH 6.** Above the carboxyl's pKa, so −COO⁻ (−1); below the amino group's, so −NH₃⁺ (+1). **0**.
- **pH 12.** Above both, so −COO⁻ (−1) and −NH₂ (0). About **−1**.

Stack the three forms from most positive to most negative, with a pKa between each pair, and you have glycine's **charge ladder**:

| Net charge | Form | Main form when |
|---|---|---|
| +1 | H₃N⁺–CH₂–COOH | pH below 2.34 |
| | *pKa₁ = 2.34* | |
| 0 | H₃N⁺–CH₂–COO⁻ | pH between 2.34 and 9.60 |
| | *pKa₂ = 9.60* | |
| −1 | H₂N–CH₂–COO⁻ | pH above 9.60 |

A molecule carrying both a positive and a negative charge is a **zwitterion** (which does not mean net zero: aspartate at 7.4 has both charges and is net −1). Glycine's net-zero zwitterion is more than 90% of it from about pH 3.3 to 8.6, one unit inside each pKa. That includes blood pH, about **7.4** (the inside of a cell runs a little lower).

The pH of zero net charge is the **isoelectric point, pI**: the average of the two pKa values around the net-zero rung. For glycine, (2.34 + 9.60) / 2 = **5.97**.

Why the average? Near the pI almost all glycine is the zwitterion, with a little +1 and a little −1 form, and zero net charge needs equal amounts of those two. By the rule, the +1 form's ratio to the zwitterion is 10 to the power (pKa₁ − pH), and the −1 form's is 10 to the power (pH − pKa₂). Set them equal and pH = (pKa₁ + pKa₂) / 2.

**Predict.** Aspartate has three switches: backbone carboxyl 1.88, side-chain carboxyl 3.65 and backbone amino 9.60. What is its net charge at pH 7.4, and what is its pI?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

At 7.4 both carboxyls have let go (−1 each) and the amino group holds on (+1): **−1**.

Walk down the ladder: +1 below pH 1.88, 0 past 1.88, −1 past 3.65, −2 past 9.60. The net-zero rung sits between 1.88 and 3.65, so pI = (1.88 + 3.65) / 2 = **2.77**. The common error is to average the two backbone values, giving 5.74, where aspartate carries −1, not 0. Find the net-zero rung first.

</details>

## Four side-chain families, read from the switches

The two charged families are switch states at pH 7.4; the other two are sorted by **polarity**, whether a side chain is comfortable in water. Recall: below its pKa a group holds its proton and is one charge more positive.

- **Acidic (Asp, Glu).** Acid-type switches near pKa 4, far below 7.4, so they have let go: **negative**.
- **Basic (Lys, Arg, His).** Lys and Arg switches sit far above 7.4, so they hold on: **positive**. His is a base whose switch sits near 6, so free histidine is mostly neutral at 7.4; it is grouped with the bases by convention, which is why the classifier files it as "Basic (+)".
- **Polar, uncharged.** The hydroxyls of Ser, Thr and Tyr, the amides of Asn and Gln, and the reactive thiol of Cys. Tyr (pKa about 10) and Cys (about 8.2) have switches, but at 7.4 they mostly stay neutral.
- **Hydrophobic (Gly, Ala, Val, Leu, Ile, Met, Pro, Phe, Trp).** Greasy hydrocarbon and aromatic chains with no switch, which bury themselves in a protein's core, away from water.

The seven ionizable side chains, **Asp, Glu, His, Cys, Tyr, Lys and Arg**, include the residues that form salt bridges, ion pairs between opposite charges (Asp and Glu with Lys and Arg), bind metals (His, Cys) and do acid-base catalysis (His above all).

## Reading the curve: titration, pKa and pI

A titration curve is the measured record of the charge ladder: add base drop by drop, with equivalents of base (base added per amino-acid molecule) along the bottom and pH up the side. Each flat part is a **buffering region**, where added base barely moves the pH because one switch is busy letting go of its protons.

**For well-separated pKa values, each pKa sits at the middle of a flat part, the half-equivalence point.** When the two pKa values on either side of the net-zero rung are far apart, as in glycine (flat parts at 2.34 and 9.60), the pI of 5.97 sits in the steep jump between them: no switch is mid-change there, so almost every molecule is the net-zero form and nothing buffers. When the two are close, as in aspartate (1.88 and 3.65) or lysine (8.95 and 10.53), their flat parts merge and the pI falls inside one broad buffering region, so find it from the ladder, not from the shape of the curve. Histidine, with its extra imidazole switch, has three (pKa₁ = 1.82, pKa₂ = 6.00, pKa₃ = 9.17) and a pI of 7.59.

## Electrophoresis: which way does it move?

Pauling's experiment applied the rule. In **electrophoresis**, molecules in a buffer sit between two electrodes: the negative **cathode** and the positive **anode**. Below its pI a molecule is net positive and moves to the cathode; above its pI it moves to the anode; at its pI it stays put.

## The payoff: one swap, two properties

Now the opening question: sickle-cell hemoglobin (HbS), with **valine** for **glutamate** at beta position 6.

**Charge explains the gel.** Glutamate's side chain is negative at 7.4; valine has no switch. Each of hemoglobin's two beta chains loses one negative charge, so HbS is +2 per molecule relative to normal hemoglobin (HbA). In an alkaline buffer, where hemoglobin is net negative, HbA runs toward the anode faster than HbS.

**Predict.** Hemoglobin C (HbC), a real variant, replaces the same glutamate with lysine. How much does that change the charge of each hemoglobin molecule, compared with normal HbA? Would you expect HbC to form fibers?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Lysine is +1 where glutamate was −1: +2 per chain, **+4 per molecule**, twice HbS's change. On the same gel HbC runs slower still, so toward the anode the order is HbA, HbS, HbC.

Yet HbC does not form sickle fibers. It forms crystals and causes a much milder anemia. Lysine, like glutamate, is charged and hydrophilic, so it plants no greasy patch.

</details>

So **charge explains how Pauling saw the defect; polarity explains why the cells sickle.** Hydrophilic glutamate is at home on the protein's water-facing surface; hydrophobic valine plants a greasy patch there. When hemoglobin gives up its oxygen, its shape shifts and a matching hydrophobic pocket opens on its surface. The valine patch of one molecule sticks into that pocket on the next, chaining hemoglobin into rigid fibers that deform the red cell into a sickle. That is where the disease starts; hemolysis, inflammation and blocked vessels follow from it. Charge and polarity, both set by structure, run from the membrane helix to the active site to the clinic.

## Going deeper

The main line above does not depend on these details.

### Assigning L and D: the CORN rule

View the alpha-carbon with the H pointing toward you and trace CO → R → N. Clockwise reads L, counterclockwise D; view from the other side and the handedness flips. The D residues in some peptides are made by enzymes after the ribosome. In R/S terms, L is S for every chiral amino acid except cysteine, whose sulfur-bearing side chain makes it R.

### Thalidomide

Chirality has a body count. Thalidomide was sold in the 1950s as a sedative; one mirror-image form is thought to carry its sedative effect and the other its power to cause severe birth defects in roughly ten thousand children. Because the body converts each form into the other within hours, even the pure "safe" form would not have been safe.

### pKa values inside proteins

A protein's local environment can shift a pKa substantially: a buried Asp or a Lys in a hydrophobic pocket can move several pH units, and histidine's is often shifted nearer to 7. Such shifts underlie pH-dependent catalysis and shape changes.

### Small print on the numbers

- **The uncharged form.** The −NH₂/−COOH form is a tiny minority at every pH in water.
- **The exact pI.** Strictly, the pI is the pH where the net-charge sum is zero; the averaging shortcut matches it to within about 0.02 for the common ionizable amino acids, with tyrosine furthest off.
- **Real curves.** At very low pH, part of the added base goes to free H⁺ in the water, so on a measured curve the carboxyl's half-equivalence point reads its pKa only approximately; the plotter draws the idealized curve.
- **Tyr and Cys.** At pH 7.4 tyrosine's side chain is about 99.8% neutral and free cysteine's about 86% (14% thiolate), so neither normally forms salt bridges.
- **Numbering.** Clinical genetics counts beta-globin from the starting methionine, which the finished chain lacks, so the sickle change Glu6Val is written p.Glu7Val.

### Detecting amino acids in the lab

**Chromatography** sorts a mixture of amino acids by charge and hydrophobicity, the two side-chain properties this lesson is built on, and **ninhydrin** stains free alpha-amino groups purple (proline turns yellow) so each spot shows. The Techniques panel has more.
