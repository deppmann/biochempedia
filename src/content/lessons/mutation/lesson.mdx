scientists:
  - name: "Salvador Luria"
    contribution: >-
      Did the experiments of the 1943 fluctuation test, comparing counts of virus-resistant
      bacteria across many independent cultures of E. coli B, and found the wide scatter that
      pointed to mutation before exposure.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Luria & Delbrück, 'Mutations of bacteria from virus sensitivity to virus resistance', Genetics 28:491–511 (1943); Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1969, nobelprize.org."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Luria trained in medicine in Italy and came to bacterial viruses, the phages, by way of
      physics-minded biology. By the early 1940s he was working in the United States on how
      bacteria and their viruses interact. One puzzle stood out. When E. coli B is flooded with a
      phage, almost every cell dies, yet a few survive and give rise to colonies that the virus
      can no longer kill. Did the virus somehow make those few cells resistant, or were they
      already different? Luria saw that the two ideas predict different patterns across many
      small cultures, and set out to count. The 1943 paper in Genetics, written with Max
      Delbrück, states in a footnote that Luria was responsible for the experiments. The answer,
      that resistance arose in that system without the virus's help, showed that bacteria have
      genes that mutate like those of flies and maize, and helped make them a subject for
      genetics. Crossing and mapping came later, after Joshua Lederberg and Edward Tatum found
      gene recombination in E. coli in 1946. In 1969 Luria shared the Nobel Prize in
      Physiology or Medicine with Delbrück and Alfred Hershey.
  - name: "Max Delbrück"
    contribution: >-
      Supplied the theory of the 1943 fluctuation test: the prediction that mutations arising at
      random times during growth give a few huge clones and many cultures with none, and a way to
      estimate the mutation rate from the cultures with no mutants.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Luria & Delbrück, Genetics 28:491–511 (1943), footnote on p. 491; Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1969, nobelprize.org."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Delbrück came to biology from theoretical physics, and his part in the 1943 paper was the
      mathematics. The footnote on its first page credits him with the theory. The question he
      answered was what each hypothesis predicts about the numbers. If the virus induces
      resistance, every cell exposed has the same small chance of surviving, and counts from
      separate cultures should scatter only as much as random sampling allows, with a variance
      close to the mean. If resistance arises by mutation during growth, a mutation that happens
      early leaves a large family of resistant descendants, a late one leaves a few cells, and
      most cultures may have none. The counts should then scatter far more widely. He also showed
      how the fraction of cultures with no mutants gives the average number of mutation events,
      a method still taught and used. The paper made counting and probability a standard tool of
      microbial genetics. Delbrück shared the 1969 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with
      Luria and Alfred Hershey for work on the replication and genetic structure of viruses.
  - name: "Esther Lederberg"
    contribution: >-
      With Joshua Lederberg, invented replica plating and used it to recover resistant bacteria
      from populations that had never met the selecting phage or drug, a direct demonstration
      that the resistant mutants were already there.

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Esther Lederberg was a microbial geneticist at the University of Wisconsin, where the 1952
      replica-plating paper was written in the Department of Genetics. The paper has two authors,
      Joshua Lederberg and Esther M. Lederberg, and the method is often credited to Joshua alone;
      the byline does not support that, and it does not divide the work between them either. The
      idea was simple enough to use in any laboratory. Press a piece of sterile velvet onto a
      plate of colonies, then press the velvet onto fresh plates, and each new plate receives a
      print of the first with every colony in its place. With E. coli K-12 strains they printed

      thousands of colonies for mutants that fail to grow under one condition, the basis of many
      bacterial and yeast mutant hunts.
  - name: "Bruce Ames"
    contribution: >-
      Built a fast bacterial test for mutagens from histidine-requiring Salmonella strains, added
      a liver extract so that chemicals the body converts into mutagens could be detected, and
      with colleagues tested hundreds of chemicals, including known carcinogens.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Ames, Durston, Yamasaki & Lee, PNAS 70:2281–2285 (1973); McCann, Choi, Yamasaki & Ames, PNAS 72:5135–5139 (1975)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Ames was a biochemist who had studied the genes for histidine synthesis in Salmonella, and
      the mutagen test he built uses histidine-requiring Salmonella mutants. Mutant strains that cannot
      make histidine form no colonies on a plate that lacks it, unless a further mutation restores
      their ability to make histidine. Comparing the count of revertant colonies with the untreated background shows whether a chemical causes mutations in these strains.
      Many carcinogens, though, do nothing to bacteria until the body's enzymes alter them. In
      1973, at the University of California, Berkeley, Ames, William Durston, Edith Yamasaki and
      Frank Lee added rat or human liver homogenate to the plates and reported that 18 tested
      carcinogens were converted into frameshift mutagens. In 1975 the laboratory introduced the
      strains TA98 and TA100, and Joyce McCann and colleagues reported that 156 of 174 carcinogens
      in their tested set were mutagenic in the assay. A chemical could now be screened in a few
      days on a bench, long before the years an animal cancer study takes.

techniques:
  - name: "Fluctuation analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Grow many small independent cultures from few cells, plate each one entirely on a selective
      medium, and count mutant colonies per culture. The scatter across cultures tells you whether
      mutants arose during growth, and the distribution gives an estimate of the average number of
      mutation events per culture and so the mutation rate.
  - name: "Replica plating"
    blurb: >-
      Press sterile velvet onto a master plate and print its colonies, in their positions, onto
      plates with different media or agents. A colony that fails to grow on one print, or grows
      only there, marks a likely mutant at that spot on the master, which you can pick without
      ever exposing it and then confirm by retesting.
  - name: "Bacterial reverse-mutation (Ames) test"
    blurb: >-
      Expose amino-acid-requiring tester strains to a chemical, with and without a liver enzyme
      fraction, on plates with only a trace of the amino acid. Count revertant colonies against
      the spontaneous background; a dose-related rise flags the chemical, or a metabolite, as a
      mutagen in bacteria.
  - name: "Mutation accumulation with whole-genome sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      Pass separate lines through repeated single-cell bottlenecks (each round restarted from one cell) so that selection barely acts, then
      sequence the genomes and count the changes. It measures mutation across the whole genome
      instead of at one selectable gene.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "A point mutation changes a C:G base pair into a T:A base pair. How is this change classified at the DNA level?"
    choices:
      - "A transversion, since a pyrimidine replaced a purine"
      - "A transition, since a pyrimidine replaced a pyrimidine"
      - "A frameshift, since the base-pair count has changed"
      - "An in-frame indel, since one codon has been altered"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      C and T are both pyrimidines (and G and A both purines), so swapping one for the other is a
      transition. A transversion would exchange a purine for a pyrimidine, which did not happen
      here. A frameshift needs an insertion or deletion, but a substitution keeps the number of
      base pairs the same. An in-frame indel also adds or removes bases, in multiples of three,
      which a single substitution does not do.
  - stem: "Ultraviolet light joins two adjacent thymines in a cell's DNA into a dimer. Which statement best describes the dimer and mutation?"
    choices:
      - "It is a mutation at once, because the chemistry of the DNA has changed"
      - "It cannot lead to a mutation, because a dimer always stops replication for good"
      - "It is damage, and becomes a mutation only if repair or copying leaves a heritable sequence change"
      - "It is a mutation only if the dimer lies inside a coding sequence"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "A dimer is damage, not yet a mutation. Whether it becomes one depends on repair and on how polymerases copy past it, so a changed sequence that is then inherited is what counts. Calling it a mutation at once treats damage as if it were a heritable sequence change. Dimers do not always stop replication for good, since cells can copy past them, sometimes with errors. Where the dimer lies does not decide whether it is a mutation; what matters is whether the sequence is changed after repair and copying."
  - stem: "A single-base change in an exon replaces one codon with a synonymous codon for the same amino acid, yet carriers show a reduced amount of the protein. Which explanation is most plausible?"
    choices:
      - "The change can disrupt a splicing signal or mRNA handling"
      - "The change must still alter the amino acid at that position"
      - "The change shifts the reading frame for the rest of the gene"
      - "The change turns the codon into a stop codon by definition"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Exons carry more than codons: sequences that guide splicing, and features that affect mRNA
      stability and translation, so a synonymous change can lower protein output. By definition a
      synonymous change keeps the same amino acid, so the amino-acid explanation is wrong. A
      substitution does not change the number of bases, so it cannot shift the frame directly; any
      frame change would come through faulty splicing, the explanation in the correct choice. A change to a stop codon would be a nonsense mutation,
      not a synonymous one.
  - stem: "One bacterial culture of 10^9 cells contains 500 resistant mutants, and suppose they all descend from a single mutation that happened early in growth. What does the ratio 500/10^9 measure?"
    choices:
      - "The mutation rate per cell division in this culture"
      - "The number of separate mutation events in the culture"
      - "The mutant frequency, which one early event can inflate"
      - "The chance that the selecting agent induced resistance"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Mutants divided by total cells is the mutant frequency, and one early event that leaves a
      large clone, a jackpot, can make it high. A rate counts mutation events per cell division,
      and here there was only one event, so the ratio greatly overstates it. The number of events
      is one, not 500. The ratio says nothing about induction, because the mutants arose during
      growth, before any selection.
  - stem: "You treat E. coli with ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) and sequence many independent mutants. Which kind of change should dominate the mutations you find?"
    choices:
      - "A:T to C:G transversions spread across the genome"
      - "G:C to A:T transitions arising from ethylated guanine"
      - "Single-base insertions at runs of repeated bases"
      - "Large deletions that span several adjacent genes"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      EMS ethylates guanine, and one product, O6-ethylguanine, tends to pair with thymine, so after
      replication a G:C pair becomes A:T, a transition. Transversions are not the main EMS
      signature. Single-base insertions at repeats are typical of replication slippage and of
      intercalators such as proflavine. Large multi-gene deletions are more typical of ionizing
      radiation, which breaks DNA strands.

mcatQuestions:
  - stem: "Twenty independent cultures of a bacterium are each started from a few cells and grown to 1 x 10^8 cells, then plated entirely on an antibiotic. Six plates have no resistant colonies; the other fourteen have between 1 and 140. Assume all mutants are detected, grow like the parent strain, and arise only before plating. What is the approximate mutation rate to resistance per cell division?"
    choices:
      - "About 3.0 x 10^-9 per cell division"
      - "About 7.0 x 10^-9 per cell division"
      - "About 1.2 x 10^-8 per cell division"
      - "About 3.0 x 10^-1 per cell division"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The fraction of cultures with no mutants is 6/20 = 0.3, and under the Poisson model for
      mutation events that fraction equals e^-m, so m = -ln 0.3 = 1.20 events per culture. With
      about 10^8 divisions per culture, the rate is 1.2 x 10^-8. Dividing 0.3 itself by 10^8 skips
      the logarithm. Using the 14/20 fraction with mutants gives 7 x 10^-9, which also skips it.
      A value of 0.3 forgets to divide by the number of divisions.
  - stem: "A master plate of E. coli grown without streptomycin is printed with sterile velvet onto three plates containing streptomycin. Resistant colonies appear at the same four positions on all three prints. Cells picked from those four positions on the master plate, which never met the drug, grow into resistant cultures. Which conclusion do these results best support?"
    choices:
      - "Resistant mutants were present on the master before any exposure"
      - "Streptomycin induced resistance in the cells it contacted on prints"
      - "Resistance arose separately on each print after cells were transferred"
      - "Crowding on the master plate raised the mutation rate of its cells"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Matching positions on separate prints mean the resistant cells came from the same spots on
      the master, and cells from those spots were resistant without exposure, so the mutants were
      already there. Induction by the drug cannot explain resistance in cells that never met it.
      Independent origin on each print would put colonies at random, unmatched positions. Nothing
      in the data compares rates, so a crowding effect on the rate is not supported.
  - stem: "A compound is tested in Salmonella strains TA98, which detects frameshift mutations, and TA100, which detects base-pair substitutions, with and without rat-liver S9. TA98 revertants rise about fourfold over background only when S9 is present. TA100 counts stay at background with and without S9. Which conclusion fits these data?"
    choices:
      - "The compound acts directly as a base-substitution mutagen"
      - "The compound has been shown to cause cancer in human liver"
      - "The compound causes base substitutions only after activation"
      - "A metabolite of the compound causes frameshifts in bacteria"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The rise appears only in the frameshift strain and only with liver enzymes, so a product of
      metabolism causes frameshifts in these bacteria. A direct-acting base-substitution mutagen
      would raise TA100 counts without S9, which did not happen. Base substitution after
      activation would raise TA100 with S9, which also did not happen. An Ames result is a

      Under the Lea and Coulson model, with equal mutant and non-mutant growth, no death and no

Selection is not the only sorter; chance (drift) also changes how common variants become, especially in small populations. ## Classify the sequence change, then its consequence

A **mutation** is a heritable change in the DNA sequence. Here, **point mutation** means a change to one base pair or the addition or removal of a few bases. Classify it twice: by the DNA change, then, in a coding sequence, by the protein effect.

**By the DNA change.** A **transition** swaps a purine for a purine (A and G) or a pyrimidine for a pyrimidine (C and T). A **transversion** swaps a purine for a pyrimidine or the reverse. Each base has one transition partner and two transversion partners, so there are 4 possible transitions and 8 possible transversions. Even so, in many organisms studied, transitions are the more common kind. An **insertion** or **deletion** (together, an **indel**) adds or removes bases.

**Predict.** A G:C base pair becomes T:A. Is that a transition or a transversion? Can this alone tell you its protein effect?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A transversion: G, a purine, is replaced by T, a pyrimidine. Its protein effect depends on whether it lies in a coding sequence and which codon it changes.

</details>

**By the protein effect.** Inside a coding sequence:

- A **synonymous** (silent) change gives a codon for the same amino acid.
- A **missense** change gives a different amino acid. Some missense changes leave the protein working; the label does not tell you the severity.
- A **nonsense** change turns an amino-acid codon into a stop codon.
- A **frameshift** is an indel whose length is not a multiple of three, so every codon downstream is read in different groups.
- An **in-frame indel** adds or removes a multiple of three bases. The downstream frame is kept, and the protein gains or loses amino acids.

Think of a sentence written in three-letter words: THE CAT ATE THE RAT. Change one letter and one word changes. Delete one letter and every group after it changes. Delete one whole word and the later groups stay aligned.

**Predict.** A deletion removes exactly three bases from the middle of a coding sequence. Must it shift the reading frame?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Three bases make one codon, so the frame is kept and later codons stay grouped as before. The coding sequence loses one codon's worth of bases. A deletion aligned with a codon usually removes one amino acid; one that straddles two codons can also change the junction amino acid, and if the junction becomes a stop codon, the protein is cut much shorter.

</details>

“Silent” describes the codon, not the consequence. Exons also carry signals for splicing, mRNA stability and translation speed, so a synonymous change can still lower the amount of protein. How the code is read is the subject of [the genetic code lesson](/lessons/genetic-code-and-translation/).

## Does selection cause the mutation? Luria and Delbrück

The first film showed both hypotheses and the test. In numbers: repeated samples of one bulk culture measure sampling and plating error alone; induction predicts a variance close to the mean; mutation first predicts one far larger. Luria (experiments) and Delbrück (theory) used *E. coli* B and phage T1, virus α in their 1943 *Genetics* paper. Independent cultures scattered far beyond sampling error, so they concluded that, in their system, resistance arose independently of the virus.

### From scatter to a mutation rate

Ten independent 0.2 mL cultures and one bulk culture of *E. coli* each grow to about 2 x 10^8 cells per 0.2 mL. Each independent culture and ten 0.2 mL bulk samples are plated on T1. Assume every mutant survives plating and is counted, mutants grow like the parent, no mutations arise after plating, and divisions roughly equal the final cell number.

Independent cultures: 0, 0, 1, 0, 3, 0, 0, 52, 2, 2. Bulk samples: 4, 8, 6, 3, 9, 5, 7, 6, 8, 4.

1. **Means.** Both sets sum to 60, so both means are 6.0.
2. **Variances.** Sample variances are about 262 for the independent cultures and 4.0 for the bulk samples (arithmetic under Going deeper).
3. **Interpretation.** The bulk variance is close to its mean, as Poisson sampling predicts. The independent variance is about 44 times its mean. Mutation before exposure fits; induction does not.
4. **Rate.** Five of ten cultures have no mutants, so p0 = 0.5. If mutation events per culture follow a Poisson distribution with mean m, then p0 = e^-m.

> **m = -ln(p0) = -ln(0.5) = 0.69 mutation events per culture**
>
> **rate = m / divisions = 0.69 / (2 x 10^8) = 3.5 x 10^-9 per cell per division**

**Mutant frequency** is mutant cells divided by all cells; **mutation rate** counts mutation events per division. In the jackpot culture, frequency is 52 / (2 x 10^8) = 2.6 x 10^-7, about 75 times the rate, from what was probably one early event. Confusing them makes one lucky culture look like a mutator, a strain with an elevated mutation rate.

**Predict.** Suppose the jackpot clone gains 100 more resistant descendants, but no new mutation event occurs. Does p0, and therefore this zero-class rate estimate, change?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. That culture was already nonempty, so p0 is unchanged. Mutant frequency rises; this rate estimate does not.

</details>

Rates do change with a cell's condition, as in pulses of mutagenesis after alkylation damage in single *E. coli* cells, and debate over stress and mutation continues; none of this reverses the 1943 result for phage resistance.

## Replica plating: seeing the mutants before the test

The fluctuation test is an argument from statistics. In 1952 Joshua and Esther Lederberg gave a direct one. They pressed sterile velvet onto a master plate grown without a selecting agent, then printed its colonies in the same positions onto fresh plates with phage or streptomycin.

**Predict.** If resistant cells were already present on the master, what spatial pattern should appear on separate selective prints?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Resistant colonies should appear at matching positions. Each match points back to the same colony on the unexposed master plate.

</details>

That is what the *E. coli* K-12 prints showed. The Lederbergs sampled those master-plate spots, whose cells had never touched the phage or drug, and purified resistant cultures through repeated growth and re-plating. Replica plating also became a workhorse for finding mutants that fail to grow on one medium, the start of many screens like those in [complementation and pathways](/lessons/complementation-and-pathways/).

## Where mutations come from: spontaneous and induced

**Spontaneous** mutations arise from the cell's own chemistry and copying, with no applied mutagen.

- **Replication errors.** DNA polymerase sometimes inserts the wrong base (see [DNA replication](/lessons/dna-replication/)).
- **Deamination.** Cytosine loses its amino group and becomes uracil, which repair enzymes recognize as foreign. 5-Methylcytosine deaminates to thymine, a normal base, so the G:T mismatch is harder to resolve correctly. CpG sites, where cytosines are often methylated in vertebrates, are hot spots for C:G to T:A transitions. The chemistry is in [nucleotide metabolism](/lessons/nucleotide-metabolism/).
- **Depurination.** The bond between a purine and its sugar breaks, leaving a site with no base to copy.
- **Slippage.** In repeated bases, the new strand can loop out or slip back during copying, adding or removing repeat units.

**Induced** mutations come from outside agents, called **mutagens**.

- **Base analogs** such as 5-bromouracil are built into DNA in place of a normal base and mispair, giving transitions.
- **Alkylating agents** such as ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) add groups to bases. EMS chiefly gives G:C to A:T transitions.
- **Intercalators** such as proflavine slip between base pairs and favor single-base insertions and deletions, so frameshifts.
- **Ultraviolet light** joins adjacent pyrimidines into dimers. Each dimer is damage until repair or copying past it leaves a heritable sequence change.
- **Ionizing radiation** breaks DNA strands and damages bases.

Hermann Muller reported in *Science* in 1927 that X-rays greatly raise the mutation rate in *Drosophila*, in experiments that counted new lethal mutations on the X chromosome. He received the Nobel Prize in 1946. How cells fix damage before it becomes mutation is the subject of [DNA repair and recombination](/lessons/dna-repair-and-recombination/).

## The Ames test and what it can and cannot say

Bruce Ames's test uses *Salmonella* strains that cannot make histidine because of a mutation in a histidine gene. On a plate with only a trace of histidine, they divide a few times and stop unless a new mutation restores histidine synthesis. Each **revertant** colony marks such an event, sometimes at the original site and sometimes at a second, compensating site. TA100 reverts mainly by base-pair substitution; TA98 reverts mainly by frameshift. Plates run with and without **S9**, a rat-liver enzyme fraction, because many carcinogens become reactive only after liver metabolism.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/mutation/ames-test-activation.webp" width={1200} height={779} alt="AI-generated illustration: a petri dish with many tiny stalled bacterial specks and three larger revertant colonies, with an inset showing a test chemical passing through an S9 liver fraction to become a reactive metabolite that reaches a bacterium." caption="Each revertant colony marks a new mutation that restored histidine synthesis; plates with and without S9 test whether liver metabolism makes a chemical mutagenic." />

Read an Ames result in a fixed order: compare with spontaneous background, look for a dose response, compare strains, then compare with and without S9.

A positive result shows that a chemical, or a metabolite, mutates bacteria; it does not measure cancer risk in people. A negative result does not prove safety, because some carcinogens act without mutating DNA or through processes bacteria lack.

**Predict.** TA100 gives about 120 spontaneous revertants per plate and TA98 about 30. Compound X is tested at three rising doses. TA100 without S9: 125, 118, 130. TA100 with S9: 240, 480, 950. TA98 with S9: 30, 28, 35. What does X do, and what does the result not show?

## Going deeper

### The arithmetic behind the variances

For the independent cultures, the squared deviations from 6 are 36, 36, 25, 36, 9, 36, 36, 2,116, 16 and 16, summing to 2,362. Divided by 9, the sample variance is about 262. For the bulk samples, the squared deviations sum to 36, so the variance is 36/9 = 4.0.

### Tautomers and mismatch shapes

Rare alternative forms of bases, called tautomers, were proposed long ago as one cause of mispairing. NMR work has since detected short-lived tautomeric and ionized forms that let a wrong pair take the shape of a correct one, though how much they add to mutation in living cells is still being worked out.

### Ames validation counts

In 1973 Ames and colleagues reported that liver homogenate converted 18 tested carcinogens into frameshift mutagens. In McCann and colleagues' 1975 set, 156 of 174 tested carcinogens were positive, a figure for that set of chemicals, not a general probability that a carcinogen will test positive.
