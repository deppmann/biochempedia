## Explain: genes, alleles and the hidden white

<WordBank terms={['gene', 'allele', 'dominant', 'recessive', 'homozygous', 'heterozygous', 'genotype', 'phenotype', 'Punnett square', 'probability', 'dihybrid cross']} />

A **gene** is a section of DNA that is a basic unit of inheritance. Many genes code for a protein. An **allele** is one version of a gene. Pea plants have a flower-color gene with a purple allele and a white allele.

For most genes, your body cells carry two alleles, one from each parent. Peas are the same. We write alleles as letters: P for purple and p for white.

- A **dominant** allele shows its trait even when only one copy is present. We write it as a capital letter.
- A **recessive** allele shows its trait only when both copies are recessive. We write it as a small letter.
- **Homozygous** means the two alleles are the same, like PP or pp.
- **Heterozygous** means the two alleles are different, like Pp.

Your **genotype** is the pair of alleles you carry, such as Pp. Your **phenotype** is the trait that shows, such as purple flowers. A phenotype is anything you can observe or measure, including chemical traits, such as whether your body makes a certain enzyme.

| Genotype | Homozygous or heterozygous? | Phenotype |
|---|---|---|
| PP | homozygous dominant | purple |
| Pp | heterozygous | purple |
| pp | homozygous recessive | white |

Now the hidden white makes sense. Mendel’s first-generation plants were all Pp. They looked purple, but each one still carried a white allele.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/punnett-squares/white-hides-a-generation.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: three rows of pea plants. A purple-flowered plant crossed with a white-flowered plant gives three purple-flowered offspring; when those fertilize themselves, the next row has three purple plants and one white plant." caption="Where the white was hiding: every first-generation plant was purple but carried a white allele, so white returned in about one plant in four." />

When a plant makes sex cells by meiosis, the two alleles of each gene separate. Each sex cell gets one allele. This is called the **law of segregation**. You can read how meiosis does this in [that lesson](/lessons/meiosis-and-variation/).

**Probability** is the chance that something will happen, from 0 (never) to 1 (always). For a Pp plant, each sex cell has a 50 percent chance of carrying P and a 50 percent chance of carrying p. It is like a coin toss. The analogy breaks in one place: a coin has no connection to the parent, while the allele in the sex cell really came from the parent’s own pair.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A heterozygous parent passes on each of its two alleles with a 50 percent chance, so a recessive allele can stay hidden for a generation and then show up again.</Support>

## Explain: building a Punnett square

A **Punnett square** is a grid that shows every way one sex cell from each parent can combine. Put one parent’s possible sex cells across the top and the other parent’s down the side. Fill each box with one allele from the top and one from the side.

Here is the cross of two Pp plants. Because both parents are heterozygous for one gene, this is a monohybrid cross.

| | P (1/2) | p (1/2) |
|---|---|---|
| **P (1/2)** | PP | Pp |
| **p (1/2)** | Pp | pp |

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/punnett-squares/pp-cross-square.webp" width={1200} height={1200} keyItems={["one parent\u2019s sex cells", "the other parent\u2019s sex cells", "homozygous dominant", "heterozygous", "homozygous recessive"]} given={[0, 1]} alt="AI-generated illustration: a two-by-two grid with two sex cells across the top and two down the side, each carrying one coloured dot for its allele. Each box combines one dot from the top and one from the side; three boxes hold purple flowers and one holds a white flower. Five numbered circles mark the parts." caption="A Pp × Pp Punnett square drawn with dots for alleles: each box takes one allele from the top and one from the side. Name the five numbered parts." />

Each box has a 1 in 4 chance, because 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4.

- Genotype ratio: 1 PP : 2 Pp : 1 pp, or 1:2:1.
- Phenotype ratio: 3 purple : 1 white, or 3:1, because PP and Pp both look purple.

A **dihybrid cross** follows two genes at once. Mendel studied plants that were heterozygous for seed shape (R round, r wrinkled) and seed color (Y yellow, y green). If the two genes sort into sex cells independently, getting R or r does not affect whether a sex cell gets Y or y. An RrYy plant then makes four kinds of sex cells, RY, Ry, rY and ry, each with a 1 in 4 chance. This is the **law of independent assortment**. It holds well for genes on different chromosomes.

| | RY | Ry | rY | ry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **RY** | RRYY | RRYy | RrYY | RrYy |
| **Ry** | RRYy | RRyy | RrYy | Rryy |
| **rY** | RrYY | RrYy | rrYY | rrYy |
| **ry** | RrYy | Rryy | rrYy | rryy |

Count the 16 boxes by phenotype:

> round yellow : round green : wrinkled yellow : wrinkled green = 9 : 3 : 3 : 1

Each box is one of 16 equally likely outcomes. It is not a promise about the next 16 seeds. Four seeds from two Pp plants could include no white plants at all. The chance of that is 3/4 × 3/4 × 3/4 × 3/4, about 32 percent.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A Punnett square gives the probability of each outcome, so real counts of offspring scatter around its ratios instead of matching them exactly.</Support>

### Design problem

You have a purple-flowered pea plant. You do not know whether it is PP or Pp. Plan a cross that would tell you, and decide how many offspring you need.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Cross the purple plant with a white plant (pp). This is a test cross. A PP parent can only give P, so every offspring would be purple. A Pp parent gives p half the time, so about half the offspring would be white.

- **Independent variable:** the unknown genotype of the purple parent.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of purple and white offspring.
- **Constants:** the same white parent line, the same soil, water, light and growing time.
- **Control of pollen:** remove the purple flower’s stamens before they ripen, brush on pollen from the white plant, and cover the flower so insects cannot bring other pollen.
- **Sample size:** grow at least 20 offspring. With 20 offspring from a Pp parent, the chance of getting no white plants by bad luck is under one in a million. With only 2 offspring it is 1 in 4.

Even one white offspring means the purple parent is Pp. All purple in a large sample means it is very likely PP.
