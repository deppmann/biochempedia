## Explain: the cell cycle

<WordBank terms={['cell cycle', 'interphase', 'mitosis', 'chromosome', 'daughter cell', 'prophase', 'metaphase', 'anaphase', 'telophase']} />

All living things grow and reproduce. When you grow, the number of cells in your body goes up. When you heal, new cells replace the ones you lost. In both cases, cells that already exist divide.

Inside the nucleus are **chromosomes**, the threads that carry a cell’s instructions (DNA). You meet the [nucleus](/lessons/inside-a-cell/) and [DNA](/lessons/dna-the-code-of-life/) in other lessons. A typical human body cell that has a nucleus has 46 chromosomes.

The **cell cycle** is the series of steps a cell goes through from when it forms until it divides. It has two main parts.

- **Interphase** is the part of the cycle when the cell grows and copies its chromosomes. It is usually the longest part. The cell is busy, not resting.
- **Mitosis** is the part of the cycle when the cell divides into two new cells with the same chromosomes.

Each new cell is called a **daughter cell**. The cell that divided is the parent cell.

Copying matters. A copied chromosome is still one chromosome. It is made of two identical copies joined together. So a cell with 4 chromosomes still has 4 after copying, but each one now has two copies.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/cells-divide/copied-chromosome-still-one.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a nucleus with four single coloured chromosomes, an arrow, and the same nucleus after copying, now with four X-shaped chromosomes, each made of two identical copies joined in the middle. A zoom shows one copied chromosome." caption="A copied chromosome is still one chromosome, made of two identical copies joined together. A cell with 4 chromosomes still has 4 after copying." />

Think of a recipe book. If you tear it in half, each person gets half the recipes. If you copy it first, each person gets the whole book. The comparison breaks in one place: a cell does not decide to copy. Its molecules do the copying, step by step.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** In interphase a cell grows and copies its chromosomes, and in mitosis it divides into two daughter cells.</Support>

## Explain: the four stages of mitosis

Mitosis has four stages. They always come in the same order.

| Stage | What the chromosomes do |
|---|---|
| **Prophase** | The chromosomes coil up and get thick enough to see. |
| **Metaphase** | The chromosomes line up in a row across the middle of the cell. |
| **Anaphase** | The two copies of each chromosome are pulled to opposite ends. |
| **Telophase** | Two new nuclei form, and the cell pinches into two. |

**Prophase** is the stage when the copied chromosomes coil up and become visible. **Metaphase** is the stage when they line up across the middle. **Anaphase** is the stage when the copies are pulled apart. Thin fibers in the cell do the pulling. **Telophase** is the stage when a new nucleus forms at each end and the cell splits. A plant cell has a stiff wall, so it cannot pinch. A new wall forms across its middle instead.

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/cells-divide/mitosis-four-stages-strip.webp" width={1200} height={450} keyItems={["prophase", "metaphase", "anaphase", "telophase", "daughter cell"]} given={[1, 4]} alt="AI-generated illustration: one round cell shown in five numbered steps from left to right. Its four coloured chromosomes, each copied into two joined copies, first coil up, then line up across the middle, then have their copies pulled to opposite ends, then the cell pinches in two, ending as two cells that each hold four chromosomes, one of each colour." caption="Mitosis always goes in the same order, and copying first means nothing is lost. Name the numbered stages and the cells at the end." />

Count the chromosomes in a model cell. It starts with 4. In interphase, each of the 4 is copied. In anaphase, one copy of each goes to each end. Each daughter cell ends up with 4 chromosomes, the same as the parent cell. Skip the copying, and each daughter cell would get only 2.

Mistakes can happen, but in normal mitosis each daughter cell gets a complete set.

A one-celled living thing, such as an amoeba, reproduces by dividing. Its two daughter cells are two new amoebas. A different kind of division, **meiosis**, makes sex cells with half the parent’s genetic material. You study it in [Reproduction and Meiosis](/lessons/reproduction-and-meiosis/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Because each chromosome is copied first, each daughter cell gets the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell.</Support>

### Design problem

Does temperature change how fast onion roots grow longer? Plan an investigation.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do onion roots grow longer each day in a warm place than in a cool place?
- **Independent variable:** temperature, such as a cool spot near 15 °C and a warm spot near 25 °C, checked with a thermometer.
- **Dependent variable:** root length in millimetres, measured with a ruler each day for 5 days.
- **Constants:** the same kind and size of onion, the same cup and amount of water, the same light.
- **Control:** onions kept at normal room temperature, to compare with.
- **Repeated trials:** at least 5 onions at each temperature. Find the mean length for each group.
- **Safety:** wash your hands after touching onions. Do not taste anything. Your teacher handles any blades.

Roots grow when cells near the tip divide and the new cells stretch. Faster growth in the warm group would suggest that temperature speeds this up. Seeing the dividing cells themselves would need stained slides, which your teacher prepares.

</details>
