> **The one idea: copy first, then share.** Before a cell divides, it copies every chromosome. Then mitosis, the dividing step, gives one copy of each chromosome to each new cell. So both get the same full set as the parent: one of every chromosome.
>
> *Where it applies:* body cells and one-celled living things that divide this way. Mistakes can happen. Sex cells come from a different kind of division (see Going deeper).

## Explain: the cell cycle

<WordBank terms={['cell cycle', 'interphase', 'mitosis', 'chromosome', 'daughter cell', 'prophase', 'metaphase', 'anaphase', 'telophase']} />

All living things grow and reproduce. When you grow, the number of cells in your body goes up; when you heal, new cells replace the ones you lost. Either way, cells that already exist divide. Key words:

- The **cell cycle** is a cell’s steps from when it forms until it divides.
- **Interphase** is when the cell grows and copies its chromosomes.
- **Mitosis** is when the cell divides into two new cells with the same chromosomes.
- Each new cell is a **daughter cell**; the cell that divided is the parent cell.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/cells-divide/copied-chromosome-still-one.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a nucleus with four single coloured chromosomes, an arrow, and the same nucleus after copying, with four chromosomes drawn as X shapes, each made of two identical copies joined in the middle. A zoom shows one copied chromosome." caption="A cell with 4 chromosomes still has 4 after copying, each now two joined copies. The X is drawn for clarity: in interphase the copies stay thin and spread out, and they coil into a thick X only in prophase." />

Think of a recipe book. Tear it in half, and each person gets half the recipes. Copy it first, and each person gets the whole book. In a cell the “book” is many separate chromosomes, and each one is copied.

## Explain: spotting the four stages of mitosis

To sort cells in a photo, you need what each stage looks like frozen:

| Stage | What you see in a stained cell |
|---|---|
| **Prophase** | Thick, coiled chromosomes, scattered, not in a row. |
| **Metaphase** | Chromosomes lined up in a row across the middle. |
| **Anaphase** | Two matching groups of chromosomes pulled to opposite ends. |
| **Telophase** | Two new nuclei forming at the ends as the cell splits in two. |

Two things the first film left out: thin fibers in the cell do the pulling in anaphase, and a plant cell, with its stiff wall, cannot pinch in two in telophase; a new wall forms across its middle instead.

### Design problem

Does temperature change how fast onion roots grow longer? Plan an investigation.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do onion roots grow longer each day in a warm place than in a cool place?
- **Independent variable:** temperature, such as a cool spot near 15 °C and a warm spot near 25 °C, checked with a thermometer.
- **Dependent variable:** root length in millimetres, measured with a ruler each day for 5 days.
- **Constants:** the same kind and size of onion, the same cup and amount of water, the same light.
- **Control:** onions kept at normal room temperature, to compare with.
- **Repeated trials:** at least 5 onions at each temperature. Find the mean length for each group.
- **Safety:** wash your hands after touching onions. Do not taste anything. Your teacher handles any blades.

Roots grow when cells near the tip divide and the new cells stretch. Faster growth in the warm group would suggest that temperature speeds this up. Seeing the dividing cells themselves would need stained slides, which your teacher prepares.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Where the onion numbers come from

The counts come from The Biology Project, University of Arizona: an onion (Allium cepa) root-tip cell cycle activity, completed-photo page dated 1997 and revised August 2004, which sorts 36 photographed cells. Its 24-hour cycle is a round number for the activity, not a measured time, and the page does not say how the photos were picked. The photos also seem to hold more dividing cells than usual, because in a real root tip most cells are in interphase.

### When a snapshot can tell time

The second film named the share-of-cells rule's limits. A stained root tip is frozen, but scientists can film living cells and follow one through every stage.

### Two fine points

Strictly, mitosis shares out the chromosomes into two new nuclei, and the rest of the cell splits at the end. Where the recipe book breaks: a cell does not decide to copy, as a person would; its molecules do it, step by step.

### One-celled living things and sex cells

A one-celled living thing, such as an amoeba, reproduces by dividing. Its two daughter cells are two new amoebas. A different kind of division, **meiosis**, makes sex cells with half the parent’s genetic material. You study it in [Reproduction and Meiosis](/lessons/reproduction-and-meiosis/).
