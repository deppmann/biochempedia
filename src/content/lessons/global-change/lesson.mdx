techniques:
  - name: "Range-shift meta-analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Published changes in range position from many studies are pooled and analysed together,
      keeping latitude and elevation apart. It gives medians and spreads across species and can
      test whether shifts are larger where warming was larger, but it inherits the geographic
      and taxonomic gaps of the studies it pools.
  - name: "Climatized-aviary temperature manipulation"
    blurb: >-
      Breeding birds are housed under controlled temperature patterns with food available, and
      laying dates are compared between treatments. It tests whether temperature itself changes
      timing, separating that effect from food and other variables that go with warm springs in
      the field.
  - name: "Factorial nutrient-addition and resource-rescue experiments"
    blurb: >-
      Plots receive added nitrogen or not, and each of those groups is split into plots with and without a second treatment that restores a
      resource suspected of being limiting (light to the understory, or water). If the restored
      resource prevents the species loss, that resource competition is implicated in that system.
  - name: "Buoyant weighing of calcifiers"
    blurb: >-
      An organism is weighed underwater before and after an exposure, and the change in its
      buoyant weight estimates how much calcium carbonate it gained or lost. It lets many species
      be compared under the same seawater chemistry.

Eleven metres uphill a decade looks feeble beside 17 kilometres poleward, the first film's two medians. Which shift kept up? Raw distances cannot say, because a species is racing its isotherm, not a distance.

> **The one idea.** Under warming, a range keeps up only by moving as fast as its **isotherm**, a line of equal temperature. The isotherm's speed, the **climate velocity**, is the warming rate divided by how fast temperature changes across the land; on a mountain that rate is the **lapse rate**, the drop in temperature with height. If a range started in step, its lag is the difference in speeds multiplied by the time.
>
> *Scope:* this is a model for a range edge set mainly by temperature, under steady warming and lapse rate. Ranges move through colonization and local loss over generations, not by animals walking, and a population can also persist without moving, through plasticity, evolution or cool pockets of habitat. A growing gap shows that something changed, not what caused it.

## Moving: species shift poleward and uphill

A species' **range** is the area where its populations live and reproduce. Temperature falls with latitude and elevation ([climate and biomes](/lessons/climate-and-biomes/)), so if temperature sets the edge of a species' tolerable conditions, part of its [niche](/lessons/niches-and-distributions/), the edge should follow its isotherm poleward and uphill.

How was the movement shown? I-Ching Chen, Chris D. Thomas and colleagues pooled published range shifts of terrestrial species in a **meta-analysis**, a statistical synthesis of results from many studies (Science, 2011). Each data point was the mean shift of one taxonomic group in one region. Shifts were greatest in studies whose regions had warmed most. That match between shift and warming is what links the shifts to climate here, though it is a correlation across studies.

Did the shifts keep up? In one European example the two medians track about the same temperature change, roughly 0.07 C per decade (Going deeper), so the medians alone cannot answer. Setting each study's shift against its own region's warming gave the verdict: poleward shifts on average roughly kept pace, but most uphill shifts fell short of what the warming predicted.

The pattern holds in the sea too. In BioShifts, a global database of 30,534 range-shift records, Lenoir and colleagues (2020) found marine species moving poleward about six times faster than terrestrial ones. Land species lagged further behind their shifting isotherms, and human activities hindered their tracking of isotherm shifts in latitude.

## Keeping pace: climate velocity on a mountain

**Predict.** On the first film's slope, whose round figures are invented, the isotherm climbs **50 m per decade**. A species moves uphill at Chen and colleagues' terrestrial median, 11.0 m per decade, the one rate here from data, and starts in step with its isotherm. What share of the isotherm's shift does it match, and how far behind is it after 30 years?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

1. *Share.* 11.0 / 50 = 0.22, or **22%** of the isotherm's shift.
2. *Lag over 30 years.* Over 3 decades the isotherm moves 3 x 50 = 150 m; the species moves 3 x 11.0 = 33 m. The species ends up **117 m** below the elevation that now has its former temperature.

</details>

Beyond the box's assumptions (constant warming and lapse rate, a range set by temperature alone), the sum assumes a species that started in step and no road, farm or cliff in the way. A real lag could reflect slow dispersal, cues other than temperature, competitors or predators already upslope, or loss of forest, and a particular species could be far ahead of its isotherm or far behind it.

**Why it matters.** Species do not move together, so communities come apart and reassemble, and hosts and pathogens meet in places they had not shared before (Van Hemert and colleagues, 2014, on Arctic birds). Pecl and colleagues (Science, 2017) reviewed evidence that climate-driven redistribution is already altering the composition of communities and changes ecosystem functioning and human well-being, through food production, disease transmission and carbon storage.

A similar gap can open in **phenology**, the seasonal timing of life-cycle events, when a consumer and its food shift by different amounts; the second film follows one bird and its food.

## Timing: phenology and mismatch

Besides the unassigned forests, the second film's "not yet" has a second reason: Both, Visser and colleagues (Nature, 2006) measured the cross-site caterpillar peaks in a single year (Going deeper), so the contrast supports, but does not by itself establish, a causal effect of timing on population size.

A loss at the nest must also carry through to the number of adults before mismatch reaches [demography](/lessons/demography/).

**Predict.** In a great tit population, mismatch cuts the number of young that fledge, or leave the nest. Must the population shrink?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not necessarily. Visser and Gienapp (2019) describe great tits in which mismatch reduced the number of young that fledged, yet more of those young survived their first winter in years when few were produced, so population growth showed no detectable effect. That is **density dependence**: a rate, here first-winter survival, that changes with the number of animals, so a loss at the nest can be made up later. A mismatch has to be linked to population change in the data, not assumed.

</details>

## The other drivers: land, aliens, acid and nitrogen

These drivers act alongside warming and need not act solely through a moving isotherm, though cleared land can also alter local climate; attribution to warming tests those plausible for the response, place and period. Direct exploitation, such as fishing, hunting and logging, is another major driver that this lesson leaves aside.

**Land use.** Converting forest and grassland to farms, pasture and towns removes habitat outright; reviews, among them the IPBES global assessment (2019), name this habitat loss as the largest single driver of the decline in land biodiversity. Newbold and colleagues (Nature, 2015) modelled a global database of terrestrial assemblages and estimated that land use and related pressures have reduced within-sample species richness by an average of **13.6%**, and by **76.5%** in the worst-affected habitats. These are model estimates from sampled sites. Land use also acts with climate: in average elevational shifts from 43 study sites, Guo, Lenoir and Bonebrake (2018) found that forest loss in warmer regions tended to go with faster upslope movement, a correlation across sites consistent with the first film's cleared lower slope. An uphill shift is not by itself proof that climate acted alone.

**Species moved by people.** An **alien species** is one moved by people beyond its native range (Blackburn and colleagues, 2014). Using IUCN Red List data, Bellard, Cassey and Blackburn (2016) found that alien species were the second most common threat associated with plant and vertebrate extinctions since 1500, and the most common for vertebrates. Impacts run through competition, predation, hybridisation and other mechanisms, and range from minimal to massive. New aliens keep arriving: more than a third of all recorded first records of established aliens fall in 1970 to 2014 (Seebens and colleagues, 2017), though a first record can lag the real introduction, so that curve tracks detection as well as arrival.

**Ocean acidification.** Carbon dioxide dissolving in seawater forms carbonic acid (the equilibria are in [water and weak bonds](/lessons/water-weak-bonds/)):

> **CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3-, and H+ + CO3 2- → HCO3-**
>
> **Net: CO2 + H2O + CO3 2- → 2 HCO3-**

In plain terms, the acid's hydrogen ions lower pH and use up the carbonate that shells are built from, lowering the **saturation state** of calcium carbonate, which compares the calcium and carbonate in the water with the amounts at which calcium carbonate neither forms nor dissolves: above 1, forming the mineral is chemically favoured; below 1, unprotected calcium carbonate tends to dissolve. Organisms differ, and their calcification has no universal threshold at 1. Surface seawater stays alkaline, above pH 7, while this happens; "acidification" names the direction of change. Pooling 228 studies, Kroeker and colleagues (2013) found that acidification on average lowered calcification, growth and survival, with wide differences between groups. A later synthesis of 985 studies (Leung and colleagues, 2022) found many calcifiers, such as echinoderms, crustaceans and cephalopods, tolerant of near-future acidification, while corals, coccolithophores and calcifying algae appeared sensitive, and larvae were generally more sensitive than adults.

**Predict.** Ries, Cohen and McCorkle (Geology, 2009) held 18 species of benthic (sea-floor) calcifiers at controlled CO2 levels, the highest about ten times pre-industrial. Did every species calcify less?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. More than half calcified less, several calcified more and one did not respond (tally in the anecdote on the blue mussel): the same spread as the syntheses.

</details>

**Nitrogen deposition.** Burning fuel and making fertilizer put reactive nitrogen into the air, and it falls on distant land ([nutrient cycling](/lessons/nutrient-cycling/)).

**Predict.** Nitrogen often limits plant growth. On Minnesota prairie, where ambient deposition was 6 kg N per hectare per year, what would adding 10 kg N per hectare per year more do to the number of plant species?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It cut it. Christopher Clark and David Tilman (Nature, 2008) found that the addition reduced plant species number by **17%** relative to controls. In a second experiment, species number had recovered a decade after additions stopped, but species abundances had not.

</details>

Why does a nutrient cut diversity? Added nitrogen tends to reinforce the dominance of a few species, and the resource they win differs between places: light in one grassland experiment, water in a semi-arid grassland. Soil acidification adds a further route, more so at high doses (all three in Going deeper). How species loss changes ecosystem processes is the subject of [biodiversity and ecosystem function](/lessons/biodiversity-ecosystem-function/).

## How strong is the attribution?

A gap says how far a species fell behind; attribution asks why. Keep three kinds of claim apart.

- **Detection** shows that a change happened beyond what chance or natural variation would produce, not what caused it.
- **Attribution** links the change to a cause and tests the alternatives. Attribution to human-caused warming takes two links: from the biological change to regional warming, then from regional warming to human causes (the IPCC's wording is in Going deeper).
- **Projection** is what a model gives under a stated scenario of emissions or land use. It is a conditional result, not an observation of the future.

Rosenzweig and colleagues (Nature, 2008) found that continental-scale warming associated with observed changes in physical and biological systems could not be explained by natural climate variation alone. Their data were concentrated in Europe and North America, and the BioShifts range records are likewise biased toward the developed regions of the Northern Hemisphere, so global summaries describe a biased sample.

Each design answers its own question, as the second film showed. A **manipulation** that assigns the driver (aviary temperature, prairie nitrogen, seawater CO2) shows cause in that preparation but not why the field warmed; a **comparison across sites** is stronger than a single trend but can be confounded; a **covariate-adjusted association**, a statistical model that includes measured rival drivers such as warming and forest loss, can expose a confounder it measured but not one it did not. For any claim, ask five questions: what response was measured, what driver is proposed, what alternatives were tested, where the independent variation came from, and how large the uncertainty is. Decisions in [conservation](/lessons/conservation/) rest on those answers.

### Design problem

At one site, a bird's laying date has advanced 10 days over 30 years, while the peak of its caterpillar prey advanced 20 days. The population has fallen. What evidence would show that the growing mismatch caused the decline, and what would it take to connect that to human-caused warming?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

First, the arithmetic: the prey peak moved 20 - 10 = 10 days more than laying, so the offset changed by 10 days and the bird matched half its prey's shift. Laying date stands in for peak demand only if the time from laying to hungry nestlings stays the same. Whether the change created a harmful mismatch depends on how well timed the birds were at the start, and much else changed in 30 years.

1. *Replicate across populations.* Compare many populations whose prey peaks differ in timing, as Both and colleagues did for Dutch pied flycatchers. If mismatch matters, decline should be steepest where the gap is largest.
2. *Measure every year.* Record the prey peak and laying dates annually, not in a single year.
3. *Measure vital rates, the rates of birth and survival.* Track fecundity (young produced), nestling and juvenile survival, and adult survival, including on migration and in winter. Mismatch should show up mainly in breeding success and the survival of young. If a fall in adult survival on the wintering grounds explains most of the decline, mismatch is unlikely to be the main cause, though carry-over effects, where one season's conditions affect the next, can blur that line.
4. *Test alternatives.* Measure habitat change at the breeding site, conditions on the wintering grounds and along the route, predators and disease.
5. *Experiment where possible.* Supplementary feeding after the natural peak could test whether food timing limits breeding success.

Even a convincing case that mismatch drove the decline attributes it to timing, not yet to human-caused warming; that needs evidence that the local spring warming is itself attributable to human forcing.

</details>

## Going deeper

### One metre uphill, in kilometres poleward

One illustrative pair of stations: in 1991–2020 climate normals, the annual mean is 15.8 C at Rome Ciampino and 7.9 C at Stockholm, about 17.5 degrees of latitude (roughly 1,950 km) farther north, about 0.4 C per 100 km. With the first film's lapse rate of 0.6 C per 100 m, one metre uphill matches about 1.5 km poleward there. This is not a general conversion: gradients differ between regions, seasons and stations, so each study should use its own local gradient. On these figures Chen and colleagues' two medians each track roughly 0.07 C per decade.

### The two links of attribution

In the IPCC's 2007 assessment (AR4; later assessments have updated the wording), "joint attribution" links a biological change to regional climate change and then links part of that climate change to human causes, while assessing plausible alternative explanations.

### How the flycatcher food peaks were measured

The pied flycatcher is Ficedula hypoleuca. The comparison was observational: the cross-site caterpillar peaks were measured in one year, 2003, and a second index of food timing came from the share of great tits laying second broods in 1985 to 1990.

### Why nitrogen cuts diversity: light, water and soil

In experimental grassland plant communities, Hautier, Niklaus and Hector (Science, 2009) added light to the understory and prevented the loss caused by fertilization, which implicates competition for light. In a semi-arid grassland in China, Li and colleagues (2024) found that adding water prevented species loss under nitrogen alone or warming alone, but only partly when both were combined. Nitrogen also acidifies soil, and in a field experiment and meta-analysis Namuhan and colleagues (2024) found that toxic manganese and aluminium ions contribute to the loss, more so at high nitrogen doses. Neither the light nor the water mechanism is universal, and they are not the only ones.
