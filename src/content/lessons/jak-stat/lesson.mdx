techniques:
  - name: "Mutant screen in cultured cells, with complementation"
    blurb: >-
      Treat a large population of cultured cells with a mutagen and select the rare ones that
      have lost a response, here the response to interferon. Fusing two mutant lines and checking whether the response
      returns tells you whether they lack the same gene, and putting normal DNA back into a mutant until the
      response returns identifies the gene. This is how TYK2 was tied to interferon signaling.
  - name: "Gel-shift assay (electrophoretic mobility shift)"
    blurb: >-
      Mix a short labelled piece of DNA with a cell extract and run it through a gel. DNA with a
      protein bound to it moves more slowly, so a shifted band reports a DNA-binding factor and
      how much of it is active. It is how an interferon-activated DNA-binding complex can be
      seen appearing within minutes.
  - name: "Gene knockout in mice"
    blurb: >-
      Disrupt both copies of one chosen gene in a mouse and see what fails. Because every other
      gene is intact, the result tests whether related proteins can cover for the missing one.
      Mouse embryos without Jak2 make no adult-type red cells.
  - name: "Anti-phosphotyrosine detection"
    blurb: >-
      Separate a cell's proteins by size and probe them with an antibody that binds only
      phosphotyrosine. Comparing cells before and after a cytokine shows which proteins gain a
      tyrosine phosphate and how quickly.

    choices:
      - "A STAT that phosphorylates the receptor's inside tail"
      - "A JAK already bound to the receptor's inside tail"
      - "A G protein released from the receptor's inside tail"
      - "A kinase domain hidden in the receptor's outer half"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The receptor chain carries no enzyme. A JAK is bound to its inside tail before the hormone
      arrives, and binding sets two JAKs against each other so that they switch each other on.
      A hidden kinase domain is wrong because there is none anywhere in the chain, which is the
      difference from a receptor tyrosine kinase. A G protein is wrong because G proteins work
      with the seven-helix receptors of Unit 3. A STAT is wrong because it is the JAK's
      substrate and a transcription factor, with no kinase activity of its own.
  - stem: "In an active STAT pair, what holds the two STAT proteins together?"
    choices:
      - "The JAK that stays bound to both after phosphorylation"
      - "A disulfide bond between their two DNA-binding domains"
      - "The stretch of DNA that each one grips before they meet"
      - "Each SH2 domain holding the partner's phosphotyrosine"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Each STAT is phosphorylated on one tyrosine, and each SH2 domain binds that phosphotyrosine
      on the partner, so two grips hold the pair. A disulfide bond is wrong because the active pairing
      depends on the phosphate and is undone by a phosphatase. The JAK is wrong because the STAT
      leaves the receptor and the JAK before it enters the nucleus. DNA is wrong because the
      pair forms first and binds DNA afterwards.
  - stem: "Interferon switches on its target genes within minutes, and it still does so in cells treated with an inhibitor of protein synthesis. What does this result show?"
    choices:
      - "The genes are switched on by a newly made transcription factor"
      - "The proteins that carry the signal are already present"
      - "Interferon enters the nucleus and binds the genes itself"
      - "The signal must travel as a small diffusible messenger"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      If no new protein can be made and the genes still respond, every protein on the path must
      exist before interferon arrives. Interferon entering the nucleus is wrong because it is a
      protein that acts through a receptor at the cell surface, and the experiment says nothing of the kind. A small
      diffusible messenger is wrong because the result does not require one; a relay of existing
      proteins fits just as well, and that is what was found. A newly made transcription factor
      is wrong because it is exactly what the inhibitor rules out.
  - stem: "A child has severe combined immunodeficiency, with no T cells and no natural killer cells, yet the gene for the common gamma chain is normal. Which gene is the most likely site of the mutation?"
    choices:
      - "JAK2, which sits on the erythropoietin receptor"
      - "STAT1, which carries the interferon signal to DNA"
      - "JAK3, which sits on the common gamma chain's tail"
      - "TYK2, which sits on a type I interferon receptor chain"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      JAK3 is the kinase bound to the common gamma chain, so losing it silences the same set of
      interleukin receptors as losing the chain and gives the same disease. JAK2 is wrong
      because its loss stops red-cell production and is lethal to mouse embryos. STAT1 is wrong
      because its loss removes the response to interferons and leaves lymphocytes in place. TYK2
      is wrong because it serves interferon receptors and is not the gamma chain's partner.
  - stem: "A cell makes a STAT in which the one tyrosine that JAKs phosphorylate is changed to phenylalanine. The receptor, the JAKs and the STAT's SH2 domain are normal. When cytokine is added, which step is the first to fail?"
    choices:
      - "JAKs phosphorylating each other across the receptor pair"
      - "STATs docking by SH2 domains on the receptor's tails"
      - "JAKs phosphorylating tyrosines on the receptor's tails"
      - "STATs pairing through SH2 and phosphotyrosine grips"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Phenylalanine has no hydroxyl group and cannot take a phosphate, so the STAT never gains
      the phosphotyrosine that a partner's SH2 domain must hold, and no activated pair forms. JAKs
      phosphorylating each other is wrong because that step happens before any STAT is
      involved. JAKs phosphorylating the receptor tails is wrong for the same reason. Docking is
      wrong because it uses the STAT's SH2 domain and the receptor's phosphotyrosine, and both
      are intact, so the mutant STAT still reaches the receptor.

      Cytokines are protein signals such as interferon and erythropoietin. They are too big to
      enter a cell, so they bind receptors on its surface. These receptors have no enzyme of
      their own. Each one holds an enzyme called a JAK on its inside end. When the cytokine

      the receptor to the DNA, genes can respond within minutes. If a JAK is missing, the cells

  - level: "college"

**Add interferon, a protein that warns cells of a virus, to human cells and a specific set of genes switches on within minutes—even when a drug has blocked all new protein synthesis.** The first film showed how a borrowed kinase and a waiting transcription factor make that speed possible; here is how two laboratories found those parts in 1992.

## Finding the two parts in 1992

With no new protein made, every component of the relay must already be in the cell. Investigators judged the second messengers of Unit 3 a poor fit: a rise in a second messenger is fairly nonspecific, whereas interferon's transcriptional response is highly specific. Two laboratories searched from opposite ends.

James Darnell's laboratory at Rockefeller University started at the gene. Interferon-responsive genes share a short DNA sequence, and his group identified a protein complex, **ISGF3**, whose subunits wait in the cytoplasm and gain DNA-binding ability within minutes of interferon treatment. In August 1992 Christian Schindler, Ke Shuai, and colleagues reported that interferon drives tyrosine phosphorylation on proteins of ISGF3, and that **staurosporine**, a broad kinase inhibitor, blocks interferon-induced activation of the complex. That placed a tyrosine kinase upstream of the DNA-binding factor without naming which one.

George Stark and Ian Kerr's laboratory in London came from genetics. Sandra Pellegrini engineered human cells in which an interferon-responsive DNA element controlled a marker gene: in the chosen medium, any cell that still answered interferon switched on the marker and died, leaving only interferon-deaf mutants alive.

**Predict.** Pellegrini now holds an interferon-deaf mutant line, but nothing tells her which gene is broken. How could DNA from normal cells identify it?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Put normal genomic DNA into the mutant cells and look for the rare cell whose interferon response returns. The DNA that cell took up carries a working copy of the broken gene. This test is called **complementation**.

</details>

Pellegrini carried out that DNA rescue in her new laboratory at the Institut Pasteur in Paris (see *Going deeper* for why colleagues doubted the screen could succeed). In July 1992, two weeks before the Darnell paper, her group reported the rescuing gene for one mutant: it encoded the tyrosine kinase **TYK2**. The two results fit together: a JAK is required, and the DNA-binding proteins carry phosphotyrosine. Darnell's laboratory named those proteins **STATs** (signal transducers and activators of transcription).

Type I interferons also add IRF9 to STAT1 and STAT2 (forming ISGF3), STAT5A and STAT5B partly cover for each other, and how shared STATs such as STAT3 drive opposite responses to interleukin-6 and interleukin-10 remains an open question.</Support>

## Inside the relay: activation loops, two grips, and built-in restraint

A **cytokine** is a small secreted protein that tells other cells what to do, most often cells of the blood and immune system—including interferons and the interleukins that carry messages between white blood cells. Several hormones use the same class I and class II cytokine receptor family: **erythropoietin** (EPO), which tells the bone marrow to make red blood cells, along with growth hormone, prolactin, and leptin.

Unlike a receptor tyrosine kinase such as the insulin receptor from the receptor field guide, a class I or class II cytokine receptor crosses the membrane once and has no catalytic domain of its own. Instead, a stretch of its inner tail near the membrane holds a separate tyrosine kinase, a **JAK** (Janus kinase), before any cytokine arrives—an antenna with no electronics of its own and a radio bolted to its base. Named for Janus, the two-faced Roman god, each JAK carries two kinase-like domains side by side: a working kinase and a **pseudokinase** domain, which has the kinase fold but little or no catalytic activity and restrains the working kinase at rest. Mammals have four JAKs: **JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2**.

The biochemical details behind the first film's relay:

- **Positioning and trans-phosphorylation:** The simplest receptors assemble two chains and two JAKs around one cytokine, though some assemble more chains. Whether chains meet only on binding is still debated (see *Going deeper*); either way, the two JAKs phosphorylate each other's **activation loops**, the switch from the phosphorylation lesson, turning both kinases fully on.
- **Docking and two-grip pairing:** Active JAKs phosphorylate tyrosines along the receptor tails. A latent STAT docks on one phosphotyrosine through its **SH2 domain** (the phosphotyrosine-binding module from the lesson on protein interaction modules), and the JAK phosphorylates a single tyrosine near the STAT's C-terminal end (tyrosine 701 in STAT1). The STAT leaves the receptor, and two phosphorylated STATs lock into a reciprocal dimer—each SH2 domain holding its partner's phosphotyrosine, two grips where one held the STAT to the receptor. (Many STATs already sit in loose, inactive pairs; phosphorylation switches them into this active form.) The active pair accumulates in the nucleus, binds a specific DNA sequence, and turns on transcription.

**Predict.** Suppose every docking tyrosine along a cytokine receptor's inside tail is changed to phenylalanine, while the receptor's JAK-binding stretch, the JAKs, and the STATs remain normal. When cytokine binds, do the JAKs still activate each other—and can STATs still be phosphorylated?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes, the JAKs still position against each other and phosphorylate each other's activation loops, because that step depends only on receptor pairing and intact JAKs. Without phosphotyrosines on the receptor tail, however, STATs lose their SH2 docking sites beside the JAKs, so STAT recruitment and phosphorylation fail.

</details>

### Design problem

Design the shortest possible path from a cell-surface receptor to a gene. List what the moving protein must do, match those jobs to a STAT's domains, and say what this path gives up beside a three-kinase cascade.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The shortest route uses one moving protein that is marked at the active receptor and carries the signal directly to DNA. It must do five jobs: (1) distinguish an active receptor from an idle one, (2) accept a covalent mark recording that meeting, (3) change state once marked so active copies pair and build up in the nucleus, (4) bind a specific DNA sequence, and (5) recruit transcription machinery. (Unmarked STATs also pass in and out of the nucleus, but they do not bind target DNA in the same way.)

A STAT has a module for each job: the **SH2 domain** detects phosphotyrosines on the active receptor; the single **C-terminal tyrosine** takes the phosphate; the same **SH2 domain** reads that mark on a partner; the central **DNA-binding domain** grips DNA; and the **C-terminal end** recruits the transcription machinery.

What does this direct route give up? Multi-stage gain and intermediate control points. In a three-kinase cascade, each active kinase phosphorylates many copies of the next, multiplying a small receptor input; stacked stages with positive feedback can also build an all-or-none switch (as in Ferrell's frog oocytes), and each tier is a junction where another pathway can cut in. Here JAK is the only kinase: it can mark many STATs in turn, but no downstream kinase stage multiplies the signal again. The reward is speed and a direct line from occupied receptors to genes.

</details>

A direct relay explains the speed, yet dozens of cytokines run through only four JAKs and seven STATs—and each signal must be kept in check.

## Four JAKs, seven STATs: what knockouts and patients show

Because receptor tails select specific JAKs and STATs, deleting one gene in **knockout** mice—or finding a natural mutation in a patient—shows which cytokines depend on it.

| Missing component | Primary cytokines / pathways affected | Observed phenotype |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **STAT1** | Interferons | No interferon response; mice succumb to infections normal mice survive |
| **STAT2** | Type I interferons (with STAT1 and IRF9 in ISGF3) | Required in human mutant cell lines for type I interferon signaling |
| **STAT3** | Multiple developmental & cytokine signals | Embryo dies early; functions mapped one tissue at a time |
| **STAT4** | Interleukin-12 | Impaired response to interleukin-12 |
| **STAT5A / STAT5B** | Prolactin, growth hormone | Failed prolactin and growth-hormone responses; the two partly cover for each other |
| **STAT6** | Interleukin-4 | Impaired response to interleukin-4 |

You might expect the four similar JAKs to cover for each other too. Two results say that on at least two receptors they do not. First, James Ihle's laboratory showed in 1993 that JAK2 sits on the EPO receptor's tail. In 1998 both Ihle's group and Klaus Pfeffer's group reported (in the same journal issue) that mouse embryos lacking JAK2 were anaemic and died at about day 12.5 of gestation, and their fetal livers, where a mouse embryo makes its adult-type red cells, held no red-cell precursors that could be grown in culture—even though the genes for JAK1, JAK3, and TYK2 are untouched.

Second came human **severe combined immunodeficiency** (SCID), in which a child is born without working T cells, and so with almost no adaptive immune defence. In 1993 Warren Leonard's laboratory found that boys with the X-linked form, who lack natural killer cells as well, carry mutations in the gamma chain of the interleukin-2 receptor.

**Predict.** The boys' disease is far more severe than losing interleukin-2 alone. What does that imply about the gamma chain?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Several cytokine receptors must share that chain—now called the **common gamma chain** (used by receptors for interleukins 2, 4, 7, 9, 15, and 21)—so breaking it silences a whole cluster of immune signals.

</details>

Rebecca Buckley of Duke University studied a SCID patient whose cells failed to answer gamma-chain cytokines despite making a normal gamma chain. In 1995 John O'Shea's and Leonard's groups looked one step along the pathway and found a mutation in each copy of **JAK3**, the kinase on the gamma chain's tail, about two months after an Italian group working with O'Shea reported JAK3 mutations in other non-X-linked SCID patients.

## Turning signaling off, stuck-on JAK2, and targeted drugs

Two kinds of brake do most of the work of ending the signal:
1. **Tyrosine phosphatases** strip phosphates from JAKs, receptor tails, and STATs. A STAT dephosphorylated in the nucleus returns to the cytoplasm ready for reuse.
2. **SOCS proteins** (suppressors of cytokine signaling) are themselves encoded by STAT target genes—delayed negative feedback, as in the signaling circuits lesson. Each SOCS protein carries an SH2 domain that targets phosphotyrosines on active receptors and JAKs. Some, including SOCS1 and SOCS3, block the JAK's substrate-binding groove directly, and all carry a region that recruits a ubiquitin ligase; for several, including SOCS1 and SOCS3, this can mark the receptor complex for the proteasome.

**Predict.** Suppose a cell can no longer induce SOCS proteins after cytokine stimulation, while its tyrosine phosphatases remain intact. How would the STAT signal differ from normal?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Active STAT would climb higher and stay elevated longer while cytokine is present, because the delayed negative-feedback brake on the receptor–JAK complex is gone. Phosphatases still dephosphorylate STATs and reset the pathway once signaling stops, so the response does not stay locked on permanently without cytokine.

</details>

The pseudokinase domain is a brake that works before any cytokine arrives. In 2005 several research groups reported the same mutation in most patients with **polycythaemia vera**, a disease in which the bone marrow makes too many red blood cells: **valine 617 of JAK2 changed to phenylalanine (V617F)**. Residue 617 sits in the pseudokinase domain. The change weakens that domain's restraint and helps two JAK2 molecules hold together in the active pair, so JAK2 signals from the EPO receptor with little or no EPO present.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/jak-stat/jak2-restraint-stuck-on.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two panels of the erythropoietin receptor with a JAK2 on each tail and no hormone present; in the normal panel the pseudokinase lobe holds the kinase lobe idle, in the mutant panel the restraint is weakened and the kinase is active and a STAT is phosphorylated." caption="JAK2's pseudokinase domain normally holds the working kinase idle until EPO arrives; the V617F change weakens that restraint so JAK2 signals with little or no EPO." />

Because missing JAK2 halts adult-type red-cell production and overactive JAK2 drives excess red cells, JAKs became major drug targets. **Ruxolitinib**, which inhibits JAK1 and JAK2, was approved in the United States in 2011 for myelofibrosis, a bone-marrow disease related to polycythaemia vera. **Tofacitinib** followed in 2012 for rheumatoid arthritis. Its original rationale came from JAK3 SCID: a person without JAK3 has an immune defect and little else, suggesting a JAK3 blocker could quiet autoimmunity while sparing other tissues. In practice, tofacitinib inhibits JAK1 as strongly as JAK3—and JAK2 more weakly—with much of its clinical effect coming through JAK1. Like imatinib, both drugs compete for the kinase's ATP pocket.

**Predict.** Why is anaemia a known side effect of several ATP-pocket JAK inhibitors used for immune disease, and how could targeting a JAK's pseudokinase domain instead improve selectivity?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The ATP pockets of the four JAKs are very similar, so drugs aimed there struggle to distinguish one JAK from another and partly inhibit JAK2—blunting EPO-driven red-cell production. A drug that binds one JAK's pseudokinase domain instead, and locks it in its restraining state, can block that JAK while sparing the others.

</details>

That pseudokinase strategy is now in the clinic: **deucravacitinib**, approved for psoriasis, binds the pseudokinase domain of TYK2 and holds it in its restraining state, blocking TYK2 while sparing JAK1, JAK2, and JAK3.

## Going deeper

### Do cytokine receptors pair before or after the cytokine binds?

One growth hormone molecule carries two different surfaces, each gripping one copy of the same receptor chain. For some receptors—the EPO receptor among them—crystal structures suggested the chains might be loosely paired beforehand, with hormone binding resetting the angle between them. Single-molecule tracking of receptor chains in living cells points the other way, toward chains coming together when hormone binds. The question is still argued; either way, ligand binding places two JAKs in position to trans-phosphorylate each other.

### Inside Pellegrini's 1992 screen and the ISGF3 complex

A human cell carries two copies of nearly every gene, so many colleagues doubted a random mutagen could disable both copies of a pathway gene while keeping the cell alive. Pellegrini screened about a hundred million mutagenized cells over many months; the few mutant lines that came through later showed that JAK1, STAT1, and STAT2 are required too. Beyond simple STAT pairs, type I interferons assemble **ISGF3**—STAT1, STAT2, and the partner protein IRF9—to bind interferon-responsive DNA.

### One STAT, opposite outcomes

One question remains open: interleukin-6 and interleukin-10 both signal mainly through STAT3, yet produce largely opposite effects on inflammation. Differences in how long the STAT3 signal lasts have been proposed, and the question is not settled.
