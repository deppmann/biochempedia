## Explain: how extra nutrients use up oxygen

<WordBank terms={['nutrient', 'algae bloom', 'decomposer', 'eutrophication', 'dissolved oxygen', 'dead zone', 'climate change', 'catastrophic disturbance', 'biodiversity']} />

Pond water left on a sunny windowsill often turns green, because tiny living things called algae grow in it.

A **nutrient** is a substance living things need to grow. Algae and water plants need nitrogen and phosphorus. Farm fertilizer and manure contain them. So do wastewater and some air pollution.

Here is the chain, step by step:

1. Rain washes nutrients from fields and lawns into rivers. Some wastewater adds more.
2. In the Bay, algae grow very fast. A thick, fast growth of algae is an **algae bloom**. The green water shades underwater grasses.
3. The algae die and sink to the bottom.
4. A **decomposer** is a living thing that breaks down dead matter. Bacteria decompose the dead algae, and they use oxygen as they do.  ([More on decomposers](/lessons/producers-consumers-decomposers/).)
5. The deep water runs low on oxygen. Fish and crabs often swim away. Oysters cannot swim, and animals that cannot leave may get sick or die.

**Eutrophication** is when extra nutrients in water feed too much algae growth, which can lead to low oxygen.

> extra nutrients → algae bloom → algae die and sink → decomposers use up oxygen → animals leave or die

**Dissolved oxygen** is oxygen gas mixed into water. Scientists measure it in milligrams per liter (mg/L). In this lesson, a **dead zone** is water with less than 2 mg/L of dissolved oxygen. It is not empty: bacteria live there, and the water may hold only a little oxygen, or almost none.

Why the deep water, and why summer?

- Warm water can hold less dissolved oxygen than cool water.
- In summer, fresher river water floats on saltier Bay water. The layers mix poorly, so new oxygen reaches the deep water slowly.
- Strong wind can stir the layers and carry oxygen down.

## Explain: climate changes and sudden disasters

A **climate change** is a shift in a place’s usual temperature, rain or weather that lasts for many years.

- Warmer water holds less oxygen. A 2022 model study found that warming canceled out part of the oxygen gained by cutting nitrogen.
- Sea level is rising along Virginia’s coast. Salt water can now reach some low marshes and forests near the shore.
- A 2022 world report found that about half of the species studied had moved toward the poles or up mountains.

A **catastrophic disturbance** is a sudden event, such as a hurricane, flood or forest fire, that harms many living things at once. In September 2003, Hurricane Isabel brought strong winds and flooding to eastern Virginia. Forest fires also burn in Virginia’s mountains.

## Explain: losing variety, and coming back

**Biodiversity** is the variety of living things in an area. A big change can lower it. In a dead zone, animals leave the deep water or die.

A disturbance does not lower every kind of life, though. In the Puerto Rico forest, a review reported that opening the treetops let more kinds of plants grow, while several animal groups became less common.

Ecosystems are usually stable, but they can change. Whether one recovers depends on how severe the change is and how long it lasts.

- In fall, cooler water and winds usually mix the Bay, and oxygen returns to the deep water.
- Northern Rocky Mountain forests have grown back after fires 100 to 300 years apart.
- Forests that burn again before they recover may not grow back the same way.

An ecosystem is a bit like a rubber band: stretch it a little and it snaps back, too far and it stays out of shape. The comparison is not perfect, because a changed ecosystem can still work, just differently.

People cause many changes ([People Change Habitats](/lessons/people-change-habitats/)), and people can help ([Helping Ecosystems Recover](/lessons/restoring-ecosystems/)).

### Design problem

Does adding more fertilizer to pond water change how much oxygen is left a few weeks later? Plan a fair test with clear jars.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** How does the amount of fertilizer in pond water affect algae and dissolved oxygen after three weeks?
- **Independent variable:** fertilizer added to each jar: 0, 1, 2 and 4 drops of liquid plant fertilizer per liter.
- **Dependent variables:** how green the water is (a color chart, every three days) and dissolved oxygen in mg/L (a test kit, at the end).
- **Control:** jars with no fertilizer.
- **Constants:** the same pond water, 1 liter per jar, window, temperature and time.
- **Repeated trials:** three jars per amount; find each mean.
- **Safety:** wear gloves and goggles, never taste the water, wash your hands, and dispose of it as your teacher says.
- **Meaning:** greener jars that later test lower in oxygen support the chain. Jars are a model with no layers or tides.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

Fertilizer does not take oxygen from the Bay by itself. Its nutrients feed algae blooms. The algae die and sink, and decomposers use up the oxygen in deep summer water that does not mix. Climate changes and disasters change ecosystems too; some recover and some stay changed.
