      Cells or tissue slices take up 32P-phosphate, which ends up in any molecule that gets a

  - name: "Thin-layer chromatography of lipids"
    blurb: >-
      A drop of lipid extract is run up a coated plate in a solvent. Lipids with different head
      groups travel different distances, so a new spot a small distance away from a known standard
      is evidence for a new lipid.
  - name: "PH domain–GFP imaging in live cells"

  - name: "Mutating the lipid-attachment site"
    blurb: >-

      be modified, then tracking the mutant protein, shows which anchor a protein depends on.

**Ras has no stretch of amino acids that crosses a membrane, yet most of it sits on the inner face of the plasma membrane. What holds it there, and why does the cell care where a protein sits?**

Ras is a small GTP-binding switch that relays a growth signal inward from a receptor. The answer to the first question is a fatty tail. The answer to the second is that a membrane works as a floor to search on and as a wall that carries labels. This lesson follows both: lipids that anchor proteins, and lipids that tell proteins where they are.

## The idea: location is part of the message

Location, location, location. Picture a worker who must find one machine. Searching a whole warehouse takes a long time, and searching a single floor takes less. A membrane is that floor. A protein held on its surface wanders in two dimensions instead of three, so it meets its partners far more often than it would drifting through the cytosol, the watery interior of the cell. Ras needs this. It must meet SOS, the exchange factor that loads it with GTP, and later Raf, the kinase it switches on. SOS is recruited to the membrane by the adaptor Grb2, and Raf is recruited by active Ras.

The inner face of the plasma membrane also differs from the outer face and from the membrane of the Golgi, the stack of flattened sacs that sorts new proteins. The inner face is rich in negatively charged lipids, and a protein can use that charge as a landmark. The lessons on lipids and on membrane structure and function cover the chemistry. You need only one fact from them here: a long hydrocarbon chain is comfortable buried in a bilayer and uncomfortable in water.

That leaves the puzzle. How does Ras mostly hang out at the membrane? Something must be attached to it.

## The anchors: lipids can be attached

Cells attach lipids to proteins with dedicated enzymes, and the chemistry of the bond sets the rules.

| Anchor | Size | Where it goes | Enzyme | Can it come off? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Myristate | 14-carbon fatty acid | N-terminal glycine, through an amide bond | N-myristoyltransferase | No |
| Palmitate | 16-carbon fatty acid | A cysteine, through a thioester | Palmitoyl acyltransferases (PATs) | Yes, by thioesterases |
| Farnesyl | 15-carbon isoprenoid | Cysteine of a C-terminal CAAX box, through a thioether | Farnesyltransferase | No |
| Geranylgeranyl | 20-carbon isoprenoid | Same CAAX cysteine, when the last residue favors it | Geranylgeranyltransferase I | No |

Myristate goes on a glycine that becomes the first residue once the starting methionine is clipped off, usually while the protein is still being made. Src-family kinases carry one. Prenyl groups are built from five-carbon isoprene units, three for farnesyl and four for geranylgeranyl. A CAAX box is a cysteine, two aliphatic amino acids, and a last residue that picks the enzyme. After the lipid is on, the last three residues are clipped off and the new end is methylated, which hides its charge. H-Ras, N-Ras and K-Ras all end in a CAAX box.

The bond decides the behavior. Amide and thioether bonds are essentially permanent, so myristate and the prenyl groups stay for the life of the protein. A thioester is easily hydrolyzed, and cells have thioesterases that do it. Palmitate is the one anchor that can be put on and taken off.

## One anchor is not enough

A single myristate or farnesyl is a weak grip. (A geranylgeranyl, with 20 carbons, can hold some proteins on its own.) It buries one short chain in the bilayer, and chains pop out again. The protein stays tied to the lipid, but the lipid does not stay in the membrane, so a protein held this way visits membranes and leaves them. Cells add a second signal, most often of one of two kinds:

- **A second lipid.** The Src-family kinases Fyn and Lck carry myristate plus palmitate. H-Ras and N-Ras carry farnesyl plus palmitate.
- **A patch of positive charge.** K-Ras4B carries a farnesyl plus a run of lysines, which are attracted to the negative lipids of the inner face.

Binding free energies add, which means affinities multiply:

> **ΔG(total) ≈ ΔG(first anchor) + ΔG(second signal), so K(total) ≈ K(first anchor) × K(second signal)**

Each extra 1.4 kcal/mol at body temperature makes binding about ten times tighter. One hook holds a coat until someone bumps it, and two hooks hold it.

The second signal is also a handle for control. Remove it and the protein loosens its hold. In K-Ras4B, protein kinase C phosphorylates Ser181, next to the lysine patch. The phosphate adds negative charge beside a positive patch, the attraction to the plasma membrane weakens, and K-Ras4B leaves it for internal membranes such as those of mitochondria. This is a farnesyl-electrostatic switch. A carrier protein, PDEδ, has a hydrophobic pocket that holds a farnesyl group, and it helps farnesylated Ras proteins cross the cytosol between membranes without their tails touching water.

For H-Ras the handle is palmitate, and a thioester that can come off turns the anchor into a cycle. A palmitoyl acyltransferase at the Golgi adds palmitate to H-Ras. The protein, now held tightly, rides membrane vesicles to the plasma membrane. A thioesterase removes the palmitate, and it can do so on any membrane. The grip weakens, and H-Ras drifts from membrane to membrane until it reaches the Golgi again. It's a kinetic bilayer trap: palmitoylation traps the protein on whatever membrane it is sitting on, transport carries it to the plasma membrane, and depalmitoylation resets it. No binding site specific to the plasma membrane is needed. The rates of the cycle do the sorting.


Some models place palmitoylated proteins in lipid rafts, small cholesterol-rich patches of the bilayer. Living membranes do contain small, short-lived ordered domains, but how large and long-lived they are, and how much they sort particular proteins, is still debated, so treat raft models with care.

## How can we exploit this to treat cancer?

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

If Ras must carry a farnesyl to work, block the enzyme that attaches it. That was the idea behind farnesyltransferase inhibitors (FTIs). Ras genes are mutated in roughly one in five human cancers, and a Ras that cannot reach the membrane cannot signal. One FTI, tipifarnib, reached Phase III clinical trials.

The drugs worked as drugs and still disappointed against the Ras that matters most. K-Ras is by far the most commonly mutated of the three Ras genes, and H-Ras is the least. When farnesylation is blocked, K-Ras and N-Ras can be picked up by geranylgeranyltransferase I, which puts a geranylgeranyl group on the same cysteine. The protein keeps its anchor and keeps signaling. H-Ras is not rescued this way. The logic has not died: tipifarnib has been tested in cancers driven by mutant H-Ras, and the FTI lonafarnib is approved for progeria, a disease in which a farnesylated protein of the nuclear envelope is the culprit.

## Lipids as addresses: written by PI3-kinase, read by PH domains, erased by PTEN

Anchors hold a protein on a membrane. Addresses say which membrane and which spot. The labels are phosphoinositides: phosphatidylinositol (PI) lipids whose inositol ring faces the cytosol and can carry phosphates at positions 3, 4 and 5. Cells make seven of these combinations, and they collect in different membranes: PI4P on the Golgi, PI3P on early endosomes, PI(4,5)P2 (PIP2) on the plasma membrane. Phosphates can be added to the ring and removed again quickly, which is what a signal needs.

The first hint that a signal can rewrite a lipid came by accident in the early 1950s. Mabel and Lowell Hokin were following radioactive phosphate into the RNA of pigeon pancreas slices stimulated with acetylcholine. The counts kept vanishing through the purification. Instead of fixing the leak, they analyzed the discarded material, and it was phospholipid. Their 1953 paper lists Mabel Hokin first and has a title that leads with enzyme secretion and names the phospholipids only at the end. What it meant stayed unclear for two decades. In 1975 Robert Michell argued that this lipid turnover was tied to receptors. Where that lipid goes next belongs to a later lesson. Here the question is what a phosphate on one position does.

The label that matters here sits on a place nobody had mapped. Lewis Cantley's group was studying a lipid kinase activity that came down with a viral oncoprotein such as v-Src or polyoma middle-T antigen. Cantley later recalled that the product ran about a millimetre away from the known 4-phosphate lipid on a thin-layer plate. With a collaborator, Peter Downes, they analyzed the inositol head group and worked out which position of the inositol ring carried the phosphate, and it was the 3 position. In 1988 they showed the kinase made phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate, a lipid with no precedent.

In stimulated cells the enzyme, PI3-kinase, adds a phosphate to PIP2 and makes PIP3. The two fatty acid tails stay buried in the bilayer, so PIP3 stays in the membrane. It can spread sideways, and phosphatases keep removing it. Where the kinase is switched on in one place, as at the front of a crawling cell, PIP3 marks that region. When a growth factor bathes the whole cell, PIP3 can rise over much of the plasma membrane.

Proteins read the label with a PH domain, a fold of roughly 100 amino acids, some of which have a pocket for a phosphoinositide head group. Akt carries one that grips PIP3. Before PIP3 appears, Akt drifts in the cytosol. Once PIP3 is there, Akt is pulled onto the membrane where it sits, not onto the PIP2 around it. Akt's PH domain also binds PI(3,4)P2, which forms when PIP3 loses its 5-phosphate. What Akt does when it arrives belongs to a later lesson.

You can watch this happen. The method behind "Visualizing PIP3" is a PH domain fused to GFP, which glows green wherever the domain goes. In a resting cell the glow is spread through the cytosol. After a growth factor, it gathers at the plasma membrane.

PTEN runs the reaction backwards. It removes the 3-phosphate from PIP3 and leaves PIP2. Other phosphatases, SHIP1 and SHIP2, remove the 5-phosphate instead and leave PI(3,4)P2, which Akt can still bind. In a cell with broken PTEN, PIP3 stays higher for longer and PH-domain proteins keep arriving. Loss of PTEN is common in human cancers.

### Design problem

Build a reporter that lights up the plasma membrane only where and when PI3-kinase is active. What would you fuse to GFP, and what control shows the signal is specific?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Fuse GFP to a PH domain that binds PIP3 tightly and selectively. The PH domain of Akt also binds PI(3,4)P2, so a domain that prefers PIP3, such as that of GRP1, is a cleaner choice. In a resting cell with little PIP3, the glow is spread through the cytosol. When PI3-kinase is switched on, PIP3 builds up in the membrane under the receptor and pulls the reporter into a bright patch there.

Three controls test whether the signal is specific. First, the same fusion carrying a mutation in the lipid-binding pocket should stay in the cytosol after stimulation, which shows recruitment needs the lipid. Second, pretreating cells with a PI3-kinase inhibitor such as wortmannin should block the recruitment, which shows the lipid comes from PI3-kinase. Third, cells lacking PTEN should show the reporter at the membrane even at rest, which shows the reporter follows the PIP3 level, not the stimulus.

Also image a red fluorescent protein that is free in the cytosol and report the green-to-red ratio at the membrane, since a thick membrane ruffle can look bright for no chemical reason.
