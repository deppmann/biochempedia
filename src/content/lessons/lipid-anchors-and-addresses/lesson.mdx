    profileSourceAnchor: "Hokin & Hokin, J. Biol. Chem. 203:967 (1953); Michell, Biochim. Biophys. Acta 415:81 (1975)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In the early 1950s the Hokins were not looking for a signal. They were studying how
      acetylcholine, the signal that tells a pancreas cell to secrete digestive enzymes, affects the making of RNA.
      They floated slices of pigeon pancreas in radioactive phosphate, stimulated some with
      acetylcholine, and tried to purify the RNA, where they expected the label to end up. The
      stimulated slices took up more label, but through the purification the counts
      kept falling away. Instead of fixing the leak, they analyzed what they had been throwing
      out. It was phospholipid. A membrane lipid was
      taking up phosphate faster when the cell was stimulated. Their 1953 paper, with Mabel Hokin
      as first author, has a title that leads with enzyme secretion and names the phospholipids only at the end. What it meant stayed
      unclear for two decades. In 1975 Robert Michell argued that this lipid turnover was tied to
      receptors, and phosphoinositide signaling grew from it.

      about a millimetre away from the known 4-phosphate lipid. A millimetre can be a badly poured

techniques:
  - name: "Labeling with radioactive phosphate (32P)"
    blurb: >-
      Cells or tissue slices take up 32P-phosphate, which ends up in any molecule that gets a
      phosphate. Counting radioactivity in purified fractions shows which molecules took up new
      phosphate. Turning counts into true rates also needs to know how heavily the precursor pools were labeled.
  - name: "Thin-layer chromatography of lipids"
    blurb: >-
      A drop of lipid extract is run up a coated plate in a solvent. Lipids with different head
      groups travel different distances, so a new spot a small distance away from a known standard
      is evidence for a new lipid.
  - name: "PH domain–GFP imaging in live cells"
    blurb: >-
      A PH domain fused to green fluorescent protein moves to the membrane when its lipid ligand
      appears there. Watching where the glow goes shows where and when PIP3 builds up.
  - name: "Mutating the lipid-attachment site"
    blurb: >-
      Changing the N-terminal glycine or the cysteines that receive a lipid to residues that cannot
      be modified, then tracking the mutant protein, shows which anchor a protein depends on.

    choices:
      - "A 14-carbon acyl group joined by an amide bond to an N-terminal glycine, added while the protein is being made"
      - "A 15-carbon isoprenoid joined by a thioether to the cysteine of a C-terminal CAAX box"
      - "A 16-carbon acyl group joined by a thioester to a cysteine side chain, which thioesterases can remove again"
      - "A phosphate joined to a serine side chain that gives the protein an affinity for membranes"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      A 16-carbon palmitoyl group on a cysteine through a thioester is S-palmitoylation, and
      because a thioester can be hydrolyzed it is the one anchor cells can take off and put back.
      The amide-linked 14-carbon group on an N-terminal glycine is myristate, which is attached
      stably. The thioether-linked 15-carbon isoprenoid on a CAAX cysteine is a farnesyl group,
      also stable. The phosphate on a serine is a phosphorylation, a modification that is not a
      lipid at all, so it cannot be an acyl anchor.
  - stem: "A cell makes a protein whose only membrane attachment is one myristate on its N-terminal glycine. Which prediction is best?"
    choices:
      - "It is bound permanently, because the amide bond that holds myristate to the protein cannot be broken"
      - "It stays dissolved in the cytosol, because a 14-carbon chain is too short to enter a bilayer at all"
      - "It binds only the Golgi, because myristoylation takes place there"
      - "It sticks to membranes only weakly and comes off again, so a second signal is needed to hold it in place"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      One 14-carbon chain buries only a modest amount of hydrophobic surface, so the protein
      associates with a membrane and then lets go, and a second lipid or a patch of positive
      charge is what turns the weak grip into a lasting one. The claim of permanent binding
      confuses the bond between lipid and protein, which is stable, with the grip of the lipid on
      the bilayer, which is weak. The claim that the chain is too short to enter a bilayer is
      wrong because the chain is plenty hydrophobic to insert, just not strongly enough to stay.
      Myristate is added while the protein is still being made in the cytosol, so it is not
      confined to the Golgi, where the palmitate of H-Ras is added.
  - stem: "K-Ras4B has a farnesyl group and a run of lysines near its C-terminus. Protein kinase C phosphorylates Ser181, next to the lysines. Why does that phosphorylation tend to release K-Ras4B from the plasma membrane?"
    choices:
      - "The phosphate adds negative charge beside the lysines, weakening their attraction to the membrane"
      - "The phosphate takes the place of the farnesyl group, so the lipid is lost from the protein"
      - "The phosphate makes the protein hold GTP more tightly, and GTP-bound Ras is soluble in the cytosol"
      - "The phosphate attracts a thioesterase that strips the palmitate anchor from the protein"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The lysines grip the negative lipids of the inner face, so a phosphate with its two negative
      charges next to them cancels part of that attraction and the protein can drift away; this is a
      farnesyl-electrostatic switch. A kinase does not remove a farnesyl group, which sits on the
      protein through a stable thioether. Whether Ras holds GTP or GDP is a separate switch and does
      not decide whether it sits on a membrane. K-Ras4B has no palmitate to strip, so no thioesterase
      is involved.

  - stem: "H-Ras cycles: a palmitoyl acyltransferase at the Golgi adds palmitate, vesicles carry the protein to the plasma membrane, a thioesterase removes the palmitate, and the protein travels back to the Golgi. A drug blocks that thioesterase. What would you expect to see in cells?"
    choices:
      - "H-Ras becomes soluble in the cytosol, because palmitate is needed for any membrane binding"
      - "H-Ras stops cycling and spreads over internal membranes, losing plasma membrane enrichment"
      - "H-Ras loses its farnesyl group and floats free in the cytosol, away from all membranes"
      - "H-Ras gathers more tightly at the plasma membrane, because its palmitate now stays on longer"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The enrichment at the plasma membrane is kept up by the cycle itself. When depalmitoylation is
      blocked, H-Ras that reaches any membrane stays there, so the cycle that returns it to the Golgi
      and re-sorts it stalls and the protein spreads out. The claim that H-Ras becomes soluble is
      wrong, since blocking the removal of palmitate keeps it on membranes. The idea that H-Ras
      collects more tightly at the plasma membrane assumes more palmitate means a better address, but
      palmitate holds a protein on any membrane. The farnesyl group is attached through a stable bond
      and is not touched by a thioesterase inhibitor, so it is not lost.

    rationale: >-
      PI3-kinase keeps some basal activity in most cells, and PTEN is what keeps resting PIP3 low, so without PTEN

**Ras has no membrane-spanning segment, yet most Ras sits on the inner face of the plasma membrane. What holds it there, and how can a membrane tell a protein where to work?**

Ras, a small GTP-binding switch that relays a growth signal inward, has to meet the proteins that control and receive its signal.

> **The one idea.** Lipids control signaling protein location by supplying grip, address, or both.
>
> *Scope:* this rule covers the peripheral membrane proteins and cytosolic lipid readers in this lesson. An anchor need not specify one organelle, not every PH domain reads PIP3, and other protein contacts can contribute.

## Covalent anchors: what is attached, and where?

The first film left out the bond chemistry, which tells you which contacts a cell can reset.

| Anchor | Size | Where it goes | Enzyme | Can it come off? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Myristate | 14-carbon fatty acid | N-terminal glycine, through an amide bond | N-myristoyltransferase | No |
| Palmitate | 16-carbon fatty acid | A cysteine, through a thioester | Palmitoyl acyltransferases (PATs) | Yes, by thioesterases |
| Farnesyl | 15-carbon isoprenoid | Cysteine of a C-terminal CAAX box, through a thioether | Farnesyltransferase | No |
| Geranylgeranyl | 20-carbon isoprenoid | The same CAAX cysteine, when the final residue favors it | Geranylgeranyltransferase I | No |

Myristate goes on a glycine that becomes the first residue after the starting methionine is clipped off, usually while the protein is still being made. Src-family kinases carry one. Prenyl groups contain five-carbon isoprene units: three in farnesyl and four in geranylgeranyl. A **CAAX box** is a cysteine, two aliphatic amino acids and a final residue that helps determine which prenyltransferase acts. After prenylation, the last three residues are removed and the new end is methylated, hiding its charge. H-Ras, N-Ras and K-Ras all end in a CAAX box.

Amide and thioether bonds are essentially permanent, so myristate and prenyl groups usually remain for the protein's life; only palmitate's thioester is readily hydrolyzed, by thioesterases.

## Two contacts make residence strong and regulatable

The first film's Ras pairings are not the only ones: Fyn and Lck carry myristate plus palmitate. A 20-carbon geranylgeranyl can hold some proteins by itself.

The first film's curve rests on one number. At body temperature, an extra favourable contribution of about 1.4 kcal/mol changes the equilibrium by about tenfold. The exact gain depends on geometry and local concentration, so no universal rule simply multiplies two binding constants.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/lipid-anchors-and-addresses/one-anchor-or-two.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: three panels of a protein under the negatively charged inner face of the plasma membrane. With one lipid tail, the protein is shown both with its tail in the membrane and with its tail out, a two-way arrow between them. With two lipid tails, both are buried and the protein is held. With one lipid tail plus a patch of positive charges facing the negative membrane face, the protein is held." caption="One lipid lets a protein sample membranes; a second lipid or a compatible charge patch makes residence much stronger and creates a point of regulation." />

For K-Ras4B the switch is a kinase. Protein kinase C phosphorylates Ser181 beside the lysine patch. The added negative charge weakens attraction to the plasma membrane, so K-Ras4B tends to move to internal membranes, including mitochondrial membranes. This is a **farnesyl-electrostatic switch**. PDEδ, a carrier with a hydrophobic pocket, can shield a farnesyl group and help Ras cross the cytosol between membranes.

## Palmitate turns a hold into a cycle

The first film carried H-Ras from the Golgi to the plasma membrane but left out where palmitate comes off. Thioesterases can remove palmitate wherever H-Ras sits, which weakens its grip and lets it sample internal membranes and return toward the Golgi for another round. Which thioesterases act, and where they are most active, is not fully settled; the robust point is that palmitate goes on mainly at the Golgi and can come off anywhere.

This is a **kinetic bilayer trap**. No plasma-membrane-specific receptor for H-Ras is required: the rates of trapping, vesicle transport, release and recapture create steady-state enrichment.

**Predict.** A drug blocks the thioesterase that normally resets this cycle. Does H-Ras become soluble, collect ever more tightly at the plasma membrane, or lose its focused distribution?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It should lose its focused distribution and spread across membranes. Palmitate still provides membrane binding, so H-Ras does not become soluble. But without release, return and recapture at the Golgi, the cycle that maintains plasma-membrane enrichment stalls; the farnesyl group stays attached.

</details>

## Can blocking an anchor block cancer signaling?

If Ras requires farnesylation, a farnesyltransferase inhibitor (FTI) should keep newly made Ras from the membrane, and a Ras that cannot reach the membrane cannot signal. Ras genes are mutated in roughly one in five human cancers, and one FTI, tipifarnib, reached Phase III clinical trials.

**Predict.** Farnesyltransferase is blocked, but geranylgeranyltransferase I remains active. Which Ras proteins might keep a lipid anchor, and what observation would support that explanation?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

K-Ras and N-Ras can receive geranylgeranyl on the same CAAX cysteine and remain membrane-associated; H-Ras is not rescued this way. Membrane localization that falls only when both prenyltransferases are inhibited supports a backup enzyme.

</details>

This backup helps explain why the drugs worked biochemically yet disappointed against the Ras that matters most. K-Ras is by far the most commonly mutated of the three Ras genes; H-Ras is the least. The logic still has uses: tipifarnib has been tested in mutant H-Ras cancers, and lonafarnib is approved for progeria, where a farnesylated nuclear-envelope protein is the culprit.

An FTI keeps newly made Ras from acquiring its farnesyl grip; it does not strip farnesyl that is already attached. A cell can also move a protein without changing its anchor, by rewriting the address on the membrane it lands on.

## Phosphoinositides make dynamic addresses

The second film followed one address, PIP3, and left out the other phosphoinositides, the other erasers and the controls a reporter needs. **Phosphoinositides** carry phosphates at positions 3, 4 and 5 of an inositol ring, and cells make seven combinations. They are enriched differently: PI4P at the Golgi, PI3P on early endosomes and PI(4,5)P2, called PIP2, at the plasma membrane.

**Predict.** Replace a PIP3-reading domain in a fluorescent reporter with a domain that selectively reads PI4P. Which compartment should become most prominent?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The expected answer is the Golgi, the classic PI4P-rich compartment. In practice, sensitive PI4P probes also light up the plasma membrane and late endosomes, and several Golgi PI4P readers also need the small GTPase ARF. A reader's pattern reflects its ligand plus any coincident contacts, not PI4P alone.

</details>

The second film's writer is class I PI3-kinase, which adds the 3-phosphate to PIP2 to make PI(3,4,5)P3, or **PIP3**. A local source can mark the front of a crawling cell, whereas a growth factor bathing the cell can raise PIP3 over much of the plasma membrane.

## Readers and erasers

The second film's **PH domain** (pleckstrin homology domain) is a fold of roughly 100 amino acids. Only some PH domains have a pocket for a particular phosphoinositide head group, and many do not read PIP3. Akt's does, and it also binds PI(3,4)P2, formed when PIP3 loses its 5-phosphate.

PTEN, which removes the 3-phosphate, is not the only eraser: SHIP1 and SHIP2 remove the 5-phosphate instead, producing PI(3,4)P2.

**Predict.** If SHIP activity rises while PI3-kinase activity stays the same, must Akt recruitment fall in direct proportion to PIP3?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. SHIP lowers PIP3 but makes PI(3,4)P2, which Akt's PH domain can still bind. Recruitment can therefore outlast or differ from the PIP3 pattern.

</details>

Without PTEN, PIP3 stays high longer and PH-domain proteins keep arriving. Loss of PTEN is common in human cancers.

### Design problem

Build a reporter that lights up the plasma membrane only where and when PI3-kinase is active. What would you fuse to GFP, and what controls show that the signal is specific?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Fuse GFP to a PH domain that binds PIP3 tightly and selectively. Akt's PH domain also binds PI(3,4)P2, so a PIP3-preferring domain such as the GRP1 PH domain is cleaner. It stays cytosolic until local PI3-kinase activity recruits it to a bright membrane region.

## Going deeper

### Why membrane location matters

A membrane reduces a search from three dimensions to two, raising encounter frequency for partners confined there. Ras must meet SOS, the exchange factor that loads GTP, and Raf, the kinase active Ras recruits. Grb2 recruits SOS to the membrane. The inner plasma-membrane face also differs from the outer face and the Golgi: it is rich in negatively charged lipids. A hydrocarbon chain favours the bilayer interior over the watery cytosol.

### The discovery path

In the early 1950s Mabel and Lowell Hokin followed radioactive phosphate while studying RNA in acetylcholine-stimulated pigeon pancreas slices. The radioactivity lost during purification was in phospholipid they had been discarding. In 1975 Robert Michell connected this lipid turnover to receptors.

Lewis Cantley's group later followed a lipid kinase that came down with viral oncoproteins such as v-Src and polyoma middle-T antigen. Cantley recalled that its product ran about a millimetre from the known 4-phosphate lipid on thin-layer chromatography. With Peter Downes, the group mapped the phosphate to the 3 position. Their 1988 work identified phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate, a lipid with no precedent in the literature; later work connected the enzyme to PIP3 in stimulated cells.

### Rafts and reporter limits

Some models place palmitoylated proteins in small cholesterol-rich **lipid rafts**. Living membranes contain small, short-lived ordered domains, but their size, lifetime and importance for sorting particular proteins remain debated.

A PH-domain reporter can also reflect other lipids, general charge and coincident protein contacts. Membrane shape changes apparent intensity.
