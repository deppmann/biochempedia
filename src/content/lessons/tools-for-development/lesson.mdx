    story: >-
      Before gene targeting, a mouse geneticist could add DNA at random but could not choose which gene to change. Capecchi's

      sides of an intended change, and let the cell's own homologous recombination machinery swap
      the new version into the gene's own place. With Kirk Thomas he reported in 1987 that this

      circuit that results.

techniques:
  - name: "Thymidine-analog birth-dating"
    blurb: >-
      Give a pregnant animal tritiated thymidine or BrdU, which cells incorporate while they synthesize
      DNA, principally in S phase but also during repair. Later, cells that are still heavily labeled divided few times after the pulse,
      so the pulse approximately dates their last S phase. Autoradiography or an anti-BrdU antibody
      reveals them.
  - name: "Axonal tract tracing"
    blurb: >-
      Inject a tracer that neurons take up and transport. An anterograde tracer such as biotinylated dextran amine or PHA-L is carried from cell body toward terminals; a retrograde tracer such as Fluorogold or cholera toxin B subunit
      moves from terminals back to cell bodies. Cholera toxin B travels both ways but is used mainly retrogradely. The pattern of labeled cells maps a connection.
  - name: "Interspecies grafting with a nuclear marker"
    blurb: >-
      Transplant tissue between two species whose nuclei can be told apart, classically quail into
      chick. Donor descendants stay recognizable after they migrate and differentiate, so you learn
      where a cell population goes rather than only where it starts.
  - name: "Conditional gene targeting with Cre and lox"
    blurb: >-
      Flank part of a gene with loxP sites and supply Cre recombinase from a cell-type-specific
      promoter. Cre deletes the flanked segment in cells that make Cre, as completely and as exclusively as that driver turns out to be. Fusing Cre to a
      modified estrogen receptor concentrates recombination after tamoxifen, although ligand-independent activity must be tested.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "You want to know which neurons in a mouse cortex finished dividing on embryonic day 13. Which method answers that question most directly?"
    choices:
      - "Nissl staining of cell bodies in a section of the adult cortex"
      - "A retrograde tracer injected into the adult cortex's target area"
      - "An antibody against a protein that marks mature pyramidal cells"
      - "A BrdU injection on embryonic day 13, read out in the adult"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      BrdU is incorporated during DNA synthesis, so a pulse on that day marks cells copying DNA
      then, and heavy label later identifies cells that divided little afterwards. Nissl stain
      shows cell bodies and carries no information about time. A retrograde tracer reports
      where a cell sends its axon, not when it was born. A marker of mature pyramidal identity
      reports cell type, again with no timing.
  - stem: "A quail neural tube is grafted into a chick embryo, and quail-derived cells are later found in a peripheral ganglion. What does the nuclear marker let you conclude?"
    choices:
      - "That each grafted cell could have produced any ganglion cell type"
      - "That the marked ganglion cells descend from the graft, not the host"
      - "That the host contributes no cells at all to the ganglion in question"
      - "That the grafted cells needed a chick signal to become neurons"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The marker reports ancestry after the tissue has moved, so donor descendants can be assigned
      to the graft rather than the host. It says nothing about what signals were required, because
      no signal was manipulated. It cannot report the potential of a single founder, since the
      graft carried many cells. And it cannot rule out a host contribution, which would simply go
      unmarked.
  - stem: "In an Advillin-Cre line crossed to a floxed gene and a Cre-dependent GFP reporter, some GFP-positive cells still show the target protein. What can you infer about target deletion in those cells?"
    choices:
      - "The floxed allele cannot be deleted in this cell type at any age"
      - "GFP has spread from recombined cells into their unrecombined neighbors"
      - "GFP reports recombination of the reporter; the target allele may be unrecombined, or old target protein may remain after recombination"
      - "The reporter is a poor read-out of where Cre has acted in this cross"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      GFP directly reports recombination at the reporter locus, not loss of the target gene or
      protein. Separate lox-flanked loci in the same cell can recombine at different rates, and
      target protein made before recombination can persist even after the target allele is cut. The
      reporter is still informative about reporter recombination. Nothing here shows the floxed
      allele is undeletable, since other cells may have lost it. GFP does not pass between neurons.
  - stem: "Overexpressing protein A in a region makes structure B larger than in matched controls. Verified knockout of A leaves structure B forming normally at its usual size. Which statement fits both results?"
    choices:
      - "A is sufficient to enlarge B under the tested conditions, but is not necessary for baseline B formation and normal size"
      - "A is both necessary and sufficient for B to reach normal size"
      - "A is neither necessary nor sufficient for any part of B's growth"
      - "A is necessary for B to form but is not sufficient to enlarge it"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Extra A enlarges B relative to controls, supporting sufficiency to enlarge B under those
      conditions. Because B still forms and reaches normal size after verified loss of A, A is
      dispensable for baseline B formation and size under those conditions, whereas testing whether
      A is required for induced enlargement would require deleting A under conditions that
      ordinarily induce enlargement. The gain-of-function may also have acted at non-physiological
      levels, or a related gene may cover for A. Calling A necessary for B to form contradicts the
      normal knockout. Calling it both contradicts that same result. Calling it neither ignores the
      gain-of-function enlargement, which is evidence of sufficiency.
  - stem: "You want to test whether firing one class of interneuron is enough to stop a mouse grooming. Which genetic tool do you need in those cells?"
    choices:
      - "An antibody against a marker of that interneuron class"
      - "A fluorescent protein fused to a synaptic scaffold protein"
      - "A light-gated cation channel driven by a cell-specific Cre"
      - "A calcium sensor read out through an implanted optical fiber"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Asking whether activity is enough to cause a behavior requires an actuator, and a light-gated
      cation channel expressed from a Cre-dependent allele depolarizes just those cells on command.
      A calcium sensor only reports activity and cannot impose it. A tagged synaptic protein reports
      localization. An antibody identifies the cells in fixed tissue and changes nothing in a
      living animal.

**A neuron carries no label saying when it was born, which cell it descends from, or which genes it needs.** The first film introduced the four labeling questions that uncover those histories; here we pair each tool with its mechanism, controls, and limits, then build conditional genetic tests.

> **The guiding idea.** For the methods here, the readout and the verified intervention limit the conclusions you can draw; for a causal claim, check the intervention, the outcome measure, and the comparison with matched controls.
>
> *Scope:* this rule guides choosing and interpreting the marking, tracing, genetic, and manipulation methods in this lesson. It states what the readout or verified intervention justifies under the tested conditions once matched controls pass—not a complete account of the biology, and not a claim that skips checking whether the intended event occurred.

| Question | Event carrying the information | Strongest direct claim |
|---|---|---|
| What is the cell? | Stain highlights a structure or antibody binds its target | The highlighted structure or target protein was detected there |
| Where does it project? | A validated tracer is taken up and transported along axons | Retrograde label identifies sampled neurons projecting to the uptake site; anterograde label identifies sampled projections from the labelled source population |
| When did it stop dividing? | A short pulse labels DNA during synthesis, and subsequent divisions generally dilute the label | In a proliferating cohort, heavy retention after a chase supports few further divisions; for identified postmitotic cells under suitable controls, the pulse approximates the terminal S-phase window |
| Whose descendant is it? | A durable mark passes to daughters | Marked cells descend from the marked founder or population |
| What does a gene or activity do? | Intervention removes or imposes it | Tests necessity or sufficiency under those conditions |

## What is this cell, and where does it connect?

The first film introduced three ways to see cells, each working differently and carrying a distinct limit. A **Nissl stain** highlights RNA-rich structures around a nucleus, marking cell bodies across a section plus glial nuclei and some proximal dendrite—good for cell counts and layer boundaries. **Golgi impregnation** fills a small, unpredictable fraction of cells completely with silver chromate precipitate, including dendrites, showing the whole shape of a few neurons against a clear background. **Immunostaining** uses an antibody against one chosen protein, reporting whether that protein target was detected. Specificity cuts both ways: an antibody raised against a mouse protein may miss the chick version, and a monoclonal antibody seeing one site can fail when that site is masked (antibody detection logic is covered in [detecting a signal](/lessons/detecting-a-signal/)).

**Predict.** An antibody gives no signal in a chick section, although it detects the mouse version of the protein. Does the negative result prove that chick cells lack the protein?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The antibody may not recognize the chick version, or its single recognized site may be masked. A negative detection result is not automatically evidence that the target is absent.

</details>

As the first film showed, tract tracers use the neuron's own axonal transport. Inject an **anterograde tracer**—carried from cell body toward terminals, such as biotinylated dextran amine—to map where a region's neurons project. Inject a **retrograde tracer**—taken up by terminals and carried back to cell bodies, such as Fluorogold—into a target to label neurons whose terminals take up enough tracer there. Direction must be established for each tracer: cholera toxin B subunit travels both ways and is used mainly retrogradely. Conventional dye injections give population wiring, not one cell's synapses. A **monosynaptic rabies virus**, genetically restricted to one synaptic step, goes further by labeling cells directly presynaptic to a defined starter population or even one starter cell.

## When was this cell born?

As the first film showed, **tritiated thymidine** and the thymidine analog **BrdU** turn DNA synthesis—**S phase** in dividing cells—into a rough clock. You give a brief **pulse** (a short exposure to the label) and wait through a **chase** (the survival period before examining the tissue).

Each further division usually dilutes label among descendants. Heavy residual label therefore supports few additional divisions; weak label is consistent with continued cycling, although uptake and detection also vary. The inferred “birthday” is thus an approximate last S phase, not an exact timestamp or a readout of cell identity.

**Predict.** A BrdU-positive nucleus appears in injured tissue whose cells are thought to be non-dividing. Must that cell have passed through S phase?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Thymidine analogs can also enter DNA during repair. In dividing tissue the main interpretation is S phase, but BrdU alone does not separate repair from replication in injured tissue; cell-cycle evidence and context are needed.

</details>

Human birth-dating cannot use routine pulses and relies on special cases and atmospheric carbon-14, covered in Going deeper.

## Whose descendant is this cell?

A birth date marks a cohort; a **lineage** is a family sharing a common ancestor. The first film introduced three ways descendants can retain a recognizable feature—a species-specific nuclear pattern, an integrated genetic marker, or a recombined reporter allele; each can persist through division, with detectability depending on the marker and tissue, and each has a distinct scope.

**Interspecies grafting with a nuclear marker** is the classical route. Quail nuclei carry distinctive condensed chromatin around a large nucleolus, so quail cells grafted into a chick embryo stay recognizable after they migrate and differentiate. Nicole Le Douarin used that difference to follow grafted populations into peripheral ganglia, the gut, and head mesenchyme.

**Retroviral labeling** works inside one animal: a retrovirus integrates into a dividing cell's DNA so every descendant inherits the insert and marker gene. At low labeling density, an isolated cluster can be interpreted as a candidate clone if spatial distribution and controls make multiple founders unlikely—still a sampling assumption rather than an observation, since clones can disperse or merge, whereas a founder-specific marker gives stronger evidence of clonality.

**Predict.** Quail-derived cells later appear in a chick peripheral ganglion. Does the marker show that the host contributed no ganglion cells?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The marker shows only that the marked cells descend from the graft. Unmarked host cells may also contribute, and the graft alone reveals neither required signals nor the potential of any single founder.

</details>

In mice, **Cre-dependent reporters** write a permanent, heritable DNA mark into chosen cells. Common designs place a **loxP-flanked** transcriptional stop ahead of a fluorescent protein gene, or use an inversion design that locks the fluorescent gene in its expressible orientation after **Cre recombinase** acts. In the stop-cassette design, Cre excises the transcriptional stop, allowing the reporter to be expressed where its promoter is active; in either design, descendants inherit the recombined allele and stay labeled as long as that promoter is active. Because a cell-type Cre recombining many founders in one colour yields a **population fate map** rather than separate clones, sparse induction, multicolour reporters, and founder-specific barcodes can help resolve individual families; claim a single clone only when the labeling and analysis can distinguish it from nearby independently marked families. For human lineage, naturally occurring somatic mutations play that role (see Going deeper).

## How to make a conditional mouse

The same recombinase switch that marks a lineage can remove a gene in a chosen cell type and time window, as the second film walks through.

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The second film showed how **loxP** sites—each a directional, 34-base-pair DNA sequence—flank a target segment to make a **floxed** allele, and walked through the two loci and their matched controls. Here we add how an engineered allele reaches the mouse germ line and where an Advillin driver can act.

In classical gene targeting, mouse embryonic stem cells from an early embryo can contribute to any tissue, including the germ line. Supply DNA carrying the intended change between two homology arms matching the target gene; in rare cells, **homologous recombination** swaps the new sequence into the gene's own place.

Because targeting is rare, select twice. Positive selection retains cells that stably express the selectable marker inside the construct, including both correctly targeted and random integrants. Negative selection uses a marker outside the arms—classically a thymidine kinase gene making ganciclovir lethal—to eliminate cells expressing that outside marker. Correctly targeted clones usually exclude the outside marker, but some random integrants still escape negative selection, so survivors require genotyping. Disrupting a gene makes a **knockout**; inserting a sequence such as a fluorescent reporter makes a **knock-in**. Injected into an early embryo, selected cells yield a **chimera** (an animal built from both host and stem-cell tissues), and the allele becomes heritable if those cells form its gametes.

Why build a conditional allele? A straight knockout can kill an embryo before the question matters, and whole-body deletion cannot identify which tissue mattered. When two loxP sites face the same direction around an essential exon, Cre deletes the bracketed segment; opposite-orientation sites instead invert it (and paired-site inversion designs can lock an inverted reporter in its expressible orientation). To control timing, **CreER** fuses Cre to a modified estrogen receptor that keeps the enzyme largely outside the nucleus until tamoxifen arrives; a doxycycline-driven tetracycline-controlled system offers an alternative timing switch.

Return to the guiding idea: **a causal claim depends on the verified intervention, the outcome measure, and comparison with matched controls.** In genotype notation, a superscript names what sits at a locus, a slash separates its alleles, and a semicolon separates loci: `p75NTR-/-` is a homozygous knockout, `DR6+/-` a heterozygote, and `p75NTR-/-;TNFR1-/-` a double knockout.

Now go beyond the second film's locus-by-locus reading of **`AvilCre/+;TrkAfl/-`**. A mouse that inherits `TrkAfl/-` starts with one floxed allele and one null allele, so eliminating the remaining potential `TrkA` function requires recombining one allele per cell rather than two in a `TrkAfl/fl` cell. That can increase the fraction of fully inactivated cells if per-allele recombination is comparable, though the two genotypes differ in baseline dosage; verify target inactivation and protein loss in either cross. Likewise, the `Avil` driver uses Advillin regulatory activity, and Advillin is found in sensory neurons as well as several autonomic populations—including sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric neurons—so an `Avil-Cre` line can affect cells outside the intended sensory population. Verify recombination of the `TrkA` allele and loss of `TrkA` protein in both sensory and autonomic populations of the specific cross and age.

### Design problem

Gene X is needed for the embryo to survive, so a straight knockout dies before birth. You want to know what X does in sensory neurons after birth. Design the mouse: list the alleles, the drug, the reporter, and three controls.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Alleles: a `CreER` driver validated to restrict gene X loss to the intended sensory neurons at the chosen age (or a validated intersectional design); X floxed over floxed; and a Cre-dependent fluorescent reporter. If using an `Advillin-CreER` line, map recombination in autonomic ganglia too because advillin is not sensory-specific, and interpret any phenotype as reflecting the measured combined targeting unless an independent comparison isolates the sensory contribution. Dose tamoxifen after birth to concentrate induction in the postnatal period. Check embryonic tissue for target recombination and verify that X is retained during embryonic development. Use age-matched vehicle controls to assess drug-independent recombination, and measure when X is lost after dosing. Fluorescence marks detectable reporter recombination in a cell or its ancestor, not proven loss of X.

Use three comparison groups and a shared baseline. Compare tamoxifen-treated mice carrying `CreER` and nonfloxed X alleles with matched tamoxifen-treated mice carrying the same X alleles without `CreER` to test for effects associated with the driver under tamoxifen. Floxed over floxed with no `CreER`, given tamoxifen, tests whether the floxed allele is hypomorphic (partly defective without Cre) when compared with that same shared tamoxifen-treated, nonfloxed, Cre-negative baseline. The full experimental genotype given vehicle establishes the no-tamoxifen baseline and tests ligand-independent `CreER` activity at both reporter and target loci. In the same animals, verify target recombination and measure how much X protein is actually gone in reporter-positive cells—and check beyond reporter-positive cells as needed—because phenotype size depends on actual target loss.

## Necessary, sufficient, reporter, actuator

Two pairs of words finish the experimental logic previewed in the second film.

The first pair separates causal claims. **Sufficient under the tested conditions** means imposing the factor produces the specified outcome relative to matched controls. **Necessary under the tested conditions** means verified removal prevents the specified outcome relative to matched controls. Each can hold without the other.

**Predict.** Overexpressing protein A makes structure B expand relative to controls, while verified knockout of A leaves structure B forming normally at its usual size. What does this pair of experiments support about sufficiency and necessity?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Extra A is sufficient to enlarge B in the gain-of-function test. Because B forms and reaches normal size after verified loss of A, A is not necessary for baseline B formation and size under those conditions. Testing whether A is necessary for induced enlargement would require deleting A under conditions that ordinarily trigger enlargement. Gain-of-function may place A at levels or locations it never normally reaches, or a redundant relative may cover its baseline loss. Necessity without sufficiency would mean A is one requirement among several.

</details>

The second pair separates reading a state from imposing it (see [hacking the cell](/lessons/hacking-the-cell/) for how these genetically encoded tools are built). A **reporter** reads out a state: a fluorescent protein marks a cell, a tagged protein locates a molecule, and a calcium sensor brightens when calcium rises. An **actuator** imposes a state: blue light opens **channelrhodopsin**, a light-gated cation channel, to drive firing; yellow light drives **halorhodopsin**, a light-driven chloride pump, to move chloride in and suppress activity (light-gated channels are introduced in [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/)).

Chemical actuators follow the same logic: express **TRPV1**, a capsaicin-gated channel, in chosen neurons of a mouse lacking its own TRPV1, so capsaicin can directly activate the introduced TRPV1 in those targeted cells; check drug effects in mice without the introduced receptor and distinguish direct activation from downstream circuit responses. An activity reporter alone shows correlation with an outcome; an actuator asks whether that activity is enough.

## Going deeper

### Human birth-dating and lineage

Giving a thymidine analog to people solely to study the brain is not done. The classic exception, Eriksson and colleagues in 1998, used postmortem tissue from patients already given BrdU during cancer care. For humans generally, atmospheric nuclear weapons testing raised carbon-14 sharply before the 1963 test ban treaty, after which it fell, so genomic-DNA carbon-14 reflects when a cell's DNA was last made; Spalding and colleagues matched genomic-DNA carbon-14 to that record in 2005. The method is the point here; turnover in particular human brain regions remains contested and belongs to adult neurogenesis. For human lineage, where experimental marks cannot be introduced, reconstructing somatic mutations is the main route.

### From classical targeting to newer routes

Capecchi's laboratory, with Smithies's and Evans's, established classical gene targeting. CRISPR shortened that path: a guide RNA directs Cas9 nuclease to a matching sequence in a fertilized egg, letting many alleles be built without stem cells or chimeras.
