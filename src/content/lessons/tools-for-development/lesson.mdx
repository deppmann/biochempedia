techniques:
  - name: "Thymidine-analog birth-dating"
    blurb: >-
      Give a pregnant animal tritiated thymidine or BrdU, which cells incorporate while they synthesize
      DNA, principally in S phase but also during repair. Later, cells that are still heavily labeled divided few times after the pulse,
      so the pulse approximately dates their last S phase. Autoradiography or an anti-BrdU antibody
      reveals them.
  - name: "Axonal tract tracing"
    blurb: >-
      Inject a tracer that neurons take up and transport. An anterograde tracer such as biotinylated dextran amine or PHA-L is carried from cell body toward terminals; a retrograde tracer such as Fluorogold or cholera toxin B subunit
      moves from terminals back to cell bodies. Cholera toxin B travels both ways but is used mainly retrogradely. The pattern of labeled cells maps a connection.
  - name: "Interspecies grafting with a nuclear marker"
    blurb: >-
      Transplant tissue between two species whose nuclei can be told apart, classically quail into
      chick. Donor descendants stay recognizable after they migrate and differentiate, so you learn
      where a cell population goes rather than only where it starts.
  - name: "Conditional gene targeting with Cre and lox"
    blurb: >-
      Flank part of a gene with loxP sites and supply Cre recombinase from a cell-type-specific
      promoter. Cre deletes the flanked segment in cells that make Cre, as completely and as exclusively as that driver turns out to be. Fusing Cre to a
      modified estrogen receptor makes the deletion wait for tamoxifen.

**A neuron carries no label saying when it was born, which cell it descends from, or which genes it needs.** Every claim in this course rests on a method that supplies one of those labels. So before the biology, the toolkit, sorted by the question each tool can answer.

Four questions come up again and again. What is this cell? Where does it send its axon? When did it stop dividing? And what does it need a given gene for? A method that answers one of them answers the others badly or not at all, which is why most of the mistakes in this field are mismatches between a tool and a claim.

## What is this cell, and where does it connect?

Three staining strategies, three uses. A Nissl stain binds the RNA-rich cytoplasm around a nucleus, so it shows cell bodies across a section, along with glial nuclei and some proximal dendrite: good for counting cells and finding the boundaries of layers. A Golgi stain fills a small, unpredictable fraction of cells completely, including their dendrites, so you see the whole shape of a few neurons against a clear background. Immunostaining uses an antibody against one chosen protein, so it reports a molecular identity. The cost is specificity in the other direction: an antibody raised against a mouse protein may not recognize the chick version, and a monoclonal antibody that sees one site can fail when that site is masked. The logic of antibodies as detection reagents is covered in [detecting a signal](/lessons/detecting-a-signal/).

Connections need a different trick, and the trick is the neuron's own transport machinery. Inject an anterograde tracer such as biotinylated dextran amine into a region and neurons there take it up and carry it toward their terminals, so it reports where those cells project. Cholera toxin B subunit travels in both directions and is used mainly retrogradely, so the direction has to be established for each tracer. Inject Fluorogold into a target region instead, and terminals take it up and carry it back to the cell bodies, which lights up the neurons whose terminals take up enough tracer there: a retrograde tracer. Either way, what a dye injection gives you is a population's wiring, not one cell's synapses. Genetically targeted viruses go further: a rabies virus restricted to one synaptic step labels the cells directly presynaptic to a defined starter population, or even to a single starter cell.

## When was this cell born?

How do you label newborn neurons? The answer uses DNA replication as a clock. Tritiated thymidine, and later the thymidine analog BrdU, are incorporated wherever DNA is being synthesized, which in a dividing cell means S phase. Give a pulse, wait, and look for label.

The reading needs care. Each further division splits the label between two daughters, so a nucleus that is still heavily labeled after a long chase (the wait after the pulse) divided only a few more times. A cell's "birthday" from such an experiment means its last S phase, inferred from heavy residual label, and weak label is consistent with descendants of cells that kept cycling, though uptake and detection also vary. The timestamp is approximate, and it says nothing about what the cell became.

Giving a thymidine analog to people only to study the brain is not done. The classic exception, Eriksson and colleagues in 1998, used postmortem tissue from patients who had already been treated with BrdU as part of cancer care. For humans generally there is a retrospective method: atmospheric nuclear weapons testing raised atmospheric carbon-14 sharply before the 1963 test ban treaty and it has fallen since, so the carbon-14 content of a cell's genomic DNA reflects the year its DNA was last made. Spalding and colleagues described this approach in 2005, matching the carbon-14 in genomic DNA to the atmospheric record. Here the method is the point; what it has found about turnover in particular human brain regions is contested and belongs to the lesson on adult neurogenesis.

## Whose descendant is this cell?

A birth date is one cohort of cells. A lineage is one family. Three classical ways to follow one, then the modern additions: multicolor reporters and genetic barcodes that separate one founder's family from its neighbours, and reconstruction from somatic mutations, which is the main route to lineage in the human brain.

Grafting with a nuclear marker is the classic approach. Quail nuclei carry a distinctive condensed-chromatin pattern, so quail cells grafted into a chick embryo stay recognizable in sections after they migrate and differentiate. Le Douarin built an experimental program on that difference, following grafted populations into ganglia, gut and head mesenchyme.

Retroviral labeling works inside one animal. A retrovirus integrates its genome into the DNA of a dividing cell, so every descendant of that cell inherits the insert and the marker gene it carries. Keep the dose low enough that labeled clones do not overlap, and a cluster of marked cells is one progenitor's family. That assumption is a sampling argument, not an observation, and it is where this method can fail.

The genetic version is the one you will use. A Cre-dependent reporter is commonly a fluorescent protein gene with a transcriptional stop signal flanked by loxP sites in front of it; other designs leave the gene inverted until Cre flips it. When Cre recombinase appears in a cell, it deletes the stop, and the recombined allele passes to that cell's descendants, which stay labeled as long as the reporter's promoter is active. The marking is permanent and heritable through division. But a cell-type Cre recombines in many founders at once, so what you read out is a fate map of a population, not one founder's family. Following a single family needs sparse or low-dose induction, multicolor reporters, or barcodes that differ between founders. Reporters and actuators themselves are taught in [hacking the cell](/lessons/hacking-the-cell/); here the point is the recombination.

## How to make a mouse

To ask what a gene does, change it. In the classical route you work in mouse embryonic stem cells, cells from an early embryo that can still contribute to any tissue. Supply DNA matching the target gene on both sides of your intended change, and in rare cells homologous recombination swaps your version into the gene's own place. Capecchi's laboratory, together with Smithies's and Evans's, established this. Because the events are rare, you select twice: a positive marker inside the construct keeps cells that took the DNA up, and a negative marker outside the matching arms, classically a thymidine kinase gene that makes ganciclovir lethal, kills most cells that inserted the construct at a random site and kept that marker, so survivors still have to be genotyped. Destroying the gene is a knockout; writing a new sequence in, such as a fluorescent reporter, is a knock-in. The selected cells go into an early embryo, the pup is a chimera, and if those cells made its gametes the allele is heritable. CRISPR has shortened this path: a guide RNA brings the Cas9 nuclease to a matching sequence in a fertilized egg, so many alleles can now be built without stem cells or chimeras.

A straight knockout has two limits. If the gene is needed early, the embryo dies before the question you care about. And a whole-body deletion cannot tell you which tissue mattered. Cre and lox solve both. Flank the important part of the gene with loxP sites, a 34-base-pair sequence; that allele is "floxed" and is designed to work normally, which a Cre-free control has to confirm. Then supply Cre from a promoter active in one cell type, and the gene is deleted largely in those cells. How exclusive and how complete that deletion is has to be measured for each driver. For timing, fuse Cre to a modified estrogen receptor (CreER) so it stays out of the nucleus until the animal is given tamoxifen, or use a tetracycline-controlled system driven by doxycycline. How do you get expression in two different neuron types? Add a second recombinase with its own sites, Flp, and run two independent switches; requiring both in the same cell is how intersectional specificity is achieved.

Read the notation and you can read the cross. A superscript gives what is written into a locus and a slash separates the two alleles: p75NTR-/- is a homozygous knockout; DR6+/- is a heterozygote; p75NTR-/-;TNFR1-/- is a double knockout; AvilCre/+ is one copy of a Cre allele controlled by Advillin, long used as a sensory-neuron gene, though advillin and Avil-Cre recombination are also found in sympathetic, parasympathetic and enteric neurons; TrkAfl/- carries one floxed TrkA allele and one null (nonfunctional) allele. So AvilCre/+;TrkAfl/- deletes TrkA wherever Avil-Cre is active, which includes sensory neurons and some autonomic ones, and keeps only the floxed copy available to lose, while the matched control is a Cre-negative TrkAfl/- littermate, which carries the same two alleles and no Cre; AvilCre/+;TrkAfl/+ is a further control for Cre activity itself.

One trap. A reporter cross tells you where Cre acted. It does not tell you where the floxed gene was lost. The two lox-flanked targets recombine independently, drivers can be leaky (recombining where or when they should not) or mosaic (recombining in only some target cells), and protein made before the cut persists.

### Design problem

Gene X is needed for the embryo to survive, so a straight knockout dies before birth. You want to know what X does in sensory neurons after birth. Design the mouse: list the alleles, the drug, the reporter, and three controls.

Alleles: a sensory-neuron CreER driver (an Advillin-CreER line, whose recombination has to be mapped in autonomic ganglia too, because advillin is not sensory-specific after birth), X floxed over floxed, and a Cre-dependent fluorescent reporter. Give tamoxifen after birth, so X survives the embryonic period and is then deleted on your schedule, and the reporter marks the cells in which Cre was active.

Three controls. First, CreER with no floxed allele, given tamoxifen: tests whether Cre plus tamoxifen harms neurons by itself. Second, floxed over floxed with no CreER, given tamoxifen: tests whether the floxed allele is itself hypomorphic, that is, partly defective even without Cre. Third, the full experimental genotype given vehicle instead of tamoxifen: tests ligand-independent CreER activity. Then measure, in the same animals, how much X protein is actually gone in reporter-positive cells, because the size of your phenotype depends on it.

## Necessary, sufficient, reporter, actuator

Two pairs of words will carry you through most papers in this course.

The first pair is about inference. "Sufficient" means you added the thing and the outcome followed: overexpress protein A and structure B expands. "Necessary" means you removed it and the outcome failed: knock out A and B disappears. Each can hold without the other. Sufficiency without necessity has more than one reading: the gain-of-function may have put the protein at levels or in places it never normally reaches, or a redundant relative may cover the loss; necessity without sufficiency means A is one requirement among several.

The second pair is about tools. A reporter reads out a state: a fluorescent protein marking where a cell is, a tagged protein showing where it sits inside a cell, a calcium sensor brightening when calcium rises. An actuator imposes a state: channelrhodopsin, a cation channel that blue light opens, to drive firing; halorhodopsin, which moves chloride under yellow light, to suppress it. Light-gated channels are introduced in [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/). Chemical actuators work the same way. Put TRPV1, a capsaicin-gated channel, into chosen neurons of a mouse whose own TRPV1 is knocked out, and capsaicin activates those neurons and not the rest. An activity reporter on its own tells you that activity goes with something, not that it caused it. Only an actuator can ask whether the activity is enough.
