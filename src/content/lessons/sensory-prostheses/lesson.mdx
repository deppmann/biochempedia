**A cochlear implant with 12 to 22 contacts gives many adults with severe hearing loss useful hearing. The same engineering idea in an epiretinal implant often gives streaks, not pixels. What decides how much detail a prosthesis can deliver?**

A cochlear implant is a thin array of contacts, small electrodes, threaded into the cochlea. It skips the damaged hair cells and excites the auditory nerve directly, and it is the established success of sensory prostheses. Users often describe speech through a new implant as harsh or robotic at first, and many understand much more after weeks to months of use. Still, even 22 contacts do not carry all the information normal hearing does. In the eye the gap is wider. People with an **epiretinal** implant, a grid of electrodes on the retina's inner surface, were asked to draw what a single electrode made them see. They often drew elongated or distorted shapes, not the dot, the pixel, that a camera-like grid would promise.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A prosthesis can deliver detail only as far as it drives distinct groups of neurons, each in the right place on the brain's map. Current spread blurs neighbouring groups together, and current that reaches the wrong neurons puts the signal in the wrong place. So a count of contacts or pixels is not a count of usable channels. That has to be measured, by testing what the user can tell apart.

The idea covers spatial detail: frequency channels in the cochlea, acuity in the retina, separate patches of skin in touch. It is not a formula. Overlapping fields can still carry some extra information, and steering current between neighbouring contacts can create pitches between them. Neural survival, placement and learning also shape any one user's score. The idea says nothing about safety, about how strong the trial evidence is, or about what pattern each group of neurons receives; that second half of the problem ends the lesson.

The lesson tests the idea on the cochlear implant and two kinds of retinal implant, then on optogenetic therapy and touch written into a nerve or cortex. It is about mechanisms and evidence, not about what any person should do.

## Borrowing a map: why place coding is kind to engineers

The [hearing and balance](/lessons/hearing-and-balance/) lesson gave you the cochlea as a frequency map. That map is what a **cochlear implant** borrows. Clinical maps assign higher frequencies to more basal contacts and lower frequencies to more apical ones, which turns the place code into an addressing scheme. The cochlea is kind to engineers because it is a one-dimensional map with the nerve lying along it.

Three words are easy to blur. A **contact** is a piece of hardware on the array. A **channel**, here, is a band of frequency information assigned to a contact. What you need, for the channels to be truly separate, is an **independently accessible neural population** for each: its own group of auditory-nerve neurons.

**Predict.** Take an illustrative array of 22 contacts spaced 0.75 mm apart, spanning about 16 mm of cochlea. Current from each contact passes through fluid and bone before it reaches the nerve, so it spreads. If each contact excites neurons over about 1.75 mm, how many non-overlapping stretches of nerve fit along the array?

About 16 mm ÷ 1.75 mm ≈ 9, far fewer than 22. Neighbouring contacts produce overlapping electric fields, called **channel interaction**, so they excite overlapping groups of neurons, and many contacts do not give the same number of independent channels. This is a toy, one-dimensional packing exercise, not a prediction of anyone's channel count: real fields fade gradually rather than ending at an edge. The 1.75 mm is an illustrative width, picked so the arithmetic lands near what listeners show. In 18 adult implant users, tested with maps that switched on more or fewer channels, speech recognition reached a plateau at 8 to 10 channels (Berg and colleagues, 2020). So the match illustrates the idea; it does not test it. Channel interaction is one main reason for that plateau. Neural survival, the distance from contacts to neurons and how far the array reaches into the cochlea also matter.

The real test is a prediction the arithmetic was not tuned to. The nerve cell bodies the implant targets, the spiral ganglion, sit in the cochlea's central core, the modiolus. Some arrays are straight and lie along the outer wall. Others are **precurved**, shaped to hug the inner wall near the modiolus. A precurved array can also be **translocated**: during insertion it crosses from the lower chamber, the scala tympani, into the upper one, the scala vestibuli. In the reported cohorts, straight and translocated precurved arrays generally sat farther from the modiolus than well-placed precurved ones. The 18 listeners above had straight arrays or translocated precurved arrays.

**Predict.** If current spread sets the limit, what should listeners with precurved arrays well placed in the scala tympani show as more channels are switched on?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Contacts closer to the nerve should excite narrower groups of neurons, so these listeners should keep improving past 8 to 10 channels. In an earlier cohort from the same group, they did (Berg and colleagues, 2019). Greater channel interaction is the proposed explanation for the difference, so the plateau depends on array type and placement, as the idea says it should.

</details>

So no single channel number fits all listeners; it depends on the listener, the array placement and the test. Nor does a plateau in one test mean the extra contacts do nothing. In harder listening, extra electrodes can still help: in seven adults, going from 20 active electrodes to 8 made the **speech-in-noise threshold** worse, meaning speech had to be louder relative to background noise to be understood (Schvartz-Leyzac, Zwolan and Pfingst, 2017). A benefit under hard conditions does not mean each contact is independent, either.

None of this means the implant works poorly; the gains are large. **CNC words** are single consonant-vowel-consonant words, scored as percent correct. In 96 adults who used a hearing aid before implantation, the marginal (model-estimated) mean improvement in CNC word recognition at six months was 40.5 percentage points, tested with the implant plus a hearing aid in the other ear (Buchman and colleagues, 2020). The implanted ear alone, a separate listening condition, is under Going deeper.

## Why a retinal electrode does not make a pixel

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

An eye is a camera, so the tempting design is a grid of electrodes that makes a grid of dots. The Argus II tried it: a camera converted images into stimulation patterns for an array of 60 epiretinal electrodes. A prospective trial tested it in 30 adults with end-stage outer-retinal degeneration, 29 with retinitis pigmentosa and one with choroideremia (da Cruz and colleagues, 2016).

Recall from [the retina](/lessons/retina/) that ganglion-cell axons run across the retina's inner surface toward the optic disk, which is where an epiretinal electrode sits (over the macular retina in these implants).

**Predict.** Axons from ganglion cells elsewhere in the retina pass under the electrode. Why would stimulating that one electrode give a streak rather than a dot?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The drawings in the opening come from Beyeler and colleagues (2019): four adults with severe retinitis pigmentosa (one Argus I and three Argus II implants) drew what they saw when single electrodes were stimulated. A model that traces axon pathways across the retina accounted for the shapes: current excites **axons of passage**, and the percept follows the places those axons normally serve, each carrying signals from a ganglion cell that reports a different part of the visual field. Recruiting cells along a bundle of passing axons activates a stretch of visual locations at once, which can account for an elongated percept, a streak. That is a model-supported explanation of the drawings, not a direct recording of the axons.

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/sensory-prostheses/axons-of-passage-streak.webp" width={1200} height={795} alt="AI-generated illustration: a top-down patch of inner retina with ganglion cells whose axons run in parallel toward the optic disk, and one gold electrode lying over passing axons. Two insets compare an expected single dot with an elongated streak that participants drew." caption="In four adults with retinitis pigmentosa, single-electrode percepts were often elongated or distorted. A model of axon pathways accounted for the shapes: current excites axons of passage and the percept follows the places those axons normally serve. This is a model-supported explanation, not a recording of the axons." />

One electrode therefore does not make one pixel, and a simulation that draws a clean grid of dots overstates what the implant user sees: the current reaches neurons whose place on the map is somewhere else.

The idea suggests a fix: move the electrode away from the axons and keep each field small. **PRIMA** does both. A camera image is projected into the eye as near-infrared light, which both powers the pixels and carries the picture. Photovoltaic pixels under the retina (**subretinal**) convert that light into current that stimulates bipolar cells, the first surviving inner retinal neurons, so the signal still passes through the remaining inner retinal network to the ganglion cells and may keep some of the retina's own processing. Each pixel has a local return electrode around it, so current flows out and back within a small area instead of spreading toward a distant return, which is meant to confine its field. Sitting beneath the retina also puts the pixels farther from the axon layer. These are design arguments, not the findings of a controlled comparison with epiretinal implants.

To ask what such a chip should achieve, you need a scale for acuity. **logMAR** is the base-10 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution, the smallest detail a person can resolve, in arcminutes (sixtieths of a degree). A logMAR of 0 is 20/20, 1.0 is 20/200, and higher is worse. The Snellen denominator is 20 times 10 to the power of the logMAR value. A drop of 0.2 logMAR is about a 1.6-fold finer limit. The converter does the arithmetic.

<LogMarSnellenConverter />

**Predict.** PRIMA pixels are 100 micrometres (µm) across. On the human retina roughly 288 µm spans one degree of visual angle, though the figure varies with the length of the eye. If each pixel's field stays within that pixel and each pixel acts as a separate channel, what is the finest acuity the chip could give?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

100 µm ÷ 288 µm per degree ≈ 0.35 degree, about 20.8 arcminutes. One pixel width is the minimum angle because the finest pattern the grid can draw alternates one pixel on and one off, so each stroke or gap is one pixel wide. The base-10 log of 20.8 is about 1.32, so the pixel limit is about 1.32 logMAR, or about 20/420. That is an ideal sampling estimate, not a guaranteed score.

Now the data. In the first clinical report, five adults over 60 with advanced atrophic age-related macular degeneration and no foveal light perception received a 378-pixel chip (Palanker and colleagues, 2020). The three people with optimal chip placement reached prosthetic acuity of 20/460 to 20/550, a little worse than the estimate. In the later PRIMAvera trial (Holz and colleagues, online 2025, 2026 issue of the New England Journal of Medicine), mean implant-resolution acuity, without zoom or image processing, was 1.32 logMAR. Since 20 × 10^1.32 is about 418, that is about 20/418.

The measured mean sits near the pixel limit. Treat that as agreement, not as a precise prediction: the conversion from micrometres to degrees varies between eyes, so a match to the second decimal is partly luck. It fits what the design aims for, pixels that act as separate channels, but one trial's mean does not prove that each field is confined.

</details>

Run the same conversion for the Argus II. Its electrodes are about 575 µm apart, which is about 2 degrees, so an ideal static grid of perfect pixels would give about 20/2400. Yet the best grating acuity reported in its trial was about 20/1260 (Humayun and colleagues, 2012). A conversion like this is a nominal estimate, not a hard limit: how the camera image is mapped onto the array, the test used and distorted percepts all change real scores.

Two cautions keep the PRIMA result in proportion. First, PRIMAvera participants could adjust the zoom from 1 to 12 times during acuity testing, and the trial's primary endpoint was measured with zoom allowed. A zoom-assisted chart score measures the implant plus a magnifier, not extra pixels. Second, the trial had no comparison group, and serious device- or procedure-related events were reported beside the benefit; the Design problem reads its denominators. Neither acuity number applies to people with other retinal diseases.

## Putting the signal in with light instead of current

A third approach changes the cells instead of adding electrodes. In an **optogenetic** therapy, a viral vector makes surviving cells express a light-sensitive protein; in the trials below it is a light-gated channel. The [photoreceptor adaptation and degeneration](/lessons/photoreceptor-adaptation-and-degeneration/) lesson covered why photoreceptors are lost in retinitis pigmentosa; the actuators come from [the cell hacking toolkit](/lessons/hacking-the-cell/).

Sahel and colleagues (2021, Nature Medicine) combined an intraocular vector encoding the red-shifted channel ChrimsonR with engineered light-stimulating goggles in one adult with blindness from retinitis pigmentosa. The goggles detected changes in light intensity in the scene and projected matching pulses of amber light onto the retina to activate the transduced retinal ganglion cells. They were needed for two reasons. Microbial light sensors such as ChrimsonR need far brighter light than photoreceptors do, so ordinary room light does not drive them. And driving the retina's output cells directly skips the processing that bipolar and amacrine cells would normally do, so the goggles have to do part of that scene encoding themselves.

The tests compared conditions. With the treated eye and the goggles, the patient perceived, located, counted and touched objects. After the injection and without the goggles, the patient did not detect objects. EEG showed object-related activity above visual cortex during perception. The with-goggles and without-goggles comparison is the informative one: it shows that, in this patient and in these tasks, the response depended on the goggle light. It does not show restored normal vision, and one patient cannot establish how often this works. A later ten-person trial, described among the anecdotes, added safety data and a goggle-tested gain in light sensitivity.

Here the goggles' light pattern, not an electrode grid, decides which ganglion cells are driven. The results in this lesson are object tasks and light sensitivity, not acuity, so they do not say how many separate places this approach can address.

## Writing touch: addresses, then patterns

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Touch shows both halves of the problem: where each signal lands, and what pattern it carries. It can be written in at the peripheral nerve or at the cortex. For the natural afferents being imitated, see [touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) and [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/); the course lessons on [vibration corpuscles](/lessons/vibration-corpuscles/) and the [dorsal horn](/lessons/dorsal-horn-touch/) show the code in more detail.

**Cortex: electrodes as addresses.** Flesher and colleagues (2016) stimulated primary somatosensory cortex through microelectrode arrays in one man with a long-term cervical spinal cord injury. Stimulating an electrode evoked a sensation felt in a region of the hand, its **projected field**. Fields can overlap and change with the stimulus, so an electrode is an approximate address, not a guaranteed separate channel. Changing the amplitude graded the perceived intensity, and many percepts remained stable for months.

**Peripheral nerve.** Tan and colleagues (2014) tested nonpenetrating cuffs on residual nerves in two men with upper-limb amputations, in laboratory sessions. Stimulation produced touch in the phantom hand, and the responses were repeatable and stable for 16 and 24 months. Patterned stimulation produced sensations the participants described as natural, and artificial touch helped them manipulate delicate objects with the prosthesis. Two participants and subjective ratings do not establish normal touch.

**Does the feedback help a task?** In the 2021 follow-up, the man from the 2016 study, who was 28 at implantation, controlled a robotic arm through a brain-computer interface that decoded his intended movements from motor cortex, while somatosensory stimulation provided tactile feedback. The median task time fell from 20.9 s without feedback to 10.2 s with it, mainly through less time spent trying to grasp objects.

**Predict.** By what percentage did his median task time fall? Choose the baseline before you divide.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

1. Absolute reduction = 20.9 s - 10.2 s = 10.7 s.
2. Percentage reduction relative to the no-feedback median:

> **percentage reduction = 100 x (20.9 - 10.2) / 20.9 = 100 x 10.7 / 20.9 = 51.2 percent**

3. Scope: this compares two medians from one participant. It is not a mean paired effect and not a population effect. Repeated trials in one person are not independent replicates. The authors checked practice and decoder effects with 23 earlier sessions in which feedback was absent or unreliable and a daily decoder-only task, so the result supports the usefulness of feedback in that person.

</details>

**Patterns across addresses write features.** Valle and colleagues (2025) studied two adults with cervical spinal cord injury who had arrays in the hand representation of Brodmann area 1, part of primary somatosensory cortex. Spatially patterned multi-electrode stimulation evoked sensations of tactile edges, and spatiotemporal patterns evoked apparent motion with controllable speed and direction. This widens what can be written in. It does not show that every quality of touch has been restored.

## The second half: sending the right pattern

Reaching the right neurons is half the job. Valle's edges and motion already used patterns across electrodes; the other half is the pattern of pulses each group receives. The strategy running through these studies is **biomimicry**: reproduce the task-relevant features of the natural neural code, then test the percept and the behaviour. The place map in the cochlea and the natural-feeling patterns in touch are examples.

Here a **biomimetic pulse train** approximates how touch-responsive neurons in cortex respond, with the strongest activity at contact onset and offset. In the simple scheme the pulse amplitude changes over the contact while the pulse rate stays fixed; the advanced scheme also varies the rate and how many electrodes are recruited. Hobbs and colleagues (2025) tested the strategy directly in three adults with cervical spinal cord injury and arrays in primary somatosensory cortex (S1). They compared cortical stimulation with a real press: mechanical indentation delivered to residual sensate hand regions, parts of the hand the person could still feel. Simple single-electrode biomimetic stimulation felt more like the press than the non-biomimetic pattern did on 32 percent of tested electrodes, and advanced four-electrode biomimetic stimulation did so on 75 percent of tested electrode groups. The denominators differ (electrodes against electrode groups), so the two percentages are not a controlled head-to-head comparison. Biomimetic pulse trains also needed less charge for intensity-matched sensations.

This supports biomimicry as an evidence-backed strategy. It is not a proven law that performance depends on how exactly the code is reproduced, and it says nothing about ranking one disorder's prosthesis against another's: the studies cited here do not compare cochlear and retinal devices head to head.

So the lesson comes down to two steps: find out how many distinct groups of neurons the device can drive, each in its place on the map, then send each group a pattern it can read.

### Design problem

A company reports that its visual implant improved acuity in a single-arm trial. Design the study you would want before believing the claim, and interpret the published numbers from PRIMAvera.

PRIMAvera was **open-label** (no one was masked to the treatment), **nonrandomized** and **single-arm** (every participant got the implant, with no comparison group). It enrolled adults with geographic atrophy, the advanced dry form of age-related macular degeneration, aged at least 60 years, and implanted 38 of them. At twelve months 32 were assessed. The primary endpoint was an improvement of at least 0.2 logMAR in prosthetic acuity measured with the PRIMA glasses, where participants could adjust zoom, compared with baseline. In 26 of the 32 assessed participants the endpoint was met; the authors' multiple-imputation estimate for all 38, a statistical fill-in of the missing results, was 80 percent. Nineteen participants had 26 serious device- or procedure-related events over the twelve months, and mean natural peripheral acuity did not change significantly.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with what a single arm confounds: surgery, device use, training and time all change together. So ask for:

1. A prespecified primary endpoint with a stated minimum change, as PRIMAvera had.
2. A comparison group, such as an untreated or deferred-implantation group, so the change can be separated from practice and time.
3. Masked assessors for acuity testing where the device allows.
4. The full denominator. 26/32 = 81.3 percent of those assessed, but if the six unassessed participants are counted as not meeting the endpoint, it is 26/38 = 68.4 percent. The multiple-imputation estimate of 80 percent is a third number, built on assumptions about the missing six. All belong in the report, with an account of why the six were missing.
5. Adverse events beside the benefit, as PRIMAvera reported them.
6. Native implant resolution (mean 1.32 logMAR) reported apart from zoom-assisted reading, plus the unchanged natural peripheral acuity.
7. A statement of scope: adults with geographic atrophy at a mean age of 78.9 do not stand for all blindness.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Cochlear channel number and listening conditions

- In the Berg 2020 study, the 18 listeners were postlingually deafened adults, electrode position was measured by computed tomography, and the channel tests were acute, without time to adapt to each map. The 2019 cohort with well-placed precurved arrays was a separate group of listeners.
- In the Schvartz-Leyzac study the seven adults (eight ears) used experimental maps, and going from 20 active electrodes to 8 cost about 2 dB signal-to-noise ratio on average. The authors concluded that adult listeners could benefit from more than eight spectral channels.
- In the Buchman study, the 96 hearing-aid users had a median age of 71 years. In the implanted ear alone, 87 of the 96 improved by more than 15 percentage points. These are the same word test under different listening conditions, so 40.5 is not an implant-alone number.

### PRIMA settings

In the PRIMA design, the camera image is projected at 880 nm near-infrared light at 30 Hz. Brightness is set by pulse duration, from 0.7 to 9.8 ms, at a peak irradiance of 3.5 mW per square millimetre.
