techniques:
  - name: "Sensitized genetic screen"
    blurb: >-
      You weaken one pathway so that it is only just working, then look for mutations that matter
      only against that weak background. A mutation that does nothing in a normal animal can now
      tip the pathway over. Simon and Rubin used it in the fly eye and found ras and Sos.
  - name: "Binding-based cloning and microinjection"
    blurb: >-
      You find a gene by what its protein sticks to, for example a phosphorylated growth-factor receptor, then
      inject the purified protein into resting cells and score whether they copy their DNA. It
      tells you that a protein binds a receptor and that it feeds a pathway that drives growth.
  - name: "Co-purification of protein complexes"
    blurb: >-
      You pull one protein out of a cell extract and see what comes with it. Elion's group
      used it to show that Ste5 copurifies with Ste11, Ste7 and Fus3.
  - name: "Yeast two-hybrid assay"
    blurb: >-
      You fuse two proteins to the two halves of a transcription factor in yeast. If the proteins
      bind each other, the halves come together and switch on a reporter gene. Printen and Sprague
      used it to show that Ste5 interacts with each kinase of the mating cascade.

    choices:

      - "The freed phosphodiesterase destroys the messenger more efficiently everywhere, so the kinase never sees any"

    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The anchoring protein keeps the enzyme that destroys the messenger in the same complex as the
      kinase, so the messenger that matters is the messenger made right beside the kinase, cleared
      before it can spread. Remove the phosphodiesterase from the complex and that clearing no longer
      happens at the kinase, so the local signal is bigger and lasts longer. The choice that the
      kinase can no longer bind the messenger is wrong because the kinase-binding site was left
      intact. The choice that the freed enzyme clears messenger everywhere is wrong because an enzyme
      spread through the whole cell does not give the sharp local drop that anchoring gives. The
      choice that nothing changes is wrong because the anchoring protein's second job, placing the
      clearing enzyme, is the one that was removed.

  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      In the simplest model, a scaffold with n binding sites forms a productive complex only when all n partners occupy
      one molecule. If the scaffold is in large excess over each partner, the partners distribute
      onto different scaffold molecules, and the fraction of fully loaded scaffolds falls toward
      zero. Output is therefore biphasic in scaffold concentration, which Levchenko and colleagues
      called combinatorial inhibition, and scaffold abundance becomes a tuning parameter rather
      than a gain control. Scaffolds are not only tethers. Ste5 binding to Fus3 changes the
      conformation of Fus3 and promotes partial autophosphorylation, which feeds back to lower pathway
      output. A separate Ste5 domain raises the rate at which Ste7 phosphorylates Fus3 but not Kss1,
      and Ste5 is autoinhibited until it reaches the membrane. So scaffold function includes allosteric
      gating and catalytic unlocking, not proximity alone, and separating these takes structural
      and biochemical dissection. Scaffolds with the same function, such as KSR and JIP-1, are
      not descended from Ste5. One open question is how much of the benefit of other scaffolds is
      proximity and how much is allostery.

**A growth-factor receptor can activate Ras without touching it. The bridge is assembly: an adaptor with no enzyme activity, Grb2, brings the exchange factor Sos to Ras.**

> **The one idea.** Binding proteins route signals by controlling which partners meet, where they meet, and when they can act.
>
> *Scope:* this rule describes the adaptor, docking-protein and scaffold examples here. Binding alone may not activate a pathway, not every scaffold lacks enzyme activity, and more scaffold does not always mean more signal.

## Three ways to organize a signal

A **phosphotyrosine** is a tyrosine carrying a phosphate. In a matching local sequence, it can become a docking site for an **SH2 domain**, a folded module that recognizes phosphotyrosine and nearby amino acids.

- An **adaptor** has no enzyme activity and supplies a link between partners. Grb2 is the model.
- A **docking protein** is itself phosphorylated at several sites and supplies a regulated platform for several partners. IRS-1 is the model.
- A **scaffold** organizes several successive members of one route. Ste5 in yeast is the model.

Partner count is only a shortcut: an adaptor typically bridges two partners, and a scaffold holds several, often three. The better test is what the protein supplies. KSR plays a scaffold-like role in mammals, but this pseudokinase changes its partners' shapes and has some kinase activity of its own.

## Grb2 routes a receptor signal to Ras

The first film follows the relay, **receptor phosphotyrosine → Grb2 → Sos → Ras-GTP**. Ras is a small switch protein anchored to the inner face of the plasma membrane, so bringing Sos to the membrane brings it within reach. Sos also has a second Ras-binding site, away from its exchange site; Ras-GTP bound there can speed Sos, so each Ras it activates helps it activate more.

Grb2's two **SH3 domains** grip short proline-rich stretches, here the tail of Sos. Its SH2 domain reads more than the phosphate: nearby amino acids help decide which SH2 domain fits, so two phosphorylated tyrosines need not recruit the same SH2 protein.

In the fly eye, the receptor tyrosine kinase sevenless decides whether one cell in each unit becomes R7. In a sensitized screen, Michael Simon and Gerald Rubin weakened sevenless signaling, then found mutations in fly ras and in Sos, a relative of the yeast Ras activator CDC25. In nematodes, genetics placed the EGF-receptor-like let-23 before let-60 Ras, with sem-5 on the same pathway.

Biochemistry supplied the physical link. In 1992 Eve Lowenstein and colleagues in Joseph Schlessinger's laboratory isolated Grb2, showed that its SH2 domain associates with activated EGF and PDGF receptors, and found that Grb2 plus Ras, but neither alone, drove resting rat fibroblasts to copy DNA. In 1993 several groups, including Tony Pawson's and David Bowtell's, showed that Grb2's SH3 domains bind mammalian Sos and that growth factor recruits the pair to the receptor.

Grb2, fly Drk and worm Sem-5 are orthologs, as are Ras, fly Ras1 and worm let-60; the chain predates the split between those lineages. What follows Ras differs: three of six cells beside the worm gonad become vulval tissue, fly cells adopt R7 fate and fibroblasts copy DNA.

## IRS-1 turns one input into several branches

A docking protein expands the available surface. Insulin receptor substrate 1, **IRS-1**, reaches the membrane through a PH domain that binds membrane lipids and a PTB domain that recognizes a receptor phosphotyrosine motif. The insulin receptor then phosphorylates many tyrosines on IRS-1. The amino acids beside each phosphotyrosine decide which SH2 protein binds there, so different sites recruit PI 3-kinase, Grb2, the phosphatase Shp-2 and the adaptor Nck, sending the signal down several branches at once. Two of them:

> **Grb2 → Sos → Ras**
>
> **PI 3-kinase → PDK1 → AKT**

AKT helps regulate metabolism.

## Ste5 routes shared kinases

The second film shows Ste5 controlling partners, place and activation on the mating route. The second film traced the shared parts to Kss1 and Hog1; of the two branches of the salt-stress cascade, Ste11 feeds only one. Fus3 helps halt the cell cycle for fusion, and without Ste5 a yeast cell cannot mate.

In 1994 Choi, Satterberg, Lyons and Elaine Elion showed that the three kinases bind different regions of the non-enzyme protein Ste5.

Scaffolds are often pictured as passive assembly platforms. A kinase and its target normally meet by random drift through a crowded cytoplasm, so holding both can increase productive encounters. That model is a useful start, but incomplete.

The first specificity idea was sequestration: a pathway-dedicated scaffold keeps kinase modules from talking to each other. Wendell Lim's laboratory later found that Ste5 does not insulate by locking its kinases away; membrane gating and Fus3 unlocking do the work. Restraint also runs the other way: Hog1 helps keep high salt from activating the mating route.

Place has clearer cases elsewhere. InaD uses **PDZ domains**, modules that grip the last few amino acids of a partner, to gather light-response proteins in a fly photoreceptor. Paxillin gathers Ras-pathway proteins at focal adhesions, where a cell grips its surroundings.

### More scaffold can mean less signal

The second film's excess-scaffold result comes from a model. Levchenko, Bruck and Sternberg modeled a generic scaffolded kinase cascade in 2000, found that output rose and then fell as scaffold increased, and called the effect **combinatorial inhibition**. The model assumes fixed kinase amounts and signaling only when all three share one scaffold; cooperative assembly or other regulation can change the result.

**Predict.** A Ste5 fragment promotes Fus3 autophosphorylation at one of its two activating sites. Must that raise the pathway's final output?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The network effect must be measured. In the 2006 experiment from Wendell Lim's laboratory, Fus3 made this way was only partly active and lowered pathway output, so Ste5 tuned the signal rather than simply accelerating it.

</details>

Ste5 has no human counterpart. KSR in the Ras–MAPK pathway and JIP-1 organize cascade kinases comparably but are not descended from Ste5; sequence similarity to Ste5 would not have found them.

## Local signals and rewiring

A kinase reads a messenger, and an enzyme destroys it. What holds the two close enough for a local pool of messenger to exist? An anchoring protein. In John Scott's laboratory, Dodge and colleagues showed that muscle-selective A-kinase anchoring protein, **mAKAP**, holds protein kinase A and PDE4, a phosphodiesterase that destroys cyclic AMP. The kinase therefore responds especially to cyclic AMP made nearby, before it spreads away.

Separate binding surfaces also make scaffolds rewritable: swap one surface for another that recognizes a different partner, and an input can reach a new output. Wendell Lim's laboratory has used that logic to build signaling proteins no organism has had.

### Design problem

Two pathways share kinase K. Input A must give only output A, and input B only output B. Design two ways to keep the messages apart.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Separate assemblies.** Scaffold A holds K with A's activator and target; scaffold B holds K with B's partners. This is the original sequestration proposal for Ste5; a stronger design also gates each scaffold by its input and helps activate the final kinase. Its weak point is dose: excess scaffold can split partners across incomplete complexes.

**Separate places.** Anchor K at two locations. Input A makes the messenger at site A, where K sits beside target A; input B makes it at site B, beside target B. Each anchor also holds an enzyme that clears the messenger before it reaches the other site. This is the mAKAP logic. Its weak point is local clearance: remove the clearing enzyme and the signal can grow and spread.

Other answers can work. A good design states what enforces specificity and what failure would break it.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Discovery order

The fly screen appeared in November 1991; Utpal Banerjee's laboratory had isolated Sos earlier that year. The worm ordering came from Paul Sternberg's group; Stuart Clark, Michael Stern and Robert Horvitz cloned sem-5 in March 1992, and Lowenstein's Grb2 paper linked Grb2 to it in August. Across about nine months, a fly screen, worm clone and rat-cell experiment converged on one receptor-to-Ras chain.

The 1994 Ste5 result surprised because a protein essential for mating appeared to catalyze nothing. Elion's group combined two-hybrid mapping with co-purification; Michael Wigler's group (PNAS, 2 August) and George Sprague's (Genetics, November) reached the same result with the then-new yeast two-hybrid method. Marcus and colleagues proposed that a dedicated scaffold might sequester a kinase module.

### A debated cardiac target

The mAKAP complex also holds a target. Marx and colleagues reported that protein kinase A phosphorylates the cardiac ryanodine receptor, the calcium-release channel for each heartbeat, causing FKBP12.6 to leave. They also reported defective regulation in failing hearts. Both findings remain debated, and some groups have not reproduced FKBP12.6 loss.
