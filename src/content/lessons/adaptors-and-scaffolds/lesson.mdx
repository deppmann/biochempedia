techniques:
  - name: "Sensitized genetic screen"
    blurb: >-
      You weaken one pathway so that it is only just working, then look for mutations that matter
      only against that weak background. A mutation that does nothing in a normal animal can now
      tip the pathway over. Simon and Rubin used it in the fly eye and found ras and Sos.
  - name: "Binding-based cloning and microinjection"
    blurb: >-
      You find a gene by what its protein sticks to, for example a phosphorylated growth-factor receptor, then
      inject the purified protein into resting cells and score whether they copy their DNA. It
      tells you that a protein binds a receptor and that it feeds a pathway that drives growth.
  - name: "Co-purification of protein complexes"
    blurb: >-
      You pull one protein out of a cell extract and see what comes with it. Elion's group
      used it to show that Ste5 copurifies with Ste11, Ste7 and Fus3.
  - name: "Yeast two-hybrid assay"
    blurb: >-
      You fuse two proteins to the two halves of a transcription factor in yeast. If the proteins
      bind each other, the halves come together and switch on a reporter gene. Printen and Sprague
      used it to show that Ste5 interacts with each kinase of the mating cascade.

**Ligand activates receptor... now what? A growth-factor receptor can switch on Ras without ever touching it, and the proteins that make that possible have no enzyme activity at all.**

Ras is a small switch protein anchored to the inner face of the plasma membrane. When a growth factor binds its receptor, the receptor adds phosphates to tyrosines on its own tail. A phosphate on a tail is a message written where nothing can read it yet. The cell needs a coordinated message passed into the cytoplasm, and it gets one by recruiting proteins whose job is to put the right partners next to each other. This lesson sorts those proteins into three kinds, then asks what each kind buys the cell.

## Zoomed out: the receptor recruits helpers

Each phosphotyrosine (a tyrosine carrying a phosphate) is a handhold for proteins with an SH2 domain, a small folded module that grips a phosphorylated tyrosine. Some of the proteins that arrive are enzymes. Others have no enzyme activity and only decide who stands next to whom. Three kinds of those are worth telling apart.

- **Adaptor.** Links two partners and has no enzyme activity. Grb2 is the model.
- **Docking protein.** A platform at the membrane that the receptor loads with many phosphotyrosines, each a landing site for a different partner. IRS-1 is the model.
- **Scaffold.** Holds three or more members of one pathway on a single protein. Ste5 in yeast and KSR in mammals are the models.

The number of partners is a quick test: an adaptor typically bridges two, a scaffold holds several. A docking protein is the one that gets phosphorylated and so supplies the sites itself.

## The adaptor: Grb2 and "son of sevenless"

In August 1992, Eve Lowenstein and colleagues in Joseph Schlessinger's laboratory reported a protein with no catalytic machinery: one SH2 domain, two SH3 domains, nothing else. (An SH3 domain is another small module. It grips short proline-rich stretches.) They isolated its gene, found that it sticks by its SH2 domain to the activated receptors for epidermal growth factor and platelet-derived growth factor, and called it growth factor receptor-bound protein 2, or Grb2.

Here is how Grb2 does its job. Its SH2 domain clamps a phosphotyrosine on the receptor tail. Its two SH3 domains already hold a protein called Sos, with or without any phosphate. Sos has one task: it is an exchange factor that swaps the GDP on Ras for GTP, which flips Ras from off to on. The receptor holds Grb2, Grb2 holds Sos, and Sos now stands at the membrane where Ras is. Position does the rest.

> **receptor phosphotyrosine → Grb2 (SH2 on the receptor, SH3 on Sos) → Sos → Ras-GDP → Ras-GTP**

In resting rat fibroblasts, injected Grb2 alone did nothing, and injected Ras protein alone did nothing. Injected together, they sent the cells into copying their DNA. That is biochemistry. The same chain turned up in two animals by an entirely different route, genetics.

In the compound eye of a fly, a receptor tyrosine kinase called sevenless decides whether one cell in each unit becomes R7, the seventh light-sensing cell. Michael Simon, in Gerald Rubin's laboratory, ran a sensitized screen: weaken sevenless signaling first, then look for mutations that matter only against the weakened background. Out came alleles in a fly ras gene and in a gene resembling CDC25, the yeast protein that switches Ras on (Cell, November 1991). That gene is Sos, for son of sevenless.

In the nematode, Paul Sternberg's group had placed let-23, a gene that looks like the EGF receptor, ahead of let-60, which turned out to be Ras. Stuart Clark, Michael Stern and Robert Horvitz cloned another worm gene on the same pathway, sem-5, in March 1992. Lowenstein's abstract linked Grb2 to it.

So a fly screen, a worm clone and a rat-cell experiment, spread over nine months, landed on one chain. Grb2, the fly's Drk and the worm's Sem-5 are orthologs: one ancestral gene carried down three lineages. Ras, fly Ras1 and worm let-60 are orthologs too. The chain was sitting in animal cells before those lineages parted. What differs is what each animal does after Ras. In three of the six cells beside a larva's gonad it builds a vulva. In a resting fibroblast it tells the cell to copy its DNA.

## Docking proteins: one platform, many branches

Pathways branch through helper proteins like these, and the cleanest case is a docking protein. A receptor tail has only so much room. A docking protein solves that by being a second tail.

IRS-1 is the example. Insulin binds its receptor, and the receptor phosphorylates IRS-1. IRS-1 reaches the membrane with two modules of its own: a PH domain, which grips membrane lipids, and a PTB domain, which grips a phosphotyrosine motif on the activated receptor. Once it is phosphorylated at many tyrosines, it recruits PI 3-kinase, Grb2, the phosphatase Shp-2 and the adaptor Nck.

The few amino acids beside each phosphotyrosine decide which SH2 protein binds there. One loaded platform therefore sends the signal down several branches at once. Two of them:

> **Grb2 → Sos → Ras**
>
> **PI 3-kinase → PDK1 → AKT**

The first is the adaptor chain you just saw, hung from IRS-1 instead of from the receptor itself. The second ends in AKT, which regulates metabolism. Grb2 sits between two partners. IRS-1 is the thing that is phosphorylated, and it supplies the sites in return.

## Scaffolds: what a cell gets from holding a pathway together

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Elaine Elion's laboratory at Harvard Medical School was working out which parts of a yeast protein three enzymes stuck to. The protein was Ste5. Without it a yeast cell cannot mate. Yet the protein had no enzyme activity: it never put a phosphate on anything or took one off. In 1994 that came close to saying it had no mechanism.

Mating runs on three kinases in a row. Ste11 switches on Ste7, and Ste7 switches on Fus3, which halts the cell cycle so that the cell can fuse with a partner. The top one is shared, and the middle one is shared with the filament cascade. Ste11 also starts the cascade a yeast cell runs when the salt outside climbs too high, and the one it runs to grow in long filaments. One enzyme, three destinations, and a cell that smells pheromone mates and does nothing else.

Choi, Satterberg, Lyons and Elion reported in Cell in 1994 that Ste11, Ste7 and Fus3 bind different regions of Ste5, so one Ste5 molecule can hold all three at once. Two other laboratories reached the same result that year, both with the then-new yeast two-hybrid method: Michael Wigler's group (PNAS, 2 August) and George Sprague's (Genetics, November). A protein that catalyzes nothing earns its place by what it buys the cell.

**Speed.** Picture a pegboard with three tools hung within arm's reach. A kinase and its target normally find each other by drifting at random through a crowded cytoplasm. Two proteins fastened to one scaffold are already touching.

**Insulation.** The Ste11 that a pheromone switches on is sitting against Fus3, nowhere near the kinase that would tell the cell it was drying out. A pathway-dedicated scaffold lets the same kinase serve different pathways, which Marcus and colleagues proposed in 1994 is how scaffolds inhibit cross-talk between kinase modules.

**Location.** Scaffolds carry their kinases to a place. InaD, built from PDZ domains (modules that grip the last few amino acids of a partner), gathers the light-response proteins of the fly photoreceptor in one spot. Paxillin collects Ras-pathway proteins at focal adhesions, the places where a cell grips its surroundings.

Scaffolds are often pictured as passive assembly platforms. In 2000, Levchenko, Bruck and Sternberg modeled a kinase cascade with a generic scaffold and showed that adding more scaffold does not keep raising the output. Past a certain amount, extra scaffold pulls the kinases onto separate scaffold molecules and the signal falls again. They proposed the name combinatorial inhibition for this effect. Then in 2006 Wendell Lim's group showed that Ste5 is more than a tether: a Ste5 fragment bound to Fus3 drives Fus3 to put a phosphate on itself at one of its two activating sites.

Ste5 has no counterpart in humans. KSR, the scaffold of the Ras-MAPK pathway, and JIP-1 hold cascade kinases together in a comparable way but are not descended from it, so a search of the human genome for something that resembles STE5 would never have found them.

## Place, and rewiring: scaffolds beyond the cascade

Here is a question about heart cells. A kinase reads a messenger, and an enzyme destroys that messenger. What holds the two close enough together for a local pool of messenger to exist?

The answer is a protein whose only job is to hold other proteins next to each other, an anchoring protein. In John Scott's laboratory, Dodge and colleagues showed that mAKAP, the muscle-selective A-kinase anchoring protein, also holds a phosphodiesterase, PDE4, which destroys the messenger cyclic AMP. The kinase and its off-switch sit in the same complex, so the cyclic AMP that matters to this kinase is the cyclic AMP made right beside it, before it can move away. The complex also holds a target. Marx and colleagues reported that protein kinase A adds a phosphate to the cardiac ryanodine receptor, the channel that releases calcium for each heartbeat, and that the phosphate makes a protein called FKBP12.6 come off the channel. They also reported that this regulation is defective in failing hearts. Both findings are still debated, and some groups have not reproduced the loss of FKBP12.6.

Because a scaffold is a string of separate gripping surfaces, you can swap one surface for another that grips a different partner. Then a different input can reach a different output. You can hack scaffolds to mix inputs and outputs, and Lim's laboratory has spent much of its time since building signaling proteins that no organism has had.

### Design problem

Two pathways share one kinase, K. Input A must give output A only, and input B output B only. Design two different ways to keep the messages apart.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Design 1: separate scaffolds.** Give each pathway its own scaffold. Scaffold A holds one copy of K, the activator that input A turns on, and the target that produces output A. Scaffold B holds another copy of K with B's partners. The K that input A switches on is sitting against A's target and nowhere near B's. This is the Ste5 logic. Its weak point is dose: too much scaffold splits every complex into pieces, so the amount has to be tuned.

**Design 2: separate places.** Anchor K at two sites with two anchoring proteins. Input A makes the messenger at site A and input B makes it at site B. At each site K sits beside its own target and beside an enzyme that destroys the messenger, so the messenger from the other site is cleared before this kinase meets it. This is the mAKAP logic. Its weak point is the anchor: lose the enzyme from a complex and local control goes with it.

Other answers work. A good one says what keeps the messages apart and what breaks when a part goes missing.
