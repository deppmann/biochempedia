  How do you pull one protein out of a soup of ten thousand others — and then
  prove you got it? You need a way to find it (an assay), a way to enrich it

  sequence. This lesson follows a single enzyme from crude homogenate through four purification steps,
  watches its specific activity climb three-thousandfold, and ends with the
  methods — SDS-PAGE, immunoassays, Edman degradation, mass spectrometry — that
  turn a purified band into an identity.

techniques:
  - name: "The enzyme assay"
    blurb: >-
      You cannot purify what you cannot find. An assay reports how much of your
      target is present at each step — for lactate dehydrogenase, the NADH coenzyme
      it consumes absorbs at 340 nm, so a spectrophotometer reads activity straight
      off the falling line.
  - name: "Specific activity and the purification table"
    blurb: >-
      Specific activity = enzyme units divided by total protein (units/mg). It
      rises every time a step removes contaminant faster than it loses your target,
      so the column of specific-activity numbers — not the raw activity — tells you
      whether the purification is actually working.
  - name: "Peptide mass fingerprinting"
    blurb: >-
      Cut a protein with trypsin into a predictable set of peptides, measure their
      masses by MALDI-TOF, and match that list against the in-silico (computer-predicted) digest of every
      protein in a database. The pattern of peptide masses identifies the protein
      the way a fingerprint identifies a person.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "A crude lysate goes through three purification steps. The specific activity (units/mg) at each stage is: crude lysate = 12, ammonium sulfate cut = 40, ion exchange = 220, affinity column = 4,800. What is the overall fold-purification?"
    choices:
      - "120-fold"
      - "400-fold"
      - "40-fold"
      - "4,800-fold"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Fold-purification = final specific activity ÷ initial specific activity =
      4,800 ÷ 12 = 400-fold. The 120-fold option divides the final value by an
      intermediate step rather than the starting one; the 4,800-fold option mistakes
      the final specific activity itself for the fold; the 40-fold option mistakes an
      intermediate specific activity for the fold. Specific activity is the success metric because it tracks the
      proportion of target in total protein, independent of how much sample remains.

  - stem: "A protein mixture is loaded onto a gel-filtration (size-exclusion) column. Which protein elutes FIRST?"
    choices:
      - "The smallest protein, because it migrates fastest"
      - "The protein with the lowest pI, because it is most negatively charged"
      - "The largest protein, because it is excluded from the bead pores"
      - "The protein that binds the bead ligand most tightly"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Gel filtration separates by size in a counterintuitive way: large proteins are
      too big to enter the porous beads, so they can use only the liquid between the
      beads, and less buffer washes them through: they elute first. Small proteins
      also explore the liquid inside every bead, need more buffer, and elute last. Charge is irrelevant
      to size exclusion, and tight ligand binding describes affinity
      chromatography.

  - stem: "An enzyme has a pI of 5.0. You want it to bind an ion-exchange column at a running buffer pH of 8.0. Which resin, and why?"
    choices:
      - "A cation exchanger, because the protein is positively charged at pH 8.0"
      - "A cation exchanger, because pH 8.0 is above the pI"
      - "Either resin works, because pI does not affect binding"
      - "An anion exchanger, because the protein is negatively charged at pH 8.0"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      When the buffer pH (8.0) is above the protein's pI (5.0), the protein carries a
      net negative charge, and negative proteins bind a positively charged
      anion-exchange resin. The rule: pH > pI → protein negative → anion exchanger;
      pH < pI → protein positive → cation exchanger. Both cation-exchanger options
      reverse the charge logic, and "either resin works" ignores the pH/pI
      relationship that governs binding.

  - stem: "In SDS-PAGE, why do proteins separate according to mass alone rather than by their intrinsic charge or shape?"
    choices:
      - "SDS adds a uniform negative charge roughly proportional to chain length and denatures the protein, giving a constant charge-to-mass ratio"
      - "SDS removes all charged side chains so only the backbone mass remains"
      - "The polyacrylamide gel binds proteins by their pI"
      - "Heating in SDS converts all proteins to the same molecular weight"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      SDS is an anionic detergent that denatures proteins into linear chains and
      coats them at roughly one SDS per two residues, adding negative charge in
      proportion to length. This swamps intrinsic charge and gives nearly the same
      charge-to-mass ratio for every protein, so the only variable affecting
      migration through the sieving gel is size — smaller proteins move faster.
      SDS does not remove charged side chains, the gel does not bind proteins by pI
      (separation by pI is isoelectric focusing, which uses a pH gradient), and heating does not convert proteins to a
      single molecular weight.

  - stem: "A 2D gel is run on a cell lysate before and after drug treatment. Afterward, one spot shifts to a more acidic position (lower pI) but stays at the same molecular weight. The most likely explanation is:"
    choices:
      - "The protein was cleaved into a smaller fragment"
      - "The protein was completely degraded"
      - "The gel was run at the wrong amperage"
      - "The protein underwent a post-translational modification such as phosphorylation"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      In 2D gels the first dimension separates by pI (isoelectric focusing) and the
      second by mass (SDS-PAGE). A spot that moves to a lower pI with no change in
      apparent mass signals the addition of negatively charged groups — classically
      phosphorylation, which adds phosphate. Cleavage or degradation would change
      the apparent mass, and a running artifact like wrong amperage would not
      produce a specific, reproducible pI shift for one protein.

mcatQuestions:
  - stem: "A researcher purifying lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) tracks activity by monitoring pyruvate + NADH → lactate + NAD⁺. Which spectrophotometric measurement gives the cleanest readout of LDH activity?"
    choices:
      - "Increase in absorbance at 340 nm as NAD⁺ is reduced to NADH"
      - "Decrease in absorbance at 340 nm as NADH is oxidized to NAD⁺"
      - "Increase in absorbance at 280 nm from aromatic residues"
      - "Decrease in absorbance at 260 nm from nucleotide loss"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      NADH absorbs strongly at 340 nm whereas NAD⁺ does not. In the forward reaction
      (pyruvate + NADH → lactate + NAD⁺), NADH is consumed, so absorbance at 340 nm
      decreases over time — that drop is the direct kinetic readout of LDH activity.
      A rise at 340 nm describes the reverse direction. Absorbance at 280 nm
      reports total protein, not turnover, and 260 nm is not a specific
      NADH/NAD⁺ discriminator.

  - stem: "In the classic HIV testing algorithm, a clinical lab screened serum with an ELISA, then ran a western blot on positives. What made the western blot a useful CONFIRMATORY test after the ELISA screen?"
    choices:
      - "It quantifies antibody concentration more precisely than ELISA"
      - "It uses no antibodies and therefore avoids cross-reactivity"
      - "It separates viral antigens by size before antibody detection, so reactivity can be checked against specific proteins of expected molecular weight"
      - "It detects nucleic acid rather than protein, eliminating false positives"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The western blot runs antigens on SDS-PAGE (separation by mass), transfers them
      to a membrane, and probes with the patient's antibodies plus an
      enzyme-conjugated secondary antibody. Because reactive bands appear at the
      expected molecular weights of specific viral proteins, a true positive must
      light up bands at defined sizes — the size-resolved specificity that makes it
      confirmatory after a sensitive but less specific screen. Claiming better
      quantification reverses the strengths (ELISA is the quantitative workhorse),
      the antibody-free claim is false (western blots rely on antibodies), and
      detecting nucleic acid describes a different kind of test.

  - stem: "Edman degradation removes and identifies one residue per cycle from the N-terminus at about 98% efficiency per cycle. Roughly what fraction of correctly phased peptide remains after 50 cycles, and what limitation does this illustrate?"
    choices:
      - "About 36%, illustrating why Edman degradation is practically limited to roughly 50 residues before signal degrades"
      - "About 98%, illustrating that efficiency stays nearly constant regardless of length"
      - "About 90%, illustrating that the method works well for proteins of any size"
      - "About 2%, illustrating that no peptide longer than 10 residues can be sequenced"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Per-cycle yield compounds multiplicatively: 0.98⁵⁰ ≈ 0.36, about 36%. As signal
      from correctly phased chains falls and out-of-phase background accumulates,
      residue calls become ambiguous — which is why Edman degradation is capped near
      50 residues and longer proteins must be cleaved into peptides (e.g., by trypsin
      and chymotrypsin) and read in overlapping fragments. The 98% answer ignores
      compounding, 90% overestimates retained signal, and about 2% (the value near
      cycle 200) overstates the limitation.

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm 5"
    text: |
      Imagine a giant bag of mixed candy and you only want the red ones. First you
      find a way to spot the red ones (that's the test). Then you do tricks to throw
      away the other colors a little at a time: shake the bag so heavy candies fall to
      the bottom, pour everything through a sieve that catches big pieces, use a magnet
      that only grabs red ones. After a few tricks, almost everything left is red. Then
      you check the red candies very carefully to make sure they really are the kind
      you wanted. Scientists do exactly this, but with the tiny machines called
      proteins that live inside your cells.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: |
      A cell is full of thousands of different proteins, and biochemists often need
      just one of them, pure. The catch is you can't see proteins, so first you need
      an assay — a test that tells you how much of your target is in a tube. For an
      enzyme, you can watch the reaction it runs.

      Then you separate. Spinning samples very fast (centrifugation) sorts cell parts
      by size and density. Adding salt and running columns (chromatography) sort proteins by
      other traits: how soluble they are, how big they are, what charge they carry, or
      what they like to stick to. Each step throws away contaminants and keeps your protein, so the
      share of the sample that is your protein keeps going up, and its specific
      activity (activity per milligram of protein) rises with it.

      Finally you prove what you have. A gel (SDS-PAGE) sorts proteins by size into
      bands. Antibodies can grab a specific protein for a western blot or a pregnancy
      test. And mass spectrometry can weigh the protein precisely enough to identify
      it and even read its sequence.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: |
      Protein purification is hypothesis-driven and assay-anchored. The assay (e.g.,
      LDH oxidizing NADH, tracked at 340 nm) lets you compute specific activity =
      activity units / mg total protein. Each step multiplies specific activity by
      the fraction of activity it kept divided by the fraction of protein it kept,
      so a successful step raises it.

      The workflow: disrupt cells, then use differential centrifugation (increasing g
      pellets progressively smaller structures; final supernatant = cytosol). Then
      separation on four principles — salting out (ammonium sulfate; solubility),
      gel filtration (size; large proteins elute first because they're excluded from
      bead pores), ion exchange (charge; pH < pI → cation exchange, pH > pI → anion
      exchange), and affinity (a ligand the target binds selectively; elute with a competitor
      or a pH change). HPLC applies high pressure and fine beads for resolution. A purification
      table tracks total protein↓, total activity↓, specific activity↑, yield↓, and
      fold-purification↑.

      Characterization: SDS-PAGE separates by mass (uniform charge/mass from SDS); IEF
      separates by pI; 2D gels combine both. Antibody methods (immunoprecipitation,
      western blot, ELISA) detect specific proteins, crucial when there's no enzymatic
      assay. Sequence comes from Edman degradation (N-terminal, ~50-residue limit) or,
      more powerfully, tandem MS, which reads peptide sequence from fragment ions.
      Peptide mass fingerprinting (MALDI-TOF masses matched against database
      digests) identifies a known protein without reading its sequence.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: |
      The conceptual spine is the specific-activity ledger: SA = activity/[total
      protein], and a valid step satisfies SA_(n+1)/SA_n > 1 even as total activity
      declines, because purification is enrichment, not preservation. Yield (total activity retained ÷
      starting total activity) and fold-purification (SA_n/SA_0) are the trade-off you optimize; the
      simulator's five-row table runs SA 10→30→90→1,100→30,000 (3,000-fold) at ~36%
      yield, with affinity supplying the largest single jump.

      Separation principles map to orthogonal physical parameters, and orthogonality is
      the design goal: solubility (Hofmeister salting-out, where (NH₄)₂SO₄ reduces water
      available to solvate surface residues), hydrodynamic radius (SEC, where the
      partition coefficient Kav governs elution and exclusion inverts the intuitive size
      order), net charge as a function of pH relative to pI (IEX, eluted by
      ionic-strength or pH gradient), and specific binding (affinity, eluted by
      competitive displacement or a change in pH). SDS-PAGE collapses charge heterogeneity
      via constant SDS:residue stoichiometry, isolating mass; IEF resolves on pI; the 2D
      combination yields proteome-scale spot maps where a Δposition flags PTMs (e.g.,
      phosphorylation shifting pI).

      Sequence determination moved from Edman chemistry (PITC coupling, cyclization,
      ~98%/cycle efficiency capping practical reads near 50 residues) to MS-centric
      proteomics: MALDI-TOF for peptide mass fingerprinting (tryptic masses matched to
      in-silico digests) and CID-based MS/MS, where b/y ion series reconstruct sequence
      de novo from inter-peak mass differences. The historical compression — Sanger's
      12-year insulin sequence versus a single instrument run today — is the practical measure of how
      thoroughly these methods reorganized the discipline.

## Find it: the assay and the unit

First you need an **assay**: a way to detect your protein in a tube where you cannot see it. For an enzyme, the assay is usually its own reaction. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) converts pyruvate to lactate, consuming the coenzyme NADH. NADH absorbs light at 340 nm and its oxidized form, NAD⁺, does not, so the absorbance falls, and the slope of that fall is the activity.

A slope is a rate. To turn a rate into an amount of enzyme, biochemists count in **units**. One unit is the amount of enzyme that converts 1 µmol of substrate per minute, under conditions you state (temperature, pH, substrate concentration). A unit counts catalytic work. It is not a mass.

## Enrich it: one property per row

The film's selector wheel previews the four properties below. Step zero: break the cells open into a homogenate, then spin; what sinks is the pellet, and the liquid above it is the supernatant. **Differential centrifugation** uses force as a sieve. A gentle spin drops the heavy nuclei; crank it harder and mitochondria pellet; harder still and the smallest vesicles go down, leaving cytosol in the final supernatant. Each spin trades one mixture for two cleaner ones.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/protein-purification-techniques/differential-centrifugation-series.webp" width={1200} height={573} alt="AI-generated illustration: four centrifuge tubes in a row, a cloudy homogenate and then three successive spins of increasing force, each leaving a pellet enriched in nuclei, mitochondria or small vesicles, with the supernatant carried on to the next tube and a clear cytosol supernatant in the last tube." caption="Sedimentation depends on size and density. After each spin the supernatant moves to a fresh tube and the pellet is set aside. The fractions are enriched, not pure." />

What centrifugation cannot finish, you finish with four properties, one at a time: the first by precipitation, the other three on **chromatography** columns.

### Solubility: salting out

Add ammonium sulfate and proteins begin to drop out of solution, but not all at once. Different proteins precipitate at different salt concentrations, so a carefully chosen cut keeps most of your enzyme and leaves many contaminants behind. A round of dialysis through a semi-permeable membrane then washes the salt back out, because the next column, ion exchange, needs low salt to bind the protein.

### Charge: ion exchange

An ion-exchange column carries fixed charges on its beads, and opposite charges stick. The rule compares the buffer pH with the protein's **pI**, the pH at which its net charge is zero. At a pH below its pI, a protein is net positive; above its pI, it is net negative. Raise the salt or shift the pH and the bound protein lets go.

The names are the trap. A resin with negative charges holds and swaps positive ions, cations, so it is a **cation exchanger**. A resin with positive charges is an **anion exchanger**. Name the resin by what it catches, not by its own charge.

Suppose the enzyme has a pI of 5.0 and the column runs at pH 8.0. The buffer is above the pI, so the enzyme is net negative and sticks to a positive resin, an anion exchanger.

**Predict.** Which resin would you use for the same enzyme at pH 4.0?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A cation exchanger. At pH 4.0 the buffer is below the pI of 5.0, so the enzyme is net positive and sticks to a negative resin, which catches cations.

</details>

### Size: gel filtration

Gel filtration runs backward from intuition. The column is packed with porous beads. A large protein cannot enter the pores, so it can use only the liquid between the beads, and less buffer washes it through: it **elutes** (comes off the column) first. A small protein also explores the liquid inside every bead, so it takes more buffer to come off and elutes last.

### Binding: affinity

Decorate the beads with something your protein binds tightly and selectively (a substrate analog, a cofactor, an antibody, a metal that grips a His-tag, a run of histidines added to the protein), and your target binds while everything else flows through. Then release it, often with a flood of free ligand or a competitor, sometimes with a change in pH. Affinity is usually the most selective step. Its limits: you need a ligand your protein binds, and capacity and cost restrict how much you can load.

### Which step goes first?

A property tells you which resin to choose; load and volume set the order. Early steps must handle large volumes and a lot of protein, as salting out and ion exchange can, and as affinity capture can when you have a tag or ligand and enough resin; a His-tag or a known ligand makes affinity capture a natural first step. Gel filtration resolves well only when the load is a small fraction of the column volume, so it usually comes late, polishing a small, concentrated sample. The table's numbers follow a classic textbook example, which runs gel filtration before affinity; its 12-fold gel-filtration step is generous, since size exclusion usually gives a few-fold. ## Know when to stop

Two signs, taken together, suggest the protein is pure: specific activity stops rising when you add another step, and a gel shows one band at the right size (or one per kind of subunit, in fixed proportion, for a protein built from different chains).

The gel is a check, not a purification step: a small sample run on **SDS-PAGE**, which sorts chains by size (next section), is sacrificed; the rest of your prep is untouched. The band pattern is needed but not enough: two proteins of the same size run together, and a contaminant can sit below what the stain detects.

**Predict.** You add a sixth step. Specific activity stays at 30,000 units/mg and the yield falls. What do you conclude?

Most likely the enzyme was already pure: the step kept the same fraction of activity as of protein, a ratio of 1, and cost enzyme for nothing. Or the column sorts on a property where the leftover contaminants look like your enzyme. The flat number cannot tell these apart; the gel can help.

## Prove it: three questions

A pure protein still needs an identity. Ask three questions.

**What size is it?** Use SDS-PAGE. The detergent SDS unfolds the chain and coats it at roughly one molecule per two residues, giving nearly every protein the same charge-to-mass ratio. Proteins then sort by size alone, the small ones running fastest, and you read the size against a ladder of marker proteins (proteins of known size run in a lane alongside). (Some proteins run at the wrong size; see Going deeper.)

**Is it the protein I think it is?** One route uses antibodies, which are also how you track a protein with no reaction to assay, such as a hormone receptor. Transfer an SDS-PAGE gel to a membrane, probe it with antibodies, and light up one band with a light-producing reaction (chemiluminescence): the **western blot**.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/protein-purification-techniques/western-blot-three-steps.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: three panels. The membrane is an exact copy of the gel. A validated antibody supports identity, and the band's position against the size markers estimates its size." />

The other route uses mass. Cut your protein with trypsin, weigh the pieces by **MALDI-TOF** mass spectrometry, and match the mass list against the computer-predicted digest of every protein in a database: a **peptide mass fingerprint**. MALDI-TOF sends ionized peptides down a flight tube where heavier ones arrive later (for ions of the same charge, which in MALDI is usually +1), weighing them to a fraction of a dalton. Few proteins give the same set of masses, so the fingerprint identifies a known protein without reading its sequence.

**What is its sequence?** **Edman degradation** clips off the N-terminal residue, identifies it, and repeats. But each cycle is only about 98% efficient.

**Predict.** After fifty cycles, roughly what fraction of the chains are still in step?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

About a third: 0.98⁵⁰ ≈ 0.36. The losses multiply, just as yields do in the purification table. Signal from chains still in step falls, out-of-step background builds up, and the method stalls past roughly fifty residues. So long proteins are cut into overlapping peptides and read in pieces.

</details>

The modern answer is **tandem MS**. Fragment one peptide inside the mass spectrometer into a ladder of pieces, each one residue shorter than the next, and read its sequence from the spacing between peaks, since each gap matches the mass of one amino acid (with some blind spots, in Going deeper). Frederick Sanger spent twelve years reading the 51 amino acids of insulin by hand; today mass spectrometry can identify a protein from its peptides in a single run.

## What each method answers

The ledger says whether your protein is there, how pure it is, whether each step helped and how much you kept. The gel checks chain size and gives a first look at purity. Antibodies, masses and sequence say which protein it is.

## Going deeper

### The proteome in numbers

A single human cell holds billions of protein molecules, drawn from only about ten thousand distinct species. Across the body, alternative splicing and post-translational modification expand the roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes into a far larger set of protein forms; estimates run into the millions, but no one has counted them.

### When the ledger lies

Specific activity reads as purity only while activity per milligram of enzyme stays constant. A step that inactivates some enzyme makes specific activity understate the enrichment. A step that removes an inhibitor or adds an activator can raise specific activity with no purification, and can raise total activity too, giving a "yield" above 100%. Either way, the ledger is reporting a change in activity per milligram of enzyme, not a change in purity.

### Planning the order

Purifications are often planned as capture, intermediate purification and polishing. Sort on a different property at each stage: after a charge step, the contaminants left are the ones whose charge resembles your enzyme's at that pH, so repeating the same charge step buys little (a charge step at another pH, or on the opposite resin, can still help), while a size step can still separate them (if size and charge are not linked among them). Each step should also leave the sample in a buffer the next can take: hydrophobic interaction suits a high-salt sample straight from an ammonium sulfate cut, while ion exchange needs low salt. **HPLC** (high-performance liquid chromatography) speeds any of these columns up: fine beads under high pressure, paid for with expensive plumbing.

### Isoelectric focusing and 2D gels

**Isoelectric focusing** sorts by pI: proteins drift through a pH gradient until each stalls where its net charge is zero. Run focusing first, then SDS-PAGE at a right angle, and you get a **2D gel**, with proteins as spots placed by both pI and mass. Compare a healthy proteome to a tumor's, and a spot that shifts or brightens is a candidate biomarker. A spot that moves to a more acidic pI at the same mass suggests a modification that adds negative charge, classically phosphorylation.

### More antibody methods

An antibody-coated bead pulls its target out of a mixture (**immunoprecipitation**). Coat a plate instead and you have an **ELISA**, the clinic's workhorse, with concentration read off a standard curve; in the classic HIV algorithm, a western blot confirmed a positive ELISA screen. The same antibody-sandwich logic in a lateral-flow strip runs the pregnancy test and the COVID rapid test.

### When SDS-PAGE misleads

The charge-to-mass ratio is nearly, not exactly, the same for every protein. The one-SDS-per-two-residues figure holds: about 1.4 g of SDS binds each gram of protein, which at 110 g/mol per residue and 288 g/mol for SDS is about 0.53 SDS per residue. Glycoproteins, very basic proteins and membrane proteins can run anomalously, and disulfide bonds must be reduced for each chain to run at its own size.

### Inside the mass spectrometer

MALDI embeds the sample in a matrix and hits it with a laser pulse; the matrix absorbs the energy and launches the ions into the flight tube. Tandem MS has well-known blind spots: leucine and isoleucine have identical mass (113.08 Da); lysine and glutamine differ by only 0.04 Da, which only high-resolution instruments separate; and some single residues weigh the same as pairs (asparagine and Gly-Gly, glutamine and Ala-Gly). So MS/MS alone cannot always tell them apart.

### Edman's blind spot

Edman degradation needs a free N-terminus. Many eukaryotic proteins, including most human ones, are acetylated at the N-terminus, which blocks Edman on the intact chain; internal peptides from a digest can still be read.
