      Bacteria offer few visible features to classify them by: a shape, a stain reaction, a way of
      making a living. Woese, at the University of Illinois, looked instead for a molecule that
      every cell carries and that does the same job in each, so that it could be compared across

      they did not fall among the typical bacteria they resemble. In 1977 Woese and George Fox
      proposed that cellular life holds three primary kingdoms and called the new one
      archaebacteria. In 1990 Woese, Otto Kandler and Mark Wheelis proposed renaming the three as
      domains: Bacteria, Archaea and Eucarya. The approach made it possible to build trees of
      microbes from sequences rather than from appearance.

      Gram was a Danish physician working in Berlin when he published his method in 1884. The
      problem he addressed was practical: bacteria in a section of tissue are hard to pick out from
      the cells around them. His procedure stained the preparation with a violet dye, treated it
      with a solution of iodine, and then washed it with alcohol. Some bacteria kept the dark

      groups with a microscope and a few bottles, and it is still among the first tests run on an
      unknown bacterium.

techniques:
  - name: "Gram staining"
    blurb: >-
      Stain a smear of bacteria with crystal violet, fix the dye with iodine, wash with alcohol,
      then counterstain with safranin. Bacterial cells that stay purple have a thick peptidoglycan wall
      (Gram-positive); cells that turn pink have a thin wall and an outer membrane (Gram-negative).
      Cells with no wall, such as Mycoplasma, give no useful reaction.
  - name: "Comparative ribosomal RNA analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Extract the RNA of the small ribosomal subunit from many organisms, read part or all of its
      sequence, and score each pair by how similar the sequences are. A grouping method applied to these
      scores proposes a tree of relationships that does not depend on how the cells look.
  - name: "qPCR growth monitoring"
    blurb: >-
      Measure the number of copies of one organism's ribosomal RNA gene in a culture over time by
      quantitative PCR. A straight line on a semilogarithmic plot gives a doubling time even for a
      slow microbe in a mixed culture, though gene copies are not the same as a direct cell count.
  - name: "Fluorescence in situ hybridization with microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Add a fluorescent probe that binds the ribosomal RNA of one lineage, then look at intact
      tissue under a fluorescence microscope. It shows where cells of that lineage sit, as in the
      yeast cells found in the outer layer of some lichens.

The first film answered the opening question: a methane-producing microbe (a **methanogen**) and the gut bacterium Escherichia coli belong to different domains. This text adds what the film left out, then asks how bacteria divide and swap genes, why protist is only a convenience word, how fungi feed and partner, and why viruses stand apart. It builds on [what an organism has to do](/lessons/organism-problems/) and on the surface and size limits in [size and scaling](/lessons/size-and-scaling/).

> **The one idea.** Relatedness is read from differences in a molecule every cell shares, not from how a cell looks: small changes pile up in small-subunit ribosomal RNA as lineages separate, so fewer differences usually mean a more recent common ancestor.
>
> *Scope:* cells only; the last section shows why. "Usually," because changes pile up faster in some lineages than others, and over the deepest splits repeated changes at one site blur the count. So the scores rank closeness roughly; they do not date splits or identify an ancestor, and the deepest splits need many genes and models of change.

## Three domains from one molecule

Every cell has a **plasma membrane** (the lipid boundary of the cell), **cytoplasm** (the contents inside the membrane, outside any nucleus), DNA as its genetic material, and **ribosomes**, the particles that make proteins. A **eukaryote** is a cell, or an organism made of cells, with a **nucleus**: a membrane-enclosed compartment holding the DNA. Animals, plants, fungi and protists are eukaryotes. Bacteria and archaea have no nucleus, and for a long time they were lumped together as prokaryotes, the "before the nucleus" cells. Eukaryotic organelles are in [the measured eukaryotic cell](/lessons/measured-eukaryotic-cell/).

Three things the film left out:

- **The data were pieces, not whole sequences.** In 1977 Woese and George Fox compared catalogues of short RNA pieces, scored pair by pair (see Going deeper).
- **Eukaryotes were compared by their cytoplasmic RNA.** Mitochondria and chloroplasts carry their own bacteria-like ribosomal RNA, so the comparison used the ribosomal RNA of the cytoplasm.
- **A second, independent line of evidence agrees.** Archaea also differ from bacteria in their membranes and walls (below).

The three **domains** proposed in 1990 by Woese, Otto Kandler and Mark Wheelis, Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya, are still the standard labels, even though many-gene trees nest eukaryotes inside the archaea, closest to the **Asgard archaea**, a group first known from DNA in marine sediments. Whether eukaryotes branch within the Asgard group or beside it, and which Asgard lineage is closest, is still debated. None of these comparisons observed an ancestor or showed how eukaryotic cells arose. See [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/) and [history of life](/lessons/history-of-life/).

The second line of evidence is chemistry. Archaeal membrane lipids are branched **isoprenoid** chains joined to glycerol by **ether bonds**, where bacteria and eukaryotes mostly use fatty acids joined by **ester bonds** (see [membrane structure and function](/lessons/membrane-structure-function/)). Ether bonds alone do not mark archaea, since some bacteria and some eukaryotic lipids carry them too. The archaeal mark is the combination: isoprenoid chains, ether bonds, and a glycerol that is the mirror image of the bacterial one, like a left hand to a right. Archaeal walls also lack **peptidoglycan**, the mesh of sugars and short peptides that forms most bacterial walls. Some methanogens have a different but similar polymer, **pseudomurein**; many archaea are covered only by a **surface layer** of protein. Nor are archaea simply "extreme bacteria": many live in soils, oceans and sediments, and methanogens live in the guts of cattle and humans.

## Bacteria: shape, envelope, fission and gene swapping

Bacteria come in three common shapes: **cocci** (spheres), **bacilli** (rods) and **spirilli** (spirals). A cell of E. coli is a rod roughly 1 micrometre (um) wide and 2 um long.

The **cell envelope** is everything that surrounds the cytoplasm. In 1884 Hans Christian Gram published a stain that sorts bacteria by their envelopes. In the method used today, a smear is stained with crystal violet, treated with iodine, washed with alcohol and counterstained with red safranin. **Gram-positive** cells, such as Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus subtilis, have a thick peptidoglycan wall that keeps the crystal violet-iodine complex through the wash, so they stay purple. **Gram-negative** cells, such as E. coli, have a thin peptidoglycan wall beneath an **outer membrane**; the wash removes the violet and the safranin turns them pink. Not every bacterium has a wall at all: Mycoplasma species lack peptidoglycan and give no useful Gram reaction.

Bacteria and archaea usually reproduce by **binary fission**: the cell copies its DNA, grows, and divides into two. The **doubling time**, or generation time, is the time one round takes. Bacteria also gain genes from other cells: **horizontal gene transfer**. In **transformation** a cell takes up DNA from its surroundings. In **conjugation** DNA passes between two cells in contact. In **transduction** a bacteriophage, a virus that infects bacteria, carries bacterial DNA from one cell to another. Their genetics are in [bacterial and phage genetics](/lessons/bacterial-and-phage-genetics/), and the experiments showing DNA is the genetic material in [DNA, the genetic material](/lessons/dna-the-genetic-material/).

**Worked problem: how fast does fission add cells?** In rich laboratory medium at 37 degrees C, E. coli typically doubles about every 20 minutes. Start with 100 cells. How many are there after 3 hours?

Three hours is 180 minutes, or 180 / 20 = 9 doublings, and each doubling multiplies the count by 2: 100 x 2^9 = 100 x 512 = 51,200 cells. In general, if every cell divides once per doubling time, none die, and food and space never run short (exponential growth),

> **N = N0 x 2^g, where g = elapsed time / doubling time**

g counts generations; 2^g is how many-fold the population has grown.

**Predict.** The Asgard archaeon MK-D1 doubles roughly every 20 days. Starting from the same 100 cells, how long would it need to reach 51,200?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The same nine doublings, on a slower clock: 9 x 20 days = 180 days. Growth is set by the number of generations, not by the hours. In E. coli's 3 hours, MK-D1 grows by a factor of only about 1.004. And if a culture shows the reported **lag phase**, 30 to 60 days before it begins to grow at all, the dish would need about 210 to 240 days.

</details>

Twenty days is 20 x 1,440 = 28,800 minutes, so an MK-D1 generation lasts 28,800 / 20 = 1,440 times as long as an E. coli one.

The model fails once food runs short or cells die. Beyond the lab, each generation is another chance for a mutation or a transferred gene to be copied, so a short generation gives a population more generations in which to change.

## Protists: a word for "everything else"

**Protist** is an informal name for any eukaryote that is not an animal, a plant or a fungus, not a natural group: protists belong to many separate branches of the eukaryote tree, and their classification keeps changing. Nor are protists "early" eukaryotes; every living lineage has had as long to evolve as animals have.

Compare four ways of life:

<LessonFigure src="/figures/microbes-protists-fungi/protist-four-ways-to-move.webp" width={1200} height={643} alt="AI-generated illustration: three single-celled protists on a pale blue background. A slipper-shaped Paramecium with a fringe of short cilia and an oral groove, an irregular Amoeba with bulging pseudopodia reaching round a small prey cell, and a spindle-shaped Euglena with one long flagellum." caption="Protists share no single way of moving or feeding: Paramecium sweeps food in with cilia, Amoeba engulfs prey with pseudopodia, and Euglena swims with one long flagellum and can photosynthesize." />

- **Paramecium** swims and sweeps food into an oral groove with **cilia**, many short hair-like projections that beat together.
- **Amoeba** crawls and engulfs prey by extending **pseudopodia**, temporary bulges of cytoplasm.
- **Euglena** swims with one long **flagellum**, a whip-like projection (a second stays short, inside the cell), and is **mixotrophic**: it can photosynthesize in light and absorb organic food when light is poor.
- **Plasmodium**, the malaria parasite, passes between mosquitoes and vertebrates including humans, and in humans multiplies inside liver and red blood cells.

None of these four is closer to the others because it lacks something, and even mitochondria are not universal: the gut protist Monocercomonoides has no detectable mitochondrion, and its genome suggests the organelle was lost in that lineage, not that it never had one.

Fungi make the same point the other way. They were long grouped with plants, but their sequences put them closer to animals: the shared molecule, not the look, places an organism. Yet a fungus eats like neither a plant nor an animal.

## Fungi digest outside the body and partner with roots and algae

Beyond the second film's bread mould: fungal walls hold glucans as well as chitin, a mycelium spreads by growing at its hyphal tips, and baker's yeast is Saccharomyces cerevisiae.

The film's mating cycle, from plasmogamy through the dikaryon and karyogamy to meiosis, is a schematic found in many fungi, not the bread mould's own (see [chromosomes and meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/)). How long the dikaryon lasts varies: in many fungi, including mushroom-forming species, each cell keeps its two nuclei for long periods before karyogamy. Some fungi have no known sexual cycle at all.

Fungi also live in partnerships. **Mycorrhizae** are associations between plant roots and fungi. In **arbuscular mycorrhizae** the fungus grows into cells of the root cortex (the layer of cells between the root's surface and its central core), inside the cell wall but outside the cell membrane, and forms branched exchange structures called arbuscules; the plant membrane folds around each one, so the two never share cytoplasm. The mycelium extends far into the soil, and plant and fungus trade carbon from the plant for mineral nutrients, especially phosphorus, from the fungus.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/microbes-protists-fungi/arbuscular-mycorrhiza-exchange.webp" width={1200} height={783} alt="AI-generated illustration: a plant root in soil with a branching mass of fungal threads spreading far out, and two arrows labelled carbon and phosphorus between root and fungus. An inset shows a root cortex cell with a tree-like fungal tuft inside its wall but outside its membrane." caption="An arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus grows inside root cortex cells' walls but outside their membranes, and reaches far into the soil: the plant pays carbon for mineral nutrients, especially phosphorus." />

**Predict.** A crop is grown in soil heavily fertilized with phosphate. Does it gain more or less from its mycorrhizal fungus?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Usually less, if the fertilizer raises the phosphorus the plant can actually take up (soil chemistry can lock phosphate away). Then the fungus's phosphorus benefit falls, colonization often declines, and the carbon the plant pays can exceed that benefit. Total benefit need not always fall, because the fungus may still supply other nutrients or protection against stress.

</details>

**Lichens** pair a fungus with a photosynthetic alga or cyanobacterium. For the ecology, see [mutualism, parasitism and coevolution](/lessons/mutualism-parasitism-coevolution/), [nutrient cycling](/lessons/nutrient-cycling/) and [soil, nutrients and partners](/lessons/plant-nutrition/).

## Viruses as the contrast case

A **virus** is a genome, either DNA or RNA, usually enclosed in a protein coat called a **capsid**. Bacteriophage T2, which infects E. coli, has a DNA genome; influenza viruses have RNA genomes. A virus has no ribosomes and no cell structure, and it is copied only inside a host cell, using that cell's machinery.

**Predict.** Where would you place a virus on a tree built from small-subunit ribosomal RNA?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Nowhere. A virus has no ribosomes, so it has no small-subunit ribosomal RNA to compare, which is why the one idea is scoped to cells.

</details>

This course defines an **organism** as a living individual made of one or more cells. On that definition a virus is not an organism. That is a choice of definition, not a final verdict on what counts as life. How animals detect and fight microbes and viruses is in [innate immunity](/lessons/innate-immunity/).

### Design problem

A student cultures a microbe from a cow's rumen. Under the microscope it is small and has no nucleus. How would you decide whether it is a bacterium or an archaeon?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with the molecule Woese used: score its small-subunit ribosomal RNA against known bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes. A methanogen groups with the archaea, scoring about as low against E. coli and Bacillus as against the cytoplasmic RNA of yeast.

Then check the second line of evidence: lipids for the archaeal combination, and the wall for the absence of peptidoglycan. The RNA comparison is the main test, and lipids and wall should agree with it. Looking or staining like a bacterium tells you nothing about the answer.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How Woese compared the RNA

Woese's laboratory at the University of Illinois and its collaborators cut the RNA of methanogens and typical bacteria with an enzyme, ribonuclease T1, into short pieces and catalogued the sequence of each; a pair of organisms scored higher when their catalogues shared more pieces. Beyond the film's point that a grid of scores does not fix one tree, placing the root needs an outside reference or a model of change. The 1990 paper spelled the third domain Eucarya.

### Plant and fungus rewards: a contested mechanism

Kiers and colleagues reported in 2011 that, in experiments with the barrel medic Medicago truncatula and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, plants allocated more carbohydrate to fungal partners that cooperated better, and fungi passed more nutrient to roots that supplied more carbohydrate. Their full methods were not part of this lesson's sources. Later work questions how general the result is: a 2024 review concluded that current evidence does not show plant carbon transfer to be directly regulated by the nutrients a fungus delivers. How would you test whether a root system rewards the better fungal partner, and whether a fungus rewards the better root?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Give one root system two fungal partners that differ in how much phosphorus they deliver, one generous and one poor.

Label each resource. Feed the leaves carbon dioxide carrying a carbon isotope and measure how much label reaches each fungus. Give each fungus phosphorus carrying a separate label, placed in a compartment its hyphae can reach but roots cannot, and measure how much reaches the plant.

Add controls. A no-choice control colonizes plants with only one fungus each, which shows whether a fungus draws more carbon wherever it grows or only when the plant can compare partners. In a second run, swap which fungal species is the generous one, separating the partner's behaviour from its species identity.

Predictions: if plants reward the better partner, the generous fungus receives more carbon in the mixed plants than the poor one does, beyond any difference in the no-choice plants. If fungi reward the better root, more phosphorus flows to roots that supply more carbon.

Limit the conclusion to the species tested: a result in Medicago and two fungi does not cover all mycorrhizal partnerships.

</details>

### Exceptions and updates

A 2019 revision of eukaryote classification by Sina Adl and colleagues dropped Excavata as a formal supergroup.

A few viruses of fungi have no capsid at all.

### The slowest clock: MK-D1

The Asgard archaeon MK-D1, grown by Hiroyuki Imachi and colleagues in 2020, had a doubling time estimated at about 14 to 25 days. The worked problem's 20 days sits inside that range. Its 30 to 60 day lag came before any growth, and growth was measured as qPCR gene copies, not cell counts.
