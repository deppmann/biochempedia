**A hundred years ago, American chestnut trees were common in parts of Virginia’s Blue Ridge Mountains, and people ate their nuts. Then a fungus arrived from Asia, and by about 1950 it had killed billions of these trees.**

The short answer is that a change in the environment, here a new disease, can push a population toward extinction when too few of its members can survive it and reproduce. When a change comes fast, whether any can depends mostly on inherited differences that were already in the population. And what could help the chestnut come back?

## Explain: survive, move or die

<WordBank terms={['migrate', 'extinct', 'extinction', 'habitat loss', 'pollution', 'climate change', 'invasive species', 'genetic variation', 'resistance']} />

Every environment changes. When a change comes, the members of a population do not all share the same fate.

- Some survive and reproduce. Their offspring inherit their traits.
- Some **migrate**, which means they move to a new place. A bird can fly away; a rooted tree cannot, though its seeds can carry its kind to new places over many years.
- Some die.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/extinction-and-survival/survive-migrate-or-die.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: a forest edge after a change. On the left a healthy tree stands with seedlings at its base. In the centre a small bird flies away while a rooted tree stays put. On the right stands a dead, leafless tree." caption="When the environment changes, some members of a population survive and reproduce, some migrate, and some die. A bird can fly away; a rooted tree cannot, though its seeds can spread over many years." />

A species is **extinct** when no living members are left anywhere. **Extinction** is the dying out of a whole species. Vanishing from one forest is not extinction.

If no member of a species has traits that let it survive a change, the species may become extinct. Natural selection explains why: individuals with helpful traits leave more offspring, as you saw in [that lesson](/lessons/natural-selection-in-action/). Fossils show that many species went extinct long before people lived, which [that lesson](/lessons/evidence-for-change/) explores.

The American chestnut is not extinct. Millions of sprouts still grow from old roots. But the big nut-making trees are gone from most of the canopy, the top layer of leaves in a forest.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** When an environment changes, some organisms survive and reproduce, some move, and some die; if none can survive, the species may become extinct.</Support>

## Explain: four changes that challenge populations

Many kinds of change put species at risk today. Hunting, fishing or harvesting too much is one. This lesson looks at four others.

- **Habitat loss** is the destruction of the place where an organism lives. Clearing a forest for houses removes homes for animals that nest in old trees.
- **Pollution** is harmful material added to air, water or soil. Too much fertilizer washing into a bay can feed algae, and low oxygen for fish can follow.
- **Climate change** is a long shift in the usual weather of a region, such as warmer average temperatures. Fish that need cold streams may lose places to live as water warms.
- An **invasive species** is a living thing brought to a new place, where it spreads and causes harm. Blue catfish in Virginia’s rivers are one, as [that lesson](/lessons/people-change-habitats/) shows. The chestnut blight fungus is another.

The blight fungus reached North America on chestnut trees brought from Asia. It grows into the bark of an American chestnut and makes a sore called a canker. The canker can kill the whole trunk above the ground. The roots, safe in the soil, often live and send up new sprouts. Then the sprouts get infected and die back, a cycle that can go on for decades.

## Explain: why variation matters

**Genetic variation** is the inherited differences among members of a population. **Resistance** is the ability to hold off a disease or other threat better than others can. When a new disease arrives, a population with more variation is more likely to have some members that resist it. Those members survive and pass their traits to their offspring.

Variation does not promise survival. When a change comes fast, the helpful trait usually has to be there already. New helpful differences can appear or arrive from other populations, but often too slowly to save a species from a fast change. American chestnuts had very little resistance to this fungus, so almost all of the big trees died. Surviving chestnuts still carry plenty of genetic variation, but American chestnuts as a group resist the blight far less than Chinese chestnuts do.

A few survivors do not make a species safe; they must also reproduce.

At research farms in Meadowview, in southwest Virginia, scientists with The American Chestnut Foundation breed chestnuts. They cross American chestnuts with Chinese chestnuts. They test the young trees with blight, then cross the most resistant ones back to American chestnuts, again over several generations. The new trees are mostly American but carry some resistance from the Chinese parent. This is artificial selection: people, not the environment, choose which trees breed. Some scientists have also tried adding one gene from wheat that helps American chestnut trees survive the blight.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A population with more genetic variation is more likely to have survivors that pass on a helpful trait, but when a change comes fast, usually only if that trait is already present.</Support>

### Design problem

Plan a test of whether plants with more variation have more survivors after a hard change. Use garden seeds, not chestnuts.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** After a dry spell, do more seedlings survive in a pot with three radish varieties than in pots that each hold just one of those varieties?
- **Independent variable:** the number of varieties in a pot (one or three). Make a single-variety pot for each of the three varieties, so one hardy variety cannot explain the result alone.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of seedlings still alive after 14 days.
- **Constants:** 30 seeds per pot, the same soil, pot size, light, and 50 mL of water each time.
- **Control:** a pot of each kind watered every two days, with no dry spell.
- **The change:** after the seedlings sprout, water the test pots only once a week.
- **Repeated trials:** three pots of each kind.
- **Also measure:** seedling height in centimetres.
- **Safety:** wash your hands after handling soil, and never taste seeds, since some are coated with chemicals.

More survivors in the mixed pots than in the single-variety pots on average would support the claim. Different varieties of one plant are only a model of variation.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The American chestnut nearly disappeared because an invasive fungus arrived, and American trees had little resistance to it. Its roots still sprout, so the species is not extinct, but few trees make seeds. Breeding with Chinese chestnuts can add some of the resistance that the population lacked. So far, the best hybrids still resist less well than Chinese chestnuts at some sites, so the work goes on.

## Going deeper

### Small populations and chance

In a very small population, a trait can disappear by chance if the few members that carry it die before they reproduce.

### More about the chestnut ratings

Family D2 was one of the best hybrid families. A second American family at the Virginia site rated 2.4. About 40 percent of the Virginia trees were also hurt by an accidental weed-killer spray in year 5.
