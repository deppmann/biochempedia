    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Bateson was a Cambridge zoologist who, soon after Mendel's paper was taken up in 1900,
      became one of the rules' strongest defenders in Britain. Defending them meant testing them,
      and he did that by breeding plants and animals with colleagues. With the botanist Edith
      Saunders he wrote the first of a series of Reports to the Evolution Committee of the Royal
      Society, and Reginald Punnett joined them for the second. In sweet peas, Lathyrus odoratus,
      they crossed two white-flowered strains and got purple-flowered hybrids. Selfed, the
      hybrids gave coloured (purple or red) and white flowers in a ratio near 9 to 7, which their third report, in 1906, explained as two genes that both need a dominant allele for colour. In chickens, crossing birds with two
      different comb shapes gave a third shape, and the F2 held four kinds of comb in a ratio near
      9:3:3:1. Both results said the same thing: one visible character can depend on two genes
      at once, each segregating by Mendel's rule. Bateson later used the word epistatic for a gene
      that hides the effect of another. The work turned odd ratios from exceptions into evidence
      about how genes act together.

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Cuénot was a French zoologist at Nancy, and in the first years after 1900 he was among the
      first to show that Mendel's rules held in an animal, using the coat colours of house mice.
      Yellow would not fit. In his crosses a yellow mouse was never true-breeding: mated together,
      yellow mice always produced some non-yellow young, and he never obtained a yellow mouse that
      gave only yellow offspring. He reported this in a 1905 note in the Archives de Zoologie
      Expérimentale et Générale. In 1910 William Castle and Clarence Little, working at Harvard,
      published a short paper in Science on the modified ratio among yellow mice. They found that
      yellow by yellow matings gave close to two yellow young for every non-yellow one, and they proposed that mice carrying two yellow alleles are
      formed and then die. Later work found the dead embryos. The yellow mouse became the
      textbook case of an allele that is dominant for one trait and recessive for another.

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Landsteiner trained in medicine at the University of Vienna. In 1901, in the Wiener klinische
      Wochenschrift, he reported that serum from one person clumped the red cells of some other
      people and not others. The pattern sorted people into three groups, which he called A, B
      and C; C is now called O. A fourth and rarer group, AB, was reported in 1902 by Alfred von
      Decastello and Adriano Sturli. That ABO is inherited as three
      alleles at a single locus was a later conclusion, reached by Felix Bernstein in the 1920s
      from the frequencies of the groups in populations. Landsteiner received the 1930 Nobel
      Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery of human blood groups. The molecules came
      much later. In 1990 Fumi-ichiro Yamamoto, Sen-itiroh Hakomori and colleagues reported the
      ABO gene sequence: the A and B alleles differ by four amino acids, and the O allele they
      studied lacks a single base. Blood groups became a gene whose alleles encode enzymes.

techniques:
  - name: "Controlled crosses scored in the F2"
    blurb: >-
      True-breeding lines are crossed, the F1 is selfed or intercrossed, and the F2 is sorted into
      phenotype classes and counted. Which classes appear, and in what sixteenths, tells you how
      many genes act on the trait and how their alleles combine.
  - name: "Test cross and backcross to parental lines"
    blurb: >-
      An F1 is crossed to a homozygous recessive or to a parent line. Because the tester gives
      only known alleles, the offspring classes report the gametes of the F1, which, together
      with the F2, helps separate one-gene from two-gene explanations.
  - name: "Haemagglutination blood typing"
    blurb: >-
      Red cells are mixed with antibodies (originally, other people's serum) that recognize the A
      or B antigen. Clumping shows the antigen is on the cells, so both antigens can be detected in
      one person, the operational meaning of codominance.
  - name: "Expression of reconstructed transferases"
    blurb: >-
      Cloned ABO coding sequences, or versions with chosen codons swapped, are expressed in
      cultured human cells, and the enzyme's sugar products are read with antibodies. This links
      particular amino-acid differences to which sugar the enzyme adds.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "A red snapdragon is crossed with a white one, and every F1 plant has pink flowers. What do you expect when the pink F1 plants are self-pollinated?"
    choices:
      - "All pink, because the red and white alleles have blended for good"
      - "1 red : 2 pink : 1 white, because pink plants are heterozygotes"
      - "3 pink : 1 white, because pink is the dominant flower colour"
      - "1 red : 1 white, because pink plants make no pink gametes at all"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Pink plants are heterozygous, so selfing gives 1/4 homozygous red, 1/2 heterozygous pink and
      1/4 homozygous white, and with incomplete dominance each genotype has its own colour. All
      pink is the blending idea that the recovery of red and white disproves. Three pink to one
      white treats pink as a dominant allele, but pink is a heterozygote, not an allele. One red
      to one white forgets that heterozygotes are produced again in the F2; pink gametes do not
      exist, yet pink offspring do.
  - stem: "A mother with type A blood and a father with type B blood have a child with type O blood. Under the usual ABO model, what are the parents' genotypes?"
    choices:
      - "I^A i and I^B i"
      - "I^A I^A and I^B I^B"
      - "I^A I^B and i i"
      - "I^A I^B and I^B i"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      A type O child is ii and must have received i from each parent, so the type A mother is I^A i
      and the type B father is I^B i. Two homozygous parents could give only I^A I^B children,
      who are type AB. A parent who is I^A I^B is type AB, not A or B, and an ii parent is type O,
      so the pair with I^A I^B and ii does not match the parents' blood types. The pair with
      I^A I^B and I^B i also includes an AB parent, which the stem rules out.
  - stem: "Two yellow mice, each A^y/a, are mated many times. Among the live pups, what fraction do you expect to be yellow?"
    choices:
      - "3/4, because yellow is dominant for coat colour"
      - "1/2, because only half the gametes carry A^y"
      - "2/3, because A^y/A^y embryos die before birth"
      - "1/4, because yellow is recessive for survival"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Conceptions are 1/4 A^y/A^y, 1/2 A^y/a and 1/4 a/a. The A^y/A^y quarter dies early, so
      among survivors 2 of every 3 are A^y/a and yellow. Three quarters would be right only if all
      genotypes survived. One half is the fraction of A^y gametes, not of yellow pups, and it
      ignores that a pup needs only one A^y to be yellow. One quarter is the fraction of
      conceptions that die, not the fraction of live pups that are yellow.
  - stem: "In summer squash, a white-fruited F1 from a cross of two true-breeding lines is selfed. The F2 has white, yellow and green fruit in a ratio near 12:3:1. Which model fits?"
    choices:
      - "Two linked genes, so some expected F2 classes are missing"
      - "One gene with three alleles, each giving one fruit colour"
      - "Two genes, both of whose dominant alleles are needed for colour"
      - "Two genes, where a dominant allele at one hides the other"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Sixteenths mean two segregating genes. If a dominant W allele makes fruit white whatever
      the second gene does, then W_ gives 9 + 3 = 12 white, and among ww plants a dominant Y gives 3
      yellow and yy gives 1 green. Two genes that are both needed for colour give 9 coloured to 7
      uncoloured, not three classes. Linkage would distort the classes away from sixteenths
      rather than merge them so cleanly. A single gene cannot give sixteenths in one F2, and an
      F1 individual carries at most two alleles at a locus.
  - stem: "In a family study, 50 people carry the same dominant allele. 35 of them show the trait, and among those 35 its severity ranges from mild to severe. Which statement is correct?"
    choices:
      - "Penetrance is 35%, and the range in severity is incomplete dominance"
      - "Penetrance is 70%, and the range in severity is variable expressivity"
      - "Expressivity is 70%, and the range in severity is incomplete penetrance"
      - "Penetrance is 100%, because every one of the 50 people carries the allele"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Penetrance is the fraction of carriers who show the trait, 35/50 = 70%, and the spread of
      severity among affected carriers is expressivity. Thirty-five per cent confuses the count
      with the fraction, and incomplete dominance compares heterozygotes with homozygotes, not
      affected people with each other. The choice that calls 70% expressivity swaps the two
      terms. A penetrance of 100% would require every carrier to show the trait; carrying the
      allele is not the same as showing it.

Cells heterozygous for a different allele that makes no protein show about 50%. Which explanation fits both results?"
    choices:
      - "The missense allele is haploinsufficient, so half the protein gives half the current"
      - "The missense allele is recessive, so the normal subunits make up for the mutant ones"
      - "The missense subunit is dominant negative: (1/2)^4 = 1/16 of channels are all normal"
      - "The missense subunit is overexpressed and outcompetes normal mRNA for the ribosomes"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      If any mutant subunit spoils a channel, only channels with four normal subunits work, and
      with random assembly that is (1/2)^4 = 1/16, about 6%, matching the data. The null allele
      gives half the subunits and about half the current, which is what haploinsufficiency
      looks like, so haploinsufficiency explains the 50% but not the 6%. A recessive allele
      would leave the heterozygote near normal, not at 6%. The stem says normal and mutant
      subunits are made in equal amounts, which rules out overexpression of the mutant.
  - stem: "In Labrador retrievers, the B locus (B, black pigment; b, brown) and the E locus both affect coat colour, and the two loci are unlinked. Two black dogs produce, over several litters, 18 black, 6 chocolate and 8 yellow pups. Which explanation fits these numbers?"

      - "Both parents are BbEe, and bb pups are yellow whatever their E genotype"

      - "The parents are BbEE and BbEe, and the two loci are tightly linked"

      Allele 1 is I^A and allele 3 is an O allele. One copy of a working A transferase puts

**Mate two yellow mice as often as you like, and you will never get a yellow mouse that breeds true.**

Lucien Cuénot found this in his mouse crosses in the first years after Mendel's rules were taken up. It looked like a failure of Mendel. It was not. The short answer: two copies of the yellow allele kill the embryo, so every live yellow mouse is a heterozygote. The first film, at the top of this page, shows this with three grids; this page adds where the labels come from and how far the idea reaches.

First the terms. **Segregation** is the separation of a parent's two alleles into different gametes at meiosis, so each child gets one allele from each parent. **Independent assortment** means that alleles of unlinked genes segregate independently of each other ([Mendel's peas](/lessons/mendels-peas/)). A Punnett square lists the possible pairings of gametes, and each cell of the square, one gamete pairing, is a **box** ([probability and crosses](/lessons/probability-and-crosses/)).

> **The one idea.** Inheritance deals the boxes; the gene products write the labels. With a fair deal (each parent passes on either allele equally often, and gametes meet at random), the boxes are equally likely. Aa x Aa gives four (1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa); AaBb x AaBb, with the genes unlinked so each parent makes AB, Ab, aB and ab at 1/4 each, gives sixteen. Other parents give other counts, as the ABO cross below shows. Every ratio in this lesson is those same boxes with new labels. Some boxes share a label (dominance, epistasis), some keep their own (incomplete dominance, codominance), and one can be labelled "dies" and dropped from the count (a lethal).
>
> *Scope:* unlinked genes on autosomes, segregating normally, with one phenotype for each genotype. Linkage, sex linkage and segregation distortion change the boxes themselves, and incomplete penetrance gives a box a probability instead of a label.

That fair deal holds throughout this lesson. On top of it, Mendel's account of 3:1 and 9:3:3:1 made five quiet assumptions about the labels:

1. One allele fully hides the other.
2. A gene has two alleles.
3. Every genotype survives to be counted.
4. One gene controls one character.
5. Each genotype gives one phenotype.

Relaxing the first, third or fourth relabels the boxes, so the ratio changes predictably. Relaxing the second leaves the deal alone but allows new genotypes, crosses and ratios (ABO, below). Relaxing the fifth is where the rule ends.

## What you measure decides the word

In Mendel's peas, a plant with one purple allele has flowers as purple as a plant with two: **complete dominance**, three boxes sharing one label. The first film's snapdragons (*Antirrhinum majus*) wrote the labels differently. Red crossed with white gives pink F1 flowers, and selfing the pink plants brings back 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. Nothing blended. This is **incomplete dominance**: the heterozygote's phenotype lies between the two homozygotes', so the phenotype ratio equals the genotype ratio and the 1:2:1 hidden in Mendel's peas is in plain sight.

In **codominance**, the products of both alleles are detected in the heterozygote. People with the M and N alleles of the MN blood group carry both the M and the N antigens (molecules that antibodies recognize, here markers on the cell surface) on their red cells, and an MN by MN cross gives 1 M : 2 MN : 1 N.

**Predict.** The sickle-cell allele is an allele of the gene for β-globin, one of the protein chains of haemoglobin. A heterozygote makes both normal and sickle haemoglobin, yet usually does not have sickle cell disease. Is the sickle-cell allele dominant, recessive or codominant?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It depends on what you measure. On a gel, where proteins are separated by charge in an electric field, both haemoglobins show as bands: codominant. For anaemia, since the person usually has no sickle cell disease, the allele is close to recessive. Dominance describes the heterozygote's phenotype for the trait you score, not the strength of an allele.

</details>

## ABO: three alleles, two sugars and codominance

A gene can have more than two alleles in a population, a set called **multiple alleles**, though each diploid person carries only two. The ABO blood groups are the classic case. In the usual model there are three alleles at one locus (one gene's place on a chromosome):

- **I^A** encodes a glycosyltransferase, an enzyme that adds a sugar; this one adds N-acetylgalactosamine to a sugar chain on the cell surface called the H antigen, making the A antigen.
- **I^B** encodes a nearly identical enzyme that adds galactose instead, making the B antigen. (The antigens are carbohydrates; see [carbohydrates](/lessons/carbohydrates/).)
- **i** makes no working enzyme, so H is left unchanged.

I^A and I^B are codominant: an I^A I^B person carries both antigens and is type AB. Each is dominant to i, because one working enzyme is enough to decorate the cells. So I^A I^A and I^A i are type A, I^B I^B and I^B i are type B, and ii is type O.

**Predict.** A type A parent who is I^A i and a type B parent who is I^B i have children. Which blood types can the children have, and how likely is each?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

All four, each with probability 1/4: I^A I^B is type AB, I^A i is type A, I^B i is type B, and ii is type O. The third allele did not change the deal; it made possible a cross whose four boxes each carry a different label.

</details>

Karl Landsteiner described the groups in 1901 (the rest of the history is in Going deeper). The molecules came in 1990, when Fumi-ichiro Yamamoto, Sen-itiroh Hakomori and colleagues reported that the A and B alleles they cloned differ at four amino acids, and that the O allele they studied lacks a single base, which shifts the reading frame (the grouping of bases into three-letter codons) so no working enzyme is made.

## Why one copy is usually enough, and when it is not

Why does one I^A allele make a person fully type A, while one red allele makes a snapdragon only pink? The answer lies in what each allele's product does. Start with the commonest kind of mutant allele, a **loss-of-function** allele, which makes no working product or too little. A heterozygote for a **null** allele, one that makes no product, has one working copy, so it often makes about half the normal amount; a partial loss of function, or feedback that turns up the working copy, can leave more. Whether that half shows depends on how the trait responds to the amount.

For most enzymes, half is plenty, as with I^A over *i*. An enzyme in a pathway of many steps is rarely the only thing setting the output, so halving it slows the pathway only a little. In a toy model of ten equally important steps, halving one leaves about 91% of the output (Going deeper; see also [how enzymes work](/lessons/enzyme-action-basics/)). That is why most loss-of-function alleles of enzymes are recessive. If the trait tracks the amount of one product, as a pigment can, half the product gives a paler colour: one way to get a pink heterozygote.

A loss of function can still be dominant, in two ways.

**Haploinsufficiency: half is not enough.** Some proteins, often receptors, signals or structural parts whose output follows their dose, must be present in close to their full amount. *Notch*, a gene for a signaling receptor in flies, is the classic case: a fly with one working copy has notched wing tips, which gave the gene its name ([Notch signaling](/lessons/notch-signaling/)).

**Dominant negative: the mutant product spoils the normal one.** Many proteins work as complexes of several identical subunits.

**Predict.** A potassium channel is built from four identical subunits. Compare two heterozygotes. In the first, the mutant allele makes no protein at all (a null allele). In the second, it makes a subunit that joins channels and breaks any channel it is in. Subunits join channels at random. Which cell has less working channel?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The second, by far. In the first cell every channel is normal, built from half as many subunits. In the second, normal and mutant subunits are equally common, and each channel picks its four at random. A channel works only if all four picks are normal:

> **fraction of working channels = (1/2) × (1/2) × (1/2) × (1/2) = 1/16, about 6%**

So the first cell keeps about half the normal number of working channels and the second about 6% of normal. The dominant negative does worse than a null allele of the same gene, so it can be dominant when the null is not.

</details>

**Gain of function: a new or excess activity.** Some mutant alleles make a product that does too much of its normal job, a new job, or its job in the wrong place. One copy is enough to add that activity, so gain-of-function alleles are usually dominant. The yellow mouse is one.

## Alleles that kill: the 2:1 ratio

The mouse **agouti** gene gives wild-type mice their banded hairs: its product is a signal that switches pigment cells from black pigment to yellow, which lays a yellow band in each hair. The yellow allele, A^y, is dominant for coat colour: A^y/a mice are yellow, and a/a mice are black (no yellow band). It is recessive for survival: A^y/A^y embryos die before implantation, when the early embryo attaches to the wall of the uterus. William Castle and Clarence Little proposed this in 1910, after finding that yellow by yellow matings gave about two yellow young for every non-yellow one.

How can one allele be dominant for one trait and recessive for another? In the model of Michaud and colleagues, one deletion acts on two genes. It switches agouti on where it is normally silent, making the coat yellow: a gain of function, so one copy is enough. It also disturbs a neighbouring gene that the early embryo needs, and the embryo dies only when both copies are disturbed (anecdote below).

**Worked problem.** Cross A^y/a x A^y/a. Assume a fair deal and complete early death of A^y/A^y.

**Step 1. Conceptions.** 1/4 A^y/A^y, 1/2 A^y/a, 1/4 a/a.

**Step 2. Remove the dead class.** One quarter of conceptions die; the survivors, 1/2 and 1/4, are in the ratio 2:1.

**Step 3. Phenotypes among live pups.** 2/3 yellow (A^y/a), 1/3 black (a/a). In 12 live pups, expect 8 yellow and 4 black.

A **recessive lethal allele** is one that kills in two copies while one copy is survivable; the 2:1 is a ratio among survivors, and the 1:2:1 still holds at conception.

**Predict.** A breeder mates yellow mice either with yellow mates or with black (a/a) mates, with the same number of conceptions in each kind of mating. Which litters are smaller, and by how much?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The yellow by yellow litters, by about one quarter. A^y/a x a/a gives 1/2 A^y/a and 1/2 a/a, and none die. In yellow by yellow matings the A^y/A^y quarter dies, so three quarters of conceptions become live pups.

</details>

So far each trait has been one gene's business. Bateson, Saunders and Punnett found a flower colour that took two.

## Two genes, one trait: epistasis

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

In the second film the two genes hid each other evenly: a plant missing either step was white. Bateson's word gives the general term: **epistasis** is any case where the genotype at one gene hides the effect of another gene on the same trait. The hiding need not be even, and more than two classes can survive.

**Worked problem.** In Labrador retrievers, the B allele (black pigment) is dominant to b (brown pigment, a coat called chocolate), and the E allele (lets the hair make black or brown eumelanin) is dominant to e. An ee dog makes only yellow pheomelanin in its coat, so its coat is yellow whatever its B genotype. Assume the two loci are unlinked, and that both parents carry alleles at other coat-colour loci that let B and E show these effects (details in Going deeper). Cross BbEe x BbEe. Write B_ for B with either allele: BB or Bb.

**Step 1. Each locus alone.** P(B_) = 3/4, P(bb) = 1/4, P(E_) = 3/4, P(ee) = 1/4.

**Step 2. Multiply and label.**

> **B_E_ = 3/4 x 3/4 = 9/16 black; bbE_ = 1/4 x 3/4 = 3/16 chocolate; B_ee + bbee = 3/16 + 1/16 = 4/16 yellow**

The ratio is 9:3:4, a 9:3:3:1 with the last two classes merged. Because e masks the B locus only when homozygous, this is **recessive epistasis**. Same sixteen boxes, new labels: the four ee boxes share one.

**Step 3. Counts.** In 64 pups, expect 64 x 9/16 = 36 black, 64 x 3/16 = 12 chocolate and 64 x 4/16 = 16 yellow.

**Step 4. A conditional question.** Among yellow pups, what fraction is bbee? Of the 4 yellow sixteenths, 1 is bbee, so P(bbee given yellow) = 1/4.

Other interactions regroup the same sixteen boxes. Read the third column, not the name: it is what you can derive.

| Ratio | How the genes act | Which boxes share a label | Classic case |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9:3:4 | recessive epistasis: two recessive alleles at one gene hide the other gene | the 4 boxes homozygous recessive at the hiding gene | Labrador coat; mouse albino (cc) |
| 9:7 | both dominant alleles needed (complementary genes) | the 7 boxes missing a dominant allele at either gene | sweet-pea flower colour |
| 12:3:1 | a dominant allele at one gene masks the other (dominant epistasis) | the 12 boxes with that dominant allele | summer squash fruit colour |
| 15:1 | a dominant allele at either gene is enough (duplicate genes) | the 15 boxes with any dominant allele | shepherd's purse seed capsule shape |
| 13:3 | a dominant inhibitor at one gene, plus a colourless homozygous recessive at the other | the 12 boxes with the inhibitor allele, plus the 1 box homozygous colourless without it | White Leghorn x White Wyandotte chicken colour (design problem below) |

In the F2 of a cross between double heterozygotes, a ratio in sixteenths with fewer than four classes is a clue that two unlinked genes act on one trait. The explorer shows four of these regroupings, with a random sample beside the expected counts.

## Where the rule ends: penetrance and expressivity

So far each genotype gave one phenotype, the fifth assumption. Often it does not, and here the rule stops: a box gets a probability instead of a label. (Linkage, sex linkage and segregation distortion instead change the boxes themselves; see [linkage and mapping](/lessons/linkage-and-mapping/) and [sex linkage](/lessons/sex-linkage-and-beyond/).)

**Penetrance** is the fraction of carriers of a genotype who show the trait. If 70 of 100 carriers are affected, penetrance is 70%. It is measured in a group; for one carrier it is a probability, not a verdict. **Expressivity** is how strongly the trait shows in those who have it; with variable expressivity, the same genotype gives mild disease in one carrier and severe disease in another.

Three things loosen the map. Other genes: a polygenic score adds up the small effects of many variants across the genome, and in UK Biobank participants carrying variants for familial hypercholesterolemia, the modelled probability of coronary disease by age 75 ranged from 17.5% to 77.9% across percentiles of such a score (the study's caveats are in Going deeper). Chance in development: genetically identical animals raised side by side can differ, because development involves random molecular events. And environment, of which phenylketonuria (PKU) is the clearest case. People with loss-of-function alleles in both copies of the gene for phenylalanine hydroxylase convert little or no phenylalanine to tyrosine; how little depends on the alleles. On an ordinary diet phenylalanine accumulates and causes severe intellectual disability; in children kept on a low-phenylalanine diet from early infancy, that outcome is largely prevented. The genotype is the same; what differs is what the diet supplies.

### Design problem

White Leghorn and White Wyandotte chickens are both white. Leghorns carry a dominant inhibitor of colour, I; Wyandottes are homozygous for a recessive colourless allele, c. Take Leghorns as IICC (they carry what is needed for colour, but I stops it) and Wyandottes as iicc (nothing inhibits them, but cc makes no colour). A cross of the two gives white F1 birds, but the F2 is about 13 white to 3 coloured. Explain the F2, predict a testcross of the F1, and design crosses that show two genes are involved.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The F1 is IiCc, white because it carries I.

**The F2.** A bird is coloured only if it has no inhibitor (ii, probability 1/4) and at least one C (C_, probability 3/4): 1/4 x 3/4 = 3/16 coloured. The other 13/16 are white: 9 I_C_ + 3 I_cc + 1 iicc. It is a 9:3:3:1 with three classes sharing one label.

**The testcross.** Cross the F1 with a Wyandotte (iicc). The F1 makes four kinds of gamete equally often, IC, Ic, iC and ic, and only iC gives a coloured chick: expect 1 coloured : 3 white.

**Tests for two genes.** The F2 itself is the strongest. With one gene and two alleles, the F2 holds only the parental and F1 genotypes, all white, so any coloured F2 bird shows that more than one gene is at work, and 3/16 fits two unlinked genes. Next, cross coloured F2 birds with Wyandottes: under the model about one in three gives only coloured chicks (iiCC) and two in three give half coloured (iiCc). Crossed with Leghorns, coloured F2 birds give only white chicks, because I is dominant. Be careful with a single backcross to one parent line: its ratio can fit more than one model (Going deeper has a case). Sorting mutant lines by whether they restore function together is the logic of [complementation and pathways](/lessons/complementation-and-pathways/), taken up later.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The history of ABO

Karl Landsteiner described groups A, B and a third he called C (now O) in 1901; AB was reported in 1902 by Decastello and Sturli; and Felix Bernstein's analysis of population frequencies in the 1920s showed that three alleles at one locus fit.

### Why halving an enzyme often barely shows

Take a toy model that treats the steps of a linear pathway like resistances in series: the output (the flux) is proportional to 1 / (1/e1 + 1/e2 + ...), where each e is the activity of one enzyme. With ten equal enzymes the sum is 10/e; halving one makes it 11/e, which leaves 10/11, about 91%, of the flux. Halving one of three leaves 3/4, or 75%; if a single step sets the output, halving it leaves 50%. Real pathways need not follow this formula, since enzyme kinetics, regulation and how control is shared among the steps set their response, but the toy shows the pattern: one copy is usually enough when control is shared across many steps, and fails when output tracks the amount of one product. That is the line between a recessive loss of function and haploinsufficiency, or a pink heterozygote. For dominant negatives in the simplest model, where a complex of n identical subunits assembles at random, the mutant subunit is made as often as the normal one, and any mutant subunit spoils the complex it joins, (1/2)^n of complexes are intact: 1/4 for a dimer, 1/16 for a tetramer and about 1.6% for a hexamer.

### The Labrador example's limits

The worked problem follows only the B and E loci; the other coat-colour loci it sets aside include K. An ee dog's B genotype still shows in the colour of its nose.

### Classical and statistical epistasis

The epistasis in this lesson is classical: one gene masks another, and Mendelian classes regroup. Statistical epistasis, a departure from additivity in a population, is a related but different idea ([complex traits and GWAS](/lessons/complex-traits-and-gwas/)).

### Two white parents, one purple child: the model and a trap

The second film's model: pigment is made in two enzymatic steps, each needing a dominant allele at a different gene, A for step 1 and B for step 2. Each true-breeding white line is homozygous recessive for a different step, aaBB and AAbb. The F1, AaBb, has a working copy of both steps, so it is coloured (purple, as the real hybrids were). Selfing AaBb with independent assortment gives 9 A_B_ coloured, and 3 A_bb + 3 aaB_ + 1 aabb = 7 white: a 9:7 ratio. The model predicts only coloured against white; the real coloured F2 plants were purple or red, a split that took a further gene. A one-gene model with a dominant purple allele cannot be made from two true-breeding white parents.

A tempting test is to backcross the F1 to each parent line: AaBb x aaBB gives 1 coloured : 1 white, and so does AaBb x AAbb. But that does not show two genes. In **interallelic complementation**, two mutant alleles of one gene, each white when homozygous, can together give a working product: w1w1 white, w2w2 white, w1w2 coloured. That one-gene model also gives 1:1 in the backcross. The F2 tells them apart: 9:7 for two genes against 1:1 for one. So does crossing the F1 with F2 whites: under one gene every white gives 1:1, while under two genes an aabb white gives 1 coloured : 3 white. Intercrossing F2 whites helps too: an aaB_ plant crossed with an A_bb plant can give coloured offspring, while two whites blocked at the same step give only white.

### What the penetrance study can and cannot say

Fahed and colleagues' estimates come from UK Biobank, a cohort healthier than the general population, and the polygenic scores were less precise outside the mostly European ancestry groups they were built on. Penetrance was not one number, and the numbers belong to that cohort.
