**You carry about 15 kilograms of fat and about 100 grams of glycogen in your liver, and your brain burns about 120 grams of glucose a day.** The fat holds months of the brain's energy; the glycogen holds less than a day of its glucose. Yet in a long fast the body guards its glucose and spends muscle to do it. Why? The answer sets the order in which every pathway in this course turns up and down.

## The one-way funnel: why fat cannot become glucose

Hans Krebs saw the shared core: *"all foodstuffs are burnt through a common terminal pathway."* Carbohydrate, fat and protein are stripped to acetyl-CoA, a two-carbon fragment carried on coenzyme A (some amino acids enter as other citric acid cycle intermediates instead), and burned in the same cycle. That is why, in cells with mitochondria, most fuels can pay for ATP.

**Predict.** If every fuel ends up as acetyl-CoA, why can't the liver turn the acetyl-CoA from fat into glucose?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Glucose made from acetyl-CoA would have to pass through pyruvate or oxaloacetate, and acetyl-CoA cannot get back to either.

- **Backward is closed.** Pyruvate dehydrogenase turns pyruvate into acetyl-CoA and releases CO₂. In animals the step cannot run in reverse.
- **Through the cycle gains nothing.** Acetyl-CoA (2 carbons) joins oxaloacetate (4) to make citrate (6), and the cycle releases two CO₂ (6 carbons to 5 to 4) before oxaloacetate comes back. Two carbons in, two out: no extra oxaloacetate for glucose.

Of fat, only the glycerol backbone, about a tenth of its mass, and a little from odd-chain fatty acids can become glucose.

</details>

Some tissues must have glucose. Red blood cells have no mitochondria, so glycolysis is their only source of ATP. Long-chain fatty acids reach brain tissue, and astrocytes burn some, but they supply only a minor share of the brain's energy; neurons rely on other fuels.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/integration-of-metabolism/one-way-funnel.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: Schematic funnel: carbohydrate, fat and protein drain to acetyl-CoA and the citric acid cycle; a one-way barrier blocks acetyl-CoA from returning to pyruvate, and only glycerol from fat and amino acids from protein have side arrows back to glucose." caption="The one-way funnel. Every fuel can be burned through acetyl-CoA, but acetyl-CoA cannot be turned back into glucose: the pyruvate dehydrogenase step runs one way, and the citric acid cycle gives back as CO2 the two carbons it takes in. > **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** The fatty acids of fat can be burned but cannot be turned into glucose (only fat's small glycerol backbone can), and the brain and red blood cells need glucose. So fasting is glucose rationing:
>
> 1. Low insulin switches every tissue that can burn fat over to fat.
> 2. The liver supplies glucose, first from its glycogen, then by making it new (**gluconeogenesis**) from the only new carbon it can use: glycerol from fat's backbone and amino acids, mostly from muscle protein.
> 3. The liver turns surplus fatty acids into ketone bodies, which the brain can burn in place of most of its glucose, so less muscle has to be spent.

This holds for a healthy adult fasting up to weeks; hard exercise strains the cell's own ATP (Going deeper, "AMPK").

## One signal, read differently by each tissue

Many pathways are opposites that would cancel into an ATP-wasting futile cycle if both ran flat out, so two pancreatic hormones set them as a group. **Insulin** rises after a meal and means *store*. **Glucagon** rises when blood glucose falls and means *release*. Both enter the portal vein, which runs straight to the liver, so the liver meets them first and at the highest concentrations. Because they push its enzymes in opposite directions, the liver responds to their balance, the insulin-to-glucagon *ratio*.

| Tissue | Insulin high (fed) | Insulin low, glucagon high | Releases glucose into blood? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Liver | stores glycogen | breaks down glycogen, makes new glucose and ketone bodies | **Yes**: has glucose-6-phosphatase |
| Muscle | takes up glucose (insulin brings GLUT4 to the surface), stores glycogen | burns fatty acids; releases amino acids | **No** |
| Fat tissue | stores fat | insulin's brake on fat breakdown (lipolysis) comes off | No |

**Predict.** Muscle holds three to four times as much glycogen as the liver. Overnight, why doesn't it help hold up your blood glucose?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Glycogen breaks down (through glucose-1-phosphate) to glucose-6-phosphate, whose phosphate traps the sugar in the cell; only cells with glucose-6-phosphatase (chiefly liver and kidney) can remove it. Muscle also lacks glucagon receptors, so it keeps its glycogen for its own work, when epinephrine or hard exercise calls.

</details>

In the liver, glucagon raises cyclic AMP, which turns on protein kinase A (PKA). PKA's phosphates switch glycogen synthase off and glycogen phosphorylase (through phosphorylase kinase) on, so storing stops and breakdown starts at once. Glucagon signaling also keeps **acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC)**, the first enzyme of fat synthesis, phosphorylated and off (the key phosphate is added mainly by AMPK). Insulin reverses both, mainly through a phosphatase. ACC's product, malonyl-CoA, normally blocks the carnitine shuttle (CPT1) that lets fatty acids into mitochondria. Hold that for day three.

## One person's fast, from lunch to five weeks

Follow yourself into a long fast (values rounded from classic human fasting studies). After the first hours blood glucose drifts down only modestly while the liver's source of it changes completely.

| Time since the meal | Blood glucose | Insulin : glucagon | Ketones | The liver's glucose comes from | Brain burns |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 h | about 6–7 mM | high | about 0.1 mM | none exported; storing | glucose |
| 12 h | about 4.5–5 mM | falling | about 0.1–0.2 mM | glycogen and new glucose, about half each | glucose |
| 24 h | about 4–4.5 mM | low | about 0.3–0.5 mM | mostly new glucose | glucose |
| 3 days | about 3.5–4 mM | low | about 2–3 mM | new glucose, kidney's share rising; protein breakdown near its peak | glucose, more ketones |
| 5 weeks | about 3.5–4 mM | low | about 7–8 mM (mostly β-hydroxybutyrate) | new glucose only, about 40 g a day, with the kidney now making about as much again | about two-thirds ketones |

### The first day: store, then spend glycogen

After lunch, high insulin runs the fed column of the tissue table; the liver can also turn surplus carbohydrate into fat, though fat tissue stores mostly dietary fat. Once the gut is empty, glucagon rises and the liver breaks down its glycogen, a finite store that, with new glucose sharing the load (about half each by the end of an overnight fast), is mostly used up within a day or two. Fat tissue releases free fatty acids, the major fuel for muscle and liver, which the funnel keeps from becoming glucose.

### Where the new glucose comes from

In gluconeogenesis the liver, joined more and more by the kidney, makes glucose from lactate, glycerol and amino acids (chiefly alanine for the liver, glutamine for the kidney). The film lists them as equals: as chemistry they are, as accounting they are not.

**Predict.** Red blood cells and working muscle turn glucose into lactate, and the liver rebuilds glucose from it. Does this **Cori cycle** give the brain any *new* glucose?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The Cori cycle, first mapped by the Coris, sends the same carbon around again at a cost to the liver of about 6 ATP equivalents (4 ATP and 2 GTP) per glucose rebuilt, against 2 ATP gained by glycolysis. The brain burns nearly all of its glucose to CO₂, and that carbon does not come back. The brain is the drain; only glycerol and the carbon skeletons of amino acids from broken-down protein bring new carbon.

</details>

In the **glucose-alanine cycle** muscle exports carbon *and* nitrogen as alanine, handing the liver a gluconeogenic substrate (part of its carbon is recycled glucose, like lactate's) and the nitrogen it will dump as urea. Toggle the map to see the traffic shift.

### By day three: ketogenesis in full swing

**Predict.** With ACC off, what happens to fatty acids reaching the liver, and why does a liver burning fat flat out make ketone bodies it cannot use?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Malonyl-CoA is low, so the carnitine shuttle opens and fatty acids pour into the liver's mitochondria. β-oxidation makes acetyl-CoA faster than the liver's own citric acid cycle burns it: the liver needs only so much ATP, and its oxaloacetate, which acetyl-CoA needs to enter the cycle, is being pulled toward gluconeogenesis. ### Weeks: the brain switches, and muscle is spared

If the brain burned only glucose, gluconeogenesis would have to make all 120 grams a day. Glycerol covers about 15–20 grams, so the main raw material would be your own muscle protein, about 180 grams a day. You would consume yourself within weeks.

Ketone bodies are the escape: small and water-soluble, they cross into the brain. As blood ketones climb over days to weeks (see the table), the brain takes up more of them (and, in animal studies, makes more of the transporters that carry them in). Its glucose need falls to roughly 40 grams a day and the protein bill for the brain's share falls to about a fifth. That **protein-sparing** effect is what makes a long fast survivable. These are rough sums; measured losses run lower (Going deeper, "The protein arithmetic").

Tissues respond to the hormone signal, not directly to the blood glucose level. Every row of the fast table is the one idea at work: fat burned wherever possible, glucose rationed for the tissues that need it, ketones stretching the ration. Metabolism is not a list of pathways but one switched, organ-spanning system for keeping you alive between meals.

## Going deeper

### Reciprocal control

Insulin and glucagon also set the bifunctional enzyme PFK-2/FBPase-2, whose product, fructose-2,6-bisphosphate, chooses between glycolysis and gluconeogenesis. These phosphorylation switches act in seconds to minutes, as does epinephrine; cortisol acts over hours through gene expression.

### AMPK

Inside each cell, AMPK reads the AMP:ATP and ADP:ATP ratios. When ATP runs low, as in hard exercise, it shuts off building (ACC among its targets) and turns on burning, answering the cell's own energy stress rather than the meal clock.

### The protein arithmetic

Round numbers for a 70 kg adult: about 180 g of protein a day would strip a third of the body's roughly 10 kg of protein in about two and a half weeks. At about 40 g a day, the fat (about 75 days of energy) runs out first. These show direction and size only; measured protein loss in prolonged starvation is nearer 20 g a day.

### Glycogen and ketogenesis, in detail

Studies of fasting people show liver glycogen still contributing at about 22 hours, with near-total reliance on new glucose by about two to three days. Beyond the malonyl-CoA gate on CPT1, the main driver, fasting raises mitochondrial HMG-CoA synthase, the liver enzyme that commits acetyl-CoA to ketone bodies.

### Two small exceptions

The funnel has two small leaks besides glycerol. Odd-chain fatty acids end in propionyl-CoA, which becomes succinyl-CoA, so they give a little glucose. Plants and many bacteria bypass the cycle's two CO₂ steps with the glyoxylate cycle, which mammals lack.
