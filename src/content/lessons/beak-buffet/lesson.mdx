## The big idea

Hand your child a clothespin and a bowl of floating cereal and they will soon want a different tool. That small frustration is the whole idea of this activity. Each beak shape is good at some jobs and poor at others.

Birds have no hands and no teeth. The beak picks up, cracks, grabs, strains or reaches for food. A beak shape that suits a bird's food is a **physical adaptation**, a body feature that helps it survive. The way a bird uses it, like a heron standing still until a fish swims close, is a **behavioral adaptation**, something the animal does.

Virginia's grade 3 standard adds one more idea: **populations**, groups of the same kind of animal living in one place, can change over many generations. A single bird does not grow a new beak because its food changed. Keep that line clear when your child asks.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time. Wipe up spilled water and pick up dropped beans or seeds right away so no one slips. Empty the bowls and pan when you finish, and keep toddlers away from them, since a young child can drown in an inch or two of water.
- Beans, seeds, cereal, pasta and cut-up sponge are choking hazards for children under 4, and little ones sometimes push small things like these into their noses or ears. Keep them out of reach of younger brothers and sisters.
- Nobody eats or drinks anything from the stations. The food has been handled and soaked, and raw dry beans of any kind can make a person sick, so keep them out of mouths. Do not use red kidney beans, which are the most toxic: as few as four or five raw ones can cause severe vomiting.
- Check for food allergies (wheat, and seeds if you use sunflower seeds) and for celiac disease or other gluten-free needs before you choose the foods. Many cereals, most pasta and most play dough contain wheat. If anyone has a wheat allergy or must avoid gluten, swap in yarn, sponge, foam or beans, and have everyone wash their hands afterward.
- Give each child their own straw, and use it as a dropper with a finger over the top, not in the mouth.
- No toothpicks, skewers or other sharp points. Tongs and chopsticks do the job; keep chopstick and tweezer tips pointing down and away from faces. An adult does any cutting of sponge or foam.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Set the buffet.** Put out five stations around the table: seeds, pond food, fish, worms and nectar. Lay the five beak tools in a row.
2. **Learn the rules.** A bird has no hands, so only the beak touches the food. Your child holds the beak tool in one hand and the stomach cup in the other. Food goes from the station into the cup.
3. **Predict.** At each station, ask, "Which beak do you think will get the most food?" Write the guess down.
4. **Run timed trials.** At each station, your child picks three beaks and tries each one for 30 seconds. Count the pieces in the cup after each try, then tip them back. For the nectar, dip the straw in the water, put a finger over the top, lift it over the empty cup, and take your finger off. Count the strawfuls.
5. **Find the winners.** Circle the best beak for each food. Often the clothespin wins at seeds, the slotted spoon at pond food, tongs at fish, tweezers at worms and the straw at nectar. Your results may differ, and that is useful to talk about.
6. **Meet the real birds.** Show the bird pictures. A northern cardinal has a thick, cone-shaped beak for cracking seeds. A mallard has a flat bill with comb-like edges that strain food from water. A great blue heron has a long, pointed bill for grabbing fish. A ruby-throated hummingbird has a long, thin bill and a long tongue for nectar. A Carolina wren has a thin, slightly curved bill for picking insects off leaves and bark.
7. **Talk about behavior.** Ask how each bird uses its beak. A heron stands still and waits. A mallard tips its tail up and reaches under the water. A hummingbird hovers in front of a flower.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "What do you think will happen if we try the clothespin on the floating cereal?"
- "I wonder which beak will be best at every food. Is there one?"
- "If all the seeds were gone, which bird would have a hard time? What could it do?"
- "What do you think a hummingbird would do at the seed station?"
- "Was it only the tool, or also how you used it? What helped?"
- "Birds don't have teeth. What do you think happens to a seed after a cardinal swallows it?"

## Why it works

Beak shape and food fit together because of a long history. In any bird population, beaks vary a little. When food is scarce, birds whose beaks handle the food that is left are more likely to survive and raise chicks, and chicks tend to inherit their parents' beak shape. Over many generations the average beak in the population shifts. This is **natural selection**. Scientists watched it happen on a Galápagos island in 1977. A drought left mostly large, hard seeds, and medium ground finches with deeper beaks survived better.

Your tools are rough models. A real mallard's bill has comb-like plates called **lamellae** along its edges, which let water out and hold food in. Your slotted spoon does the same job. Behavior matters as much as shape, and your child's technique will change the counts.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use three stations (seeds, pond food, fish) and three tools. Skip the timer and count to 20 out loud instead. Your child can draw the winning beak at each station rather than write numbers. Show one real bird for each station.

### Ages 8 to 10
Make it a fair test. Use the same time, the same amount of food and the same person for every trial. Run each trial twice and average the counts. Draw a bar graph for one station. Then change one thing, such as removing all the beans, and ask which "bird" would go hungry first.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If counts are all close, the trials are too long or the food is too easy. Cut trials to 20 seconds or spread the food out more. If the cereal sinks or turns to mush, use fresh cereal or small foam pieces. If your child reaches for the food with their hands, remind them that in this game a bird uses only its beak, then let them try one round "beak only" and one round with hands to see the difference.
