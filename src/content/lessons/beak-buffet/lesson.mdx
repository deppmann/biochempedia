## The big idea

Which beak works best for each food? In about 35 minutes, your child can test five kitchen tools and compare what each one picks up. Give your child a clothespin and a bowl of floating cereal. See what happens before trying a different tool.

> **Say it together:** "A bird has no hands and no teeth. Its beak does the job of a fork, a spoon and a straw. Each beak shape works best for a certain food."

A beak picks up, cracks, grabs, strains or reaches for food. A beak shape that fits a bird's food is a **physical adaptation**, a body feature that helps it survive. Test beak shapes first, then add how the bird uses them. Waiting still until a fish swims close is a **behavioral adaptation**, something the animal does.

Keep one more line clear if your child asks: a single bird does not grow a new beak because its food changed. Instead, **populations**—groups of the same kind of animal living in one place—can shift over many generations.

<SafetyNote>
- **Supervision and water:** Stay with your child the whole time. Keep toddlers away from the bowls and pan. Never leave a child alone near the water. A young child can drown in an inch or two of water. Wipe up spilled water and pick up dropped beans or seeds right away so no one slips. Empty the bowls and pan when you finish.
- **Choking:** Beans, seeds, cereal, pasta, short pieces of yarn, and small bits of play dough, sponge or foam are choking hazards for children under 4. Keep all small pieces out of their reach. Young children may also put small pieces in their noses or ears. **Call 911 if choking.**
- **No eating or tasting:** Nobody eats or drinks anything from the stations. The food has been handled and soaked, and raw dry beans of any kind can make a person sick. Do not use red kidney beans, which are the most toxic: as few as four or five raw ones can cause severe vomiting. If someone swallows raw dry beans, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222.
- **Food allergies and dyes:** Before choosing foods, check for allergies to wheat, sunflower seeds or beans. Check for dyes in cereal or play dough, possible nut cross-contact and any gluten-free needs, including celiac disease. Many cereals, most pasta and most play dough contain wheat. Choose yarn, sponge or foam as needed. Use beans as a substitute only if they are safe for everyone taking part. Do not substitute nuts.
- **Tools and clean hands:** Give each child their own straw, and use it as a dropper with a finger over the top, never in the mouth. No toothpicks, skewers or other sharp points. Keep chopstick and tweezer tips pointing down and away from faces. An adult does any cutting of sponge or foam. When you finish, everyone washes their hands with soap and water before eating or touching their face.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Set the buffet.** Put five stations around the table: seeds, pond food, fish, worms and nectar. Lay the five beak tools in a row.
2. **Teach one rule and try one practice move.** Say: *"A bird has no hands, so only your beak tool can touch the food."* (Some real birds, such as hawks and chickadees, also hold food with their feet.) Have your child hold the tool in one hand and the stomach cup in the other, and move one piece into the cup. Show the nectar move separately: dip the straw in water, cover the top with a finger, lift it over the empty cup, and lift the finger off.
3. **Predict first, then check.** At each station, have your child pick three beaks. For pond food, include the clothespin and slotted spoon. Ask, *"Which beak do you think will get the most food?"* Write down the guess before you start the timer.
4. **Run timed trials.** Try each chosen beak for 30 seconds. Count what lands in the cup, then tip it back before the next try. For nectar, count strawfuls. At the pond station, watch what each tool picks up and whether the water drains away. Compare the results with the guess.
5. **Find the winners.** Circle the best beak for each food. Often the clothespin wins at seeds, the slotted spoon at pond food, tongs at fish, tweezers at worms and the straw at nectar. If your results differ, ask: *"Why did that tool work well for you?"*
6. **Match shapes to real birds first.** Show the bird pictures and pair each tool with a bill shape:
   - **Northern cardinal:** thick, cone-shaped beak for cracking seeds.
   - **Mallard:** flat bill with comb-like edges that strain food from water.
   - **Great blue heron:** long, pointed bill for grabbing fish.
   - **Ruby-throated hummingbird:** long, thin bill and a long tongue for nectar.
   - **Carolina wren:** thin, slightly curved bill for picking insects off leaves and bark.
7. **Add behavior, then debrief.** Now ask: *"Shape is only half of it. How does each bird use its beak?"* A heron stands still and waits. A mallard tips its tail up and reaches under water. A hummingbird hovers in front of a flower. Close with two quick questions to bring your child back to the big idea:
   - *"Was one beak the best for every food, or did each food need a different shape?"*
   - *"If your beak worked like a slotted spoon, where would you look for dinner?"*

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "What do you think will happen if we try the clothespin on the floating cereal?"
- "I wonder which beak will be best at every food. Is there one?"
- "If all the seeds were gone, which bird would have a hard time? What could it do?"
- "What do you think a hummingbird would do at the seed station?"
- "Was it only the tool, or also how you used it? What helped?"
- "Birds don't have teeth. What do you think happens to a seed after a cardinal swallows it?"

## Why it works

Beak shape and food fit together because of a long history. In any bird population, beaks vary a little. When food is scarce, birds whose beaks handle what is left are more likely to survive and raise chicks. Chicks tend to inherit their parents' beak shape, so over many generations the average beak shifts. This is **natural selection**. Scientists watched it happen on a Galápagos island in 1977: a drought left mostly large, hard seeds, and medium ground finches with deeper beaks survived better.

Your tools are rough models. A clothespin picks up a seed, but a cardinal's thick beak also cracks the hard shell open. A mallard's bill has comb-like plates called **lamellae** along its edges that let water out and hold food in, much like your slotted spoon. Behavior matters too, and your child's technique will change the counts.

> **For teachers (Virginia SOL 1.5 b, 3.4 a, 3.4 b):** Virginia's grade 3 standard connects beak shape (physical adaptation) and feeding habits (behavioral adaptation) to populations changing over many generations—not single animals changing on demand.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use three stations (seeds, pond food, fish) and three tools. Skip the timer and count to 20 out loud instead. Your child can draw the winning beak at each station instead of writing numbers. Show one real bird for each station.

### Ages 8 to 10
Make it a fair test. Use the same time, the same amount of food and the same person for every trial. Run each trial twice and average the counts, then draw a bar graph for one station. Next, change one thing—such as removing all the beans—and ask which "bird" would go hungry first.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If counts are all close, the trials may be too long or the food too easy. Cut trials to 20 seconds or spread the food out more. If the cereal sinks or turns to mush, swap in fresh cereal or small foam pieces. If your child grabs food with their hands, remind them that in this game a bird uses only its beak. Then try one round "beak only" and one round with hands to see the difference.
