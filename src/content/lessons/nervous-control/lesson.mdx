scientists:
  - name: "Hermann von Helmholtz"
    contribution: >-
      Stimulated a frog's sciatic nerve at two distances from the calf muscle and timed the
      difference in delay to the twitch, giving a finite conduction speed of a few tens of metres
      per second in that preparation.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Helmholtz, Messungen über den zeitlichen Verlauf der Zuckung animalischer Muskeln und die Fortpflanzungsgeschwindigkeit der Reizung in den Nerven, Archiv für Anatomie, Physiologie und wissenschaftliche Medicin (1850); Schmidgen, The Helmholtz Curves (Fordham University Press, 2014)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Helmholtz was a physician who also worked as a physicist, and in 1850 he turned physics on a
      question many physiologists of his day treated as out of reach: how fast does a nerve carry
      its signal? He worked with an isolated frog calf muscle, the gastrocnemius, still attached to
      its sciatic nerve. He gave the nerve a brief electric stimulus close to the muscle and timed
      the delay before the muscle began to twitch, then repeated it with the stimulus placed farther
      up the nerve. The farther point always added a small, measurable delay. The trick was the
      subtraction. Whatever time the muscle itself took to respond was the same in both trials, so
      the extra delay belonged to the extra length of nerve alone. Dividing that length by that time
      gave a speed of a few tens of metres per second. The result made the nerve signal a physical
      process with a speed that could be measured, and opened the timing of nerves and reflexes to
      experiment.

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Sherrington spent decades studying reflexes in animals whose spinal cord had been separated
      from the brain, so that what remained was the cord's own work. In such preparations a stimulus
      to the skin or a stretch of a muscle produced a movement that was repeatable enough to measure.
      He found that a reflex was never a single wire from one sensory nerve to one muscle. When a
      flexor muscle was excited, the motor neurons of its opposing extensor were inhibited at the
      same time, an arrangement he called reciprocal innervation. He gathered this work into lectures
      given at Yale and published in 1906 as The Integrative Action of the Nervous System, where the
      reflex became the building block of coordination. He shared the 1932 Nobel Prize in Physiology
      or Medicine with Edgar Adrian. His fuller story is told in the Neuroscience lesson on motor
      units and reflexes; here the point is the reflex arc as a unit you can trace and time.

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Langley worked at Cambridge on the nerves that run to the organs rather than to skeletal
      muscle. These nerves pass through clusters of nerve cell bodies, the ganglia, on their way out,
      and the question was where each fibre made its connection. With W. L. Dickinson he applied
      nicotine to a ganglion and found that, at the right dose, stimulating nerve fibres entering the
      ganglion no longer had an effect on the organ, while stimulating fibres leaving it still did.
      That located a junction inside the ganglion, and the method let him trace which fibres relayed
      where, one ganglion at a time. Over many years he sorted the outflow into a thoracolumbar part,
      which kept the older name sympathetic, and a cranial and sacral part, which he called parasympathetic. He
      gave the whole system the name autonomic, and his 1921 book set out the scheme. The two-neuron
      chain and the two divisions in textbook figures today still follow his map.

techniques:
  - name: "Two-point timing of nerve conduction"
    blurb: >-
      Stimulate a nerve at two known distances from the muscle or recording site and measure the
      delay from each. The difference in distance divided by the difference in time gives the
      conduction velocity, because delays common to both trials, such as the junction and the
      muscle's own response, cancel.
  - name: "Pharmacological blockade of autonomic input"
    blurb: >-
      Give a drug that blocks one division's effect on an organ, then the other, then both, and
      measure the organ's output each time. The change after each block shows how much that division
      was contributing; with both blocked, the organ shows its intrinsic behaviour.
  - name: "Microneurography"
    blurb: >-
      Insert a fine needle electrode through the skin into a peripheral nerve of a conscious human
      and record the impulses of single identified nerve fibres, such as the sensory fibres from
      muscle spindles, while the person moves or the limb is stretched.
  - name: "Comparative anatomy of nervous systems"
    blurb: >-
      Dissect, section and stain the nervous systems of animals from different groups and map where
      the cell bodies, cords and ganglia lie. Comparing the maps shows which arrangements recur and
      which are confined to one lineage.

**Tap the tendon below your kneecap and your thigh muscle fires about 20 milliseconds (ms, thousandths of a second) later; the leg swings soon after, before you decide anything. Step on a tack and your foot lifts too, but later. Where does the time go?**

> **The one idea.** A nervous response runs a route: receptor (which detects the change), sensory neuron, integrating centre (where the spinal cord or brain passes the signal on), motor neuron, effector (the muscle or gland that responds). Its time is the sum of its legs: each axon's length over its speed, about a millisecond per synapse in the cord, and short delays at the ends.
>
> **response time ≈ (L/v for each axon, added up) + (synapses in the cord × about 1 ms) + end delays**, where L is an axon's length, v its **conduction velocity** (the impulse's speed along it), and the end delays are at the receptor (from stimulus to firing), the nerve-muscle junction and the muscle.
>
> *Scope:* a simple model of a chain of neurons; numbers below are model numbers unless marked measured. The 1 ms per synapse is a teaching assumption: real synapses and cord processing vary and often take longer, so a delay suggests a synapse count but cannot prove it. Nor does the sum say what the effector does: whether the heart speeds up or slows down depends on which division reaches it, its transmitter and the receptor that transmitter acts on.

## One neuron: what the first film left out

The first film named a **neuron**'s (nerve cell's) parts, from **dendrites** to **axon terminals** (the **cell body**, or soma, also holds the nucleus), and its **all-or-none** impulse, the **action potential**, a brief, self-renewing electrical change across the membrane. So a stronger stimulus makes the neuron fire more often, and can bring more neurons into action, but cannot make an impulse taller. How ions moving through membrane channels make the impulse is in [the action potential](/lessons/action-potential/) and [the resting membrane](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/); the resting voltage of -70 mV in many figures is an illustration, and real values vary between cells.

Where the film's terminal released its chemical is a **synapse**, a junction between cells; the chemical is a **neurotransmitter**, and the gap it crosses is the synaptic cleft. Some synapses are electrical, with ions passing straight between cells ([synaptic transmission](/lessons/synaptic-transmission/)). The film's wrapping is **myelin**, layers of membrane from **glial cells**, the nervous system's support cells (Schwann cells outside the brain and spinal cord, oligodendrocytes inside); its bare gaps are the **nodes of Ranvier** ([myelination](/lessons/myelination/); for neurons as separate cells, [the neuron doctrine](/lessons/neuron-doctrine/)).

## How fast? What Helmholtz's subtraction can tell you

The first film showed Helmholtz's 1850 two-point timing of a frog nerve and its answer, a few tens of metres per second.

**Predict.** Had Helmholtz skipped the subtraction and divided whole length by whole delay, what speed would he report for an illustrative nerve with a true speed of 25 m per s, stimulated 40 mm from the muscle, with 2 ms of junction and muscle delay?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

About 11 m per s, less than half the true speed. The nerve takes 0.040 m / 25 m per s = 1.6 ms, so the whole delay is 3.6 ms, and 0.040 m / 0.0036 s is about 11 m per s. The difference between two trials gives 25 m per s whatever the shared delay.

</details>

The subtraction rules in a physical process of finite speed in this preparation, not what the signal is. And the number is the frog's: a frog is an **ectotherm**, whose body temperature follows its surroundings, and cooling slows conduction in a given fibre, so a room-temperature frog value is not a human value at 37 °C.

> **conduction velocity v = difference in distance / difference in time**

**Worked problem.** These numbers are illustrative, not Helmholtz's.

1. *Velocity from two points.* Two stimulus points on a frog nerve are 30 mm apart. Contraction starts 1.2 ms later from the far point than from the near one. v = 0.030 m / 0.0012 s = 25 m per s.
2. *The knee jerk, in this model.* The film's sensory and motor axons are each about 0.5 m at 60 m per s: 0.5 / 60 = 0.0083 s each, so with 1.0 ms for the synapse, 8.3 + 8.3 + 1.0 = 17.7 ms, and an assumed 1.5 ms at the junction and muscle makes about 19 ms. The measured adult latency, about 20 ms, runs from the tap to the start of the thigh muscle's electrical response; the sum leaves out the short time from tap to spindle firing, so the near match is not a full accounting. The visible kick begins tens of milliseconds later, while the muscle builds force.
3. *The same path without speed.* At 1 m per s each axon takes 0.5 s, so axons and synapse take 500 + 500 + 1 = 1,001 ms instead of 17.7 ms, about 57 times longer.

<ReflexLatencySum />

In this model axon time is the large term, and two main features of the axon set its speed (temperature and other membrane properties matter too). **Width:** a wider axon offers less resistance to the current spreading ahead of the impulse, so the impulse travels faster. **Myelin:** the impulse is renewed only at the nodes, not along every bit of membrane, so it skips ahead. The squid *Loligo*'s unmyelinated giant axon, about half a millimetre across, conducts at roughly 20 m per s near sea temperature; the largest myelinated vertebrate motor axons, about 20 micrometres across, conduct faster still. Myelin buys speed without width, so a vertebrate fits many fast axons into one nerve.

The sum has three kinds of term: axons, synapses and end delays. The second film gives two reflexes the same end delays and races them: does the slower one lose its time on axons or at synapses?

## The reflex arc: what the second film left out

Both reflexes run through the spinal cord, which with the brain makes up the **central nervous system** (CNS). The **peripheral nervous system** (PNS) is everything outside: nerves and **ganglia**, clusters of nerve cell bodies. Its **somatic** division drives skeletal muscle; its **autonomic** division drives cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands ([nervous system map](/lessons/nervous-system-map/)).

A **reflex** is a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus; its route is a **reflex arc**. The film skipped how the knee jerk starts: the tap stretches the quadriceps through its tendon, so its **muscle spindles**, stretch receptors, fire through large, fast sensory fibres called Ia fibres. Of the film's **monosynaptic** path and **interneurons** (neurons wholly within the CNS), one detail: in the knee jerk only the direct excitatory path lacks an interneuron ([motor units and reflexes](/lessons/motor-units-reflexes/)).

| Sensory axon | 0.5 m at 60 m per s: 8.3 ms | 0.9 m at 20 m per s: 45 ms |
| Synapses in the cord | 1, about 1 ms | 3, about 3 ms |
| Motor axon | 0.5 m at 60 m per s: 8.3 ms | 0.9 m at 60 m per s: 15 ms |
| Junction and muscle | about 1.5 ms | about 1.5 ms |
| **Total** | **about 19 ms** | **about 65 ms** |

Measured human withdrawals are usually slower. After electrical stimulation of the skin or a sensory nerve of the foot, leg-muscle responses commonly begin near a tenth of a second (latency varies with stimulus, muscle and task), and the foot lifts later still: pain fibres can be slower than 20 m per s, and cord processing adds time, in shares that vary.

**Predict.** You can time a reflex and measure its axons, but you cannot see its synapses. How could a clock estimate how many there are?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Subtract. The **central delay**, the latency minus axon conduction time and the delays at receptor, junction and muscle, is the time left in the cord. If each synapse takes about 1 ms and little else adds time, near 1 ms fits one synapse and near 3 ms about three in series: an inference, not a count, which anatomy or other recordings must confirm. Measure the conduction speeds too: set a slow fibre's speed too high and its extra time looks like extra synapses.

</details>

## Two autonomic divisions and their exceptions

Adding up the legs also works for routes to the heart, gut and glands, but the knee jerk's numbers do not carry over. Their motor path usually has two neurons in series, a **preganglionic neuron** in the CNS and a **postganglionic neuron** whose cell body sits in a peripheral ganglion and whose axon reaches the organ. That adds a synapse in the ganglion and an axon, and cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands have end delays unlike skeletal muscle's. Whether the organ speeds up or slows down depends on the division, not the sum. John Newport Langley, who named the autonomic nervous system, divided its outflow into two and set apart the gut's own **enteric** network, the neurons in its wall. In humans, the **sympathetic division** leaves from the thoracic and upper lumbar spinal cord (thoracolumbar). The **parasympathetic division** leaves from the brainstem, in cranial nerves such as the vagus, and from the sacral cord (craniosacral). Where the sacral outflow belongs is debated: a 2016 mouse study proposed calling it sympathetic, which is disputed; this course keeps the standard grouping.

| Human target | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
|---|---|---|
| Heart pacemaker | Raises heart rate (norepinephrine) | Lowers heart rate (acetylcholine, vagus) |
| Pupil | Dilates (radial iris muscle) | Constricts (circular iris muscle) |
| Gut | Inhibits peristalsis (the waves that push food along) | Promotes peristalsis and digestion |
| Most blood vessels | Main neural control of tone | Little or no direct supply |
| Sweat glands (eccrine) | Supplied, and the fibres release acetylcholine | No supply |

The divisions oppose each other at the heart, the iris and the gut, but not everywhere. Most vessels and the sweat glands have only a sympathetic supply, turned up or down. In the rabbit parotid salivary gland, sympathetic stimulation increased flow on top of a parasympathetic background secretion, so there the two worked in the same direction. For hypothalamic control see [autonomic control and the hypothalamus](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/); for transmitters and drugs, [transmitter systems](/lessons/transmitter-systems/). Hormones, the second kind of control, are in [the endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/), and the pacemaker in [circulation and the heart](/lessons/circulation-and-heart/).

### Design problem

The numbers below are illustrative, in the pattern of human drug-blockade studies such as Jose and Collison's (1970). Healthy young adults at rest have a heart rate of 70 beats per minute. After atropine, which blocks the parasympathetic effect on the heart, it is 110. After propranolol, which blocks the sympathetic effect through beta receptors (the pacemaker's receptors for norepinephrine), it is 62. After both drugs, it is 100. Which division dominates the resting heart rate, and how strong is each?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

With both divisions blocked, the pacemaker runs at its **intrinsic rate**, the rate with no autonomic input: about 100 beats per minute here. Measure every effect from it. The resting rate, 70, is 30 below, so the net autonomic effect at rest is braking.

With only parasympathetic input left (propranolol), the rate is 62: vagal braking takes it 38 below intrinsic. With only sympathetic input left (atropine), it is 110: sympathetic drive adds 10. So at rest the parasympathetic division dominates, and sympathetic drive adds a little. The two effects, -38 and +10, sum to -28, close to the observed -30 but not exactly, so they do not combine as simple addition.

One check: the conclusion holds only if each drug fully blocks its own division, and nothing else, at the dose used.

</details>

## Four layouts of neurons

Every animal with neurons links sensing to acting, but the layouts differ, and a nerve net has no separate integrating centre (the groups are in [animal body plans](/lessons/animal-body-plans/)).

- **Hydra**, a freshwater cnidarian, has a **nerve net**: neurons spread through the body wall and connected in a mesh, with no brain.
- A **planarian**, a free-living flatworm, has two nerve cords along its underside, joined by cross-connections like the rungs of a ladder, and a ganglion in the head.
- The earthworm **Lumbricus**, an annelid, has a single ventral nerve cord with a ganglion in each body segment, linked to a pair of ganglia above the pharynx near the front.
- **Vertebrates**, such as a frog or a human, have a **dorsal hollow nerve cord**, the spinal cord, running along the back, with a brain at its front end.

Flatworm and earthworm cords are solid. Hydra's net is a working nervous system in a living lineage, not an unfinished brain.

### Helmholtz's apparatus and the speed numbers

Helmholtz timed these tiny intervals with a galvanometer method and with a recording myograph that wrote the twitch on a moving surface. His teacher Johannes Müller had doubted that the speed could be measured at all. The squid giant axon is sometimes wider than half a millimetre; Hodgkin and Huxley's 1952 analysis of the impulse used it ([action potential](/lessons/action-potential/)). Vertebrate motor axons range from a few to about 20 micrometres.
