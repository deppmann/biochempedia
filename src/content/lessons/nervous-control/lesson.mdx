      Helmholtz was a physician who also worked as a physicist, and in 1850 he turned physics on a

      its signal? He worked with an isolated frog calf muscle, the gastrocnemius, still attached to

      the extra delay belonged to the extra length of nerve alone. Dividing that length by that time

      process with a speed that could be measured, and opened the timing of nerves and reflexes to
      experiment.

techniques:
  - name: "Two-point timing of nerve conduction"
    blurb: >-
      Stimulate a nerve at two known distances from the muscle or recording site and measure the
      delay from each. The difference in distance divided by the difference in time gives the
      conduction velocity, because delays common to both trials, such as the junction and the
      muscle's own response, cancel.
  - name: "Pharmacological blockade of autonomic input"
    blurb: >-
      Give a drug that blocks one division's effect on an organ, then the other, then both, and
      measure the organ's output each time. The change after each block shows how much that division
      was contributing; with both blocked, the organ shows its intrinsic behaviour.
  - name: "Microneurography"
    blurb: >-
      Insert a fine needle electrode through the skin into a peripheral nerve of a conscious human
      and record the impulses of single identified nerve fibres, such as the sensory fibres from
      muscle spindles, while the person moves or the limb is stretched.
  - name: "Comparative anatomy of nervous systems"
    blurb: >-
      Dissect, section and stain the nervous systems of animals from different groups and map where
      the cell bodies, cords and ganglia lie. Comparing the maps shows which arrangements recur and
      which are confined to one lineage.

**A nervous system does one job again and again: a sense organ detects a change, neurons carry the signal to a circuit, and a motor neuron makes a muscle or gland respond. In 1850, in a frog's leg, Hermann von Helmholtz timed such a signal and found it ran at a few tens of metres per second: about the speed of a car on a motorway.**

The nerve signal has a speed you can calculate from a ruler and a clock. This lesson meets the neuron that carries the signal, shows how its speed is worked out, compares how four animal groups arrange their neurons, traces the simplest circuits from a sense organ to a muscle, the knee jerk and the withdrawal reflex, and looks at the division that runs your heart and gut without asking you.

## One neuron: in at the dendrites, out along the axon

A **neuron** is a nerve cell: a cell specialized to carry electrical signals and pass them to other cells. A typical vertebrate motor neuron has four parts. The **dendrites** are short branches that receive signals from other cells. The **cell body** (soma) holds the nucleus and gathers that input. The **axon** is a single long extension that carries the signal away from the cell body, sometimes for a metre or more in a large mammal. The **axon terminals** at its far end pass the signal on.

The signal travelling along the axon is an **action potential**, or nerve impulse: a brief, self-renewing electrical change across the cell membrane. It is **all-or-none**. Once the stimulus passes a threshold, the impulse fires at full size; below threshold, no impulse is sent along the axon. So a stronger stimulus does not make an impulse taller. It makes the neuron fire more often, and it can bring more neurons into action. How the impulse is made, by ions moving through channels in the membrane, is the subject of [the action potential](/lessons/action-potential/) and [the resting membrane](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/) in Neuroscience. Figures often show a resting voltage of -70 mV; that is an illustration, and real values vary between cells.

Where an axon meets the next cell is a **synapse**. At most synapses the cells do not touch: the arriving impulse makes the terminal release a chemical, a **neurotransmitter**, which crosses a narrow gap, the synaptic cleft, and acts on the next cell. Some synapses are electrical instead, with ions passing straight from cell to cell. The steps of release are in [synaptic transmission](/lessons/synaptic-transmission/).

Many vertebrate axons are wrapped in **myelin**, layers of membrane from glial cells (Schwann cells outside the brain and spinal cord, oligodendrocytes inside). The impulse is renewed only at gaps between wrappings ([myelination](/lessons/myelination/); for neurons as separate cells, [the neuron doctrine](/lessons/neuron-doctrine/)).

## How fast? Helmholtz times a frog nerve

Helmholtz used an isolated frog gastrocnemius (calf) muscle still attached to its sciatic nerve. He stimulated the nerve with a brief electric shock near the muscle and timed the delay before the muscle began to twitch. Then he stimulated it farther up and timed it again.

Two stimulating electrodes sit on the nerve, one near the muscle and one farther up, with a bracket marking the extra length of nerve between them. An inset shows the nerve-muscle junction and muscle as two blocks." caption="Helmholtz stimulated a frog's sciatic nerve near the gastrocnemius and then farther up, and timed the delay to the twitch each time. The junction and muscle respond in the same time in both trials, so the extra delay belongs only to the extra length of nerve." />

Helmholtz saw that the farther stimulus point always added a small, repeatable delay. The experiment works by subtraction. The time the junction and the muscle take to respond is the same in both trials, so the *extra* delay belongs only to the *extra* length of nerve. His estimate came out at a few tens of metres per second.

That rules in a physical process of finite speed in this preparation. It does not show what the signal is, and the nerve of an ectothermic frog, an animal whose body temperature follows its surroundings, at room temperature is not a human nerve at 37 degrees C.

> **conduction velocity v = difference in distance / difference in time**

**Worked problem.** These numbers are illustrative, not Helmholtz's.

1. *Velocity from two points.* Two stimulus points on a frog nerve are 30 mm apart. Contraction starts 1.2 ms later from the far point than from the near one. v = 0.030 m / 0.0012 s = 25 m per s.
2. *A model reflex.* Suppose a reflex path in a tall adult human has a 1.0 m sensory axon and a 1.0 m motor axon, both conducting at 50 m per s, joined by one synapse with a 1.0 ms delay. Ignore the time the sense organ, the nerve-muscle junction and the muscle take. Each axon takes 1.0 m / 50 m per s = 0.020 s. Total = 0.020 + 0.020 + 0.001 = 0.041 s, or 41 ms.
3. *The same path without speed.* Make both axons conduct at 1 m per s. Each now takes 1.0 s, and the total is 1.0 + 1.0 + 0.001 = 2.001 s, about 49 times longer (2.001 / 0.041 = 48.8).

This is a model sum, not a measured knee-jerk latency. Animals have reached fast conduction in two ways. The giant axon of the squid *Loligo* has no myelin and is fast because it is very wide. Vertebrate motor axons are far narrower, yet the larger myelinated ones conduct as fast or faster. Hodgkin and Huxley's 1952 analysis of the impulse used that squid axon ([action potential](/lessons/action-potential/)).

## Nerve nets, cords and brains in four groups

Animals with neurons all carry signals from where they are sensed to where they are acted on, but they arrange the neurons differently (the groups are in [animal body plans](/lessons/animal-body-plans/)).

- **Hydra**, a freshwater cnidarian, has a **nerve net**: neurons spread through the body wall and connected in a mesh, with no brain.
- A **planarian**, a free-living flatworm, has two nerve cords running along its underside, joined by cross-connections like the rungs of a ladder, and a cluster of neurons, a **ganglion**, in the head.
- The earthworm **Lumbricus**, an annelid, has a single ventral nerve cord with a ganglion in each body segment, linked to a pair of ganglia above the pharynx near the front.
- **Vertebrates**, such as a frog or a human, have a **dorsal hollow nerve cord**, the spinal cord, running along the back, with a brain at its front end.

Flatworm and earthworm cords are ventral and solid; the vertebrate cord is dorsal and hollow. None is a step toward another: Hydra's net is a working nervous system in a living lineage, not an unfinished brain.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/nervous-control/four-nervous-systems.webp" width={1200} height={740} alt="AI-generated illustration: four animals in a row, each with its nervous system in gold. Hydra has a mesh-like nerve net; a planarian has two cords joined by rungs and a head ganglion; an earthworm has a ventral cord with a ganglion in each segment and paired ganglia at the front; a frog has a hollow dorsal spinal cord and a brain." caption="Hydra has a nerve net with no brain, a planarian two cords joined like ladder rungs with a head ganglion, Lumbricus a ventral cord with a ganglion in each segment, and a vertebrate a dorsal hollow cord with a brain. In vertebrates the **central nervous system** (CNS) is the brain and spinal cord. The **peripheral nervous system** (PNS) is everything outside them: the nerves and ganglia. Its motor output has two parts. The **somatic** division drives skeletal muscle. The **autonomic** division drives cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands. Somatic does not mean voluntary, as the next section shows. The full map: [nervous system map](/lessons/nervous-system-map/).

## The reflex arc

A **reflex** is a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus. Its pathway, the **reflex arc**, has five parts: a **receptor** that detects the stimulus, a **sensory neuron** that carries it into the CNS, an **integrating centre** in the CNS, a **motor neuron** that carries the output, and an **effector**, the muscle or gland that responds.

In the **knee-jerk (stretch) reflex** of humans, a tap on the tendon below the kneecap stretches the quadriceps muscle at the front of the thigh. Stretch receptors in the muscle, the **muscle spindles**, fire. Their sensory fibres (called Ia fibres) enter the spinal cord and synapse directly on the motor neurons of the same quadriceps; the muscle contracts and the leg kicks. That limb has one synapse in the cord: it is **monosynaptic**. At the same time, branches of the Ia fibres excite small **interneurons**, neurons that lie wholly within the CNS, which inhibit the motor neurons of the opposing hamstring muscles. Only the direct excitatory limb is monosynaptic. The knee jerk is a somatic reflex: its effector is skeletal muscle, and no decision is involved.

The **withdrawal reflex**, pulling your foot off a tack, works through interneurons from the start. The pain-sensing endings of sensory neurons in the skin fire, and those sensory neurons excite interneurons in the cord. These activate motor neurons of flexor muscles that lift the limb, while other interneurons inhibit the opposing extensors. Many reflexes use interneurons, though the direct limb of the knee jerk does not. Charles Sherrington, working with mammals, made such reflexes the units of coordination; more is in [motor units and reflexes](/lessons/motor-units-reflexes/).

## Two autonomic divisions and their exceptions

The autonomic route to an organ usually has two neurons in series: a **preganglionic neuron** in the CNS, and a **postganglionic neuron** whose cell body sits in a peripheral ganglion and whose axon reaches the organ. John Newport Langley, who named the autonomic nervous system, divided its outflow into two, and set the gut's own enteric network apart. In humans, the **sympathetic division** leaves from the thoracic and upper lumbar spinal cord (thoracolumbar). The **parasympathetic division** leaves from the brainstem, in cranial nerves such as the vagus, and from the sacral cord (craniosacral). Where the sacral outflow belongs is disputed; this course keeps the standard grouping (see Going deeper).

| Human target | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
|---|---|---|
| Heart pacemaker | Raises heart rate (norepinephrine) | Lowers heart rate (acetylcholine, vagus) |
| Pupil | Dilates (radial iris muscle) | Constricts (circular iris muscle) |
| Gut | Inhibits peristalsis | Promotes peristalsis and digestion |
| Most blood vessels | Main neural control of tone | Little or no direct supply |
| Sweat glands (eccrine) | Supplied, and the fibres release acetylcholine | No supply |

The divisions oppose each other at the heart, the iris and the gut. They do not oppose everywhere. Most vessels and the sweat glands have only a sympathetic supply, so their control is turned up and down by one division. In one rabbit salivary gland, the parotid, the two divisions worked in the same direction (see Going deeper). For hypothalamic control see [autonomic control and the hypothalamus](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/); for transmitters and drugs, [transmitter systems](/lessons/transmitter-systems/). Hormones, the second kind of control, are in [the endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/), and the pacemaker in [circulation and the heart](/lessons/circulation-and-heart/).

### Design problem

The numbers below are illustrative, in the pattern of human drug-blockade studies such as Jose and Collison's (1970). Healthy young adults at rest have a heart rate of 70 beats per minute. After atropine, which blocks the parasympathetic effect on the heart, it is 110. After propranolol, which blocks the sympathetic effect through beta receptors, it is 62. After both drugs, it is 100. Which division dominates the resting heart rate, and how strong is each?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

With both divisions blocked, the pacemaker runs at its **intrinsic rate**, about 100 beats per minute here. The resting rate, 70, is 30 below that, so the net autonomic effect at rest is braking.

With only parasympathetic input left (propranolol), the rate is 62: vagal braking takes it 38 below intrinsic. With only sympathetic input left (atropine), it is 110: sympathetic drive adds 10. So at rest the parasympathetic division dominates, and sympathetic drive adds a little. The two effects, -38 and +10, sum to -28, close to the observed -30 but not exactly, so they do not combine as simple addition.

Two checks. The conclusion holds only if each drug fully blocks its own division, and nothing else, at the dose used. And the divisions oppose at this pacemaker, which does not carry over to sweat glands or most vessels, with one supply, or to the rabbit parotid, where they add.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Helmholtz's apparatus and the speed numbers

Helmholtz timed these tiny intervals with a galvanometer method and with a recording myograph that wrote the twitch on a moving surface. His teacher Johannes Müller had doubted that the speed could be measured at all. The giant axon of the squid *Loligo* is typically about half a millimetre across and sometimes wider. Vertebrate motor axons are far narrower, a few to about 20 micrometres.

### Exceptions in autonomic control

A 2016 mouse study proposed calling the sacral part sympathetic; that is disputed, and this course keeps the standard grouping. In the rabbit parotid salivary gland, sympathetic stimulation increased flow on top of a parasympathetic background secretion, so there the two worked in the same direction.
