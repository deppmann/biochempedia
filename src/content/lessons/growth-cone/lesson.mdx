    story: >-
      Mitchison came to growth cones from microtubules. As a graduate student in Marc Kirschner's
      laboratory he reported with Kirschner in 1984 that individual microtubules switch at random
      between growing and rapidly shrinking, a behaviour they called dynamic instability. In 1988
      the two wrote a review in the first volume of Neuron on what the cytoskeleton does in a
      growing axon. Its central proposal was the clutch. Actin is assembled at the front of the
      growth cone and pulled backward, they argued, so the network is a running
      engine. If the network is linked through adhesion molecules to a fixed surface, the backward
      pull is resisted and the front moves forward; if the link is loose, the engine runs without
      moving the car. The model made a prediction that others could test: grip should slow the
      backward flow and speed advance. Later measurements in neurons and in
      other moving cells were designed around that prediction.

    rationale: >-
      Engaging the clutch resists the backward pull on the actin network, so less of the new actin
      is carried back and more of it pushes the edge forward. In this simplest clutch example,
      assembly is held fixed while engagement slows retrograde flow, so stopping polymerization is
      not the expected effect; in real growth cones, adhesion can also change actin assembly.

**A growing axon finds its partner because the growth cone at its tip senses guidance cues around it and steers toward or away from them.**

An axon is the long fibre a nerve cell sends out to reach its partner. The growth cone is the flat, moving fan at its end. Guidance cues are molecules outside the cell that attract or repel axons. When a cue reaches one side of the growth cone more strongly than the other, it changes the machinery on that side, and the growth cone turns. Turn by turn, the growth cone leads the axon to the right target.

## The layout

We know the axon is drawn out from its tip because someone watched one grow alive. In 1907 Ross Harrison kept a piece of frog embryo alive in a drop of clotted lymph and watched fibres grow out of it while their swollen ends changed shape: an axon is drawn out from one cell by movement at its tip. Santiago Ramón y Cajal had already named that swollen end the growth cone, from fixed tissue.

In culture, the fan's parts divide the labour.

- **The central domain** sits where the axon shaft widens into the fan. It is packed with microtubules, hollow tubes about 25 nanometres wide built from dimers of alpha and beta tubulin.
- **The peripheral domain** is the fan itself, built of actin filaments, which are much thinner (roughly 6 to 10 nanometres). In **filopodia**, the thin spikes at the edge, actin filaments are bundled in parallel. In **lamellipodia**, the sheets between the spikes, they form a branched mesh.
- **The surface** carries receptors for guidance cues and adhesion molecules that grip the substrate (the surface it grows on) or other cells.

The filopodia are both feelers and feet: they carry receptors and adhesion molecules out ahead of the growth cone, sample what is there, and attach. The same tip senses its surroundings and moves through them.

The layout raises three puzzles.

1. **The actin in the fan streams backward even while the edge advances.** How does the edge move forward?
2. **In the standard pipette assay, a cue is only 5 to 10% stronger on one side of the growth cone than the other.** How does so small a difference turn the axon?
3. **The tip is far from the nucleus.** Can it make the decision on its own?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** On a flat, adhesive surface, a growth cone's edge moves forward by however much actin is added there minus however much the network slides back (retrograde flow). Adhesion to the surface, the clutch, holds back part of that slide. A turn sticks on the side where microtubules move in and turn the fan into axon, usually the side protruding more; microtubules can also start the bias themselves. A guidance cue gives a direction by making the two sides differ. The overall amount of cue gives no direction, though it changes whether and how strongly the cone responds.

## Puzzle 1: how the edge advances

### Actin streams backward

Inside the fan, actin is never still. New subunits join the filaments at the end that faces the leading edge. The network is pulled back toward the centre by motor proteins (myosin II) and pushed back by its own assembly against the membrane (Medeiros and colleagues, 2006), and near the centre the filaments are taken apart. This backward movement is called **retrograde flow**. When addition and disassembly balance, it keeps roughly the same size while its parts stream backward.

How do we know the flow is there, and not just a still mesh? Paul Forscher and Stephen Smith filmed growth cones of Aplysia, a sea slug with large, flat growth cones, and in 1988 added a cytochalasin, a drug that stops actin subunits adding at the leading edge.

**Predict.** The drug blocks addition at the front. What happens to the band of actin just behind the edge? And what do the microtubules in the centre do?

A band free of actin opened at the front and widened, while the remaining network kept moving back. A still mesh would have stayed in place; a widening band means it was moving. So the actin had been flowing backward all along; addition at the front had been hiding it. Microtubules, normally kept in the centre, then pushed forward into the emptied band. The actin mesh had been holding them back.

### The clutch

How does the edge move forward against that flow? Tim Mitchison and Marc Kirschner proposed an answer in 1988: **the clutch hypothesis**. Think of the actin network as a running engine and the substrate as the road. Suppose actin is added at the front and carried back at the same rate. Then the engine runs, the clutch is disengaged, and the edge goes nowhere. When adhesion molecules link the network to the substrate, the clutch engages and retrograde flow slows. In the simplest case, where addition stays the same, the same polymerization now pushes the edge forward.

> **rate of advance = rate of actin addition at the edge − rate of retrograde flow**

This is bookkeeping at the edge, with both rates measured along the same direction: whatever is added and not carried back is the distance the edge gains. The grip on the surface runs through immunoglobulin-family adhesion molecules such as L1 and through integrins, receptors for the extracellular matrix.

The prediction was testable: grip should slow the flow and speed advance. In 1995 Forscher's laboratory (Lin and Forscher) reported that where an Aplysia growth cone made a firm contact, flow slowed behind it and the growth cone advanced that way; across conditions, advance was inversely related to retrograde flow. Suter and colleagues (1998) then made the grip themselves. They held a bead coated with an adhesion molecule against the edge of an Aplysia growth cone, and backward flow slowed in line with the bead.

**Worked problem: grip from a bead.** Use illustrative values. Actin is added at the edge at 4.0 micrometres per minute, and the network flows back at 3.5 micrometres per minute. Under a restrained adhesive bead, flow falls to 0.5 micrometres per minute, and addition stays at 4.0.

*Step 1: before the bead.* Advance = 4.0 − 3.5 = 0.5 micrometres per minute. Most of the addition is carried straight back.

*Step 2: under the bead.* Advance = 4.0 − 0.5 = 3.5 micrometres per minute.

*Step 3: compare.* 3.5 / 0.5 = 7. The edge moves seven times faster, with no change in how fast actin is built.

**Predict.** Step 2 assumed addition holds steady when the clutch engages, and that need not be true. Suppose grip also slows addition by a fifth. Does the edge still speed up a lot, a little, or not at all?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A lot. Addition falls to 0.8 × 4.0 = 3.2 micrometres per minute, so advance = 3.2 − 0.5 = 2.7 micrometres per minute, still more than five times the 0.5 before the bead. Grip would stop speeding the edge only if it cut addition to 1.0 micrometre per minute, a quarter of 4.0, or less. The conclusion, that grip speeds advance by slowing flow, does not hang on addition being exactly fixed.

</details>

## Puzzle 2: how a small difference turns the axon

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

### The record

The test for steering is a pipette. In 1979 Gundersen and Barrett placed a micropipette releasing nerve growth factor (NGF) near growing axons of chick sensory neurons. The growth cones turned toward the pipette within about 20 minutes (Science 206:1079; characterized further in 1980, J Cell Biol 87:546). The pipette makes a gradient: the side of the growth cone nearer the source meets more of the cue than the far side. A growth cone that turns toward the source is attracted, and one that turns away is repelled. Either way, the cue can steer the axon.

In the standard form of the assay used today, a fine pipette about 100 micrometres from a growth cone releases a cue in brief, steady pulses, usually at 45° to the direction the axon is heading. The experimenter photographs the growth cone every few minutes for about an hour. Then they draw the axon's path and measure the angle between its starting heading and the line its new stretch has taken.

One growth cone's path bends toward or away from the pipette, but a single growth cone wanders, so steering is read across many: their turning angles form a distribution shifted to one side of zero. In a control dish, with a uniform bath of the same cue or a pipette with no cue, the distribution centres on zero.

### How big is the difference?

**Predict.** The cue spreads out from the pipette tip and thins with distance. In these assays the growth cone is about 10 micrometres wide and about 100 micrometres away. Does its near side meet twice as much cue as its far side, a little more, or the same?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A little more. Treat the pipette as a small source releasing steadily into still fluid in three dimensions; then concentration falls roughly as one over the distance. With the pipette side-on, the near edge is about 95 micrometres away and the far edge about 105, so the near edge meets about 105/95 ≈ 1.1 times as much: roughly 10% more. At the usual 45° it is about 7%. Real gradients depend on the cue and how it is delivered, and estimates for the standard assay are about 5 to 10% across the growth cone's width. A uniform bath of ten times the concentration contains far more cue but no difference, so it offers no direction: across many growth cones, no net turn.

</details>

### From a difference to a turn

Now apply the rule to each side. Each side of the edge advances by its rate of actin addition minus its rate of backward flow; call that its margin. In one common picture, an attractive cue raises the near side's margin, by adding actin faster, by easing the myosin-driven flow, by engaging more clutch, or by a mix. A repellent lowers it, and some repellents collapse that side or, at high enough exposure, the whole growth cone. Signalling inside the cell can turn a 5 to 10% difference outside into a larger one between the sides (the guidance-receptor lessons).

The gaining side puts out more filopodia and lamellipodia, and the fan's mass shifts that way. Microtubules are the stable scaffold of the axon shaft, so where they settle, the axon goes. Microtubules from the central domain enter the gaining side more often and stay longer. Behind them the fan narrows, and that stretch becomes axon shaft pointing in the new direction. Repeated growth biased toward one side adds up to a turn.

### Microtubules steer as well as follow

If microtubules matter for the turn, could biasing them alone turn the cone with no cue at all? Buck and Zheng (2002) tested this in Xenopus spinal neurons with drugs released from a pipette onto one side. Zhou, Waterman-Storer and Cohan (2002) did the matching test on actin, removing the actin bundles from one side of the periphery.

Removing actin bundles on one side turned growth cones away from that side: microtubules withdrew from the region that lost its bundles and extended into regions where bundles remained, and the axon bent that way (Zhou, Waterman-Storer and Cohan, 2002). The two microtubule rows are Buck and Zheng's results. Local taxol made the near side protrude more, and the cone turned toward the drug; that turn needed actin assembly and Rho-family GTPase activity. So microtubules can start a turn, working through actin, not only lock in one that actin began. Nocodazole that broke down microtubules on the near side turned the cone away; a tenfold lower dose in the pipette gave no turn. No cue gradient was imposed, so local changes to microtubules can set direction downstream of the cue. Some cues also act on microtubules directly. Actin and microtubules work together, and either one can decide which side wins.

One caution for the design problem: a turning angle means little if the cone barely advanced. In every arm, measure how far the treated cone grew, set a minimum before reading its direction, and record stalls and collapse separately.

**What the rule covers.** It is the cytoskeletal account of advance and turning for a growth cone on a flat, adhesive surface in culture, over minutes to about an hour. Three limits matter. First, it says how a difference between the sides becomes a turn, not which signalling route makes the difference (calcium, cyclic nucleotides, Rho GTPases, local translation) or what makes a cue attract rather than repel; those belong to the guidance-receptor lessons. Second, "advance equals addition minus flow" accounts for the leading edge only; turning the edge's gain into axon also needs microtubules and organelles to move up behind it. Third, in the embryo axons also change course by collapsing and re-extending and by sprouting branches that are kept or pruned. In three-dimensional gels some central nervous system axons have been reported to extend with little dependence on adhesion (Santos and colleagues, 2020, Cell Reports); how much of the clutch account carries over to three dimensions is an open question.

## Puzzle 3: can the tip decide on its own?

Then they ran the turning assay on the isolated tips.

### Design problem

## Going deeper

### Fixed tissue against a living fibre

By the late 1800s two pictures of how an axon formed were in play. In one, the axon grows out from a single nerve cell, as an extension of that cell. In the other, the nerve forms in place along its route, from a chain of cells or from strands of living material (one version, associated with Victor Hensen, called them protoplasmic bridges) already joining the nerve centre to its destination.

Wilhelm His and Santiago Ramón y Cajal argued for the first picture from dead tissue. Cajal used Golgi's silver chromate stain, which blackens only a few cells in a sample but blackens each of them almost entirely. In the spinal cords of chick embryos a few days old, he found young nerve fibres of different lengths, each ending in a swollen club with spikes and fine projections. In 1890 he named it the growth cone and argued that it was the front of an axon on the move.

The trouble was that every one of his specimens was fixed. A row of fibres of different lengths can be read as one fibre growing, or as fibres that were always there becoming visible as they mature. A still picture cannot show movement, and more stained sections would not settle it.

Harrison's preparation was built to make the answer unambiguous. He removed a piece of the neural tube (the embryo's first nervous system, a tube of cells along its back) from frog embryos at a stage when no nerve fibres could yet be seen. He placed it in a drop of fresh lymph from an adult frog on a coverslip. The lymph clotted quickly into a soft mesh that held the tissue. Then he inverted the coverslip over a hollow slide and sealed it. This is an explant: a piece of tissue kept alive outside the body.

Each feature of the design closed off an alternative. The tissue was taken before any fibres existed, so the fibres that grew were not old fibres exposed by dissection. The clot contained no living chain of cells for a nerve to form from. And in some cultures a fibre could be seen arising from a single isolated cell. He reported this in 1907, and a longer paper in 1910 drew mostly on later preparations he judged more convincing.

Harrison also left a question open. His fibres grew into an unorganized clot, with nothing to point them anywhere. In the embryo an axon has to reach a particular partner, and the pipette assay was the tool that later tested steering.

### Harrison's 1914 test: does a cell need a solid?

In 1914 Harrison asked whether cells need a solid surface or can move through fluid alone. He gave cultures three kinds of support: clotted plasma, smooth glass, and threads of spider web. He also suspended some pieces of tissue in large drops so that they touched nothing. Cells from frog embryos wandered along the web threads, but cells in unsupported drops generally did not move. Pieces of forebrain from five-day chick embryos produced nerve fibres in all four clotted-plasma preparations, and none on spider web or in unsupported drops. Harrison concluded that cells move only on a solid. His chick cultures also suggested that not every solid suits nerve fibres, but he said they were too few to be conclusive, and the web could hold only small drops.

### Adhesion: preference is not speed

Grip also shapes the path an axon takes. Later culture experiments put growth cones on dishes patterned with surfaces of different adhesiveness. In Paul Letourneau's 1975 study with chick sensory neurons, growth cones given a choice tended to follow the more adhesive surface. Preference is not speed. In culture, how adhesive a surface is turns out to be a poor predictor of how fast axons grow on it (Lemmon and colleagues, 1992). Motor-clutch models, in which clutch bonds load and break under force, give one reason the relationship need not be simple.

### What sets addition and grip

The Rho-family GTPases Rho, Rac and Cdc42 control where actin is built and how hard myosin pulls (see [that lesson](/lessons/integrins-and-rho-gtpases/)); like most small GTPases, each is a switch that is on when bound to GTP ([that lesson](/lessons/gtpase-switches/)). Calcium, cyclic nucleotides and local translation are other routes, covered in the guidance-receptor lessons.

### Why a growth cone is not a car

A vehicle steered by the speeds of its two sides, like a tracked vehicle, has both sides joined through its rigid body. If its near side moves faster, it swings toward its far, slower side. Growth cones in an attractive gradient turn toward the side that gains. The difference is that a growth cone is not rigid: it changes shape, the gaining side protrudes more, and microtubules and organelles move into selected regions and make them axon. That is why the engine-and-clutch picture in this lesson is used for advance only.

### From protrusion to axon

Advance at the edge is only the first step in making axon. The edge protrudes; the peripheral actin network is then remodelled so that organelles and microtubules can move up into the new space, with remaining actin bundles guiding the microtubules; and the stretch behind narrows into shaft: protrusion, engorgement and consolidation (Goldberg and Burmeister, 1986). These steps are regulated separately, so more protrusion on one side does not by itself fix where the axon goes.

### When does a growth cone need to make protein?

Turning without a cell body and turning without new protein are separate tests, and the need for local translation found by Campbell and Holt is not true of every cue or every neuron. In the same study, one kind of collapse did not need new protein, and Roche, Marsick and Letourneau (2009) found that chick growth cones, including sensory growth cones turning toward NGF, still responded to cues with protein synthesis blocked.

### The molecules of the clutch

Later work traced the linkage in growth cones themselves. In rat hippocampal growth cones a protein called shootin1 links L1 to the flowing actin (Shimada and colleagues, 2008). Similar clutches work in other migrating cells (reviewed by Case and Waterman, 2015). At the tips of filopodia, Ena/VASP proteins keep filament ends from being capped, so the spike can keep lengthening.
