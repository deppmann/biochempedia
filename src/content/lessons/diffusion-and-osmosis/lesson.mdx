> **The one idea.** Particles are always moving at random, so on average more of them leave a crowded side than come back. A substance that can cross drifts from where it is more concentrated (more crowded) to where it is less concentrated, with no push from the cell. Water does the same. Dissolved particles that cannot cross leave a little less water on their side, so water drifts toward the side with more of them.
>
> *Scope:* the model's dye and small uncharged particles such as oxygen, carbon dioxide and water, with nothing else pushing them. The rule gives the overall **direction** on average, not the speed; a short count can go either way. For water, it holds while the dissolved particles cannot cross easily. A push back, such as from a cell wall, can slow or stop the drift. Charged particles also feel electrical pulls (later courses).

## Explain: particles spread from more to less

<WordBank terms={['concentration', 'diffusion', 'passive transport', 'cell membrane', 'selectively permeable', 'osmosis', 'homeostasis']} />

Cells must move materials in, out and within to stay alive. The first film named two words. **Concentration** is how much of a substance is in a given space. **Diffusion** is the movement of a substance from where it is more concentrated to where it is less concentrated. Diffusion also happens inside cells, and water diffuses too.

In a glass, the drift ends once the dye is even. Across your cell membranes it does not. Your cells keep using oxygen, so oxygen stays scarce inside and keeps diffusing in. They keep making carbon dioxide, so it keeps diffusing out ([that lesson](/lessons/releasing-energy-from-food/)). The film called this no-energy movement **passive transport**; osmosis is passive too.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Particles spread on their own from more concentrated to less concentrated, and that is diffusion.</Support>

## Explain: water crosses a choosy membrane

The **cell membrane** is the thin layer around every cell ([that lesson](/lessons/inside-a-cell/)). It is **selectively permeable**: it lets some substances through and holds back others. In most cells, water, oxygen and carbon dioxide cross it easily. Many **dissolved particles** (tiny bits of a substance mixed into water), such as salt and sugar, cross much less easily.

Oxygen can cross, so it drifts toward the side with less oxygen. Salt is different: the membrane holds most of it back. A model where only water crosses is a good simple picture of the salted cucumber. Most real cell membranes let some salt through, but much more slowly than water. The second film follows the water.

### When does the drift stop?

The film named the water drift **osmosis**, the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane. Water molecules never stop crossing. The overall drift stops once as much water leaves as enters.

1. **In salty water, like the cucumber,** water leaves. The inside gets more crowded and the outside a little less. In this simple model, the drift stops when the two sides match, or sooner if something pushes back.
2. **In plain water, like the lettuce,** the swelling cell presses on its stiff **cell wall** ([that lesson](/lessons/plant-and-animal-cells/)) long before the sides are even. The wall presses back. That push stops the drift and keeps the leaf crisp, even though the inside still has more dissolved particles.

**Predict.** An animal cell has no cell wall. What could happen to it in plain water?

### Design problem

Plan an investigation: how does the amount of salt in water change the mass of potato pieces? Name your variables, constants, control and number of trials, and say how you will stay safe.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** How does the amount of salt change the percent change in mass of potato pieces?
- **Independent variable:** grams of salt in each 100 mL of water, for example 0, 1, 2, 4 and 6 g.
- **Dependent variable:** the percent change in mass of each piece after 30 minutes.
- **Constants:** one potato, same-size pieces, 100 mL of liquid per cup, the same time, temperature and patting method.
- **Control:** the cup with 0 g of salt, which is plain water.
- **Trials:** three pieces in each cup. Find each piece's percent change, then the mean.
- **Measure:** mass in grams on a balance, before and after, patted dry each time.
- **Safety:** an adult cuts the potato. Label every cup, wipe up spills, and never taste anything.

If pieces gain mass in plain water and lose more as the salt goes up, that fits osmosis.

</details>

## Going deeper

**How much less water?** Very little. Salt water and the inside of a cell are both almost all water, so every drawing here, including the film's grid, exaggerates the salt. College courses describe this with pressure and water potential instead of counting water.
