      built a stage-by-stage account of how mouse olfactory neurons come to express one receptor
      gene: repressive chromatin, enhancer contacts between receptor gene clusters, and feedback.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Magklara et al., Cell (2011); Lyons et al., Cell (2013); Dalton, Lyons & Lomvardas, Cell (2013); Monahan, Horta & Lomvardas, Nature (2019); Pourmorady et al., Nature (2024)."

    story: >-
      If a mouse olfactory neuron carries about a thousand receptor genes, what keeps it from using
      more than one? Chess and colleagues, in Richard Axel's laboratory, had shown that the receptor
      transcript in these neurons can come from a single allele. Later papers from the laboratory
      of Stavros Lomvardas asked how. Magklara and colleagues compared chromatin from sorted mouse
      olfactory neurons and found repressive histone marks over receptor loci, lighter on the one
      allele that was active. Lyons and Dalton, each working with Lomvardas, described a feedback
      circuit: receptor expression engages a cellular stress pathway, which raises adenylyl cyclase
      3 and helps lock the choice in place. Monahan, Horta and Lomvardas then extended earlier
      contact studies across the whole genome and found that receptor gene clusters from 18
      chromosomes contact one another, under the control of the factors LHX2 and LDB1. Each paper
      added one stage to a sequence running from silencing through enhancer contacts to feedback.
      The sequence gave the field named steps, and for each step a mutant that could be tested.

techniques:
  - name: "ChIP-seq and in situ Hi-C"
    blurb: >-
      Chromatin immunoprecipitation pulls down DNA bound to a chosen histone mark or transcription
      factor, and sequencing says where it sat. In situ Hi-C ligates DNA segments that were close
      together in the nucleus and counts the pairs, giving a genome-wide contact map. Applied to
      sorted mouse olfactory cells, they show where repressive marks and factors sit and which
      receptor gene clusters touch.
  - name: "Conditional genetics and lineage tracing"
    blurb: >-
      A gene is deleted in a chosen cell population at a chosen time, or a recombinase permanently
      marks neurons that activated a given receptor locus long enough for recombination, which takes hours. Comparing the mark with the
      receptor a neuron expresses now reveals whether it switched. Each result holds for that
      deletion, age and assay.
  - name: "Single-cell chromatin contact and expression assays"
    blurb: >-
      Single-nucleus assays read, in one cell, which receptor genes are transcribed and which
      regions are accessible or in contact. They let the population averages of Hi-C be broken down
      into cells at different stages, including cells that have not yet settled on one receptor.
  - name: "Cryo-EM with mutagenesis and a cAMP assay"
    blurb: >-
      A purified receptor is frozen and imaged to give a three-dimensional map, a pocket residue is
      changed, and a luciferase reporter of cAMP in cultured cells reports whether the response to a
      ligand shifts. Structure suggests a prediction and the assay tests it, in cells and not in
      neurons.

**In 1994 Chess and colleagues, in Richard Axel's laboratory, found that a mouse olfactory neuron's receptor transcript comes from just one of its two alleles, the gene copies inherited from each parent.**

That made an old puzzle sharper. A mouse olfactory neuron holds about a thousand odorant-receptor genes in each of its two genome copies, so about two thousand candidates. A mature neuron uses one copy of one gene, and it keeps using it. How does a cell make a choice that complete, and how does it keep it?

The core course states the rule and moves on: [smell and taste](/lessons/smell-and-taste/) covers the Buck and Axel cloning, one receptor and two glomeruli, and the combinatorial code. The rule matters because the receptor a neuron chooses fixes which odorants it responds to and helps set where its axon goes. This lesson starts where that one stops, with the mechanism.

## One in two thousand: the puzzle

**Singular choice** means that in nearly every mature mouse olfactory sensory neuron, one receptor gene is transcribed from one allele (Chess and colleagues, 1994; Monahan and colleagues, 2017). It describes where development ends, not every stage on the way. Three broad phases, without sharp boundaries, frame the whole lesson.

| Phase | Receptor genes | What the mouse evidence suggests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early progenitor | silenced; little or no receptor RNA | repressive chromatin covers receptor loci, apparently before receptor expression is detectable |
| Late precursor to immature neuron | several transcribed weakly, then one comes to dominate | single-cell data infer several competing enhancer hubs |
| Mature neuron | one gene, one allele, kept | feedback helps hold the choice |

Singular choice does not mean one receptor molecule or one odour: an insect receptor near the end of the lesson binds chemically unrelated molecules.

Why is singular choice hard? Suppose a neuron let receptor genes out of silence at random: each candidate gene has a small chance of escaping, independent of every other gene, and nothing afterwards shuts off extra genes or removes the neurons that have them. Then the number of escapes in a neuron is close to a **Poisson distribution** with some average, m, and the fraction of neurons with exactly one escape is m × e^(−m).

**Predict.** You may set m to anything you like. What is the largest fraction of neurons that can end up with exactly one receptor gene?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In this model, about 37 percent, never more. The fraction m × e^(−m) peaks at m = 1, where it equals 1/e.

| Average escapes per neuron, m | No receptor | Exactly one | Two or more | Exactly one, counting only neurons that express any |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.1 | 90% | 9% | 0.5% | 95% |
| 0.5 | 61% | 30% | 9% | 77% |
| 1 | 37% | 37% | 26% | 58% |
| 3 | 5% | 15% | 80% | 16% |

Make escapes rare enough that 95 percent of expressing neurons have only one (m ≈ 0.1), and nine neurons in ten express nothing. Make them common enough that 95 percent express something (m = 3), and most express two or more, only 15 percent exactly one.

</details>

Mature mouse neurons nearly all express exactly one receptor, so this toy model leaves out important biology: rare, independent, unregulated escapes cannot give nearly universal singular expression in an unselected population counted at one time. The calculation alone does not say what is missing, or tell regulation apart from selection of neurons during maturation. Mouse experiments support two mechanisms: competition at enhancer hubs and receptor-dependent stabilization.

The paradox breaks into five puzzles.

1. **Escape.** If the genes are silenced, how does any of them get out?
2. **One winner.** Why does only one of about two thousand candidates win?
3. **Staying chosen.** Why does the choice stay made?
4. **Address.** How does the choice become a destination for the axon?
5. **Payoff.** What does the choice give the neuron?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson. Silence every gene, let a few compete, and lock in the winner.** Repressive chromatin covers the receptor genes before any one is strongly expressed. A few are transcribed weakly and compete at enhancer hubs (clusters of DNA switches), and the leading gene's RNA helps quiet its rivals. Once its receptor protein is made, it sets off a stress signal that turns down Lsd1, an enzyme that new receptor expression depends on, so further escapes become rare. The broad silence was there before any winner; the winner adds suppression and stability on top of it.

Picture a library. Every book is chained shut. A key frees a few, and those books compete to be read; the loudest reader drowns out the rest. Once the winner is read aloud, it rings a bell that takes the key away, so new books rarely open. In the mouse, the chains are repressive chromatin, the key an Lsd1-dependent step, the competition happens at enhancer hubs, the drowning out is the winner's RNA, and the bell is a stress pathway. Bell and lost key together are what this lesson calls **the lock**.

## Silence first

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The first stage is silence. Magklara and colleagues (2011) sorted olfactory neurons from the epithelium of mice aged 6 to 10 weeks and used **chromatin immunoprecipitation**, which pulls down the DNA wrapped around histones that carry a chosen chemical mark. Receptor loci were enriched for two repressive marks of **heterochromatin** (tightly packed, silenced chromatin), H3K9me3 and H4K20me3.

They then compared the two alleles of one gene. In neurons labelled for the receptor P2, the active P2 allele carried less of the repressive marks than its inactive partner.

**Predict.** A common picture says the chosen gene silences the others. If the winner caused the first silencing, when should the repressive marks appear? And can the P2 comparison decide the question?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If the winner caused the first silencing, the marks would appear only after a receptor was expressed. Instead, Magklara's data suggested that silencing comes before detectable expression. That argues against receptor expression being the sole source of the initial broad repression, while leaving room for later receptor-dependent suppression or reinforcement of it. The P2 comparison alone cannot establish the causal order: the chosen allele sits in a less repressed state, but a comparison of two alleles is an association and does not say which change starts the choice.

</details>

A second, separate line of evidence shows the silent genes gathered together. Clowney and colleagues (2012) found that receptor genes from different chromosomes collect in a few dense heterochromatic foci as mouse neurons mature, in step with the loss of the lamin B receptor. Forcing lamin B receptor back into mature neurons broke up the foci and disrupted singular receptor expression.

### Lsd1, the candidate key

In the model, the candidate key that lets a gene out of silence is the histone demethylase Lsd1 (see [eukaryotic gene regulation](/lessons/eukaryotic-regulation/)). Lyons and colleagues (2013) deleted Lsd1 early in the mouse epithelium and read the RNA of the whole tissue at embryonic day 18.5. Control tissue yielded 662 detected receptor genes and the knockout 212, about a third as many (212 / 662 = 0.32). The authors concluded that early Lsd1 deletion impaired initiation of receptor expression.

This is dependence, not mechanism, and the chemistry gives a reason for care. LSD1 is an FAD-dependent amine oxidase. It removes one or two methyl groups from a lysine, but it cannot remove a third, because its oxidation needs a free lone pair of electrons on the lysine nitrogen, and a trimethylated lysine, with a fixed positive charge, has none to spare. So the result does not show that LSD1 itself removes H3K9me3 at receptor loci; clearing that mark would need another demethylase or an intermediate step. And a count from bulk tissue depends on the assay and the cells in the sample. In this lesson, then, "the key" is shorthand for an Lsd1-dependent step.

## Escaping the silence: enhancer hubs that compete

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Three terms carry this section.

- An **enhancer** is a DNA element that raises transcription of a gene it contacts. The receptor genes have candidate enhancers, which the field calls the Greek Islands.
- Contacts **in trans** are contacts between different chromosomes.
- A **hub** is a cluster of enhancers, often from different chromosomes. An active hub sits around the receptor gene it drives.

Many factor and method names follow. Keep the idea, not the list: enhancers from many chromosomes gather into hubs.

Markenscoff-Papadimitriou and colleagues (2014), working in adult mouse epithelium with 4C-seq (which maps contacts made by one chosen DNA site) and DNA FISH (which labels loci to see how near they sit), associated receptor transcription with contacts between candidate enhancers on different chromosomes. The chromatin-remodeling factor Bptf facilitated those contacts, and disrupting them was associated with weak expression of several receptors at once.

What assembles the hubs? Monahan and colleagues (2017) found that the factors Lhx2 and Ebf bind cooperatively at enhancer motifs with a stereotyped spacing. In their ChIP-seq of sorted mature neurons, Lhx2 marked nearly every Ebf site in the receptor clusters, but not the other way round: 63 of 65 Ebf peaks (97 percent) were also bound by Lhx2, against 63 of 114 Lhx2 peaks (55 percent) bound by Ebf. An array of enhancer elements inserted into the mouse genome raised the frequency with which linked receptors were chosen, which is a sufficiency-style test of the idea.

Monahan, Horta and Lomvardas (2019) extended these contact studies across the genome with **in situ Hi-C**, which captures DNA segments that were close together in the nucleus and counts the pairs. In sorted mouse neurons and their progenitors, receptor clusters on 18 chromosomes made contacts with one another, and LHX2 and LDB1 regulated assembly and maintenance of these compartments and hubs. A population contact map averages over many cells, though, and cannot show that each cell has a single hub.

**Predict.** Look inside one developing neuron. Is there one hub, or several?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Several. Pourmorady and colleagues (2024) inferred several competing hubs in developing neurons before one gene became dominant, combining single-cell multiomes (RNA plus chromatin accessibility) of 5- and 12-week-old mouse olfactory epithelium with single-cell Hi-C (Dip-C) on sorted mouse neurons.

</details>

Their evidence supported two further steps. Receptor RNA suppressed the transcription of competing receptors at a distance, and translation of the receptor then stabilized transcription at the prevailing hub. The machinery that carries out the long-range suppression is still unidentified. So competition can work without receptor protein, and protein then helps stabilize the winner.

## The lock: the winner's protein holds the choice

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

Recall the rule: silence every gene, let a few compete, and lock in the winner. One gene has prevailed at its hub. What keeps that choice stable, so that no new gene escapes later? Part of the answer is the lock.

**Predict.** If the lock is closed by the receptor the winning gene makes, what should a neuron do when its chosen allele cannot supply that stabilizing feedback, for example because the receptor coding sequence is deleted?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In the model the lock does not close, so the neuron should keep going and choose again. It does: when the chosen allele cannot supply the feedback, as with a coding deletion, the neuron often expresses a second receptor gene as well. Sensory dysfunction alone does not establish loss of feedback: a receptor that cannot respond to odorants may still hold the lock. Conversely, neurons given a functional receptor transgene early rarely express their own receptor genes, as if the lock had closed before any of them escaped (reviewed by Monahan and Lomvardas, 2015). So the lock depends on receptor protein.

</details>

**What the lock is made of.** Dalton, Lyons and Lomvardas (2013) traced it to the **unfolded protein response**, the pathway by which a cell reacts to misfolded protein in its endoplasmic reticulum ([stress signals](/lessons/stress-signals/)). In developing mouse neurons each step has one job.

1. **The alarm.** Receptor expression engages the response.
2. **The relay.** PERK-dependent phosphorylation of eIF2α slows most translation but favours a few messages, including the one for the transcription factor ATF5.
3. **The order.** ATF5 promotes transcription of the gene for adenylyl cyclase 3, Adcy3.
4. **Two effects.** Adcy3 helps turn down Lsd1 after choice (Lyons and colleagues, 2013), so, in the model, the key that let a receptor gene out of silence is withdrawn and later escapes become unlikely. Adcy3 induction also relieves the stress response. This is the two-step model: the alarm goes on, and the response it causes switches the alarm off, so the stress is brief.

The lock did not create the broad silence, which came first, and it works alongside the RNA step that quiets rivals; these experiments do not test whether feedback also adds repression. The evidence for the order comes from epistasis experiments in mice (which order genes in a pathway by asking which defect another gene can rescue), including newborn eIF2α-mutant animals rescued with ATF5. They place the signals in order, but they do not show how a receptor activates PERK.

Adcy3 is also the cyclase that serves odour transduction in the cilia ([vision, smell and cyclic nucleotides](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/)). In this model, though, the lock is set by receptor protein and needs no odour, though odour-driven activity may still play a part.

**Predict.** You can look at each neuron only once. How could you tell that it had switched receptors?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Leave a permanent mark. In **lineage tracing**, a recombinase made from one receptor's locus, here MOR28, permanently marks any neuron that has activated that locus long enough for recombination, which takes hours. A marked neuron that now expresses a different receptor has switched. In neurons lacking Adcy3, lineage tracing of MOR28 at postnatal day 2 showed more switching, at an age when most neurons are still immature: the ratio of singly labelled neurons (the mark without MOR28, or MOR28 without the mark) to doubly labelled ones was about twice that in controls (Lyons and colleagues, 2013).

</details>

| Neuron (mouse) | Receptor protein made? | The lock | What was seen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | yes, from the chosen gene | closes | one receptor, kept |
| Chosen allele cannot supply stabilizing feedback (for example, its receptor coding sequence is deleted) | absent, or altered so that feedback fails | choice not stably held | more switching, or a second receptor gene expressed |
| Functional receptor transgene, early | yes, before the neuron's own genes | closes early | own receptor genes rarely expressed |
| Lacking Adcy3 | yes | weakened | singly-to-doubly labelled MOR28 lineage ratio about twofold higher at postnatal day 2 |

**How leaky could a lock be?** Suppose further escapes form a homogeneous Poisson process (independent events at a constant rate λ), and the lock closes a fixed time τ after the first escape. The chance that another gene escapes in that window is 1 − e^(−λτ). With illustrative values, a mean wait of 48 hours for an escape and 2.4 hours for the lock to close, λτ = 0.05 and about 5 percent of neurons let a second gene through. Double τ and that rises to about 9.5 percent, close to double: while λτ is small, the leak grows in proportion to the delay, so a lock need not be perfect. The numbers are invented, and the model computes a second escape, not the measured lineage ratio. It therefore cannot say whether the Adcy3 step was slowed or lost in the mutant, while other steps, such as the RNA-level competition, may still limit expression.

## The choice becomes an address

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

The core course's rule of one receptor and two glomeruli needs each neuron's axon to find the right target. Receptor identity helps it do so. The 1996 receptor-swap experiments (Mombaerts and colleagues, mice) suggested an instructive but nonexclusive role for receptor identity in axon targeting. Imai and colleagues (2006) reported that receptor-derived cAMP signals shift expression of guidance molecules and bulb projection sites along the anterior-posterior axis, and Serizawa and colleagues (2006) found that Kirrel2, Kirrel3, ephrin-A5 and EphA5 vary with receptor activity.

The lock's own pathway reaches the wiring too. Shayya and colleagues (2022) reported that different receptor sequences produce different ER-stress patterns in developing mouse neurons, and that Ddit3 links PERK signalling to guidance and adhesion genes. The map-building rules are in [partners, layers and self-avoidance](/lessons/partners-layers-self-avoidance/) and [topographic maps](/lessons/topographic-maps/); here the point is that the choice and the address can share a signal.

## What the choice buys: one pocket

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

The choice gives the neuron one kind of receptor, and that receptor's binding pocket sets which molecules the neuron responds to. Del Mármol, Yedlin and Ruta (2021) solved cryo-EM structures of the bristletail receptor MhOR5, expressed in human cultured cells: a **homotetramer** (four identical subunits) that is itself an odorant-gated ion channel, with eugenol and the repellent DEET bound in the same pocket, one in each subunit. In a calcium-imaging screen MhOR5 responded to more than 65 percent of the odorants in a 54-molecule panel. It is an atypical insect receptor, from a lineage without Orco, the co-receptor that partners with the odorant-specific subunit in most insect receptors, and an ion channel, not a G-protein-coupled receptor, so it is a contrast, not a template.

Mammals use the other design. Billesbølle and colleagues (2023) solved active human OR51E2 bound to propionate, as a purified, engineered complex with a mini G protein. The human genome encodes about 400 odorant GPCRs; that is the human repertoire, not the mouse count. In a cAMP assay, the pocket mutants F155A and L158A gained responses to longer-chain fatty acids. Read that as a shift in selectivity, not a better receptor. F155A also lost potency for propionate, its small ligand, and the worked problem measures that loss.

**Worked problem: reading a dose-response shift.** In the cAMP assay, propionate gave an EC50 (the concentration giving half the maximum response) of 0.824 mM for wild-type OR51E2 and 12.3 mM for F155A. Assume a Hill slope of 1 (the simplest curve shape), zero baseline, and each construct normalized to its own maximum, so the response is

> **R(c) = c / (EC50 + c)**

The responses calculated below are model values, not measured light outputs.

**Predict.** At 0.824 mM, wild type gives half its maximum. Roughly what fraction will F155A give?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

About 6 percent of its maximum. *Step 1: both at 0.824 mM.* Wild type: R = 0.824 / (0.824 + 0.824) = 0.500. F155A: R = 0.824 / (12.3 + 0.824) = 0.824 / 13.124 = 0.063.

</details>

*Step 2: the potency shift.* 12.3 / 0.824 = 14.9, a rightward shift of about 15-fold.

*Step 3: both at 12.3 mM.* Wild type: R = 12.3 / 13.124 = 0.937. F155A: R = 12.3 / 24.6 = 0.500.

*Step 4: check.* For wild type to give R = 0.063, c = EC50 × R / (1 − R) = 0.824 × 0.063 / 0.937 = 0.055 mM, and 0.824 / 0.055 = 15, matching the shift.

The size of the shift is uncertain (see Going deeper). Two more cautions: these are functional EC50 values, not binding constants, and they say nothing about the airborne concentration a human nose detects.

### Design problem

A structure predicts that changing one residue to widen the pocket should let a receptor respond to a longer-chain ligand. Design the test, and say what a non-response would and would not mean.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Make the point mutant and confirm that it reaches the cell surface, since a protein that never arrives cannot respond. Compare it with wild type in the same assay, with empty-vector and mock-transfected controls, so that background and the host cell's own cAMP signalling are accounted for. Run full dose-response curves instead of one concentration, and report EC50 and maximum response separately: a shift in EC50 and a drop in maximum mean different things. Include a ligand the wild type already responds to as a positive control for the mutant's coupling.

A non-response in this assay would not show that the ligand cannot bind. A flat curve can mean no binding, no G-protein coupling, no trafficking to the surface, or an assay ceiling, so a negative in vitro is not a deorphanization, the assignment of a ligand to a receptor. Nor would either outcome show what the receptor does in an olfactory neuron, where chaperones, cilia and the feedback pathway above are present.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Where the guiding rule applies

The one-gene, one-allele endpoint applies to mature canonical olfactory sensory neurons of the mouse main olfactory epithelium; the three-part rule summarizes their path there, including the immature phase when several receptor genes are transcribed at low levels. Other animals reach one receptor per neuron by other routes. In the fruit fly, developmental programs of transcription factors do much of the choosing, so the mouse model is not a template there, though this does not exclude receptor feedback in flies. Some mouse chemosensory neurons, such as the MS4A-expressing necklace neurons, are reported to express several chemoreceptors and fall outside the rule. In humans, single-cell data are consistent with mostly one receptor per neuron, but the chromatin, hub and feedback steps described here have been studied mainly in mice.

### A step after transcription: Mex3a

A 2025 mouse study (Duffié and colleagues) added a Mex3a-dependent step that acts after transcription. Its conditional deletion caused premature ER stress, the alarm of the lock, during the stage when several receptors are transcribed. So something must also keep the early, weak transcripts from setting off the alarm too soon.

### Two tables, two shifts

The worked problem uses the mean EC50 values in Extended Data Table 2 of the OR51E2 paper. Its Table 3 gives 1.40 mM and 9.13 mM for the same two constructs, a shift of about 6.5-fold, and the F155A pEC50 in Table 2 has a standard error of 0.77 log units. That is why the size of the shift is uncertain.

### Where the OR51E2 structure and screen came from

The active human OR51E2 structure was solved at 3.1 angstroms, with the complex made in HEK293-derived Expi293F cells and the cAMP assays done in HEK293T cells. The 511 clones of a human screening library (Mainland and colleagues, 2015) are a screening set, not the human repertoire.

### Engineered consensus receptors

Consensus-sequence structures from 2024 (de March and colleagues) extend structural coverage of the family, but they are engineered sequences, not natural receptors.
