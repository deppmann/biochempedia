      By the 1950s two kinds of explanation competed for how an animal makes an antibody that fits
      a molecule it has never seen. Instructive theories held that the antigen acts as a template
      and folds a general antibody into a matching shape. Selective theories held that matching
      antibodies exist beforehand and antigen picks them out; Niels Jerne had proposed a version
      in which antigen selects circulating antibody molecules. Burnet, working in Melbourne, moved
      the selection from molecules to cells. In a short paper in the Australian Journal of Science
      in 1957 he proposed that each lymphocyte makes antibody of a single specificity, that
      antigen binding drives that cell to divide, and that its descendants, a clone, carry the
      same specificity. The idea made several predictions: one cell, one specificity; memory as an
      expanded clone; and tolerance as the removal of self-reactive clones. Burnet shared the 1960
      Nobel Prize with Peter Medawar for work on acquired immunological tolerance. Clonal
      selection became the framework in which the rest of this lesson makes sense.

    story: >-
      Clonal selection left a genetic puzzle. If every B cell makes a different antibody, and an
      animal can make millions, how can a genome of limited size encode them all? One answer was
      that the genes are not fixed in the germline but altered in each lymphocyte. Tonegawa,
      working at the Basel Institute for Immunology with Nobumichi Hozumi, tested this directly in
      BALB/c mice. They cut DNA from early embryos and DNA from MOPC 321, a plasmacytoma (a tumor
      of antibody-secreting cells), with a restriction enzyme, separated the fragments by size on
      agarose gels, and asked which fragments hybridized to immunoglobulin sequences. If the gene
      were the same in every cell, the patterns would match. They did not. Hozumi and Tonegawa

      in the embryo during lymphocyte differentiation. The work opened the study of how gene
      segments are assembled, and Tonegawa received the 1987 Nobel Prize.

    blurb: >-

  - name: "Restriction digestion and hybridization"
    blurb: >-
      Cut genomic DNA with a restriction enzyme, separate the fragments by size, and probe them

  - name: "Antigen-probe flow cytometry"

      count, one cell at a time, the B cells that bind it (T cells need antigen as peptide on MHC instead). This measures memory cells
      separately from the antibody in plasma, which is measured by ELISA.

**You have never met most of the viruses that will ever infect you, yet your body already holds B cells whose receptors can bind them.** How can a defense be ready for a molecule before it exists in your world? This lesson shows how lymphocytes make that guess in advance and how the body keeps the guesses that pay off.

## One clone, one receptor: clonal selection

In the [innate immunity](/lessons/innate-immunity/) lesson you met defenses that recognize broad classes of microbes, such as bacterial cell walls, with receptors encoded unchanged in the genome. **Adaptive immunity** is the second layer in vertebrates: responses by **lymphocytes** (B cells and T cells, two kinds of white blood cell) that are specific for one target and that leave **memory**, a faster and larger response the next time. The molecule a lymphocyte's receptor binds is an **antigen**, such as a viral or pollen protein.

The central rule is **clonal selection**, proposed by Macfarlane Burnet in 1957. Each lymphocyte makes receptors of one specificity, and it makes them before it ever meets antigen. When an antigen arrives, it binds only the few cells whose receptors already fit, and those cells divide into a **clone**, a population of identical descendants. Think of a shoe shop with millions of pairs on the shelves: a customer finds a pair that fits, and the shop makes thousands more of it. The antigen chooses; it does not shape.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/adaptive-immunity/clonal-selection-strip.webp" width={1200} height={450} alt="AI-generated illustration: three frames. Five lymphocytes with five different receptor shapes; an antigen fits the receptor of one cell, which turns gold; that cell has divided into a group of identical gold cells while the other four are unchanged." caption="Receptors are made before antigen arrives. The antigen binds only the cell whose receptor already fits, and that cell divides into a clone." />

Fit is not perfect one-to-one matching. Wooldridge and colleagues (2012) estimated, from tested peptides plus a mathematical model, that one human CD8 T cell clone from a person with type 1 diabetes responds to over a million different ten-amino-acid peptides presented on one MHC class I molecule. A receptor is specific in that it ignores almost everything, but it binds many related targets.

How does a body make millions of receptor shapes from a human genome of about 20,000 protein-coding genes? Each antibody is built from **heavy chains** and **light chains**, and the part that binds antigen, the **variable region**, is not encoded as one piece. It is assembled in each developing lymphocyte by **somatic recombination**: the cell cuts out the DNA between one **V** (variable), one **D** (diversity) and one **J** (joining) segment for a heavy chain, or one V and one J for a light chain, and joins the chosen pieces. The cutting needs the **RAG** enzymes; in fibroblasts given a recombination test sequence, Oettinger and colleagues (1990) found that adding RAG2 to RAG1 raised recombination at least 1,000-fold. At each joint, nucleotides are added or removed (**junctional variation**), and each B cell then pairs one heavy chain with one light chain. T cell receptors are built the same way, with their own segments. The molecular detail belongs to [antigen receptors and checkpoints](/lessons/antigen-receptors-and-checkpoints/).

**Worked problem: counting receptors.** The segment counts below are illustrative values for humans; the real numbers differ between people and between sources. Assume every combination is functional and equally likely.

1. *Heavy chain.* 40 V x 23 D x 6 J = 5,520 combinations.
2. *Light chains.* Humans have two kinds of light chain. Kappa: 40 V x 5 J = 200. Lambda: 30 V x 4 J = 120. Total light chains: 200 + 120 = 320.
3. *Pairing.* Any heavy chain can pair with any light chain: 5,520 x 320 = 1,766,400, about 1.8 million.

> **receptors = (V x D x J for heavy) x (V x J for light)**

That is from fewer than 150 segments. Junctional variation multiplies the total many times over. The point is that no antigen is needed to make the shapes. After activation, B cells (but not T cells) refine their receptors further by **somatic hypermutation**, point mutations in the variable region; cells whose mutated receptors bind better are favored, so antibody affinity rises during a response.

## Where B and T cells come from: a chicken's bursa

Birds have a pouch of lymphoid tissue opening into the cloaca, the **bursa of Fabricius**. In the 1950s nobody knew what it did. Bruce Glick, Timothy Chang and R. George Jaap, at Ohio State University, removed it from young chickens (bursectomy) and later immunized these birds and intact controls with a bacterial antigen, usually described as a Salmonella preparation. Antibody against bacteria is classically measured by **agglutination**: serial dilutions of serum are mixed with bacteria, and the highest dilution that still clumps them gives the titer.

If the bursa had no immune role, both groups should have made similar antibody. Instead, most bursectomized birds made little or no detectable antibody (Poultry Science, 1956). That ruled in a need for the bursa in normal antibody production in chickens. It did not identify the cell, or say anything about mammals.

Two later experiments split the system in two. In mice, J. F. A. P. Miller reported in 1961 that removing the thymus at birth impaired immune function. In chickens, Max Cooper, Raymond Peterson and Robert Good (1965, and in full in 1966) removed the bursa or the thymus in irradiated newly hatched birds: losing the bursa removed antibody while skin grafts were still rejected, only more slowly, and losing the thymus impaired graft rejection. Two lineages, then: bursa-derived **B cells** and thymus-derived **T cells**.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/adaptive-immunity/bursa-thymus-chicken-experiment.webp" width={1200} height={727} alt="AI-generated illustration: three chickens in a row labelled intact, bursa removed and thymus removed. Under each, an antibody icon and a skin graft icon show that losing the bursa lowers antibody while grafts are still rejected, and losing the thymus impairs graft rejection." caption="Removing the bursa from newly hatched chickens removed antibody but grafts were still rejected, only more slowly; removing the thymus impaired graft rejection. Here the comparison matters. Mammals have no bursa. In humans and mice B cells develop in the fetal liver and then the bone marrow, from blood-forming stem cells (see [stem cells](/lessons/stem-cells/)), and T cells mature in the thymus in mammals and birds alike. The B honors the bird organ, not the bone.

## Antibodies and killer T cells: class I and class II

B cells carry the **humoral** response, the part carried in body fluids. A B cell's receptor can bind an intact antigen floating in lymph or blood, without processing. When activated, usually with signals from a helper T cell, it divides and some descendants become **plasma cells**, B cells that secrete large amounts of antibody. Antibodies do not kill microbes on their own. They **neutralize** (coat a virus or toxin so it cannot attach to cells), **opsonize** (tag a microbe for phagocytes to engulf) and activate **complement**, the blood proteins you met in innate immunity.

T cells carry the **cell-mediated** response. A conventional (alpha-beta) T cell receptor does not bind free antigen. It reads short peptides displayed on **MHC** molecules (the major histocompatibility complex) on another cell's surface. There are two classes:

- **MHC class I** is on almost all nucleated cells. It displays peptides from proteins made inside the cell, so a virus-infected liver cell shows viral peptides. **CD8 cytotoxic T cells** read class I and kill the infected cell by triggering its self-destruction (apoptosis; the machinery is in [death receptors, BCL-2 and caspases](/lessons/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases/)).
- **MHC class II** is normally found on antigen-presenting cells: dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells. It displays peptides from material the cell has taken in. **CD4 helper T cells** read class II and release signaling proteins (cytokines; see [JAK-STAT signaling](/lessons/jak-stat/)) that activate macrophages and help B cells.

A memory aid: CD8 x class I = 8, CD4 x class II = 8. The split by origin is not absolute: in **cross-presentation**, some antigen-presenting cells place peptides from engulfed material on class I. Rolf Zinkernagel and Peter Doherty (1974) showed in virus-infected mice that cytotoxic T cells kill infected cells only when they share MHC type, evidence that T cells read antigen together with MHC.

## Memory, vaccines, mothers' antibodies and blood groups

After a first, **primary response**, which takes days to build, most expanded cells die, but **memory B cells** and **memory T cells** remain. A second encounter starts from a larger, already selected population, so the **secondary response** is faster and larger. **Active immunity** is protection your own lymphocytes make, after infection or after a **vaccine**, which presents antigen without the disease. **Passive immunity** is protection by antibody made in another body. It works at once but lasts only until the antibody is broken down, and it leaves the recipient no memory cells.

Mothers supply passive immunity in different ways in different animals. In humans the antibody class **IgG** crosses the placenta before birth. In cattle and horses, whose placentas let little antibody through, the newborn gets antibody mainly from **colostrum**, the first milk, absorbed by its gut in the first day or so. Birds deposit their antibody, **IgY**, in the egg yolk. More in [animal reproduction](/lessons/animal-reproduction/).

Blood groups apply the same rules to red cells. In the **ABO** system, group A cells carry the A antigen and B cells the B antigen; group O cells carry the unmodified **H** antigen, not no antigen. People normally have antibodies against the ABO antigens they lack: group O serum contains anti-A and anti-B, and group AB serum neither. Those antibodies bind mismatched transfused cells at once. The genetics are in [extending Mendel](/lessons/extending-mendel/). The **Rh** antigen differs: an Rh-negative person makes anti-Rh only after exposure, and most such antibodies are IgG. An Rh-negative mother exposed to an Rh-positive fetus's red cells can make anti-Rh IgG that crosses the placenta in a later pregnancy and can destroy fetal red cells.

The same machinery can misfire. In **allergy**, a person makes **IgE** against a harmless antigen such as a pollen protein; IgE sits on **mast cells**, and when allergen binds it the mast cells release histamine and other mediators within minutes. In **autoimmunity**, lymphocytes attack self. Normally self-reactive clones are deleted or silenced during development, and regulatory T cells restrain others; autoimmunity is a failure of this **tolerance**.

## Adaptive immunity compared: lampreys and jawed vertebrates

The system above is shared by jawed vertebrates, from sharks and other cartilaginous fish through bony fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals: RAG-built antibodies, T cell receptors and MHC. Invertebrates, such as insects, have no lymphocytes and rely on innate defenses. The jawless vertebrates, lampreys and hagfish, were a puzzle: they seemed to mount adaptive responses, yet no antibody, T cell receptor or RAG genes could be found. Then Pancer and colleagues (2004) found that sea lamprey (Petromyzon marinus) lymphocyte-like cells carry **variable lymphocyte receptors**, assembled in each cell from a library of leucine-rich-repeat modules, with no antibody genes and no RAG. Each lamprey lymphocyte appears to make one receptor, and antigen selects clones. The rule is the same and the molecules are different, a case where two lineages reached a similar solution separately (see [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/) for how to tell convergence from shared ancestry).

### Design problem

Dan and colleagues (Science, 2021) sampled blood from 188 people up to eight months after SARS-CoV-2 infection. Spike antibody fell, with an apparent half-life of about 140 days in a cross-sectional fit, while spike-specific memory B cells were more abundant at six months than at one month. A headline says "immunity fades in months." Design a study that tests whether falling antibody means lost memory.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Treat memory as several compartments and measure each one separately in the same people. Measure plasma antibody by ELISA. Count antigen-binding memory B cells by antigen-probe flow cytometry, and count CD4 and CD8 T cells with a separate T cell assay, such as peptide stimulation followed by activation markers. Sample each person repeatedly (paired longitudinal samples), not different people at different times, and report each compartment's trend. Antibody comes mainly from plasma cells and decays as they and the antibody turn over; memory B cells can persist or even rise while antibody falls, as they did here. State the limit plainly: none of these readouts is a measure of protection. Protection needs its own endpoint, such as reinfection rates, which this design lacks. It supports no advice about vaccines.

</details>
