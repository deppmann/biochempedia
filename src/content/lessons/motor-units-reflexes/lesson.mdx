**Tap the tendon just below your kneecap and your leg kicks, through a path in the spinal cord with only one synapse between sensor and motor neuron.** The same few segments of cord decide how hard a muscle pulls, keep a joint steady and can lay down the rhythm of a step. This lesson takes those jobs one at a time.

Directions here use the spinal-cord frame: dorsal means toward the back, ventral toward the belly. **Ipsilateral** means on the same side of the body, and **contralateral** on the opposite side.

## Motor units and the size principle

The cells that command skeletal muscle are **alpha motor neurons**, whose cell bodies sit in the ventral horn of the cord and whose axons leave through the ventral roots. Each axon branches to many muscle fibres. A **motor unit** is one alpha motor neuron and all the fibres it innervates; a **motor pool** is all the motor neurons that supply one muscle. In mature muscle each fibre is contacted by one motor neuron, after an early period of multiple innervation that [synapse elimination](/lessons/synapse-elimination/) covers; how a motor neuron gets its identity is in [the motor neuron code](/lessons/motor-neuron-code/), and how its junction is built in [the neuromuscular junction](/lessons/neuromuscular-junction/).

At the junction, acetylcholine released by the motor neuron depolarizes the fibre ([synaptic transmission](/lessons/synaptic-transmission/)), and normally each motor-neuron spike gives every fibre of its unit an action potential and a twitch. So the unit is the smallest piece of force the cord can command. Units differ widely: small motor neurons supply few fibres and make weak units, large ones supply many and make strong units.

When the drive to a pool rises, which units start first? Under shared input, smaller motor neurons are recruited before larger ones, the **size principle**. The reason is Ohm's law. **Input resistance** is the voltage change a cell shows per unit of steady current injected into it ([the resting membrane and passive signals](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/)). A small cell has less membrane, so fewer channels in parallel and a higher input resistance. The same synaptic current therefore depolarizes it more:

> **ΔV = I × R_in, so the current needed to reach threshold is I = ΔV / R_in**

How synaptic currents sum on the way is the subject of [synaptic integration](/lessons/synaptic-integration/). The order is a tendency, first measured in cats, and it holds under common input. Differences in threshold, in persistent inward currents and in neuromodulation can shift it, and separate inputs to parts of a pool can break it.

## Grading force: recruitment and rate

**Worked problem.** Three motor neurons, S, M and L, in one pool receive the same synaptic current, ramped up from 0 at 1 nA per second. These are illustrative round numbers, in the range reported for cat lumbar motor neurons, not readings from one recording. Input resistances are S 4.0 MΩ, M 2.0 MΩ and L 1.0 MΩ. Assume steady state (the ramp is slow compared with the membrane time constant) and that each cell must depolarize 10 mV from rest to fire. That last assumption is a simplification, since real thresholds also differ.

*Step 1: current to threshold.* A millivolt divided by a megohm is a nanoampere.

> S: 10 mV / 4.0 MΩ = 2.5 nA; M: 10 mV / 2.0 MΩ = 5.0 nA; L: 10 mV / 1.0 MΩ = 10 nA

*Step 2: time to threshold.* At 1 nA per second, S fires at 2.5 s, M at 5.0 s and L at 10 s. The order is smallest first.

*Step 3: force.* Give the units forces of 0.05 N, 0.30 N and 1.50 N. As each joins, total force goes 0.05 N, then 0.35 N, then 1.85 N. The steps get bigger as force grows, so the smallest steps come when force is low, where a small error matters most. In a real pool of many units the same logic means each new recruit adds force roughly in step with the force already present.

<SizePrincipleRamp />

Recruitment is half the story. **Rate coding** is the other half: an active unit can fire faster. One spike gives a twitch. If the next spike arrives before the twitch has relaxed, the second twitch adds to the first, and at high rates the twitches fuse into a smooth contraction, a tetanus. So force rises both by adding units and by driving the active ones harder.

How firing rate rises with force differs from unit to unit. Avrillon and colleagues (2024) recorded the tibialis anterior and vastus lateralis of eight people per muscle with grids of 64 surface electrodes, during contractions up to 80% of maximal force, and sorted the signals into single motor units, about 129 units per participant in tibialis anterior. After an initial steep acceleration just above each unit's recruitment, firing rates rose between force plateaus 10% of maximal force apart by 3.5 ± 1.7 pulses per second in tibialis anterior and 2.0 ± 1.1 in vastus lateralis. Trajectories varied across units, and most fitted a logarithm better than a straight line or an exponential. The input to each motor neuron was inferred from force, not measured inside the cell.

## The stretch reflex and its inhibitory partner

A **muscle spindle** is a stretch sensor: a capsule of small, specialized **intrafusal** fibres lying parallel to the ordinary **extrafusal** fibres that make force. Stretching the muscle stretches the spindle. Its **Ia** (primary) afferents respond to both the speed and the amount of stretch; its **group II** (secondary) afferents mainly to the amount (relaxed human muscle; reviewed by Dimitriou, 2022).

A tendon tap gives a brief stretch, and the Ia afferents fire. They enter the cord through the dorsal root and branch.

- One branch excites the motor neurons of the same muscle, the **homonymous** motor neurons, directly: one synapse, a **monosynaptic** path. The muscle contracts against the stretch.
- Another branch excites a **Ia inhibitory interneuron** on the same side of the cord, which inhibits the motor neurons of the **antagonist**, the muscle that moves the joint the other way. That path has two synapses, so it is **disynaptic**, and it is ipsilateral. Sherrington called this pairing of excitation and inhibition reciprocal innervation.

The excitatory branch is monosynaptic, and Ia afferents also excite the motor neurons of synergist muscles directly. The inhibitory branch to the antagonist is not, so the reflex as a whole is not purely monosynaptic.

The **Golgi tendon organ** sits at the junction of muscle and tendon, in series with the fibres, so it signals the force the muscle makes rather than its length. Its **Ib** afferents act through interneurons. The textbook pathway inhibits the muscle's own motor neurons, but Ib effects change with task and are not simply a safety cutout against tearing.

The **crossed-extensor reflex** is the one that crosses. A painful stimulus to one foot excites, through several interneurons, the flexors of that leg, which withdraws, and through interneurons whose axons cross the midline, the extensors of the other leg, which takes the weight. It is polysynaptic and bilateral, where the stretch reflex is local and one-sided.

What descending pathways add, and the signs that follow damage to them, are taken up in [the basal ganglia](/lessons/basal-ganglia/) and [the cerebellum](/lessons/cerebellum/); diseases of motor neurons are in [neurodegeneration](/lessons/neurodegeneration/).

## Gamma: keeping the spindle in range

A spindle has a problem. When the muscle shortens, the spindle shortens too and can fall slack, so its firing drops or stops. **Gamma motor neurons** fix this. They supply muscle spindles only, ending on the contractile poles of the intrafusal fibres. When the poles contract, the centre of the spindle, where the Ia endings wrap, is pulled taut again.

Think of a tape measure that winds itself in as the muscle shortens, so it never goes loose. When alpha and gamma motor neurons fire together, **alpha-gamma coactivation**, the spindle can be kept from falling slack during a contraction.

Gamma drive is not simply locked to alpha drive. Papaioannou and Dimitriou (2021) recorded single Ia afferents from wrist and finger extensors of volunteers with microneurography during an instructed delay before a reach. About 80 ms after the target was cued, Ia firing fell when the coming reach would stretch the spindle-bearing muscle, while kinematics and surface EMG showed no systematic change with the target. The principal analysis used eight afferents from six people, and separate experiments with a robot measured whole-arm stretch reflexes. The authors interpreted the results as compatible with goal-directed changes in gamma drive. Gamma neurons were not recorded, so that drive is an inference.

## Rhythm without a conductor

A **central pattern generator** is a neural network capable of producing rhythmic output without patterned descending or sensory input. Graham Brown's 1911 cats, with the cord cut and the dorsal roots of the hind limbs severed, still alternated ankle flexors and extensors.

Much modern evidence comes from **fictive locomotion**, locomotor-like motor output with no movement. It is also studied in paralysed adult cats, but a common preparation is an isolated spinal cord from a newborn rodent, bathed in drugs such as NMDA and serotonin, producing alternating bursts in the ventral roots that would drive flexors and extensors. Hägglund and colleagues (2013) used optogenetics in the isolated neonatal mouse cord to activate or inhibit selected populations, and found that locomotor-like bursting could be induced on one side alone. That shows a spinal network can make rhythm. It does not show a single pacemaker cell. A 2021 review of mouse work reports that rhythm persisted after silencing or ablating each individual genetically defined ventral population it considered, so the rhythm generator is still an open question.

Sensory input still matters. In the work of Li, Garcia-Ramirez and Dougherty (2019) on cords of mice one to three days old, ankle nerve stimulation between flexor bursts could start a new flexor burst early. The network makes the rhythm and feedback adjusts its timing. For people, evidence for a spinal pattern generator is indirect; these results come from neonatal rodents and cats.

### Design problem

Design an experiment to show that the mammalian spinal cord can generate a locomotor rhythm by itself, and that sensory input can reset it. Say what you record, what you predict, your controls and what your result cannot show.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Isolate the spinal cord of a newborn mouse (one to three days old) with ankle nerves attached, so there is no descending input and no movement feedback. Evoke activity with NMDA and serotonin in the bath; Li and colleagues (2019) used 7 µM and 8 µM. Record a flexor-related lumbar ventral root and an extensor-related one on both sides. Li and colleagues used L1 for flexor-related and L4 for extensor-related output; many laboratories use L2 and L5.

Predict that the flexor-related and extensor-related roots on one side alternate, and that the left and right roots of each pair alternate. Controls: record baseline activity in saline before adding the drugs, since neonatal cords can burst on their own, and check that the drug-evoked locomotor pattern fades on washout.

To test resetting, first transect the ventral roots proximal to the spinal nerve, so pulses cannot travel back into the cord along motor axons, then stimulate an ankle nerve with brief trains (for example 4 to 6 pulses at 20 Hz, onsets 50 ms apart) at several phases of the cycle. Plot the time to the next flexor burst against the phase of stimulation, using unstimulated cycles as the baseline. An early flexor burst after stimulation between flexor bursts, with the following cycles shifted in time compared with unstimulated cycles, shows resetting.

Limits: this is a neonatal cord driven by drugs, not adult or human walking. Rhythm with no patterned input shows the circuit is enough under these conditions; it does not identify a single pacemaker cell or prove that normal walking dispenses with the brain or with feedback.
