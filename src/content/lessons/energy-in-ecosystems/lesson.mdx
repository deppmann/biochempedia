## Explain: who eats whom in the Bay

<WordBank terms={['ecosystem', 'producer', 'consumer', 'decomposer', 'trophic level', 'food chain', 'food web', 'energy pyramid', 'biomass', 'biomass pyramid']} />

An **ecosystem** is all the living things in an area together with the nonliving environment they interact with, such as water, light and temperature. The Chesapeake Bay is one ecosystem, and a single marsh inside it can be studied as another.

Every living thing in it gets energy in one of three ways.
- A **producer** captures energy from sunlight and stores it in food. In the open water of the Bay, the main producers are phytoplankton, tiny algae and bacteria that drift in the water. Underwater grasses are producers too. A few producers in other places use energy from chemicals instead.
- A **consumer** gets energy by eating other living things. A primary consumer eats producers. A secondary consumer eats primary consumers, and a tertiary consumer eats secondary consumers.
- A **decomposer**, such as many bacteria and fungi, gets energy from dead matter and wastes.

A **trophic level** is a feeding step: producers are the first level, primary consumers the second, and so on.

A **food chain** follows one feeding path. A **food web** links many food chains together. Arrows point from the food to the eater, the way the energy moves:

> sunlight → phytoplankton → zooplankton → Atlantic menhaden → osprey

Menhaden filter both phytoplankton and zooplankton, tiny animals that drift in the water. Striped bass eat menhaden too. Because menhaden eat at two levels, the osprey is a secondary consumer on one path and a tertiary consumer on another. A web shows this; a single chain hides it. You will add bacteria and their recycling in [Matter Cycles](/lessons/matter-cycles/).

## Explain: why each level gets less

Phytoplankton store light energy in sugars by photosynthesis, which you met in [Photosynthesis](/lessons/photosynthesis-energy-capture/). When a zooplankton eats a phytoplankton cell, most of that energy does not become new zooplankton.
- Some is released as heat during **cellular respiration**, the process that releases energy from food, explained in [Respiration and ATP](/lessons/respiration-and-atp/).
- Some is in parts that are not eaten or not digested. Much of that material ends up with decomposers.
- Only the rest becomes new body tissue that the next level can eat.

As a rule of thumb, about 10 percent of the energy at one level ends up in the next. Real measurements are often higher or lower. A 2026 synthesis of 2,052 estimates from 122 studies found an average energy transfer of about 6 percent, lower on land than in the sea. The simulation lets you try 5 to 20 percent.

Here is a worked example with made-up starting numbers. Suppose phytoplankton in a model patch of bay add 100,000 kJ per square meter per year, and 10 percent passes at each step.

| Level | Calculation | Energy (kJ per square meter per year) |
|---|---|---|
| Phytoplankton | given | 100,000 |
| Zooplankton | 100,000 × 0.10 | 10,000 |
| Menhaden | 10,000 × 0.10 | 1,000 |
| Osprey | 1,000 × 0.10 | 100 |

Only one thousandth of the producers’ energy reaches the osprey level. Loss at each step is a major reason food chains rarely have more than about five levels.

An **energy pyramid** stacks the energy at each level, producers at the bottom. It always narrows going up. A **biomass pyramid** stacks **biomass**, the mass of living things at each level, such as grams per square meter. Biomass pyramids usually narrow too, but not always. In the English Channel, phytoplankton divide so fast and are eaten so quickly that, at any moment, the zooplankton can outweigh them. That pyramid is upside down, while the energy pyramid stays upright.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** at each step much energy is released as heat or left in uneaten and undigested matter, so only a fraction, often around 10 percent, reaches the next level.</Support>

## Explain: energy flows, matter cycles

Energy is never made or destroyed; it changes form. Light becomes chemical energy in food, and food energy becomes motion and heat. Heat spreads into the water and air, and no living thing can capture it to build food again. So energy flows one way: in as sunlight, out as heat. Nearly every ecosystem needs a steady supply of new energy, which in the Bay is sunlight; a few, such as deep-sea vent communities, run on chemical energy instead.

Matter behaves differently. Decomposers release much of the carbon in a dead menhaden as carbon dioxide, and phytoplankton can take that carbon up again.

| | Energy | Matter (such as carbon) |
|---|---|---|
| Where it enters | sunlight captured by producers | already present in air, water and soil |
| What happens at each level | much is released as heat | atoms are passed on or released, not lost |
| Path | one way | a cycle, used again |

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** energy flows through an ecosystem once and leaves as heat, while matter cycles.</Support>

### Design problem

Plan a study to test whether osprey chicks fed extra fish are more likely to survive to flying age. Name your variables, control, constants, sample size and how you protect the birds.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Question: does extra food raise the share of nests that raise young?
- Independent variable: extra menhaden placed at the nest, or none.
- Dependent variable: whether each nest raises at least one chick to flying age, and how many.
- Control: nests that get no extra fish but are visited the same way.
- Constants: the same area, the same season, and nests that started with eggs at about the same time.
- Allocation: assign nests to groups at random. If some nests can only be control, as when boats cannot reach them, say so, because it could bias the result.
- Sample size: at least 15 nests per group, and repeat in more than one year.
- Ethics and safety: work under wildlife permits, keep visits short, and stay out of the nest during heat or storms.
- What results mean: higher success in the fed group supports food limitation at that site. It does not say why fish are scarce.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The osprey chick’s energy began as sunlight caught by phytoplankton. At every step, from plankton to menhaden to osprey, much of the energy was released as heat or left in uneaten and undigested matter, so only a small share, often around 10 percent, passed upward. Much of the carbon in those fish can cycle back to the plankton, but the energy will not.
