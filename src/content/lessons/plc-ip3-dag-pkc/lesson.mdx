techniques:
  - name: "Permeabilized cells"
    blurb: >-
      Make the plasma membrane leaky to small molecules while the compartments inside the cell
      stay sealed. A charged compound that cannot cross a membrane, such as IP3, can then be
      added straight to the cytosol, and you can ask whether that one molecule is enough to
      produce a response with no hormone present.
  - name: "Radiolabeled inositol tracing"
    blurb: >-
      Feed cells inositol that carries tritium so their inositol lipids become radioactive,
      stimulate them, then separate the water-soluble inositol phosphates and count each
      fraction in a scintillation counter. Adding lithium blocks the last step of their
      breakdown, so the products pile up and are easier to measure.
  - name: "Reconstituted kinase assay with defined lipids"
    blurb: >-
      Mix purified kinase, a substrate protein, ATP labeled with phosphorus-32 and lipid
      vesicles of known composition, then measure the radioactivity transferred to the
      substrate. Leaving out calcium, phosphatidylserine or DAG one at a time shows which
      cofactors the enzyme needs and whether they act together.
  - name: "Correcting a tumor mutation by genome editing"
    blurb: >-
      Use CRISPR to change one mutated base in a cancer cell line back to the normal sequence,
      then compare the growth of corrected and uncorrected cells. If the corrected cells grow
      less well, the mutation was helping the tumor.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "Phospholipase C hydrolyzes PIP2. Which products form, and where does each one end up?"
    choices:
      - "PIP3 stays in the membrane and ADP enters the cytosol"
      - "IP3 stays in the membrane and DAG enters the cytosol"
      - "Inositol enters the cytosol and phosphatidic acid stays in the membrane"
      - "IP3 enters the cytosol and DAG stays in the membrane"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The cut separates the phosphorylated inositol head group from the glycerol that carries
      the two fatty acid tails. The head group, IP3, is charged and water-soluble, so it
      leaves; the tails on glycerol, DAG, are oily and stay in the inner leaflet. Putting IP3
      in the membrane and DAG in the cytosol gets the solubility backwards, since three
      phosphates cannot sit in a bilayer and two fatty acid tails cannot dissolve in water.
      PIP3 and ADP are the products of PI 3-kinase, which adds a phosphate to PIP2 and does not
      cut it. Phosphatidic acid would need a cut on the other side of the
      linking phosphate, which is not the bond phospholipase C breaks; that cut would also release an inositol phosphate, not free inositol.
  - stem: "Which feature lets PLC-gamma, but not PLC-beta, be switched on directly by a receptor tyrosine kinase?"
    choices:
      - "Its SH2 domains, which bind receptor phosphotyrosines"
      - "Its X and Y regions, which fold into the catalytic barrel"
      - "Its PH domain, which binds lipids in the plasma membrane"
      - "Its C2 domain, which follows the catalytic barrel"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      SH2 domains bind phosphorylated tyrosines, so PLC-gamma can dock on an activated receptor
      tyrosine kinase, where it is then phosphorylated and switched on. PLC-beta has no SH2
      domains. The C2 domain, the PH domain and the X and Y catalytic regions are present in
      both enzymes, so none of them can explain a difference between the two. They help the
      enzymes reach the membrane and do the chemistry, but they do not read phosphotyrosine.
  - stem: "In the 1983 test of IP3, why was the compound added to permeabilized cells and not to intact ones?"
    choices:
      - "IP3 is quickly destroyed by enzymes on the outer face of the cell"
      - "IP3 is highly charged and cannot cross an intact plasma membrane"
      - "IP3 is made only from lipids in the outer leaflet of the membrane"
      - "IP3 is active only after the mitochondria have been washed away"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      IP3 carries three phosphate groups, and a molecule with that much negative charge does
      not pass through a lipid bilayer. Making the plasma membrane leaky lets it reach the
      cytosol, where its target is. Destruction by enzymes on the outer face of the cell is
      not the reason, because the enzymes that end the IP3 signal are inside the cell.
      Permeabilization does not wash mitochondria away; the experiment showed that the calcium
      came from a different store while mitochondria were still there. PIP2 sits in the inner
      leaflet, not the outer one, and where IP3 is made has no bearing on how an experimenter
      gets it into a cell.
  - stem: "A cell is loaded with a calcium buffer that keeps cytosolic calcium at its resting level. A Gq-coupled receptor is then stimulated, and DAG is produced normally. Which PKC class is most likely to be activated?"
    choices:
      - "Conventional PKCs, because their C2 domain binds DAG"
      - "No PKC class, because every PKC requires calcium"
      - "Novel PKCs, because their C1 domain needs only DAG"
      - "Atypical PKCs, because their PB1 domain binds calcium"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Novel PKCs have a C1 domain that binds DAG and a C2-like domain that does not bind
      calcium, so DAG alone can bring them to the membrane and activate them. Conventional
      PKCs need calcium on the C2 domain as well as DAG on C1, and their C2 domain does not
      bind DAG, so with calcium held at rest they stay largely off. Atypical PKCs respond to
      neither messenger, and their PB1 domain binds protein partners, not calcium. The claim
      that every PKC requires calcium describes only the conventional class.
  - stem: "A student treats cells with a phorbol ester for 24 hours, expecting PKC signaling to be at its highest. Instead, a known PKC substrate is less phosphorylated than in untreated cells given a brief Gq stimulus. Which explanation is most likely?"
    choices:
      - "The phorbol ester blocks the ATP site of the kinase domain"
      - "The persistently active PKC is degraded by the cell"
      - "The persistently bound C1 domain can no longer bind calcium"
      - "The phorbol ester is cut into IP3 and leaves the membrane"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A phorbol ester sits in the DAG site of the C1 domain and is not cleared the way DAG is,
      so PKC is held at the membrane in its open, active form for hours. The cell degrades PKC
      that stays in that state, and after a day there is little enzyme left to phosphorylate
      anything. Phorbol esters bind C1, not the ATP site; they are activators, which is why
      short treatments raise substrate phosphorylation. A phorbol ester is not a substrate of
      phospholipase C and yields no IP3. Calcium binds the C2 domain, not C1, so an occupied
      C1 domain does not stop calcium from binding.

    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Calcium alone adds 4 to the baseline of 2 and DAG alone adds 3, so additive effects
      predict 2 + 4 + 3 = 9. The measured 90 is ten times that, which is synergy: each
      activator makes the other far more effective, the signature of a coincidence detector.
      Calling the result additive with an expected value of 9 ignores the tenfold excess. The
      value 11 comes from adding 6 and 5 without subtracting the baseline that both
      measurements share. The value 30 comes from multiplying 6 by 5, which is not what
      additivity means, and 90 does not match it either.

> **The one idea.** One PIP2 cut sends IP3 away and leaves DAG at the membrane. Conventional PKC integrates calcium targeting with DAG activation at membranes.
>
> *Scope:* this rule covers PLC cleavage of PIP2 and conventional PKC. IP3 opens a calcium channel; it does not carry calcium. Other routes can raise calcium or make DAG, and novel and atypical PKCs use different inputs. "Integrates" means a stronger, longer response where the two inputs overlap, not an all-or-none switch.

## One cut makes two messengers

The first film showed where each product goes; here is the chemistry behind it. The substrate is **PIP2** (phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate), the inner-leaflet label read by PH domains in [lipid anchors and addresses](/lessons/lipid-anchors-and-addresses/). Two fatty acid tails on a glycerol backbone sit in the membrane. The cytosolic head is a six-carbon inositol ring, with a hydroxyl on each carbon, joined to glycerol through one phosphate and carrying two more at positions 4 and 5.

**Phospholipase C (PLC)** hydrolyzes the bond between glycerol and the linking phosphate. The phosphate leaves with the ring, so a lipid named for two phosphates yields a soluble product named for three:

> **PIP2 + H2O → IP3 (inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate) + DAG (diacylglycerol)**

Each product is a **second messenger**, a small molecule made inside a cell in response to an external signal.

## Two receptor routes converge on the cut

The first film showed Gq switching on PLC-beta, and PLC-gamma docking on a receptor and then being phosphorylated by its kinase. Both fold their X and Y regions into one catalytic barrel; their regulatory parts read different receptors.

A receptor coupled to Gq loads GTP onto Gq alpha, as in [GTPase switches](/lessons/gtpase-switches/), and Gq-GTP activates PLC-beta at the membrane. Some forms, especially PLC-beta2 and PLC-beta3, also respond to beta-gamma subunits released from G proteins such as Gi. PLC-beta accelerates GTP hydrolysis by Gq, helping reset its own activator; cutting continues only while receptors reload Gq.

PLC-gamma carries two **SH2 domains**, which recognize phosphorylated tyrosines in particular sequence contexts, and an **SH3 domain**, which binds proline-rich protein segments. For the platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) receptor, the growth factor pairs two receptors, and each phosphorylates tyrosines on the other's tail. Docking alone is not activation: a PLC-gamma lacking the tyrosines the receptor kinase phosphorylates would still dock but cut little PIP2, because the brake on its catalytic barrel stays on.

Thus acetylcholine at a Gq-coupled muscarinic receptor can use PLC-beta, and PDGF at its receptor tyrosine kinase can use PLC-gamma.

## How IP3 earned the name messenger

By 1983 Michael Berridge, working on blowfly salivary glands, had shown that IP3 appears within seconds of hormone arrival. Robert Michell had proposed eight years earlier that breaking down inositol lipids is how receptors raise calcium. Correlation was not enough: a hormone starts many events, so IP3 and calcium could both follow a third cause.

The first film showed the 1983 test that closed the gap. In it H. Streb **permeabilized** pancreatic acinar cells in Irene Schulz's laboratory, making the plasma membrane admit small molecules while internal compartments stayed sealed. IP3 is too charged to cross an intact membrane, which is why the cells had to be opened. Robin Irvine prepared the IP3 from human red blood cells.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/plc-ip3-dag-pkc/permeabilized-cell-ip3.webp" width={1200} height={755} alt="AI-generated illustration: IP3 prepared from human red blood cells enters a permeabilized pancreatic acinar cell through gaps in its plasma membrane. With no hormone added, calcium is released from a membrane-enclosed internal store, rather than from the mitochondrion." caption="Added IP3 released calcium from a sealed nonmitochondrial store without hormone, establishing that IP3 was sufficient for the response." />

The store, later identified as the **endoplasmic reticulum (ER)**, holds free calcium at a thousand times or more the resting cytosolic level of about 100 nM. Its **IP3 receptor** is a four-subunit calcium channel, so opening it produces a fast rise. The next lesson follows how cells read and clear that rise.

## Conventional PKC joins the branches

The second film showed calcium bringing conventional PKC to the membrane and DAG helping displace its pseudosubstrate. The evidence came from assays with purified kinase and defined lipids: by 1980 Yasutomi Nishizuka's laboratory had traced the extra activity of impure lipid preparations to DAG, which lets the kinase work at much lower calcium.

**Protein kinase C (PKC)** phosphorylates serines and threonines. Its **pseudosubstrate** resembles a substrate but has alanine where the target serine or threonine would be, so it occupies the active site and blocks it. The calcium-loaded **C2 domain** binds anionic membrane lipids such as phosphatidylserine, and conventional and novel PKCs carry two DAG-binding **C1 domains**, C1A and C1B.

### Energies add, preferences multiply

**Predict.** A purified conventional PKC reads 1 unit of activity with no additions, 4 with calcium and 3 with DAG. What would simple addition predict with both, and what would a measured 40 units imply?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Calcium adds 3 above baseline and DAG adds 2, so simple addition predicts 1 + 3 + 2 = 6. A value of 40 is about sevenfold above that expectation, evidence that the inputs act synergistically under these assay conditions.

</details>

Synergy comes from two membrane contacts, each weak alone. Binding energies add, which means preferences multiply:

> ΔG(both) ≈ ΔG(C1) + ΔG(C2), so the combined membrane preference ≈ preference(C1) × preference(C2)

A **membrane preference** is a plain ratio: how many times more the kinase favors the membrane with that contact than without it. If each contact alone favors the membrane thirtyfold, say, the pair favors it about 30 × 30 = 900-fold. The numbers are illustrative, not measurements, and assume the two contacts act independently.

Calcium also rises when a voltage-gated channel opens, and other lipid reactions make DAG, so a kinase that answered either input alone would fire for many unrelated reasons. Calcium and DAG appear together at the inner face of the plasma membrane mainly when PLC has just cut PIP2 there. Conventional PKC is therefore a **coincidence detector** in a graded sense: it gives a stronger, longer response where calcium targeting and DAG activation overlap. It recognizes the cut by sensing both halves of it.

## A DAG mimic shows why duration matters

Phorbol esters from croton oil promote tumors in animal skin without mutating DNA. In 1982 Monique Castagna and Nishizuka's group showed that a phorbol ester can substitute for DAG and activate PKC directly; later work showed that it binds the same C1 site. As the second film showed, cells clear DAG quickly but cannot efficiently break down a phorbol ester, and PKC held on for hours is degraded. A long exposure therefore ends with less enzyme than the cell started with, so a brief signal and a prolonged one can have opposite outcomes.

**Predict.** If PKC mainly drove tumors, what kind of PKC mutations would you expect to find in human cancers? What would it mean if most of them disabled the kinase?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

An enzyme that drives tumors should pick up activating mutations, as oncogenes do. If most tumor mutations disable it instead, the working enzyme behaves more like a brake, at least for the isozymes tested.

</details>

Alexandra Newton's laboratory tested 46 PKC mutations from human tumors: most disabled the kinase and none activated it. For many isozymes that points to a brake on tumors, although the direction depends on isozyme and context; PKC-iota in lung cancer can act as an oncogene.

## Ending and rebuilding the signal

Both branches are reversible. Phosphatases dephosphorylate IP3, or a kinase adds a fourth phosphate. DAG kinase converts DAG to phosphatidic acid. The inositol and lipid backbone are recycled into PIP2. Meanwhile Gq hydrolyzes GTP and calcium is pumped away, so conventional PKC returns toward its closed cytosolic state.

**Predict.** Suppose IP3 breakdown is blocked but DAG kinase remains active. Which messenger should outlast the other, and why should conventional PKC output still fall?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

IP3 should outlast DAG, because its breakdown is blocked. That does not guarantee high cytosolic calcium for as long: calcium feeds back on the IP3 receptor, the ER store can run down, and pumps keep removing calcium. Conventional PKC output can fall either way as DAG is converted to phosphatidic acid, because calcium targeting without the DAG contact is a weaker input.

</details>

### Design problem

Design a detector that responds strongly only when two of these signals overlap: calcium, DAG, phosphorylation, cAMP, an active small G protein or a protein partner. Name its sensor domains and explain why either contact alone should be weak.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Choose DAG and an active small G protein; copy the PKC plan and swap one sensor.

1. **Output:** a kinase domain blocked by a tethered pseudosubstrate.
2. **First sensor:** a C1 domain for DAG.
3. **Second sensor:** replace C2 with a domain that binds a small G protein only in its GTP form. Raf has such a domain for Ras, which is membrane-anchored by lipid.
4. **Tuning:** make each contact weak enough that either signal alone gives only brief membrane visits.

DAG alone says a lipid reaction occurred; Ras-GTP alone says a receptor activated Ras; together they report both events at one membrane patch. Each alone is ambiguous: if either could switch the kinase on fully, you would have built an OR gate, whose output cannot tell the pair of events from a single one. Disabling either sensor should weaken the response; DAG alone should not strongly activate the detector while Ras holds GDP.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Domain architecture

The fuller maps are `PLC-beta: PH · EF hands · X · Y · C2 · long tail` and `PLC-gamma: PH · EF hands · X · SH2 · SH2 · SH3 · Y · C2`. PLC-gamma also contains a split PH domain in the insert. The shared X and Y regions make the catalytic barrel; the inserted modules regulate access and recruitment.

### Proteolysis bypasses the switch

Cutting the tether between a PKC pseudosubstrate and kinase domain creates activity that no longer needs the normal signal, explaining how Nishizuka's laboratory first activated the kinase with a protease in 1977. A related event occurs in programmed cell death, where a caspase clips PKC-delta and frees its kinase domain.

### Two routes from environment to cancer

Soot has been tied to cancer since Percivall Pott's 1775 report on chimney sweeps, and hydrocarbons later isolated from coal tar damage DNA. A phorbol ester can contribute by impersonating a second messenger instead.
