techniques:
  - name: "Tip covering and barrier experiments"
    blurb: >-
      Cover a seedling tip with an opaque cap or a transparent tube, or insert an impermeable sheet
      such as mica into a cut on one side, then light the seedling from one side. Comparing the
      treatments shows where light is sensed and which route the signal takes, provided a control
      shows the covering or cut alone does not stop growth.
  - name: "Oat coleoptile curvature bioassay"
    blurb: >-
      Let excised tips release their growth substance into agar, then place the block on one side
      of a decapitated coleoptile in the dark and measure the angle of curvature. Within a
      calibrated range, more curvature means more auxin activity in the block.
  - name: "Sequential red and far-red light treatments"
    blurb: >-
      Give imbibed seeds or plants brief flashes of red or far-red light in different orders, with
      dark controls, and score germination or flowering. A response set by the last flash points
      to a pigment that switches reversibly between two forms.
  - name: "Grafting with tagged proteins"
    blurb: >-
      Join the shoot of one plant to another, or make a protein carrying a visible tag only in
      certain cells, and look for the tag elsewhere. Finding a phloem-made FT protein at the shoot
      apex shows that the protein itself can travel.

**Light an oat seedling from one side and its shoot bends toward the lamp. The light is sensed mainly at the tip, but the part that bends is lower down. What carries the message between them?**

The answer is a chemical signal, a hormone. This lesson names the main plant hormones, follows the experiments that tracked down the first one, and shows how plants use hormones and light-sensing pigments to bend, flower and defend themselves.

## Plant hormones, many effects

A **hormone** is a chemical made in small amounts that changes what cells do, in the cell that made it or in cells elsewhere in the body. Vertebrates make most of theirs in glands and carry them in blood ([the endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/)). Plants have no endocrine glands: many kinds of plant cell make hormones, and the response is often a change in growth. The main ones, each with one effect and the organism it was shown in:

- **Auxin** (the main natural form is indole-3-acetic acid, IAA) speeds elongation of shoot cells, drives tropisms, and helps maintain **apical dominance**, the suppression of side buds by the growing shoot tip. It does this together with cytokinins and **strigolactones**, a further hormone class that holds side buds back. In Arabidopsis thaliana it promotes growth on the shaded side of the **hypocotyl**, the seedling stem below the seed leaves.
- **Cytokinins** promote cell division and slow leaf ageing. In transgenic tobacco that made extra cytokinin in ageing leaves, leaf **senescence**, the programmed yellowing and death of a leaf, was delayed.
- **Gibberellins** promote stem elongation and seed germination. In isolated aleurone layers (the living outer layer of a cereal grain's food store) of Himalaya barley, gibberellic acid increased synthesis of alpha-amylase, a starch-digesting enzyme, after a lag of 6 to 8 hours.
- **Abscisic acid (ABA)** induces stomatal closure in Arabidopsis ([plant transport](/lessons/plant-transport/) has the guard cells) and maintains **dormancy**, a state of arrested growth in seeds and buds.
- **Ethylene**, a gas, promotes fruit ripening. Tomatoes engineered to make little ethylene failed to ripen normally, and supplied ethylene restored ripening.
- **Brassinosteroids**, steroid hormones, promote cell elongation. Supplied brassinosteroids could make up for the short, poorly elongated cells of several Arabidopsis mutants.
- **Jasmonates** signal defence against plant-eating animals, and **salicylic acid** against some pathogens (end of the lesson).

Inside cells, auxin binds a receptor that triggers destruction of repressor proteins ([ubiquitin and proteolysis](/lessons/ubiquitin-and-proteolysis/); receptor logic is in [the receptor field guide](/lessons/receptor-field-guide/)). Hold on to one point: the same hormone can act differently in different organs. Auxin speeds elongation in shoots and slows it in roots.

## Bending toward light: the Darwins, Boysen-Jensen and Went

A **tropism** is growth toward or away from a one-sided stimulus. **Phototropism** is directional growth in response to light. The classic experiments used grass **coleoptiles**, the sheath that covers the first leaf of a grass seedling as it pushes up through the soil.

Charles Darwin, with his son Francis credited for assistance, described the first set in The Power of Movement in Plants (1880). They lit seedlings of canary grass, Phalaris canariensis, from one side. Opaque caps over the tips greatly reduced bending, even though the lower part, where the curve forms, was still lit. Transparent glass tubes over the tips did not stop bending. Light is sensed mainly near the tip, and something passes down to where bending happens.

Peter Boysen-Jensen, working with oat (Avena sativa) coleoptiles in reports from 1910 to 1913, cut off tips and set them back in place. The coleoptile still bent below the cut when its tip was lit from one side, so the influence crossed a wound. He then slipped mica, a mineral sheet that dissolved chemicals cannot cross, into a cut on one side below the tip. Mica on the lit side left bending intact. Mica on the shaded side blocked it. He concluded that the signal runs down the shaded side, though others disputed this at the time, and later barrier experiments were needed to settle it.

Frits Went, working on oat coleoptiles at Utrecht and publishing in 1928, took the signal out of the plant. He stood cut tips of dark-grown coleoptiles on small blocks of agar, removed the tips, and set a block on one side of a decapitated coleoptile in the dark. If the tip acted only through living tissue, the block should do nothing. The coleoptile curved away from the block, and plain agar caused no curve. A substance from the tips had entered the agar and sped elongation beneath it. The angle could be measured, making a **bioassay**: a measurement of a chemical through the response of living tissue. Went measured growth activity, not the chemical; IAA was identified later by others.

Why does the shaded side get more? The **Cholodny-Went hypothesis** says that light from one side moves auxin sideways toward the shaded flank, which then elongates faster, so the shoot bends toward the light. In Arabidopsis hypocotyls, light changes the position in the cell membrane of **PIN3**, a carrier that exports auxin from cells. Many PIN carriers sit mainly on one face of a cell, so repositioning them steers auxin. The light itself is detected by a blue-light receptor first studied as NPH1, now called phototropin.

### Worked problem: reading a curvature bioassay

These numbers are illustrative. Assume all agar blocks are the same size and collect for the same time, no auxin is lost, and curvature is proportional to auxin concentration in this range.

> **auxin concentration (mg per L) = curvature (degrees) / 200 degrees per mg per L**

1. *Calibrate.* Standard blocks with 0.02, 0.04 and 0.08 mg per L of auxin give 4, 8 and 16 degrees. In each, curvature divided by concentration is 200 degrees per mg per L.
2. *Split tips.* Tips lit from one side stand on two blocks, separated by a thin barrier that reaches only partway up each tip, so the top stays joined and each side drains into its own block. A barrier through the whole tip stops auxin moving sideways and removes the difference. The lit-half block gives 6 degrees: 6 / 200 = 0.03 mg per L. The shaded-half block gives 12 degrees: 12 / 200 = 0.06 mg per L.
3. *Shares.* Total = 0.03 + 0.06 = 0.09 mg per L. Shaded share = 0.06 / 0.09 = 67%; lit share = 33%. The ratio is 2 to 1.
4. *Dark control.* A block from whole tips kept in the dark gives 18 degrees: 18 / 200 = 0.09 mg per L, the same total.

Light did not destroy auxin on the lit side, or the total would have fallen. It moved auxin to the shaded side, as the Cholodny-Went idea predicts. Real split-tip experiments exist; these numbers are not theirs.

## Up and down: gravitropism in roots and shoots

**Gravitropism** is a growth response to gravity. Lay a seedling on its side, and the shoot curves up while the root curves down.

Sensing starts with weight. In the central cells of the root cap, the **columella**, and in a starch sheath in shoots, plastids packed with dense starch act as **statoliths**: when Caspar and Pickard (1989) inverted wild-type Arabidopsis seedlings, these plastids sank toward the new lower side. Vertebrates use the same physics: in the inner ear, dense crystals shift with gravity and bend sensory hair cells ([sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/)). Statoliths are not the only sensor: starchless Arabidopsis mutants grown in light kept substantial root gravitropism.

In Arabidopsis root columella cells, PIN3 relocated quickly to the new lower face after the root was turned (Friml and colleagues, 2002), redirecting auxin toward the lower side.

The two organs respond in opposite ways to the same lower-side auxin. In the root, auxin inhibits elongation of epidermal cells on the lower side, the upper side grows faster, and the root curves down. In the shoot, extra auxin speeds elongation on the lower side, and the shoot curves up. The plant is not reaching for anything: each flank's growth rate follows its auxin level, and the curve is the difference.

## Light as information: phytochrome, night length and a travelling signal

**Phytochrome** is a pigment protein that switches between two forms. The **Pr** form absorbs red light and converts to **Pfr**; Pfr absorbs far-red light and converts back to Pr. In an assay of Arabidopsis phytochrome B, 660 nm light drove Pr to Pfr and 740 nm the reverse. In the simple model, Pfr is the active form. Pfr can also revert to Pr slowly in darkness. The lettuce-seed experiment that revealed this switch is the design problem.

**Photoperiodism** is a response to the relative lengths of day and night. A **short-day plant** flowers when nights are longer than a critical length; a **long-day plant** flowers when nights are shorter. Karl Hamner and James Bonner (1938) varied light and dark periods separately in cocklebur, Xanthium, a short-day plant. They reported that flowering depended mainly on the length of the dark period, and that the leaves perceived the inductive treatment. A brief flash of red light in the middle of a long night can stop a short-day plant from flowering, and phytochrome detects that flash. Phytochrome is not the timer itself: the plant's sensitivity to such a flash rises and falls with its daily clock. Flowers form at the shoot apex, so a signal must travel there from the leaf.

In Arabidopsis, a long-day plant, part of the signal is known. The daily clock makes the RNA for the protein CONSTANS rise late in the day. The protein is stabilized by light in the evening and degraded in the morning or in darkness, so it builds up only when days are long enough for that late rise to happen in light. CONSTANS then promotes transcription of the gene FT in the leaf phloem. Corbesier and colleagues (2007) showed that FT protein made in phloem moves to the shoot apex, including across grafts. The daily clock behind this is in [sleep and rhythms](/lessons/sleep-and-rhythms/), and flowering itself in [plant reproduction](/lessons/plant-reproduction/). Do not assume the CONSTANS mechanism applies to cocklebur: it was worked out in Arabidopsis.

## Signals of attack

Unlike vertebrates, plants have no circulating immune cells; each cell defends itself, and hormones spread the alarm. In Arabidopsis, a triple mutant with almost no jasmonate was attacked by larvae of the fungus gnat Bradysia impatiens, and applied methyl jasmonate substantially protected it. In tomato, caterpillars or moths that merely brushed and broke leaf hairs switched on jasmonate-regulated defence genes before any chewing.

Salicylic acid works in **systemic acquired resistance**, in which an infection in one part of a plant leaves uninfected parts more resistant to later attack. In tobacco infected with tobacco mosaic virus, salicylic acid was necessary for this response in the transgenic plants tested. Grafting experiments in tobacco showed that it is needed in the distant, protected leaves but is not itself the signal that travels there.

"Jasmonate for insects, salicylic acid for pathogens" is a tendency from tested systems, not a rule. The receptors that first detect a pathogen are shared logic with animals, and [innate immunity](/lessons/innate-immunity/) covers them: surface receptors start pattern-triggered immunity, mostly intracellular receptors start effector-triggered immunity, and in Arabidopsis tested with the bacterium Pseudomonas syringae, the two arms strengthened each other and neither alone gave effective resistance.

### Design problem

Lettuce seeds soaked in the dark get brief flashes of light, return to the dark, and germination is scored. Illustrative results, one seed batch, equal flash doses: dark only, 10%; red, 90%; red then far-red, 15%; red, far-red, red, 88%; red, far-red, red, far-red, 18%. What do they tell you, and what do they not?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Look at the last flash. Sequences ending in red give 88 to 90% germination; sequences ending in far-red give 15 to 18%, close to the dark control. Total light does not decide it: four flashes ending in far-red germinate less than one red flash.

The simplest explanation is one pigment that switches reversibly between two forms. Red light converts it to a form that promotes germination, and far-red light converts it back to the inactive form. This is the logic of Borthwick, Hendricks and colleagues' 1952 Grand Rapids lettuce experiments.

The dark seeds, equal doses and a single batch make the reading safer: differences are then likely to come from the order of flashes rather than the seeds, though replicate groups would show how reliable they are.

The data do not identify the pigment chemically, and do not show that other seeds respond the same way; many need no light to germinate.
