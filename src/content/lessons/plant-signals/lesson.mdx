      coleoptiles on small blocks of gel such as agar, removed the tips, and set a block on one
      side of a coleoptile whose own tip had been cut off, all in darkness. If the tip acted only

      a response to the total light received. The paper did not isolate the pigment. It set out

  - name: "Tip covering and barrier experiments"
    blurb: >-
      Cover a seedling tip with an opaque cap or a transparent tube, or insert an impermeable sheet
      such as mica into a cut on one side, then light the seedling from one side. Comparing the
      treatments shows where light is sensed and which route the signal takes, provided a control
      shows the covering or cut alone does not stop growth.
  - name: "Oat coleoptile curvature bioassay"
    blurb: >-
      Let excised tips release their growth substance into agar, then place the block on one side
      of a decapitated coleoptile in the dark and measure the angle of curvature. Within a
      calibrated range, more curvature means more auxin activity in the block.
  - name: "Sequential red and far-red light treatments"
    blurb: >-
      Give imbibed seeds or plants brief flashes of red or far-red light in different orders, with
      dark controls, and score germination or flowering. A response set by the last flash points
      to a pigment that switches reversibly between two forms.
  - name: "Grafting with tagged proteins"
    blurb: >-
      Join the shoot of one plant to another, or make a protein carrying a visible tag only in
      certain cells, and look for the tag elsewhere. Finding a phloem-made FT protein at the shoot
      apex shows that the protein itself can travel.

The first film answers the hook; the text adds the controls, the steering and the limits.

> **The one idea.** A shoot or root bends because its two **flanks**, its opposite sides, grow at different rates. Light or gravity sends more of the hormone **auxin** to one flank. Over the range of auxin these seedlings use, the auxin-rich flank grows faster in a shoot and slower in a root.
>
> *Scope:* tropisms of young seedlings in the species tested (oat and other grasses, and Arabidopsis). The evidence that light moves auxin rather than destroying it comes from grass coleoptile tips and supports, rather than proves, that reading; for some broad-leaved seedling stems it is still argued over. Growth is not proportional to auxin: past a peak, more auxin slows growth.

## Bending toward light: what the controls ruled out

A **hormone** is a chemical made in small amounts that changes what cells do, in the cell that made it or elsewhere in the body. Vertebrates make most of theirs in glands and carry them in blood ([the endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/)); in plants, many kinds of cell make hormones, and the response is often a change in growth. A **tropism** is growth toward or away from a one-sided stimulus, and **phototropism** is one in response to light. The classic experiments used grass **coleoptiles**, the sheath that covers the first leaf of a grass seedling as it pushes up through the soil.

Each of the film's results is only as strong as its control:

- **The Darwins.** An opaque cap might stop bending just by covering the tip. Transparent glass tubes also covered the tips, yet bending continued, and the lower part was lit throughout. Only with both controls does the cap place sensing at the tip.
- **Boysen-Jensen.** Tips cut off and set back in place still produced bending below the cut, so the influence crossed a wound. Mica (a mineral sheet that dissolved chemicals cannot cross) on the lit side controls for the shaded-side mica: both cuts wounded, but only the shaded-side barrier blocked bending.
- **Went.** Darkness ruled out one-sided light, and cutting off the coleoptile's own tip ruled out that tip. Plain **agar** (a seaweed gel) caused no curve, ruling out the gel.

Went's angle could be measured, making a **bioassay**: a measurement of a chemical through the response of living tissue. Within a calibrated range (the concentrations a standard line covers), more curvature means more auxin activity. Went measured activity, not the chemical; indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), the main natural auxin, was identified later by others.

## Moved or destroyed?

The film's split tips (Briggs and colleagues, 1957) hide one design detail: the barrier ran up from the base but stopped short of the top, so each half drained into its own agar block while auxin could still cross above.

Sideways movement of auxin toward the shaded flank is the **Cholodny-Went hypothesis**, named for the two botanists who proposed it.

How is auxin steered? It leaves a cell through carriers called **PIN** proteins, and many sit mainly on one face, so their placement sets auxin's direction. In *Arabidopsis thaliana*, a small weed that is the standard lab plant, light from one side changes where the carrier **PIN3** sits on the cell membrane in the **hypocotyl**, the seedling stem below the seed leaves, helping steer auxin toward the shaded side. The light is detected by a blue-light receptor, **phototropin** (first studied as NPH1).

## Up and down: gravitropism in roots and shoots

**Gravitropism** is a growth response to gravity. Laid on its side, a seedling's shoot turns up and its root turns down, though both gather auxin below. The second film works out how, using the growth peak from the first film.

The film stayed in the root cap, where PIN3 moves quickly to the new lower face (Friml and colleagues, 2002). Shoots have statoliths too, in the **endodermis**, a starch sheath around the stem's central tissue, and are thought to sense by weight too, though the evidence comes from roots: when Caspar and Pickard (1989) inverted wild-type Arabidopsis seedlings, the plastids in the root-cap columella sank toward the new lower side. Vertebrates use the same physics: in the inner ear, dense crystals shift with gravity and bend sensory hair cells ([sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/)).

## The other hormones

**Predict.** Cut the growing tip off a shoot. What happens to the side buds below it?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In a shoot with strong apical dominance, they usually start to grow. Losing the tip's auxin is part of the release, with cytokinins and strigolactones (below).

</details>

The main plant hormones, each with one effect and the organism it was shown in:

- **Auxin** speeds elongation of shoot cells (in Arabidopsis, on the shaded side of the hypocotyl), drives tropisms, and helps maintain **apical dominance**, the suppression of side buds by the growing shoot tip. It does this together with cytokinins and **strigolactones**, a further hormone class that holds side buds back.
- **Cytokinins** promote cell division and slow leaf ageing. In transgenic tobacco that made extra cytokinin in ageing leaves, leaf **senescence**, the programmed yellowing and death of a leaf, was delayed.
- **Gibberellins** promote stem elongation and seed germination, the opposite of abscisic acid's role in keeping seeds dormant. In the grain's outer living layer, isolated from barley, gibberellic acid increased synthesis of a starch-digesting enzyme (Going deeper).
- **Abscisic acid (ABA)** induces stomatal closure in Arabidopsis ([plant transport](/lessons/plant-transport/) has the guard cells) and maintains **dormancy**, a state of arrested growth in seeds and buds.
- **Ethylene**, a gas, promotes fruit ripening. Tomatoes engineered to make little ethylene failed to ripen normally, and supplied ethylene restored ripening.
- **Brassinosteroids**, steroid hormones, promote cell elongation. Supplied brassinosteroids could make up for the short, poorly elongated cells of several Arabidopsis mutants.
- **Jasmonates** signal defence against plant-eating animals, and **salicylic acid** against some pathogens (end of the lesson).

## Light as information: phytochrome, night length and a travelling signal

**Phytochrome** is a pigment protein that switches between two forms. The **Pr** form absorbs red light and converts to **Pfr**; Pfr absorbs far-red light and converts back to Pr. In an assay of Arabidopsis phytochrome B, 660 nm light drove Pr to Pfr and 740 nm the reverse (neither gives a pure form; Going deeper). In the simple model, Pfr is the active form. Pfr can also revert to Pr slowly in darkness. The lettuce-seed experiment that revealed this switch is the design problem.

**Photoperiodism** is a response to the relative lengths of day and night. A **short-day plant** flowers when nights are longer than a critical length; a **long-day plant** flowers when nights are shorter. Karl Hamner and James Bonner (1938) varied light and dark periods separately in cocklebur, Xanthium, a short-day plant. They reported that flowering depended mainly on the length of the dark period, and that the leaves perceived the inductive treatment. A brief flash of red light in the middle of a long night can stop a short-day plant from flowering. In the model taught here, phytochrome detects that flash but does not time the night: a daily clock does, and the plant's sensitivity to such a flash rises and falls with that clock. Flowers form at the shoot apex, so a signal must travel there from the leaf.

In Arabidopsis, a facultative long-day plant (long days speed its flowering, but it still flowers in short days), part of the signal is known. The daily clock makes the RNA for the protein CONSTANS rise late in the day. The protein is stabilized by light in the evening and degraded in the morning or in darkness, so it builds up only when days are long enough for that late rise to happen in light. CONSTANS then promotes transcription of the gene FT in the leaf phloem. Corbesier and colleagues (2007) showed that FT protein made in phloem moves to the shoot apex, including across grafts. For the clock see [sleep and rhythms](/lessons/sleep-and-rhythms/); for flowering, [plant reproduction](/lessons/plant-reproduction/). Do not assume it applies to cocklebur.

## Signals of attack

Unlike vertebrates, plants have no circulating immune cells; each cell defends itself, and hormones spread the alarm. In Arabidopsis, a triple mutant with almost no jasmonate was attacked by larvae of the fungus gnat Bradysia impatiens, and applied methyl jasmonate substantially protected it. In tomato, insects that merely broke leaf hairs switched on jasmonate-regulated defence genes before any chewing.

Salicylic acid works in **systemic acquired resistance**, in which an infection in one part of a plant leaves uninfected parts more resistant to later attack. In tobacco infected with tobacco mosaic virus, salicylic acid was necessary for this response in the transgenic plants tested. Grafting experiments in tobacco showed that it is needed in the distant, protected leaves but is not itself the signal that travels there.

"Jasmonate for insects, salicylic acid for pathogens" is a tendency from tested systems, not a rule. The receptors that first detect a pathogen share logic with animals; [innate immunity](/lessons/innate-immunity/) covers them.

### Design problem

Lettuce seeds soaked in the dark get brief flashes of light, return to the dark, and germination is scored. Illustrative results, one seed batch, equal flash doses: dark only, 10%; red, 90%; red then far-red, 15%; red, far-red, red, 88%; red, far-red, red, far-red, 18%. What do they tell you, and what do they not?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Look at the last flash. Sequences ending in red give 88 to 90% germination; sequences ending in far-red give 15 to 18%, close to the dark control. Total light does not decide it: four flashes ending in far-red germinate less than one red flash.

The simplest explanation is one pigment that switches reversibly: red light converts it to a form that promotes germination, and far-red converts it back. This is the logic of Borthwick, Hendricks and colleagues' 1952 Grand Rapids lettuce experiments.

The dark control, equal doses and a single batch point the differences at the order of flashes rather than the seeds; replicate groups would show how reliable they are.

The data neither identify the pigment chemically nor show that other seeds respond alike; many need no light to germinate.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The early experiments in more detail

The title page of The Power of Movement in Plants credits Darwin's son Francis for assistance. Boysen-Jensen's conclusion was disputed at the time: van der Wolk argued the influence could pass through any living cells, not only down the shaded side. Later barrier experiments were needed to settle it.

### Worked problem: reading a curvature bioassay

These numbers are illustrative. Assume all agar blocks are the same size and collect for the same time, no auxin is lost during collection, and curvature is proportional to auxin concentration in this range.

> **auxin concentration (mg per L) = curvature (degrees) / 200 degrees per mg per L**

1. *Calibrate.* Standard blocks with 0.02, 0.04 and 0.08 mg per L of auxin give 4, 8 and 16 degrees. In each, curvature divided by concentration is 200 degrees per mg per L.
2. *Split tips.* Tips lit from one side stand on two blocks, split partway up by a thin barrier as in Briggs's design. The lit-half block gives 6 degrees: 6 / 200 = 0.03 mg per L. The shaded-half block gives 12 degrees: 12 / 200 = 0.06 mg per L.
3. *Shares.* With block volume V litres, amounts are concentration times V: total = 0.03V + 0.06V = 0.09V mg. Shaded share = 0.06V / 0.09V = 67%; lit share = 33%. The ratio is 2 to 1.
4. *Dark control.* A block of the same size from whole tips kept in the dark gives 18 degrees: 18 / 200 = 0.09 mg per L, so 0.09V mg, the same total amount.

### Inside the cell and inside the root

Inside cells, auxin binds a receptor that triggers destruction of repressor proteins ([ubiquitin and proteolysis](/lessons/ubiquitin-and-proteolysis/); [the receptor field guide](/lessons/receptor-field-guide/)). The second film's curves are the classic textbook picture, drawn against auxin on a log scale, with the root's peak at far lower auxin; where a real flank sits on its curve has not been measured.

### Why neither colour gives a pure form

The absorption spectra of Pr and Pfr overlap, so red light leaves a mixture that is mostly Pfr and far-red light leaves a small fraction of Pfr; neither treatment produces a pure form. The design-problem values are illustrative and have no replicate variation, so do not read anything into the small gap between the far-red-last groups and the dark control.

### Gibberellin in barley aleurone

In isolated aleurone layers (the living outer layer of a cereal grain's food store) of Himalaya barley, gibberellic acid increased synthesis of alpha-amylase, a starch-digesting enzyme, after a lag of 6 to 8 hours.

### Pattern-triggered and effector-triggered immunity

Surface receptors start pattern-triggered immunity, mostly intracellular receptors start effector-triggered immunity, and in Arabidopsis tested with the bacterium Pseudomonas syringae, the two arms strengthened each other and neither alone gave effective resistance.
