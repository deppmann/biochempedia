    contribution: >-
      Found that the white-eye mutation in Drosophila is inherited differently in reciprocal
      crosses, and explained the pattern by coupling the factor for red eyes with a sex factor,
      which he and his students later placed on the X chromosome.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Morgan, Sex limited inheritance in Drosophila, Science 32:120-122 (1910); Morgan, Sturtevant, Muller & Bridges, The Mechanism of Mendelian Heredity, revised edition (1922)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In 1910 a male fruit fly with white eyes turned up among the red-eyed flies of Morgan's
      laboratory at Columbia University. Morgan crossed him to red-eyed females, and the offspring
      all but three had red eyes (Morgan set the three white-eyed males aside as a further mutation), so white behaved as a recessive. When those offspring were mated to one
      another, white eyes came back in the next generation, but only in males. He followed up with
      further crosses, including white-eyed females to red-eyed males, which gave red-eyed
      daughters and white-eyed sons. In a short paper in Science that year, titled "Sex limited
      inheritance in Drosophila", he explained the results by supposing that the factor for red eyes and a sex factor,
      X, travel together in the sperm. His students, among them Alfred
      Sturtevant, Calvin Bridges and Hermann Muller, built on these crosses; their joint book
      explained the white pattern by a gene on the X chromosome. Morgan received the 1933 Nobel
      Prize in Physiology or Medicine for work on the role of chromosomes in heredity. The
      white-eyed fly made it possible to assign a particular gene to a particular chromosome.

    story: >-
      A fertilized mouse egg holds two pronuclei for a short time, one from the egg and one from
      the sperm, before they combine. McGrath and Solter used micromanipulation to remove a

techniques:
  - name: "Reciprocal crosses with sex-stratified scoring"
    blurb: >-
      The same two phenotypes are crossed twice, swapping which sex carries the mutant allele,
      and offspring are counted separately by sex. An ordinary autosomal gene, one that is not imprinted, gives the same result both
      ways; an X-linked gene does not. This is how Morgan's white-eyed crosses pointed to the X.
  - name: "Pronuclear transplantation"
    blurb: >-
      A pronucleus is removed from a fertilized egg with a fine pipette and replaced with one
      from another egg. Embryos can be built with chosen parental combinations, with
      one-of-each controls made the same way. In mouse it showed that both parental genomes are
      needed.
  - name: "Transgenesis to test sufficiency"
    blurb: >-
      A defined piece of DNA is injected into fertilized eggs so that it integrates into the
      genome. Koopman and colleagues added a 14-kilobase fragment containing Sry to chromosomally
      female mouse embryos and asked whether testes formed.
  - name: "RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization"
    blurb: >-
      A fluorescent probe complementary to an RNA is applied to fixed cells to show where in the
      cell that RNA sits. Brown and colleagues used it to show human XIST RNA at the position of
      the Barr body, on the inactive X.

      - "His Y chromosome carries a second copy of the w allele"

    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A male fly has one X, so for X-linked genes he is hemizygous: whatever allele his X
      carries is the only one present, and a recessive allele shows. The idea that his X is
      inactivated borrows the mammalian female mechanism; flies do not silence an X, and a male
      silencing his only X would lose all X expression. The Y does not carry the white gene, so
      it cannot supply a second copy. The allele is not dominant in males; it shows because
      there is no dominant partner allele to mask it.
  - stem: "A white-eyed female fly (XwXw) is mated to a red-eyed male (X+Y). Assuming ordinary segregation, what are their F1 offspring?"
    choices:
      - "All red-eyed, in daughters and in sons"
      - "All white-eyed, in daughters and in sons"
      - "Red-eyed daughters and white-eyed sons"
      - "White-eyed daughters and red-eyed sons"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Every daughter receives her father's X+ and a maternal Xw, so she is X+Xw and red-eyed;
      every son receives his only X from his mother, Xw, and a Y from his father, so he is
      white-eyed. This is criss-cross inheritance. All red would be expected if the gene were
      autosomal and the red parent homozygous. All white would require the father's X to carry
      w. White daughters and red sons reverse the direction of X transmission, as if sons
      inherited their X from the father.

**In 1910 a white-eyed male fruit fly appeared in Thomas Hunt Morgan's laboratory, and swapping which parent was white changed the offspring.**

Mendel's peas gave the same result whichever plant was the mother.

> **The one idea.** Inheritance patterns depend on allele identity, chromosome location, and parental origin.
>
> *Scope:* the inheritance patterns in this lesson. Most alleles do not show a parent-of-origin effect, and the rule does not replace Mendelian segregation.

## One white-eyed male: follow the X

In Morgan's laboratory at Columbia University, which bred *Drosophila melanogaster*, the white-eyed male crossed to red-eyed females gave red-eyed offspring of both sexes, so white is **recessive**: it shows only when no dominant allele is present. He published the work that year in *Science* as "Sex limited inheritance in Drosophila." **Sex-linked** means carried on a sex chromosome.

A female fly is XX and a male XY ([chromosomes and meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/)). The white gene sits on the X; the Y carries no copy. A male is therefore **hemizygous** for white: one allele rather than a pair, so whatever his X carries is what you see. Write X+ for red and Xw for white.

The first film traced both F1 crosses. The table assumes ordinary segregation, equal survival, half the offspring of each sex and complete penetrance.

| Cross | Daughters | Sons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Red X+X+ female × white XwY male | X+Xw, red | X+Y, red |
| White XwXw female × red X+Y male | X+Xw, red | XwY, white |

The second row is the **reciprocal cross**, the same phenotypes with the sexes swapped. It shows **criss-cross inheritance**: father to daughter, mother to son.

The film stopped at the F1. Intercross the first row's F1, X+Xw by X+Y. Daughters get X+ from their father, so they are half X+X+ and half X+Xw, all red. Sons get X+ or Xw from their mother, so half are white. The F2 expectation is three red to one white, every white fly male.

**Predict.** Now intercross the reciprocal F1, X+Xw by XwY. What four genotype-and-sex classes should appear, and in what proportions?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The mother makes X+ and Xw eggs; the father makes Xw and Y sperm. Each pairing has probability one half times one half, one quarter:

> **X+Xw red female : XwXw white female : X+Y red male : XwY white male = 1 : 1 : 1 : 1**

Among 400 F2 flies, expect 100 in each class: 200 of each sex and 200 white. Real broods vary around an expectation. In Morgan's own F2 from the first cross, white-eyed males made up less than one quarter of all flies.

</details>

With the same parental genotypes, an ordinary non-imprinted autosomal allele gives the same F1 both ways.

### The exceptions that tested the model

Calvin Bridges crossed white XwXw females to red X+Y males. Roughly 1 in 1,600 offspring were exceptional white daughters or red sons.

**Predict.** Keep white on the X. What meiotic error could make both exceptions, and what chromosome sets should they carry?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If the mother's X chromosomes occasionally fail to separate, a **nondisjunction** covered in [chromosome changes](/lessons/chromosome-changes/), some eggs carry XwXw and others no X.

- XwXw egg plus Y sperm gives an XwXwY white daughter, with both X chromosomes from her mother.
- An egg with no X plus X+ sperm gives an X+O red son. His only X came from his father, and without a Y he should be sterile.
- XwXw plus X+, and an empty egg plus Y, would be expected to die or be rare.

The model predicts that exceptional white daughters carry a Y and exceptional red sons lack one. It also predicts that an XXY female, whose two X chromosomes and Y must sort at meiosis, produces XX and Y eggs much more often than 1 in 1,600. Bridges found both and published the work in 1916. Matching an unusual phenotype to an unusual chromosome set tied white to the X. It also showed that an XO fly is male and an XXY fly female, so the Y does not decide sex in flies.

</details>

Human X-linked conditions follow the same logic. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency ([pentose phosphate pathway](/lessons/pentose-phosphate-pathway/)) and HPRT deficiency in Lesch-Nyhan syndrome ([nucleotide metabolism](/lessons/nucleotide-metabolism/)) are both much more common in males. [Pedigrees](/lessons/pedigrees/) follows the pattern through families.

## How sex is decided: mammal versus fly

**Predict.** Bridges' exceptions were XO males and XXY females. XO and XXY people also occur. If the Y were the sex switch in humans, how should each develop?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In most mammals, including mice and humans, one Y carrying **SRY** in humans or *Sry* in mice starts testis development. Thus XO individuals with Turner syndrome develop as females, while XXY individuals with Klinefelter syndrome develop as males. Sinclair and colleagues reported SRY in 1990. In 1991 Koopman and colleagues added a 14-kilobase fragment containing *Sry* to chromosomally female mouse embryos; it was sufficient to make them develop testes.

In flies, as Bridges found, the Y is needed for male fertility, not maleness; in a diploid fly, two X chromosomes ordinarily specify female development and one specifies male.

</details>

The classic fly description uses the ratio of X chromosomes to sets of autosomes. Erickson and Quintero argued in 2007 that the embryo instead reads the combined dose of several X-linked signal genes. Either way, high X dose switches on **Sex-lethal** in females, which maintains its own activity through RNA splicing and steers splicing of genes below it.

## Balancing X-linked output

In a simplified model with every X-linked copy fully active, an XX animal begins with twice the dose of an XY or XO animal. **Dosage compensation** reduces that difference.

**Predict.** Which strategies could bring two active X copies and one active X copy toward the same output?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Turn one of two X chromosomes down, turn the single X up, or reduce output from both. Different animals use all three.

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/sex-linkage-and-beyond/dosage-compensation-three-ways.webp" width={1200} height={631} alt="AI-generated illustration: three nuclei side by side showing a female mammal nucleus with one condensed X coated by Xist RNA, a male fly nucleus whose single X is coated by the MSL complex and roX RNAs, and a worm nucleus whose two X chromosomes are both turned down." caption="Mammals mainly inactivate one female X, flies raise output from the single male X, and C. elegans hermaphrodites reduce output from both X chromosomes." />

**Mammals mainly inactivate one female X.** Mary Lyon proposed in 1961 that each cell of a female mouse inactivates either its maternal or paternal X early in development, and its descendants keep that choice. A heterozygous female is therefore a **mosaic**, a patchwork of cell lineages expressing different X-linked alleles. The tortoiseshell cat makes this visible: its X-linked Orange alleles produce orange and usually black patches. A calico is a tortoiseshell with an autosomal white-spotting allele, which adds patches that X inactivation does not explain. Tortoiseshell and calico males are rare and usually XXY.

In placental mammals the inactive X forms the condensed **Barr body** and is coated by **Xist**, an RNA that appears not to encode protein. Mouse deletions showed that Xist is required for inactivation. It acts in cis, silencing the chromosome that makes it. Mosaicism also matters in Rett syndrome, which mainly affects girls and involves X-linked *MECP2* ([synapse disorders](/lessons/synapse-disorders/)): a girl's cells can differ in which *MECP2* allele they express.

**Flies turn the male X up.** The MSL, or male-specific lethal, complex coats the single male X with help from the noncoding RNAs roX1 and roX2. It raises transcription of active X-linked genes toward the output of two female X chromosomes. In females, Sex-lethal blocks translation of the mRNA for MSL2, the subunit that starts complex assembly.

So far, a parent mattered only through the sex chromosome it sends, and in Lyon's model either parent's X can be the one silenced. The second film asks whether parent of origin matters beyond the sex chromosomes.

## Genes that remember their parent

In short, parent of origin can decide which DNA arrives or which allele acts.

### Organelle genes: which DNA arrives

As the second film showed, organelle genomes are **generally inherited from the mother**. In humans, Giles and colleagues showed in 1980 that children carried their mother's pattern of mitochondrial DNA variants rather than their father's; sons and daughters inherited the pattern, but only daughters passed it on. Human sperm mitochondria do enter the egg, but a 2023 study found that mature human sperm mitochondria lack intact mitochondrial DNA. Baur's *Pelargonium* showed plastid inheritance from both parents, and paternal or biparental organelle transmission occurs in other species.

### Genomic imprinting: which allele acts

In 1984 McGrath and Solter, and independently Surani, Barton and Norris, built mouse embryos with two maternal or two paternal genomes. Controls underwent the same manipulation but kept one genome of each parental kind.

**Predict.** If maternal and paternal nuclear genomes were functionally interchangeable, how should all three groups develop? What result would show that parental origin matters?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If the genomes were interchangeable, every diploid embryo should complete development. Instead, neither uniparental group did, while the one-of-each controls did. The parental genomes are not functionally equivalent.

</details>

**Genomic imprinting** explains the difference: a subset of nuclear genes is expressed from only one parental allele. The second film's parental mark is chiefly DNA methylation at imprinting control regions, laid down during egg or sperm formation, erased in each generation's primordial germ cells and rewritten according to the animal's sex. The mark therefore follows the parent, not the grandparent.

In mouse, the growth-factor gene *Igf2* is expressed from the paternal allele in most tissues; nearby *H19* is expressed from the maternal allele. A disrupted paternal *Igf2* makes a mouse small, while the same allele inherited from the mother leaves size normal.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/sex-linkage-and-beyond/imprinting-embryo-experiment.webp" width={1200} height={790} alt="AI-generated illustration: three mouse embryo outlines, one with a maternal and a paternal genome that develops fully, one with two maternal genomes and one with two paternal genomes, both marked as not completing development." caption="Only the operated control embryos with one maternal and one paternal genome completed normal development, showing that the parental genomes are not functionally equivalent." />

In humans, imprinted genes lie in region 15q11-q13. Loss of the paternal contribution causes Prader-Willi syndrome; loss of the maternal contribution causes Angelman syndrome.

## Going deeper

### Refining X inactivation

X inactivation is not random everywhere. It is random in the mouse embryo proper, but the paternal X is inactivated in mouse extraembryonic tissues and in marsupials. Nor is the inactive X completely silent: a 2017 human-tissue survey found incomplete inactivation in no fewer than 23 percent of the X-linked genes it assessed. [Eukaryotic regulation](/lessons/eukaryotic-regulation/) develops the silencing mechanism.

### How fly and worm compensation differ

The fly MSL complex includes the enzyme MOF, which acetylates histone H4 at lysine 16, a mark associated with open chromatin. MSL knockdown measured an effect smaller than twofold, so other mechanisms may contribute. Forcing MSL2 into females gathers the complex on their X chromosomes and lowers female viability. In the nematode *C. elegans*, XX hermaphrodites instead reduce expression from each X by about half, matching the single X of XO males.
