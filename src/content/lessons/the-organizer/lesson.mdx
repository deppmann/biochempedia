    contribution: >-
      Performed and documented, for her doctoral thesis, the grafts of dorsal lip tissue between a
      pale and a pigmented newt species that showed a transplanted organizer makes host cells form
      a second neural tube.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Spemann & Mangold, Arch. mikrosk. Anat. Entwicklungsmech. 100:599 (1924), English translation by V. Hamburger, Int. J. Dev. Biol. 45:13 (2001); Hamburger, J. Hist. Biol. 17:1 (1984)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Hilde Mangold, born Hilde Pröscholdt, came to Hans Spemann's laboratory in Freiburg as a
      doctoral student. In the summers of 1921 and 1922 she grafted small pieces of the dorsal lip
      region between gastrulae of two newt species, one pale and one pigmented. The work was hard.
      The embryos had to be freed from their protective membranes, operated on, and kept alive
      through gastrulation and the closing of the neural tube, and most of them died. She fixed the
      survivors, cut them into thin sections, and drew the figures for the paper herself, tracing
      nuclei and cell borders so that pale donor cells could be told from pigmented host cells.
      Those sections carried the argument: the second neural tube was built largely, and in one
      embryo entirely, by host cells. The paper appeared in 1924, the year she died. It gave
      embryologists a cellular fact to explain, that one tissue can change the fate of its
      neighbours, and so made the search for the signal a sensible project.
  - name: "Hans Spemann"
    contribution: >-

      make, then set the dorsal-lip graft question that Mangold answered; received the 1935 Nobel

    profileSourceAnchor: "Spemann, Nobel Lecture, 12 December 1935; Sander & Fässler, Int. J. Dev. Biol. 45:1 (2001)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In papers from 1901 to 1903, written in Würzburg, Spemann used a loop of fine hair to
      constrict newt eggs. He used loops of very fine hair; Sander and Fässler describe them as made of the hair of his baby daughter. By tightening the loop to different degrees and at different stages he
      could divide an embryo partly or completely, and he obtained doubled front ends, twin
      embryos, and halves that stayed alive without forming an axis. The loop showed that whether
      a fragment could make a whole embryo depended on its stage, its position and the material it
      contained. It could not show which tissue supplied what was missing. That needed
      transplantation. In Freiburg he posed the question of what the dorsal lip does when moved to
      a new place, supervised Hilde Mangold's grafts and developed their interpretation. The Nobel
      Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to him alone in 1935, and in his lecture he said the dorsal-lip
      experiment was first carried out at his instigation by Hilde Mangold. His organizer program made the search for inducing
      signals possible.
  - name: "Johannes Holtfreter"
    contribution: >-
      Showed from 1933 that organizer tissue killed by freezing, drying or heat could still turn
      competent amphibian ectoderm into neural tissue, and that many other tissues, when killed,

    profileSourceAnchor: "Holtfreter, Wilhelm Roux' Arch. Entwicklungsmech. Org. 128:584 (1933); Gerhart, Biographical Memoirs of the National Academy of Sciences 73:209 (1998)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Holtfreter asked which part of the organizer had to be alive. He developed a balanced salt
      solution and sterile methods that kept small pieces of amphibian embryos alive in a dish
      long enough to see what they became, so he could test tissues away from the rest of the
      embryo. Pieces of early-gastrula ectoderm cultured alone became skin. He then put such
      ectoderm against organizer tissue that he had frozen, dried or heated, in some series to
      boiling. The ectoderm often became neural tissue, sometimes folded into a tube. The inducing
      activity had survived the death of the cells that carried it. Then the controls turned
      against a simple answer. Many other tissues, once killed, induced too, and other workers found many unrelated materials that worked. In the 1940s, after Lester Barth's first report,
      he found that salamander (Ambystoma) ectoderm could become neural after damage that did not kill it. His
      experiments showed that a substance could carry induction, and also why a yes-or-no neural
      test could not say which substance was the real one.
  - name: "Wilhelm Roux"
    contribution: >-
      Killed one cell of a two-cell frog embryo with a hot needle and obtained half-embryos, which
      he read as evidence that each early cell carries its own share of the future body.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Roux, Ueber die künstliche Hervorbringung halber Embryonen (1888), reprinted in Gesammelte Abhandlungen über Entwickelungsmechanik der Organismen, vol. 2 (1895); Maienschein, in S. F. Gilbert (ed.), A Conceptual History of Modern Embryology (1991)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Roux worked with fertilized eggs of the green frog in Breslau. He first
      pricked one of the first two cells with a fine needle, but such eggs either failed to develop or developed
      normally. He changed to a heated needle, inserted once and left longer than before, and
      kept the eggs in which the injured cell stopped dividing while its sister lived. The survivor
      developed as half an embryo, and he preserved and stained such embryos for microscopic study.
      Roux took the result as support for mosaic development, in which each early cell inherits a
      fixed share of the body. Yet in some later embryos he saw the missing side begin to fill in.
      Because the dead cell stayed attached, the experiment tested damage, not isolation. It posed
      the question that Driesch, and then Spemann, attacked with gentler ways of separating cells.

  - name: "Hair-loop constriction"
    blurb: >-
      A loop of very fine hair is tied around an amphibian egg and tightened by degrees, partly or
      completely dividing it without an open wound. Comparing what each side makes shows how much
      an early fragment can form on its own and what material it needs.
  - name: "Explant culture of competent ectoderm"
    blurb: >-
      Ectoderm is cut from an early gastrula and cultured in a salt solution, alone or wrapped
      around a test tissue. Alone it usually becomes skin; if it becomes neural tissue when ectoderm cultured alone under the
      same conditions does not, the test tissue induced it. The method let Holtfreter test killed and heated tissues.
  - name: "Serial histological sectioning"
    blurb: >-
      The embryo is fixed, embedded and cut into a series of thin slices that are stained and
      examined in order. The series shows the internal anatomy, such as a second neural tube and
      notochord, and the identity of the cells that built it.

**Move a small piece of tissue from one newt embryo into the side of another, and the host can build a second neural tube, mostly out of its own cells that were on their way to becoming skin.**

That result came from grafts Hilde Mangold made in Hans Spemann's laboratory in Freiburg in 1921 and 1922, published with Spemann in 1924. The graft did more than add nervous system: the donor tissue, the organizer, organized a whole second axis. This lesson follows that graft as an argument, from the puzzle it was built to answer to the question it left open: what is the signal?

## One sheet, two fates

After fertilization the egg divides without growing, a process called **cleavage**, into a ball of smaller cells. In amphibians the ball becomes a **blastula**, a sphere of cells around a fluid-filled cavity. Then comes **gastrulation**, the stage at which cells move from the surface to the inside and the embryo sorts itself into three **germ layers**:

- The **ectoderm**, the outer layer, makes the epidermis, the outer layer of the skin, and the nervous system.
- The **mesoderm**, the middle layer, makes muscle, bone, blood, kidney and the **notochord**, a stiff rod of tissue along the back.
- The **endoderm**, the inner layer, lines the gut and forms organs such as the liver.

In an amphibian, cells move inside through a slit in the surface called the **blastopore**. It first appears as a short groove on one side of the embryo, and the edge of that groove is the **dorsal lip**, on what will become the back (dorsal means toward the back; ventral, toward the belly). Cells that roll inward over the dorsal lip become the mesoderm under the back, including the notochord.

Now the puzzle. The brain and spinal cord come from a strip of ectoderm along the back, the **neural plate**, which later rolls up into the **neural tube**. Ectoderm on the belly becomes skin. Both start as the same kind of surface tissue.

**Predict.** Give two different kinds of explanation for why the back strip becomes neural and the belly strip does not. For each, say what should happen if you put dorsal-lip tissue next to belly ectoderm.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

One kind of answer: belly ectoderm is already fixed as skin, and stays skin whatever lies next to it. The other: belly ectoderm can still change, and something nearby tells the back strip what to become; then dorsal-lip tissue placed beside belly ectoderm could make it neural. Moving tissue tests the first answer in its strong form. If belly ectoderm becomes neural beside a dorsal lip, it was not fixed as skin. That would not show that back and belly cells started identical; both kinds of answer can be partly true.

Spemann's hair-loop experiments, in the reading he developed over the following years and that entered the textbooks, had already pointed to the future dorsal side, where gastrulation will begin: a half embryo made a whole one only if it contained material from that side ([Going deeper](#roux-driesch-and-the-loop-of-hair)). The loop could not say what that material did. The graft was built to find out.

</details>

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A cell's fate can be set by its neighbours. Move the organizer somewhere new, and host cells there build much of a second back around it (the graft itself becomes notochord), but only while they are still competent, that is, able to answer.

## The graft: whose cells built the second back?

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Earlier grafts within a single species had shown that a dorsal lip moved to a new place could give extra axial structures. But in a same-species graft the donor's cells look like the host's, so you cannot count whose cells are in the new neural tube. Its origin has two possible readings:

1. **Self-differentiation.** The graft's cells develop according to their own origin and supply the new neural tissue themselves, so the second neural tube is made of donor cells. (In its own embryo the dorsal lip mainly makes notochord and other dorsal mesoderm.)
2. **Induction.** The graft changes the fate of its neighbours. Then host cells build the second neural tube.

The solution was colour. Mangold mainly used two newt species, then called *Triton cristatus* and *Triton taeniatus*. Cells of *cristatus* carry little pigment. Cells of *taeniatus* carry pigment granules that stay visible after the embryo is fixed and cut into thin sections. Natural colour became a **lineage marker**, a visible trait that shows which embryo a cell came from.

She cut a small piece of the dorsal lip region from a pale *cristatus* gastrula and placed it in the flank or belly of a pigmented *taeniatus* gastrula (host stages in the series varied), in tissue that would normally become belly skin. The operations were hard, and most operated embryos died. In the survivors, a second neural plate rose at the graft site, separate from the host's own in the embryos described in detail, folded, and closed into a second neural tube.

**Predict.** In thin sections of that second neural tube, what colour should most of the cells be if the graft simply grew? What colour if it changed its neighbours?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

| If the second tube is built by... | Pale (*cristatus*) cells in the tube | Pigmented (*taeniatus*) cells in the tube | What it would mean |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| donor cells, the graft developing by its own origin (self-differentiation) | nearly all | few or none | no induction shown |
| host cells whose fate was changed (induction) | few or none | nearly all | the graft changed its neighbours |
| both | a mix | a mix | both lineages contribute; the host cells still show induction |

</details>

The sections settled the question. In one embryo, most of the second neural tube was pigmented, and so made of host cells, with a narrow pale strip from the graft along its floor. In another, the second neural tube was entirely host tissue, while the pale graft lay beneath it and formed a second notochord and much of the nearby mesoderm. The graft had changed the fate of host ectoderm that would otherwise have made skin: for the neural tube, induction explained the sections. The graft's own cells self-differentiated into that notochord and mesoderm, so both happened at once. Spemann and Mangold called the dorsal lip region the **organizer**.

## Induction needs two parties

**Induction** is a change in the fate of one tissue caused by a signal from another, the event the pigment sections show. Every induction has two parties. The **inducer** sends the signal. The **responder** has to be able to answer it, and that ability is called **competence**.

Competence belongs to the responder, and it does not last. You can test this by wrapping ectoderm around organizer tissue in a dish, the explant method Holtfreter used, in the salt solution he developed.

**Predict.** Cut ectoderm from newt embryos at four stages: early, mid and late gastrula, and early neurula (the stage after gastrulation, when the neural plate forms). Wrap each piece around organizer tissue from an *early* gastrula and score how often it becomes neural. If the falling response is in the ectoderm, what pattern do you expect? What one extra combination would show that the organizer had not simply stopped signalling?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The response falls with the age of the ectoderm: in amphibians, by the end of gastrulation it largely stops forming neural tissue in such tests. The extra combination is *late* organizer wrapped in *early* ectoderm. If that still gives neural tissue, the organizer is still signalling, and the loss is in the responder.

</details>

Without the inducer, or without competence, that induction does not happen, although some ectoderm can become neural by other routes, as the end of this lesson shows.

## More than one induction: the organizer builds an axis

The second axis in the grafts could have a neural tube, a notochord and **somites**, blocks of mesoderm beside the neural tube that form vertebrae and muscle, aligned head to tail and back to belly. Three things happened at once:

1. The organizer's own cells became notochord and other dorsal mesoderm (pale in the sections).
2. It recruited neighbouring host mesoderm into dorsal fates such as somites.
3. It induced host ectoderm to become a neural tube.

That is why the name fits. An **organizer** is a signalling region that can make its neighbours form a coordinated set of tissues, here a whole axis, in the right order.

Now look at *where* the second axis formed. A **permissive** signal allows an outcome that something else chooses; an **instructive** signal chooses the outcome itself. One test asks who chooses the place: keep the signal present but move its source. If the pattern stays where it was, something else is choosing the place. If the pattern follows the source, the signal is instructive for place: it decides where the pattern forms.

**Predict.** The graft sat in tissue that would have made belly skin. If the organizer only allowed neural tissue where the host had already marked out a back, where should neural tissue form?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Along the host's own back, and the graft site would stay skin. Instead a second neural plate rose at the graft, so the organizer decides *where* neural tissue forms. You will ask the same question about nerve growth factor in [the neurotrophic hypothesis lesson](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/): when the source or delivery moves, does the pattern move with it?

</details>

What the test cannot say is *how*. Suppose ectoderm becomes neural unless something blocks it. An organizer that removed the block locally would also make a plate appear wherever it sat, so supplying a neural signal and releasing a brake both move the plate with the source. If it releases a brake, the neural fate itself was the ectoderm's own, and the organizer would be permissive for neural fate yet still instructive for place. The next lesson takes up which it is.

In the frog *Xenopus*, where this has been worked out best, the place where the dorsal lip forms is set soon after fertilization: β-catenin, the Wnt pathway's transcriptional co-activator, is stabilized on the future dorsal side and enters nuclei there, and with signals from the yolky cells below it switches on organizer genes in the tissue that will form the dorsal lip ([the lesson on Wnt signalling](/lessons/wnt-signaling/)).

## Dead tissue that still induces

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

If the organizer acts through a signal, the signal should be a substance. Johannes Holtfreter tested this from 1933. He froze, dried and heated pieces of newt embryos, in some series to boiling, then placed them against ectoderm from early gastrulae. On its own such ectoderm became skin.

**Predict.** Next to dead organizer, does the ectoderm become neural? And does dead organizer still build an organized second axis?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Next to dead organizer the ectoderm often became neural tissue: neural-inducing activity survived the death of the cells that carried it. Organizing did not: killed tissue made ectoderm neural but did not build an organized second axis in these tests.

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/the-organizer/holtfreter-dead-organizer.webp" width={1200} height={713} alt="AI-generated illustration: two panels. On the left, a sheet of early gastrula ectoderm on its own becomes skin. On the right, a pale killed piece of organizer tissue, treated beforehand, is pressed against the sheet, which often becomes neural tissue." caption="Holtfreter froze, dried and heated pieces of newt embryos, killing them before placing them against ectoderm from early gastrulae. On its own the ectoderm became skin; next to dead organizer it often became neural tissue." />

Two words help here, and you will meet them again in [the neurotrophic hypothesis lesson](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/). A factor is **sufficient** if it is enough, by itself, to produce an effect. It is **necessary** if the effect does not happen without it. Holtfreter's result is a sufficiency result: dead organizer was enough to make competent ectoderm neural. But sufficient does not mean natural: a factor can be enough to produce an effect without being the signal the embryo uses. The controls showed why that mattered. In the 1930s many other killed tissues, ether extracts of amphibian tissue (ether dissolves fats, so the extract holds the tissue's fat-soluble substances) and more and more unrelated materials were found to "induce", at least in some cases.

| Condition | Scores neural? |
| --- | --- |
| Early-gastrula ectoderm cultured alone (the control in these assays) | no; it becomes skin |
| Ectoderm with living organizer | yes, and in the embryo the organizer builds an axis |
| Ectoderm with organizer killed by freezing, drying or heat (Holtfreter, from 1933) | often; neural tissue, but no organized axis |
| Ectoderm with other tissues, once killed | yes, many |
| Ectoderm with ether extracts of amphibian tissue (Waddington, Needham and Needham, by 1934) | yes, in some tests |
| Ectoderm with unrelated materials, from adult tissues to synthetic chemicals | in some cases |
| Salamander (*Ambystoma*) ectoderm with no organizer, or after brief damage that did not kill it (Barth, then Holtfreter, 1940s) | yes, in some cultures |

The rows come from different labs, species and culture conditions, so they are examples, not seven matched trials; the salamander row is a different ectoderm and treatment from the control in the first row. Still, almost every row gives some neural tissue, one with no organizer at all. When almost everything scores yes, a yes cannot name the signal.

C. H. Waddington described the trap. If competent ectoderm already holds a hidden tendency to become neural, an implanted substance might be the natural signal, or it might merely injure the tissue and release that tendency. The salamander result in the table's last row adds a second blow: for that ectoderm, an organizer was not even necessary for neural tissue to form. That finding is one explanation for why the chemical hunt stalled for decades.

The confusion also pointed toward the answer. [The next lesson](/lessons/neural-induction/) shows that frog ectoderm whose cells are separated from one another becomes neural tissue with no inducer at all, and that the organizer's products, Noggin, Chordin and Follistatin, act by blocking a signal of the TGF-beta family (see [that lesson](/lessons/tgf-beta-and-smads/)). If neural is what ectoderm becomes when a brake is released, then many injuries and chemicals that weaken the brake would be expected to "induce" as well.

The guiding idea holds within its limits. Neighbours can set a cell's fate, and the host's own cells answer only while they are still competent. The experiments also mark what the idea does not cover:

- **Which tissue.** Amphibian ectoderm and mesoderm during gastrulation. After that the ectoderm's competence for the organizer's neural induction has largely gone, though it still answers other signals later in development.
- **"Can be set", not "must be set".** Signals set fate in part; the responder's own state, including competence, also counts. Nor does neural tissue always need an organizer: salamander ectoderm (Barth and Holtfreter), and frog ectoderm whose cells are separated ([the next lesson](/lessons/neural-induction/)), can become neural with no organizer at all, though signalling inside those cells still takes part.
- **Which question.** The idea says *who* changed fate and *where*. What the signal is, the graft could not say, and the dead-tissue era showed why the neural test could not say either.

### Design problem

You have a dorsal lip from a pale newt species and host gastrulae from a pigmented species. Design experiments to show (a) whether the induced neural tube is made by host or donor cells, (b) whether the signal needs the two tissues to touch, and (c) when the responding ectoderm stops being able to respond.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

(a) Graft the pale dorsal lip into the belly of the pigmented host, let a second axis form, then section it. Pigmented cells in the second neural tube are induced host cells; pale ones came from the donor. A dye injected into the donor before grafting is a more reliable lineage label than natural pigment ([how far to trust a pigment marker](#how-far-to-trust-a-pigment-marker)).

(b) Take competent ectoderm and organizer tissue out of the embryo and culture them on opposite sides of a thin porous filter. The pores should let molecules through but be too small for cells or their processes to cross. If the ectoderm still becomes neural, a diffusible signal is enough. A solid sheet in place of the filter, and ectoderm on the filter with no organizer, are the controls.

(c) Run the competence test above: early organizer with ectoderm from later and later stages, from early gastrula to neurula, plus late organizer with early ectoderm to show that the drop is in the responder and not in the signal.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Roux, Driesch and the loop of hair

The question began as an argument about the first cell divisions. In 1888 Wilhelm Roux reported experiments on the two-cell embryo of a frog. He killed one of the two cells with a hot needle and left the dead cell in place. The living cell could go on to make something like half an embryo, with structures on one side and not the other. Roux read this as a mosaic: each early cell carries its own share of the future body.

A few years later Hans Driesch shook two-cell sea-urchin embryos until the cells came apart. A separated cell, instead of making half a larva, could make a smaller whole one. The two results are less opposed than they look. The experiments differed in species, and in one detail that mattered: Roux's dead cell stayed attached to its living sister. In later experiments that separated frog cells completely, the survivor often formed a whole embryo.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/the-organizer/roux-driesch-spemann-loop.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: three experimental comparisons. Roux's two-cell frog embryo retains one killed cell, and the survivor develops incompletely on one side. Driesch's separated sea-urchin blastomeres can develop into smaller whole pluteus larvae with projecting arms. Spemann's partial hair-loop constriction produces a doubled front end; complete separation can produce two whole newt embryos or an embryo and an axis-free belly piece." caption="Roux left a killed frog blastomere attached, and the survivor could form something like half an embryo. Driesch's separated sea-urchin blastomeres could form smaller whole larvae. In Spemann's newt experiments, partial constriction produced doubled front ends; complete separation sometimes produced two whole embryos, and sometimes an embryo plus an axis-free belly piece." />

Spemann wanted a gentler tool. By 1901 he was tying a loop of very fine hair around newt eggs and tightening it slowly, dividing the embryo partly or completely without an open wound. Partial constriction gave embryos with doubled front ends. Complete separation sometimes gave two whole embryos. In other cases one half made a normal embryo while its partner stayed alive and kept dividing but formed only a "belly piece", with no neural plate and no notochord. Cells could live and divide without building an axis.

### Where the organizer comes from

Among the organizer genes that dorsal β-catenin switches on in *Xenopus*, an early example was *goosecoid*, a homeobox gene expressed in the organizer, cloned by Eddy De Robertis's laboratory in 1991.

The classical picture of what happens next is called **primary induction**: mesoderm that has moved inside signals to the ectoderm above it, and that ectoderm becomes neural. The 1924 graft showed that dorsal-lip tissue can induce; it did not itself show that the mesoderm underneath is the normal source, which came from later work. The classical picture has since been refined in two ways. In *Xenopus*, evidence indicates that neural induction starts earlier, at the blastula stage, in dorsal cells that already make BMP antagonists. And signals from the organizer also pass sideways within the ectoderm itself, not only up from the mesoderm below.

### How far to trust a pigment marker

Pigment granules are not shared evenly by every descendant. A host cell that inherits few granules can look pale and be counted as donor, which would understate the host's share. Errors can also run the other way, because *cristatus* cells carry a little pigment rather than none. Without measuring how often each mistake happens, neither the size nor the direction of the net error is known. The main result, a second tube made mostly of host cells, is a pattern across sections rather than a single count, but without validated error rates a better lineage label would make it firmer.

### Operations, priority and credit

Spemann proposed the pigment test in print early in 1921, before Mangold began, and she carried it out for her doctoral thesis. By one historical reconstruction she performed a few hundred operations over two breeding seasons, and the paper described six embryos in detail. The paper appeared in 1924 with Spemann's name first; Mangold died the same year. In 1935 Spemann alone received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, for the organizer effect, and in his Nobel lecture he said the experiment was first carried out at his instigation by her.

### The chemical search, year by year

In 1932, before Holtfreter's tests, a note from Spemann's circle reported that organizer activity could survive destructive treatment. C. H. Waddington, Joseph Needham and Dorothy Needham began physico-chemical tests of the organizer in 1933; when their ether extracts induced, they suspected a fat-soluble substance, and the Cambridge group went on to test sterols. Lester Barth's salamander report came first in the 1940s, and Holtfreter's followed.
