      inside a nephron, because a nephron is far too small to reach with ordinary tools. Richards,

      laboratory went on to refine the method and apply it to other amphibians, and micropuncture
      later became a standard way to sample fluid along the nephrons of rats and other mammals.

    story: >-
      Hargitay and Kuhn were physical chemists in Basel, and Kuhn had worked out on physical
      grounds how two tubes joined in a hairpin, with fluid flowing in opposite directions, could
      multiply a small difference in concentration into a large one along their length. The
      mammalian loop of Henle has exactly that shape. A model is only a prediction, though, and
      the prediction here was specific: if the loop multiplies, the tissue of the medulla should
      get steadily more concentrated from the cortex down to the papilla. Wirz, a kidney
      physiologist, joined them to test it. Working with rat kidneys, they measured the freezing
      point of thin tissue slices, since a solution freezes at a lower temperature the more
      particles it holds. Osmolality climbed from the cortex toward the papilla. The 1951 paper
      turned a physical idea into a measured property of the mammalian kidney, and the
      countercurrent multiplier has framed teaching of urine concentration ever since.

techniques:
  - name: "Micropuncture"
    blurb: >-
      Insert a fine glass pipette into one part of a single nephron in an anesthetized animal and
      draw off a small fluid sample for analysis. Comparing samples from different points shows
      what each segment adds or removes, in the species studied.
  - name: "Isolated perfused tubule"
    blurb: >-
      Dissect one tubule segment, hold it between pipettes in a bath, and pump fluid of known
      composition through it. Because lumen and bath are both controlled, you can measure that
      segment's permeability to water and salt and whether it transports actively.
  - name: "Clearance measurement"
    blurb: >-
      Collect urine over a timed period at steady state and measure a substance's concentration in
      urine and in plasma. C = U x V / P gives the volume of plasma cleared of that substance per
      minute, and with inulin it estimates the glomerular filtration rate.
  - name: "Tissue cryoscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Measure the freezing point of thin kidney slices taken at known depths. A lower freezing
      point means more dissolved particles, so the readings map osmolality from cortex to papilla.

      efferent arteriole carries it out, and it then runs through the peritubular capillaries (and,
      for deep nephrons, the vasa recta) around the tubule. Starting with the efferent arteriole

      blood meet the tubule before it is filtered. Starting at the glomerulus leaves out the vessel
      that supplies it and puts the arterioles in the wrong places.

The first film's 180 litres a day is about four times the volume of water in the whole body. This lesson builds on [osmoregulation](/lessons/osmoregulation/).

> **The one idea.** What leaves in urine is what the kidney filtered, minus what its tubules took back, plus what they added:
> **excreted = filtered − reabsorbed + secreted.**
> Because the kidney filters far more than it lets go, the take-back, not the filter, largely sets how much water you lose.
>
> *Scope:* a mammalian kidney, one substance, over a set time. If tubule cells make a substance and release it into the tubule, as with ammonium, count that as secreted; if the kidney makes or uses up a substance any other way, that needs its own term. No pump moves water across the tubule wall: water taken back moves passively, mainly by osmosis down gradients that solute transport creates, mostly with salt and, deep in the kidney, also with urea. Filtration is different: at the glomerulus, Starling forces push water through the filter in bulk ([blood and vessels](/lessons/blood-and-vessels/)).

## Where each term happens

An adult human kidney holds roughly a million **nephrons**, each a filter (the **glomerulus**, a knot of capillaries in the cup of **Bowman's capsule**) attached to a long tube. Blood enters and leaves the glomerulus by the **afferent** and **efferent arterioles** (afferent in, efferent out), then runs through the **peritubular capillaries** around the tube and, for nephrons that reach deep into the kidney, through long hairpin vessels, the **vasa recta**. The filtrate then flows through four stretches, the last shared by many nephrons. In mammals:

| Stretch | Takes back (reabsorbed) | Adds (secreted) |
|---|---|---|
| **Proximal tubule** | About 60 to 70% of filtered sodium, with water following so the fluid stays at nearly plasma's concentration; almost all filtered glucose and amino acids | Hydrogen ions, ammonium, some drugs |
| **Loop of Henle** (descending, then ascending limb) | Water from the descending limb, salt from the ascending limb; together they build much of the medulla's gradient (the second film) | |
| **Distal tubule** | More salt | |
| **Collecting duct** | Sodium (**principal cells**); water, when antidiuretic hormone is present | Potassium (principal cells); acid or bicarbonate (**intercalated cells**, part of acid-base balance, [gas exchange](/lessons/gas-exchange/)) |

The late distal tubule and the collecting duct set the final water and salt content of urine under hormonal control. How epithelial cells move ions is in [epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/) and [membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/).

That fitted a filter followed by reabsorption, in the frog. It did not say where along the tubule or give mammalian rates, and frog nephrons lack a loop of Henle. Later micropuncture in rats and other mammals placed glucose reabsorption mainly in the proximal tubule.

## Clearance: reading the ledger from outside

Can blood and urine alone, with no pipette, read the ledger? Urine concentration U times urine flow V is the excretion rate, the ledger's "excreted" term. Divide by plasma concentration P and you get the volume of plasma that would hold that much each minute: the **clearance**. No plasma is really wiped clean.

**Inulin**, a plant sugar polymer, is freely filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted, so excreted equals filtered and its clearance equals the **glomerular filtration rate (GFR)**, the plasma volume filtered per minute.

### Worked problem: clearance and filtered load

Illustrative numbers, for an adult human at steady state.

1. *Clearance.* 120 mL of urine in 60 min gives V = 2.0 mL per min. With inulin at U = 30 mg per mL and P = 0.50 mg per mL, C = 30 x 2.0 / 0.50 = 120 mL per min. Over a day: 120 x 1,440 = 172,800 mL, about 173 L.
2. *Filtered load.* Glucose is freely filtered. With plasma glucose at an illustrative 5.0 mmol per L, filtered load = GFR x P = 0.120 L per min x 5.0 mmol per L = 0.60 mmol per min, or 864 mmol per day (about 156 g). With no glucose in the urine, all of it was reabsorbed.
3. *Reading other clearances.* For a freely filtered substance, a clearance above GFR means net secretion: at 300 mL per min the substance leaves faster than filtration alone could carry it. One of 1 mL per min means it is mostly reabsorbed. **Creatinine**, a muscle waste, is used to estimate GFR, but a little is secreted, so creatinine clearance tends to overestimate GFR.

## Making urine more concentrated than blood

The last litres of water are the hardest to take back: they must come out of fluid that, with antidiuretic hormone (ADH) present, ends up more concentrated than blood. The second film shows how.

### Testing each limb on its own

The second film's descending limb comes from Kokko (1970), who held single thin descending limbs from rabbit kidneys between glass pipettes and perfused them, setting both lumen and bath (the **isolated perfused tubule** method). Sodium permeability was low, and no active salt transport could be detected. The thick ascending limb takes up sodium chloride mainly through a cotransporter called NKCC2, with the sodium-potassium pump supplying the energy. Not every descending segment is water-permeable in every species (Going deeper).

### Keeping the gradient

The vasa recta run in hairpins too: blood going down gains salt and loses water, and blood coming up does the reverse. So blood leaves carrying reabsorbed water but little of the salt: this **countercurrent exchange** limits washout of the gradient.

### The inner medulla, and urine without ADH

The thick ascending limb does not reach the inner medulla, yet osmolality keeps rising there toward the papilla, by means still under study (Going deeper). Urea is part of it: there, urea leaves the collecting duct through urea transporters, by facilitated diffusion rather than an active pump, and adds to the gradient there.

Without ADH, urine is *dilute*, not merely plasma-strength: the thick ascending limb has already removed salt without water, and a collecting duct mostly closed to water keeps it that way.

## Hormones tune the reabsorbed term

| Hormone | Released when | Ledger term it changes | Urine |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ADH** | Plasma osmolality rises or blood volume falls | Water reabsorbed rises (collecting duct) | Less, more concentrated |
| **Angiotensin II and aldosterone** | Blood pressure or salt is low | Sodium reabsorbed rises | Less sodium |
| **ANP** | The atria are stretched | Filtered rises; sodium and water reabsorbed fall | More sodium and water |

**ADH** is made in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary ([endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/); [hypothalamus and autonomic control](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/)). In collecting-duct cells it acts through V2 receptors and cAMP ([G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/), [cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/)). Its water channels are aquaporin-2 (Nielsen and colleagues, 1995, in perfused rat ducts).

The **renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system** answers falling pressure or salt by holding on to sodium, through a chain:

1. **Renin**, an enzyme, is released by **juxtaglomerular cells** in the wall of the afferent arteriole when renal perfusion pressure falls, when less salt reaches the **macula densa** (cells at the end of the thick ascending limb, where it touches its own glomerulus's arterioles), or when sympathetic nerves stimulate beta-adrenergic receptors.
2. Renin cuts **angiotensinogen**, a plasma protein made by the liver, into angiotensin I; angiotensin-converting enzyme turns that into **angiotensin II**.
3. Angiotensin II raises sodium reabsorption in the tubule and stimulates the adrenal cortex to release **aldosterone**, a steroid that raises sodium reabsorption through ENaC channels in principal cells and, coupled to it, potassium secretion ([nuclear receptors](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/)).

**Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)**, from heart muscle cells of the stretched atria (as after a rise in blood volume), pushes the other way. It raises GFR and suppresses the release of renin and aldosterone. In anesthetized rats it inhibited sodium reabsorption in the inner-medullary collecting duct, and in perfused rat ducts it reduced ADH-stimulated water permeability. It works through cGMP ([cGMP signaling](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/)).

## Kidneys compared: the ledger in other animals

Among vertebrates, **loops of Henle occur only in mammals and birds**, and only these make urine more concentrated than their plasma. Amphibians and most fish have no loop, and their urine is at most about as concentrated as their plasma.

Across many mammal species, Beuchat (1990) found that relative medullary thickness tracks maximum urine concentration. The kangaroo rat *Dipodomys merriami*, a seed-eater of North American deserts, has long loops and a thick inner medulla and makes urine several times more concentrated than an adult human can ([osmoregulation](/lessons/osmoregulation/) tells how it balances water).

**Predict.** To excrete an illustrative 600 mOsm of solute, how much water does urine at plasma's level (about 300 mOsm per kg) take? At the human maximum, near 1,200? At four times that?

Birds have a mix of loopless and looped nephrons and concentrate urine only modestly, to a small multiple of plasma osmolality; much of their nitrogen leaves as uric acid, which takes little water.

Some marine teleosts, including the goosefish *Lophius* and the toadfish *Opsanus*, have kidneys with **no glomeruli**. On the ledger, filtered is zero: everything in their urine reached the tubule by secretion. Tubule cells move ions such as magnesium and sulfate into the tubule, and water follows. Plants move water with no filter at all ([plant transport](/lessons/plant-transport/)).

### Design problem (optional, harder)

Urea comes from protein breakdown ([protein turnover](/lessons/protein-turnover-amino-acid-catabolism/)). Fenton and colleagues (2004) made mice lacking UT-A1 and UT-A3, the urea transporters of the collecting duct. Predict how maximal urine osmolality compares between knockout and normal mice on a 20% protein diet and on a 4% protein diet, after water restriction. Then predict inner-medullary sodium chloride under the passive model, which says urea leaving the collecting duct helps draw salt out of the thin ascending limb.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

If urea leaving the inner-medullary collecting duct adds to the gradient, knockouts should concentrate less, most on high protein, since low protein supplies little urea in either genotype. In fact knockouts concentrated less on 20% protein, the gap almost vanished on 4%, and inner-medullary sodium and chloride did not differ significantly between genotypes.

Two readings fit the deficit. Knockouts had much less urea in the inner medulla, so urea transport helps urea build up there. And, as Fenton and colleagues stressed, urea that cannot leave the collecting duct holds water in the urine, an osmotic diuresis.

The passive model predicts less inner-medullary NaCl in knockouts; they did not have less, which argues against that dependency. Keep the limits: these are mice, and one knockout does not settle how the inner medulla concentrates; that question is still open.

</details>

The ledger once more: in a filtering kidney the take-back largely sets urine volume, while all three terms together set how much of each solute leaves.

## Going deeper

### The take-back keeps pace with filtration

The first film's "about the same share" has a name, **glomerulotubular balance**. Its doubling of urine was ledger arithmetic, not what a healthy kidney lets happen.

### The descending limb across species

Kokko's water-permeable descending limb is the rabbit's; in rats, some inner-medullary descending segments lack the water channel aquaporin-1.

### Which particles draw water

"More concentrated" is shorthand: what draws water across a wall is the particles that cannot cross that wall freely (effective osmolality). While water flows, the tissue need be only slightly more concentrated than the urine.

### The inner medulla, an open question

There the thin ascending limb has no active NaCl transport. The passive model of Kokko and Rector and of Stephenson relies on urea from the collecting duct, which the Fenton knockouts challenge; other proposed mechanisms remain under study.
