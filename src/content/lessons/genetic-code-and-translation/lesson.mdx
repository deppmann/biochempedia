    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Nirenberg's laboratory at the National Institutes of Health set out to make proteins
      outside a cell. In the 1961 paper, he and J. Heinrich Matthaei broke open E. coli, kept the
      ribosomes and the soluble fraction, and added radioactive amino acids. When they added a
      synthetic RNA made only of uridine, poly(U), labelled phenylalanine went into an
      acid-insoluble product with many properties of polyphenylalanine. The reaction needed both
      the ribosomes and the soluble fraction. The experiment alone could not say how many U residues
      made one codeword; that question was left open. Three years later, with Philip Leder,
      Nirenberg dropped the protein synthesis step altogether. A trinucleotide such as UUU, mixed
      with ribosomes and labelled aminoacyl-tRNA, held the matching tRNA on the ribosome, and a
      dinucleotide did not. Because a ribosome-tRNA complex sticks to a filter while free tRNA
      passes through, each of the 64 triplets could be tested one at a time. That assay, with
      Khorana's defined polymers, assigned most of the code, and Nirenberg shared the 1968 Nobel
      Prize with Khorana and Robert Holley.

      biochemical results could be read as a dictionary.

techniques:
  - name: "Cell-free translation with radiolabel"
    blurb: >-
      Broken cells supply ribosomes, tRNAs and enzymes; you add an RNA template and radioactive
      amino acids. Acid precipitates most proteins but not free amino acids, so counts in the
      precipitate tell you which amino acids the template directed into polymer. Some products, such as polylysine, stay soluble and need another assay.
  - name: "Frameshift suppression in phage"
    blurb: >-
      Single-base insertion or deletion mutants are combined by recombination, and the double or
      triple mutant is tested for growth on a host that needs the gene. Restored growth shows
      that the reading frame has been put back in register downstream.
  - name: "Trinucleotide binding to ribosomes"
    blurb: >-
      A defined triplet, ribosomes and one labelled aminoacyl-tRNA are mixed and passed through a
      filter. Ribosomes and anything bound to them stay on the filter, so retained label means
      that triplet recruits that tRNA, with no protein synthesis needed.
  - name: "X-ray crystallography of the ribosome"
    blurb: >-
      Ribosomal subunits or whole ribosomes are crystallized, often with tRNAs, mRNA or
      substrate analogs, and the diffraction pattern is turned into an atomic model. It shows
      which parts, RNA or protein, touch the reacting groups.

How can one added base regroup every codon that follows, while one substituted base may change no amino acid at all?

<Support>**The one idea.** Once a start site fixes the reading frame, the ribosome reads nonoverlapping codons in order, and charged tRNAs connect those codons to amino acids. **Scope:** This is the main model for protein-coding mRNAs using the standard genetic code. Initiation chooses the start; special recoding signals and nonstandard organelle codes are exceptions. A stop recruits a release factor rather than a tRNA.</Support>

A **codon** is a group of three bases in messenger RNA (mRNA) that specifies one amino acid or a stop. You know the twenty side chains from [amino acids](/lessons/amino-acids/); mRNA comes from [transcription](/lessons/transcription/) and, in eukaryotes, [RNA processing](/lessons/rna-processing/).

## Finding the size and frame of a codon

The first film counted the words four bases can spell: 4, 16 and 64 for one, two and three bases. If every codeword has the same length, it needs at least three bases; that is arithmetic, not evidence that cells use triplets.

The first film's plus-and-minus pattern came from 1961 work by Francis Crick, Leslie Barnett, Sydney Brenner and Richard Watts-Tobin on the rIIB gene of phage T4, the gene you met in [complementation and pathways](/lessons/complementation-and-pathways/). A **frameshift**, an insertion or deletion that is not a multiple of three, moves the downstream codons out of register until a stop or a compensating change. Three pluses, or three minuses, restored wild or nearly wild growth in the combinations tested.

They concluded that the code is read from a fixed starting point, in nonoverlapping groups of three bases or a multiple of three, with no punctuation between words. The case against overlapping codons came from others' work on tobacco mosaic virus. The phage work assigned no codon and did not claim to prove that the unit was exactly three.

## Assigning the codon dictionary

In 1961 Marshall Nirenberg and J. Heinrich Matthaei, at the National Institutes of Health, made an E. coli **cell-free system**: broken cells that still make protein when given an RNA. They added poly(U), a synthetic RNA made only of uridine, plus radioactive amino acids. Phenylalanine entered an acid-insoluble product with many properties of polyphenylalanine. The reaction needed ribosomes and the soluble fraction.

**Predict.** Could that experiment alone distinguish a one-base word U, a two-base word UU, and a three-base word UUU?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Every grouping of poly(U) contains only U, so the experiment linked U runs to phenylalanine but could not determine codeword length. Combined with the frame evidence, UUU was the likely codon for Phe.

</details>

Random copolymers, RNAs with two bases in random order, revealed codon composition but not order; two methods supplied it. In 1964 Nirenberg and Philip Leder mixed one trinucleotide with ribosomes and an aminoacyl-tRNA. UUU held Phe-tRNA on the ribosome, AAA held Lys-tRNA, and CCC held Pro-tRNA. Ribosome complexes stick to a filter while free tRNA passes through, so each triplet could be tested separately.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/genetic-code-and-translation/trinucleotide-filter-assay.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: three panels showing a trinucleotide, ribosomes and labelled tRNA mixed in a tube, poured over a filter that keeps ribosome complexes while free tRNA passes through, and a comparison filter where a dinucleotide holds nothing." caption="A triplet can recruit its matching aminoacyl-tRNA to a ribosome, so the retained label identifies a codon assignment, while a dinucleotide does not stimulate binding." />

Har Gobind Khorana's laboratory made and translated RNAs of exact repeating sequence. Poly(UC), read as UCU CUC UCU CUC, gave an alternating peptide of serine and leucine. That linked the two codons to the two amino acids; other evidence said which was which.

The finished standard table has 64 codons: 61 **sense codons** for amino acids and three stops, UAA, UAG and UGA. AUG means methionine both at a start and inside a gene. Most amino acids have several codons, a property called **degeneracy**. Synonymous codons usually differ at the third base. Crick's 1966 **wobble** hypothesis explains why: the third codon base can pair loosely with the first anticodon base, so one tRNA can read more than one codon.

The standard table is nearly universal, not universal. Vertebrate mitochondria read UGA as Trp, some ciliates read UAA and UAG as Gln, and a special mRNA signal can make UGA encode selenocysteine.

## Use the frame before changing it

A single RNA strand has three **reading frames**, beginning at base 1, 2 or 3. Double-stranded DNA has six, three on each strand. Initiation picks the frame that is translated.

**Worked problem.** An mRNA reads 5'-GCCAUGGCUUGGAAACCUGACUAGCC-3'. Assume translation begins at the first AUG.

1. The first AUG occupies bases 4 to 6.
2. Split from there: AUG GCU UGG AAA CCU GAC UAG CC.
3. Look up the complete codons: Met-Ala-Trp-Lys-Pro-Asp, then stop. Six residues; the stop adds none.

**Predict.** Read from base 2 instead. What are the codons, and does this frame meet a stop in the bases shown?

CCA UGG CUU GGA AAC CUG ACU AGC gives Pro-Trp-Leu-Gly-Asn-Leu-Thr-Ser, with no stop here. Base 3 gives CAU GGC UUG GAA ACC UGA, or His-Gly-Leu-Glu-Thr-stop.

</details>

Now mutate the translated region.

- GCU to GCC still gives Ala, so Met-Ala-Trp-Lys-Pro-Asp is unchanged: **silent**.
- GCU to ACU gives Met-Thr-Trp-Lys-Pro-Asp, Ala to Thr: **missense**.
- UGG to UGA creates a stop after Met-Ala, a **nonsense** change.
- Inserting G after AUG gives AUG GGC UUG GAA ACC UGA: Met-Gly-Leu-Glu-Thr, then stop. This is a **frameshift** because every downstream codon is regrouped, and a formerly out-of-frame stop is now early.

## Two checks connect codons to amino acids

The second film showed the adaptor Crick had argued for, **transfer RNA (tRNA)**, and its two checks: for most tRNAs an **aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase** chooses the amino acid, and the ribosome tests the tRNA's **anticodon**, three bases that pair with a codon. What it left out is the chemistry. The amino acid rides on the tRNA's 3' CCA end. There is usually one synthetase per amino acid, and it charges its tRNA or tRNAs in two steps:

> **amino acid + ATP → aminoacyl-AMP + PPi**
>
> **aminoacyl-AMP + tRNA → aminoacyl-tRNA + AMP**

Pyrophosphate is hydrolyzed, so charging spends two high-energy phosphate bonds. Some synthetases edit, hydrolyzing a wrong amino acid before or after it reaches the tRNA. Editing matters because the ribosome checks codon-anticodon pairing, not the amino acid.

## The ribosome sets the start, extends the chain and stops

A **ribosome** has two subunits: bacterial 30S and 50S make 70S; eukaryotic cytosolic 40S and 60S make 80S. Svedberg values measure sedimentation, so they do not add. Its three tRNA-binding sites span the two subunits: the **A site** (aminoacyl), **P site** (peptidyl) and **E site** (exit).

**Initiation** places an initiator tRNA in the P site and fixes the frame, but bacteria and eukaryotes often find the start differently.

- In many bacterial mRNAs, a purine-rich **Shine-Dalgarno sequence** a few bases upstream of the start codon pairs with the 3' end of 16S rRNA, which places the start codon on the small subunit. Most bacteria use an fMet-tRNA (formylmethionine-tRNA) initiator, and GUG can serve as a start as well as AUG. Other bacterial mRNAs, including leaderless ones, begin without Shine-Dalgarno pairing.
- In most eukaryotic mRNAs, the 40S subunit and initiation factors load at the 5' cap and scan 5' to 3'. They usually start at the first AUG in good **Kozak context**, with a purine at position -3 and G at +4 when the A of AUG is +1. The initiator is Met-tRNAi.

**Predict.** Two AUGs lie near the 5' end of a mammalian mRNA. The first has poor Kozak context and the second has good context. Must every scanning ribosome start at the first?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Many scanning ribosomes can pass an AUG in poor context and begin at the next AUG in good context. This is leaky scanning, so start selection depends on more than finding the first AUG.

</details>

Either way, the large subunit then joins.

The second film's **elongation** cycle omitted its driving factors. EF-Tu (eEF1A in eukaryotes) uses GTP to deliver aminoacyl-tRNA to A; if the anticodon matches, GTP is hydrolyzed. The large subunit transfers the chain from P to the amino group of the A-site amino acid. EF-G (eEF2 in eukaryotes) uses another GTP to move the ribosome one codon. The chain grows from N to C terminus. In bacteria the catalytic RNA is the large subunit's 23S rRNA.

**Predict.** Counting charging plus one delivery and one translocation, how many high-energy phosphate bonds are spent per peptide bond?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Charging costs two, because ATP is split to AMP; EF-Tu delivery and EF-G translocation cost one GTP each. The total is about four high-energy phosphate bonds per peptide bond.

</details>

At **termination**, normally no tRNA reads the stop in the A site; a release factor promotes hydrolysis of the bond to the P-site tRNA. In bacteria RF1 reads UAA and UAG, while RF2 reads UAA and UGA; eukaryotic eRF1 reads all three stops. Translation speed and control continue in [mTOR and AMPK](/lessons/mtor-and-ampk/); folding in [protein 3D structure](/lessons/protein-3d-structure/).

## A premature stop can also destroy the message

In mammalian cells, splicing leaves an **exon junction complex** just upstream of most exon-exon junctions. A ribosome on the first round of translation displaces these complexes.

**Predict.** A human gene has six exons. One premature stop lies in exon 2, well upstream of later junctions; another lies in exon 6, the last exon. Which mRNA is more likely to be degraded?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The exon 2 mRNA is usually targeted for **nonsense-mediated decay (NMD)** because translation stops while exon junction complexes remain downstream. A stop in the last exon usually escapes because no exon junction complex remains after it.

</details>

More precisely, a stop more than about 50 to 55 bases upstream of the last exon-exon junction usually triggers NMD; one in the last exon, or within about 50 bases of the last junction, usually escapes and may make a truncated protein. This is a tendency with exceptions, not a rule for yeast or bacteria. For how such mutations arise, see [mutation](/lessons/mutation/).

## Design problem: assign codons from repeating RNAs

In an E. coli cell-free system, poly(AG), AGAGAG..., directs a peptide of alternating Arg and Glu. Poly(AAG), AAGAAG..., directs three homopolymers: polyLys, polyArg and polyGlu. Assign AGA, GAG, AAG and GAA as far as the data allow. State each assumption and name the experiment that settles what remains.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Assume codons are nonoverlapping triplets, translation can begin in any frame in this system, and each codon means one amino acid.

1. Poly(AG) gives alternating AGA and GAG; poly(AAG) gives repeated AAG, AGA or GAA, one per frame.
2. Poly(AG) says \{AGA, GAG\} = \{Arg, Glu\}. AGA is shared by both polymers, as are Arg and Glu, so the data cannot assign it.
3. If AGA is Arg, GAG is Glu and \{AAG, GAA\} = \{Lys, Glu\}. If AGA is Glu, GAG is Arg and \{AAG, GAA\} = \{Lys, Arg\}.
4. AAA was already Lys from poly(A). Similar first two bases favor AAG as Lys, but that is a guess, not data.

The Nirenberg-Leder binding test settles each trinucleotide with labelled aminoacyl-tRNAs: AGA Arg, GAG Glu, AAG Lys and GAA Glu.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How the rIIB mutants were made

The starting rIIB mutant was made with proflavin, a dye Crick and colleagues argued adds or removes a base or bases. Most suppressor mutants that followed arose spontaneously.

### Glutaminyl-tRNA and the Kozak reporter

Most bacteria and all archaea lack a glutaminyl-tRNA synthetase and make Gln-tRNA indirectly. In Kozak's preproinsulin reporter, changing bases around AUG changed protein yield over a 20-fold range.

### Where the exon junction complex sits

Splicing leaves an exon junction complex about 20 to 24 bases upstream of most exon-exon junctions.
