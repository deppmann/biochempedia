techniques:
  - name: "Light and dark bottles"
    blurb: >-
      Paired sealed bottles of water are incubated, one transparent and one dark, and dissolved
      oxygen is measured at the start and end. The light bottle gives net community production,
      the dark bottle community respiration, and their difference an estimate of gross
      production, in oxygen units, assuming respiration is the same in light and dark.
  - name: "Harvest and component summation"
    blurb: >-
      Net primary production of a forest is estimated by adding separately measured parts:
      woody growth from repeated tree measurements, litterfall from traps, and fine-root growth
      from ingrowth cores of initially root-free soil. Any part left out is missing from the
      total.
  - name: "Eddy covariance with flux partitioning"
    blurb: >-
      Instruments on a tower above the canopy record rapid fluctuations in vertical wind and CO2
      concentration, giving the net CO2 exchange between ecosystem and atmosphere. Gross primary
      production and ecosystem respiration are then separated from that net flux by a
      partitioning model, so GPP is an estimate that depends on the model.
  - name: "Mass-balance food-web modelling (Ecopath)"
    blurb: >-
      Measured biomasses and feeding rates, combined with parameters taken from other studies
      where measurements are missing, are balanced into estimated flows between groups. The
      flows and efficiencies it reports are model outputs that inherit those assumptions.

**Plants often respire roughly half of the energy they fix. Of the half it keeps, only a small share becomes herbivore, and a smaller share of that becomes predator. Where does the rest go, and how would you measure it?**

This lesson treats an ecosystem as a set of accounts. Energy comes in as sunlight fixed by photosynthesis, moves between accounts when something is eaten or dies, and leaves as heat. The mechanisms of photosynthesis and respiration belong to Biochemistry: the [light reactions](/lessons/light-reactions/) make ATP and NADPH, the [Calvin cycle](/lessons/calvin-cycle/) uses them to fix CO2, and [oxidative phosphorylation](/lessons/oxidative-phosphorylation/) recovers energy from food through a [proton-motive force](/lessons/proton-motive-force/). For the shared vocabulary of energy and free energy, see [basic concepts of metabolism](/lessons/metabolism-basic-concepts/). Here you keep the totals.

## Production is a rate: GPP, NPP and NEP

**Production** is the rate at which new organic matter, or the energy in it, is formed per unit area or volume per unit time, for example kJ m^-2 yr^-1 or g C m^-2 yr^-1. It is a flow. **Biomass** (standing crop) is a stock: how much is there at one moment, in g m^-2 with no time unit. Think of a bank account. Biomass is the balance; production is the deposits per year. A small balance can sit on a large income if the money is spent quickly, which is exactly what fast-dividing phytoplankton do.

Three production terms recur.

> **NPP = GPP - Ra      NEP = GPP - Reco**

**Gross primary production** (GPP) is the total rate of photosynthetic fixation by the producers (the first **trophic level**, a step in feeding defined by what an organism eats). **Autotrophic respiration** (Ra) is what the producers themselves respire. **Net primary production** (NPP) is what is left: the energy available to everything else. A common slip is to call GPP "what is left after plants respire"; that is NPP. **Net ecosystem production** (NEP) subtracts the respiration of the whole ecosystem, Reco, which adds the respiration of animals, fungi and bacteria to Ra. NEP is the energy or carbon the ecosystem stores over the period.

Tower studies usually report **net ecosystem exchange** (NEE) instead. With release to the atmosphere counted as positive, NEE = Reco - GPP, so an ecosystem that takes up carbon has a negative NEE. Check the sign convention before you read any flux graph.

## Measuring it: bottles, harvests and towers

No method observes GPP directly at ecosystem scale. Each method measures something nearby and infers the rest.

**Light and dark bottles.** Fill paired bottles with lake or sea water, seal them, keep one dark, and incubate both. With initial oxygen I, final light-bottle oxygen L, final dark-bottle oxygen D and time t:

> **net community production = (L - I) / t   respiration = (I - D) / t   GPP = (L - D) / t**

The calculation assumes respiration runs at the same rate in light and dark and that no gas escapes. The bottle also holds bacteria and zooplankton, so the light bottle's net change is net **community** production, not phytoplankton NPP. Results stay in oxygen units until a stated conversion to carbon is justified.

**Harvests and component sums.** In grassland, clip and weigh the plants at the peak of the season. In forest, add the separately measured parts. Ji Young An and colleagues summed woody growth, litterfall and fine-root growth in nine stands in the Gwangneung Experimental Forest, Korea, and fine roots made up 18 to 44% of the estimated NPP in those stands. Leave out the roots and the total is badly short. Where NPP is high in the first place depends largely on temperature and water, the subject of [climate and biomes](/lessons/climate-and-biomes/).

**Eddy-covariance towers.** Fast sensors above the canopy record how vertical gusts of wind carry CO2 up and down, which gives the net exchange, NEE. GPP and Reco are then separated by a partitioning model. A common approach fits respiration to night-time data, when there is no photosynthesis, and extrapolates it into the day; a 2020 study applied a neural-network method to 36 FLUXNET2015 sites. At one Scots pine forest in Finland, over 2013 to 2017, daily GPP estimates from two carbonyl sulfide methods averaged 7% and 23% above the CO2-based estimate. Treat GPP as an estimate with a method attached.

## Following energy through a consumer

Feeding links, which [predation](/lessons/predation/) and [mutualism, parasitism and coevolution](/lessons/mutualism-parasitism-coevolution/) describe, are the pipes energy moves through. An animal's budget has the same logic as a plant's. Of what it **ingests** (I), part leaves as feces (**egestion**). The rest is **assimilated** (A). Of that, part is respired (R) and lost as heat, and the remainder is **production** (P), new tissue and offspring.

> **A = I - egestion   P = A - R**

Three efficiencies follow, and their product is the **trophic transfer efficiency** (TTE): production at one level divided by production at the level below, in matched units and time.

> **TTE = (I / P below) x (A / I) x (P / A) = P / P below**

**Worked problem.** Here is a made-up closed budget for one square metre over one year. Every number is in kJ m^-2 yr^-1, there are no imports or exports, decomposers show no net growth, and producer production that is not grazed enters detritus. GPP is 12,000 and Ra is 6,000. Herbivores ingest 1,800, egest 1,080 and respire 360. Predators ingest 200, egest 100 and respire 60. Decomposers respire 3,240.

1. *Producers.* NPP = 12,000 - 6,000 = 6,000.
2. *Herbivores.* A = 1,800 - 1,080 = 720. P = 720 - 360 = 360. Consumption efficiency = 1,800 / 6,000 = 30%. Assimilation efficiency = 720 / 1,800 = 40%. Net production efficiency = 360 / 720 = 50%.
3. *First transfer.* TTE = 360 / 6,000 = 6%. Check: 0.30 x 0.40 x 0.50 = 0.06. The ingestion and assimilation terms cancel, and so do the units.
4. *Predators.* A = 200 - 100 = 100. P = 100 - 60 = 40. TTE = 40 / 360 = 11.1%.
5. *Detritus.* Inputs are ungrazed plant production 6,000 - 1,800 = 4,200, herbivore feces 1,080, herbivore production that predators did not eat 360 - 200 = 160, and predator feces 100: total 5,540. Decomposers respire 3,240, leaving 2,300 as detritus.
6. *Whole system.* Total respiration = 6,000 + 360 + 60 + 3,240 = 9,660. NEP = 12,000 - 9,660 = 2,340, which is the 2,300 of detritus plus the 40 of predator production. Check: 9,660 + 2,300 + 40 = 12,000.

Notice two things. The first transfer here is 6% and the second 11%, so no single number describes both links. And the decomposers respired more than every animal combined: in this budget most NPP never passed through an animal at all.

Notice also what is not an efficiency. **Gross growth efficiency**, P/I, is a property of one consumer, not a transfer between levels. A ratio of biomasses is not an efficiency either, because stocks carry no time unit.

## Transfer efficiency, detritus and the 10% rule

Each step loses most of what it receives, to feces and to heat. Assimilation efficiency tends to be higher for carnivores than for herbivores, because animal tissue is easier to digest than plant cell walls. Net production efficiency tends to be low in birds and mammals, which spend most of their assimilated energy keeping warm, and higher in many invertebrates and fish.

Multiplying the three efficiencies explains why food chains are short. If every step passed on 10%, a top predator at the fourth trophic level would receive 0.1% of NPP. In an analysis of 48 published aquatic food-web models, Daniel Pauly and Villy Christensen derived in 1995 a mean transfer efficiency of about 10%, which they used to estimate the primary production needed to sustain world fisheries. That is the source of the **10% rule**: a rough average, not a law, and not Lindeman's law. Real links can be far lower or higher, and food quality matters as well as quantity: producers short of nitrogen or phosphorus may limit transfer to herbivores even when carbon is plentiful. Energy is also not the only proposed limit on chain length. A 2000 study of 25 northern temperate lakes (Post, Pace and Hairston, Nature) found that food-chain length increased with lake size but not with productivity.

Detritus is the other half of the story. Dead plants, feces and carcasses feed bacteria, fungi and detritus-feeding animals, which are themselves eaten. In John Teal's Georgia salt marsh most grass production went this way rather than to grazers. Energy does not come back from this route. Each organism's respiration releases it as heat, and only matter, the carbon and nutrients, cycles back to producers, which is the subject of [nutrient cycling](/lessons/nutrient-cycling/).

Net community production = (9.2 - 8.0) / 6 = 0.20 mg O2 L^-1 h^-1. Respiration = (8.0 - 7.4) / 6 = 0.10 mg O2 L^-1 h^-1. GPP = (9.2 - 7.4) / 6 = 0.30 mg O2 L^-1 h^-1, which equals 0.20 + 0.10, a useful check.

What the numbers rest on: respiration is assumed equal in light and dark, though respiration in the light can differ, and the bottle is assumed sealed. The dark-bottle loss includes bacteria and zooplankton, so this is community respiration and the light-bottle gain is community production, not phytoplankton NPP. The figures are oxygen per litre per hour at one depth on one day. Converting them to carbon needs a stated photosynthetic quotient (moles of O2 released per mole of CO2 fixed), and converting to an annual areal rate needs measurements through the water column and the seasons.

To improve it, run replicate bottles at several depths, repeat the incubations through the year, keep incubations short enough that oxygen does not build up unrealistically, and report each result with its spread.
