techniques:
  - name: "Light and dark bottles"
    blurb: >-
      Paired sealed bottles of water are incubated, one transparent and one dark, and dissolved
      oxygen is measured at the start and end. The light bottle gives net community production,
      the dark bottle community respiration, and their sum an estimate of gross
      production, in oxygen units, assuming respiration is the same in light and dark.
  - name: "Harvest and component summation"
    blurb: >-
      Net primary production of a forest is estimated by adding separately measured parts:
      woody growth from repeated tree measurements, litterfall from traps, and fine-root growth
      from ingrowth cores of initially root-free soil. Any part left out is missing from the
      total.
  - name: "Eddy covariance with flux partitioning"
    blurb: >-
      Instruments on a tower above the canopy record rapid fluctuations in vertical wind and CO2
      concentration, giving the net CO2 exchange between ecosystem and atmosphere. Gross primary
      production and ecosystem respiration are then separated from that net flux by a
      partitioning model, so GPP is an estimate that depends on the model.
  - name: "Mass-balance food-web modelling (Ecopath)"
    blurb: >-
      Measured biomasses and feeding rates, combined with parameters taken from other studies
      where measurements are missing, are balanced into estimated flows between groups. The
      flows and efficiencies it reports are model outputs that inherit those assumptions.

  - stem: "In one year a caterpillar population ingests 500 kJ m^-2 of leaves, egests 300 kJ m^-2 as feces and respires 140 kJ m^-2. What is its net production efficiency?"
    choices:
      - "70%"
      - "12%"
      - "30%"
      - "40%"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Assimilation is ingestion minus egestion, 500 - 300 = 200, and production is assimilation
      minus respiration, 200 - 140 = 60, so net production efficiency is 60/200 = 30%. The value
      12% is production over ingestion, the gross growth efficiency. The value 40% is assimilation
      over ingestion, the assimilation efficiency. The value 70% is respiration over
      assimilation, the share of assimilated energy that was respired, not produced.
  - stem: "In a lake in midsummer, a student measures 50 g m^-2 of phytoplankton and 200 g m^-2 of zooplankton, and reports a trophic transfer efficiency of 400%. What is the main error?"
    choices:
      - "The phytoplankton biomass should first be multiplied by ten"
      - "The zooplankton biomass must have been measured wrongly"
      - "Stocks were compared where production rates were needed"
      - "Transfer efficiency is always fixed at about ten percent"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Transfer efficiency is a ratio of production rates, consumer production over prey
      production in the same units per area per time, and a biomass ratio is a ratio of stocks.
      Phytoplankton can turn over quickly, so a small standing crop can support a larger stock of
      grazers. Assuming the zooplankton were mismeasured ignores that a stock can be small while
      its production is large. Saying efficiency is fixed at ten percent treats a rough benchmark as
      a law. Multiplying by ten applies the same supposed rule as a correction, which has no basis.
  - stem: "Suppose net primary production in an illustrative grassland is 10,000 kJ m^-2 yr^-1 and transfer efficiency is 10% at every step. What production would you expect at trophic level 4, counting producers as level 1?"
    choices:

      - "1 kJ m^-2 yr^-1"
      - "2,500 kJ m^-2 yr^-1"
      - "10 kJ m^-2 yr^-1"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-

The first film opened the three doors on one feeding step and found 6% passed up to the herbivores. It left out how anyone measures these flows, what happens one step higher, and where the dead matter goes.

> **The one idea.** At every feeding step, energy leaves by one of three doors: **passed up** as consumer production, dropped into **detritus** (uneaten, or as feces), or lost as **heat**. The share passed up is a product of three fractions, consumption × assimilation × net production efficiency, so production shrinks fast up a food chain.
>
> *Scope:* a simplified budget of rates over one period (usually a year), in one currency per area. It assumes production not eaten during the period enters detritus (in real budgets some stays as living biomass, is eaten along another food-web link, or is exported). Small losses such as excreted wastes are lumped with detritus or neglected. Production shrinks as long as consumers eat no more than the production below them (Going deeper). Energy never returns to the producers: it is stored for a while or leaves as heat. Only matter cycles.

The machinery belongs to Biochemistry: the [light reactions](/lessons/light-reactions/) make ATP and NADPH, the [Calvin cycle](/lessons/calvin-cycle/) uses them to fix CO2, and [oxidative phosphorylation](/lessons/oxidative-phosphorylation/) recovers energy from food through a [proton-motive force](/lessons/proton-motive-force/) (vocabulary in [basic concepts of metabolism](/lessons/metabolism-basic-concepts/)). Here you keep the totals.

## Production is a rate: GPP, NPP and NEP

**Production** is the rate at which new organic matter, or its energy, is formed per unit area or volume per unit time, such as kJ m^-2 yr^-1 (kilojoules per square metre per year) or g C m^-2 yr^-1 (grams of carbon). **Biomass** (standing crop) is a stock: how much is there at one moment, in g m^-2 with no time unit. A small stock can sit on a large production if it turns over quickly, as fast-dividing phytoplankton do.

Real plants often respire roughly half of the energy they fix.

**Net ecosystem production** (NEP) subtracts **Reco**, the respiration of the whole ecosystem: Ra plus the respiration of animals, fungi and bacteria.

> **NEP = GPP − Reco**

NEP is what is fixed and not respired. If no organic matter is carried in or out (by water or harvest, say), it is what the ecosystem stores, and **GPP = Reco + NEP**: everything fixed is either respired or stored. In an open system some fixed carbon is exported, and imported organic matter also changes the stock.

## Measuring it: bottles, harvests and towers

No method observes GPP directly at ecosystem scale; each measures one door, or the net, and infers the rest.

**Light and dark bottles.** Seal paired bottles of lake or sea water, keep one dark, and incubate both. Write O_start for the oxygen at the start, L for the final oxygen in the light bottle, D for the final oxygen in the dark bottle, and t for the incubation time. The light bottle gains oxygen from photosynthesis and loses it to respiration, so its change is **net community production = (L − O_start) / t**.

**Predict.** The light bottle already gives you a net. What does the dark bottle tell you that the light one cannot?

**Harvests and component sums.** In grassland, clipping and weighing the plants at the season's peak gives a rough, usually low estimate of aboveground NPP. In forest, add the separately measured parts: Ji Young An and colleagues summed woody growth, litterfall and fine-root growth in nine stands in the Gwangneung Experimental Forest, Korea, where fine roots made up 18 to 44% of the estimated NPP. Where NPP is high in the first place depends largely on temperature and water, the subject of [climate and biomes](/lessons/climate-and-biomes/).

Any part left out is missing from the total, and in nine Korean stands fine roots made up 18 to 44% of the estimated NPP." />

**Eddy-covariance towers.** Fast sensors above the canopy record how vertical gusts carry CO2 up and down. When rising gusts carry less CO2 than sinking ones, the forest is taking carbon in. Averaged over a year, this gives **net ecosystem exchange** (NEE), the net flow of CO2 from the ecosystem to the air. The tower sees only this net; a **partitioning model** splits it into GPP and Reco, commonly by fitting respiration to night-time data (no photosynthesis) and extrapolating it into the day. Methods disagree at the same site (Going deeper), so GPP is an estimate with a method attached.

**Predict.** A forest stores carbon this year, and its tower counts release to the air as positive. Will its NEE be positive or negative?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Negative. With release counted as positive, NEE = Reco − GPP, the mirror image of NEP, so an ecosystem that takes up carbon has a negative NEE. Check the sign convention before you read any flux graph.

</details>

## Following energy through a consumer

Feeding links ([predation](/lessons/predation/), [mutualism, parasitism and coevolution](/lessons/mutualism-parasitism-coevolution/)) are the pipes. In symbols, the first film's doors for one consumer are what it **ingests**, I; its feces, **egestion**; what it **assimilates**, A; its respiration, R; and its **production**, P.

> **A = I − egestion   P = A − R**

The first film's three valves are three ratios: **consumption efficiency**, I / P below (P below is the production of the level eaten); **assimilation efficiency**, A / I; and **net production efficiency**, P / A. Their product is the **trophic transfer efficiency** (TTE): production at one level over production at the level below, in matched units and time.

Door sizes differ between animals: assimilation efficiency tends to be higher for carnivores, because animal tissue is easier to digest than plant cell walls. Net production efficiency tends to be low in birds and mammals, which respire most of what they assimilate, much of it to keep warm, and higher in many invertebrates and fish.

**Worked problem.** Carry the first film's made-up budget (one square metre, one year, kJ m^-2 yr^-1) up to the predators and into the dead matter, with no imports, exports or net decomposer growth. Production that is not eaten enters detritus; predator production, with no one above it, is kept as new predator biomass. From the first film: NPP 6,000, and herbivores ingest 1,800, egest 1,080 and produce 360. Predators ingest 200, egest 100 and respire 60. Decomposers respire 3,240.

**Predict.** The predators keep the same three doors. Will their link pass on 6% too?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No, about 11%.

1. *Predators.* A = 200 − 100 = 100. P = 100 − 60 = 40. TTE = 40 / 360 = 11.1%. Check with the three efficiencies: 200 / 360 = 0.556, 100 / 200 = 0.50 and 40 / 100 = 0.40, and 0.556 × 0.50 × 0.40 = 0.111.

## Detritus, transfer efficiency and the 10% rule

Two things the second film left out. Later reviews found little quantitative evidence that Teal's tidal remainder was actually exported; how much marsh production supports food webs in the estuary beyond became a question for later work. And how far the detrital route it showed, rather than the grazing chain, supports higher trophic levels in a given system is still open. The matter that returns to producers is the subject of [nutrient cycling](/lessons/nutrient-cycling/).

How big is the passed-up share in real food webs? From 48 published aquatic food-web models, Daniel Pauly and Villy Christensen derived in 1995 a mean transfer efficiency of about 10%, the classic support for the **10% rule**: a rough benchmark from aquatic models, not a law (Going deeper). A 2026 synthesis of 2,052 estimates from 122 studies found a lower average energy transfer efficiency, about 6% (by coincidence, the made-up first link's value), highest in marine systems and lowest on land. Real links can be far lower or higher.

**Predict.** At 10% per step, a top predator at the fourth trophic level (counting producers as level 1) receives 0.1% of NPP: three transfers, 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.001. What does it receive at 6% per step?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

0.06 × 0.06 × 0.06 = 0.000216, about 0.022% of NPP, roughly a fifth of what the 10% rule gives. Small changes in efficiency matter once multiplied.

</details>

That fast shrinking is one likely reason food chains are short, though not the only proposed limit (Going deeper).

### Design problem

Students incubate light and dark bottles of lake water for 6 hours. Initial dissolved oxygen is 8.0 mg/L; at the end the light bottle reads 9.2 mg/L and the dark bottle 7.4 mg/L. These are invented numbers. Calculate net community production, respiration and GPP per hour. Then say what these numbers can and cannot tell you about the lake's phytoplankton, and how you would improve the study.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Net community production = (9.2 - 8.0) / 6 = 0.20 mg O2 L^-1 h^-1. Respiration = (8.0 - 7.4) / 6 = 0.10 mg O2 L^-1 h^-1. GPP = (9.2 - 7.4) / 6 = 0.30 mg O2 L^-1 h^-1, which equals 0.20 + 0.10, a useful check.

What the numbers rest on: respiration is assumed equal in light and dark (in the light it can differ) and the bottle sealed. Bacteria and zooplankton are in the bottle, so these are community respiration and community production, not phytoplankton NPP. The figures are oxygen per litre per hour at one depth on one day. Converting them to carbon needs stated quotients: a photosynthetic quotient (moles of O2 released per mole of CO2 fixed) for production and a respiratory quotient (moles of CO2 released per mole of O2 used) for respiration, and an annual areal rate needs measurements through the water column and the seasons.

To improve it, run replicates at several depths through the year, keep incubations short enough that oxygen does not build up unrealistically, and report each result with its spread.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Splitting a tower's net flux

Besides night-time extrapolation there are light-response fits and neural networks trained on environmental drivers; a 2020 study applied a neural-network method to 36 FLUXNET2015 sites, and agreement among methods is not independent validation. At one Scots pine forest at Hyytiälä, Finland, over 2013 to 2017, daily GPP estimates from two methods based on carbonyl sulfide, a gas leaves take up along with CO2, averaged 7% and 23% above the CO2-based estimate.

### When a fraction exceeds 1

Assimilation and net production efficiency cannot exceed 1, because each is a share of what the consumer took in. Consumption efficiency can: when consumers eat down a standing prey stock, they ingest more than the production below them. The product still holds by algebra, but consumption efficiency is then no longer a share of the prey's production, and a single link's transfer can look larger over that period.

### Gross growth efficiency

**Gross growth efficiency**, P / I, is production over ingestion: a property of one consumer, not a transfer between levels.

### Where the 10% figure came from

Pauly and Christensen used their mean to estimate the primary production needed to sustain world fisheries. It is also not Lindeman's law: Lindeman compared successive levels of his lake as ratios, but defined them on a different basis.

### Food quality

Producers short of nitrogen or phosphorus may limit transfer to herbivores even when carbon is plentiful, so energy is not the only currency that matters.

### Chain length and ecosystem size

A 2000 study of 25 northern temperate lakes (Post, Pace and Hairston, Nature) found that food-chain length increased with lake size but not with productivity.
