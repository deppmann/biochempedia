summary: >-
  Gluconeogenesis is how the body builds glucose from non-sugar parts, a job that matters most when food runs out, and its single organizing idea is that it is not glycolysis run backward. It shares seven near-equilibrium enzymes with glycolysis but routes around the three one-way glycolytic steps with four different enzymes, working across three compartments and spending enough ATP and GTP that the whole pathway runs downhill toward glucose. Because both pathways are then downhill, the cell has to choose between them by regulation at each enzyme pair, so in a healthy liver cell one direction dominates and wasteful cycling stays small.
hook: "When food runs out, your liver (with help from the kidney) builds the glucose your brain and red blood cells still need, and it does it with four new enzymes, three compartments, and real ATP and GTP spent to make the climb."

techniques:
  - name: "Isotopic carbon tracing of precursors"
    blurb: >-
      Feed a precursor labeled at a known carbon — ¹⁴C-lactate, ¹³C-alanine, or
      ¹³C-glycerol — and track where the atoms land in newly made glucose. The carbon
      skeleton is what becomes glucose (alanine's nitrogen is stripped by transamination
      first), so the label has to sit on carbon. Tracing also exposed a subtlety at the
      heart of the chapter: label from an even-chain fatty acid can turn up in glucose,
      because the cycle shuffles acetyl carbons into oxaloacetate, yet there is never any
      net glucose, because for every two carbons that enter as acetyl-CoA, two leave as CO₂.
  - name: "Stable-isotope tracing of glucose production"
    blurb: >-
      To measure how much glucose the body is making, infuse a labeled glucose tracer in
      an overnight-fasted (basal) subject and read the dilution: total glucose appearance
      minus any infused glucose is endogenous glucose production, from liver and kidney. After
      an overnight fast roughly half of it still comes from glycogen breakdown, so splitting out
      gluconeogenesis needs a second method, such as heavy water or ¹³C NMR.
      A hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp answers a different question — it raises insulin
      while holding blood glucose constant to see how well insulin SUPPRESSES that output, so failure to suppress is the read-out of
      hepatic insulin resistance (insulin's action on fat, which sets substrate supply to the liver, feeds into it too), not a measure of fasting gluconeogenesis itself.
  - name: "Allosteric enzyme assay with single effectors"
    blurb: >-
      To map a control point you purify the enzyme and change one effector at a time while
      watching velocity. Adding acetyl-CoA to pyruvate carboxylase, AMP or
      fructose-2,6-bisphosphate to FBPase-1, or running the bifunctional PFK-2/FBPase-2
      enzyme with and without PKA phosphorylation is how the reciprocal regulators of this
      pathway were assigned to their enzymes, one molecule at a time.

      - "Adipose tissue, because it stores the glycerol used to build glucose"
      - "The brain, because it consumes the most glucose and therefore makes its own"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The liver is the primary gluconeogenic organ, with the kidney cortex contributing a share that grows in
      prolonged fasting. Muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase, the final bypass enzyme that
      strips the phosphate off glucose-6-phosphate, so it cannot release free glucose and
      instead exports lactate. The pancreas secretes hormones but does not synthesize
      glucose; adipose supplies glycerol but is not a gluconeogenic site; and the brain is a
      glucose consumer, not a producer.
  - stem: "A student claims that gluconeogenesis is simply glycolysis run in reverse. What is the strongest reason this is incorrect?"
    choices:
      - "Gluconeogenesis uses four different enzymes to bypass the three irreversible glycolytic steps, in different compartments and at net energetic cost"
      - "Gluconeogenesis and glycolysis use entirely different sets of enzymes for all of their steps"
      - "Glycolysis occurs in mitochondria while gluconeogenesis occurs only in the cytosol"
      - "Gluconeogenesis produces ATP, whereas glycolysis consumes it"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Gluconeogenesis shares its seven near-equilibrium enzymes with glycolysis but cannot
      reverse the three irreversible steps; it routes around them with pyruvate carboxylase
      plus PEPCK, FBPase-1, and glucose-6-phosphatase, spanning mitochondrion, cytosol, and
      ER and spending energy. It does not use entirely different enzymes for all steps,
      glycolysis is cytosolic rather than mitochondrial, and it is gluconeogenesis that
      consumes energy while glycolysis nets ATP.
  - stem: "Why can even-chain fatty acids not serve as a source of net glucose synthesis?"
    choices:
      - "Fatty acids cannot cross the mitochondrial membrane to reach gluconeogenic enzymes"
      - "Acetyl-CoA from β-oxidation cannot enter the citric acid cycle at all"
      - "Acetyl-CoA enters the TCA cycle but two carbons are lost as CO₂ per turn, yielding no net oxaloacetate gain"
      - "Fatty acid carbons are converted directly to lactate, which cannot make glucose"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Each acetyl-CoA condenses with one oxaloacetate to enter the cycle, and over one turn
      two carbons leave as CO₂, so the OAA consumed is exactly regenerated with no net gain.
      Without net OAA there is no net glucose. Fatty acids do reach the matrix and acetyl-CoA
      does enter the cycle, so those options are wrong, and fatty acid carbons are not routed
      to lactate.
  - stem: "In the pyruvate-to-PEP bypass, what is the purpose of converting oxaloacetate to malate before exporting it from the mitochondrion?"
    choices:
      - "To generate ATP needed for the cytosolic steps"
      - "To activate pyruvate carboxylase allosterically"
      - "To detoxify oxaloacetate, which is toxic to the cytosol"
      - "Oxaloacetate lacks an inner-membrane transporter, so it is shuttled as malate, which (from pyruvate or alanine) also delivers cytosolic NADH"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Oxaloacetate cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane, so it is reduced to
      malate, exported on the malate–α-ketoglutarate carrier, and re-oxidized to OAA in the
      cytosol — a step that, with pyruvate or alanine precursors, also supplies the NADH
      that GAPDH later needs. The shuttle does not generate ATP, acetyl-CoA (not malate
      conversion) activates pyruvate carboxylase, and OAA is not a cytosolic toxin.
  - stem: "Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate is best described as which of the following?"
    choices:
      - "A glycolytic pathway intermediate converted to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate"
      - "The high-energy phosphate donor used by PEPCK"
      - "A purely regulatory molecule that activates PFK-1 and inhibits FBPase-1"
      - "A precursor that enters gluconeogenesis at the level of DHAP"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate is a regulator only: it activates PFK-1 (favoring glycolysis)
      and inhibits FBPase-1 (suppressing gluconeogenesis). It is not a pathway intermediate —
      that is fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. PEPCK uses GTP as its phosphate donor, and the
      gluconeogenic precursor entering at DHAP is glycerol.

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm 5"
    text: |
      When you don't eat for a long time, your body runs low on sugar, and your brain
      really, really needs sugar to work. So your liver becomes a little sugar factory. It
      takes leftover bits — like the stuff your muscles make when you run hard — and glues
      them back together into brand-new sugar. Building sugar costs energy, the way sliding
      down a hill is easy but climbing back up is hard work. That is why your body mostly
      builds sugar when it has to, and tries not to build it and tear it down at the same
      time.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: |
      Gluconeogenesis is making new glucose from things that aren't carbohydrates: lactate,
      certain amino acids like alanine, and glycerol from fat. It happens mostly in the liver
      during fasting, because the brain and red blood cells depend on glucose. It looks like
      glycolysis running backward, but it isn't. Glycolysis has three one-way steps that
      can't be reversed, so gluconeogenesis goes around them with four different enzymes, and
      it has to spend energy to do it: about 6 high-energy phosphates and 2 NADH per glucose,
      versus the 2 ATP glycolysis makes. One key fact: you can't turn the fatty acids in fat
      into sugar, because the citric acid cycle loses two carbons as CO₂ for every two a
      fatty acid brings in, so nothing is left over to build glucose. Because both pathways
      run downhill, the cell has to choose between them. Switches at each of the three
      enzyme pairs, the best known a molecule called fructose-2,6-bisphosphate, make sure the
      cell runs mostly glycolysis or mostly gluconeogenesis, not both at full speed.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: |
      Gluconeogenesis synthesizes glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors (lactate via LDH,
      glucogenic amino acids, glycerol entering as DHAP). It shares the seven near-equilibrium
      enzymes of glycolysis and bypasses the three irreversible steps with four enzymes.
      Bypass 1: mitochondrial pyruvate carboxylase (biotin-dependent, acetyl-CoA-activated)
      makes oxaloacetate, which exits as malate via the malate shuttle (also delivering
      cytosolic NADH for GAPDH from pyruvate/alanine precursors); cytosolic PEPCK then
      converts OAA to PEP using GTP, with the PC-added CO₂ removed as a thermodynamic lever.
      Bypass 2: FBPase-1 hydrolyzes fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. Bypass 3: glucose-6-phosphatase,
      in the ER lumen, releases free glucose (liver, kidney cortex and small intestine; muscle lacks it).
      Cost is 4 ATP + 2 GTP + 2 NADH per glucose. Reciprocal regulation prevents futile cycling,
      and each pair has its own controls. At the middle pair, fructose-2,6-bisphosphate, set by
      the bifunctional PFK-2/FBPase-2 enzyme under insulin/glucagon (PKA) control, activates
      PFK-1 and inhibits FBPase-1. At the pyruvate pair, PKA phosphorylation inhibits liver
      pyruvate kinase and acetyl-CoA activates pyruvate carboxylase; the glucose pair is set
      by glucokinase regulation and enzyme amounts.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: |
      The architecture of gluconeogenesis is a study in how irreversibility is bypassed
      without sacrificing thermodynamic control. The PK bypass is split spatially: pyruvate
      carboxylase performs an ATP- and biotin-dependent carboxylation (an anaplerotic
      reaction allosterically gated by acetyl-CoA, signaling that acetyl-CoA is plentiful and
      OAA should be replenished rather than oxidized), and PEPCK performs a GTP-dependent
      β-decarboxylation. The carboxylation/decarboxylation cycle is not wasteful: the
      transient CO₂ fixation raises the group-transfer potential needed to generate PEP, and
      the malate shuttle (used with pyruvate or alanine precursors) co-transports reducing equivalents matched to cytosolic
      GAPDH demand, so redox and carbon are balanced by design. G6Pase acts in the ER lumen
      behind the G6PT transporter, yet it draws on the same cytosolic G6P pool as glucokinase, so
      limiting that substrate cycle falls to regulation, as fructose-2,6-bisphosphate does
      allosterically at the F6P/FBP node. F-2,6-BP is the integrator: made and degraded by a
      single bifunctional polypeptide whose kinase/phosphatase balance is set by PKA
      phosphorylation, coupling hormonal state to the simultaneous, opposite control of PFK-1
      and FBPase-1. Flux direction at a substrate cycle is set by the ratio of opposing
      activities, not by which enzyme is present. That is why the excess glucose output of insulin resistance, driven largely by substrate
      supply from fat, hepatic acetyl-CoA and glucagon, is a regulatory failure rather than a missing enzyme.

  - title: "Why you really can't turn fat into sugar"
    source: "CH17 lecture notes — \"you consume one OAA to bring acetyl-CoA in, get one back; net glucose = zero\""
    text: >-
      It feels like fat should be convertible to glucose; it's an energy store, after all.
      But even-chain fatty acids cannot make net glucose, and the reason is an accounting
      trick worth memorizing. Acetyl-CoA enters the citric acid cycle by combining with one
      oxaloacetate, and over a turn of the cycle two carbons leave as CO₂. You spend one OAA
      to bring acetyl-CoA in and you get exactly one OAA back, so net OAA gain is zero and net
      glucose is zero. Only odd-chain fatty acids escape, by sending their final propionyl-CoA
      to succinyl-CoA and on to OAA.
  - title: "CO₂ as a thermodynamic lever"
    source: "CH17 lecture notes — \"the CO₂ added by PC is removed by PEPCK; CO₂ is a thermodynamic lever\""
    text: >-
      It looks pointless: pyruvate carboxylase bolts a CO₂ onto pyruvate, and a few steps
      later PEPCK pries the same CO₂ right back off. Why add a carbon just to remove it?
      Because the round trip is a lever. Putting the carboxyl on and then taking it off is
      what makes forming high-energy phosphoenolpyruvate thermodynamically favorable. The ATP
      that pyruvate carboxylase spends is stored in the new carboxyl group, and when PEPCK
      pulls that CO₂ back off, the favorable decarboxylation, together with a GTP, pays for
      the high-energy enol phosphate. Read the two enzymes as one maneuver.
  - title: "One missing enzyme, a glycogen-stuffed liver"
    source: "CH17 lecture notes — Von Gierke disease (glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency)."

**Skip breakfast and lunch, and by the early afternoon your liver is building much of your glucose out of spare parts.** It has to. Your brain runs almost entirely on glucose until a fast lasts several days and ketones take over part of the load, and your red blood cells, which have no mitochondria, run on nothing else. Glycogen breakdown supplies most of the glucose in the first hours of a fast, with gluconeogenesis already contributing; gluconeogenesis rises as glycogen falls, and the two share the load until glycogen runs low within a day or two. After that, blood sugar stays in its narrow lane (about 70–100 mg/dL fasting) only because the liver, with help from the kidney cortex, is manufacturing fresh glucose. (When the film at the top says the body "switches", it means this gradual handover.) Fasting is not running out of energy but switching fuels: gluconeogenesis supplies the glucose-dependent tissues while most others switch to fat and ketones.

On a map, gluconeogenesis looks like glycolysis run backward. That raises two puzzles. If glycolysis runs downhill, its steps run backward should run uphill, so how does the liver make glucose from pyruvate at all? And once it can, what stops a liver cell from making glucose and burning it at the same time?

## Where the carbons come from

There are three precursors to know. **Lactate** comes back from hard-working muscle and red cells; lactate dehydrogenase converts it to pyruvate. **Glucogenic amino acids**, alanine chief among them, plus glutamine, the kidney's main gluconeogenic amino acid, lose their nitrogen and enter at pyruvate or at citric acid cycle intermediates that become oxaloacetate. **Glycerol**, released when fat is mobilized, enters as DHAP by way of glycerol kinase and glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase.

Notice what is missing: the fatty acids that make up most of fat. Even-chain fatty acids cannot become glucose. This trips up almost everyone; the reason comes after Bypass 1.

## Three cliffs: why the pathway can't just run backward

An enzyme speeds a reaction both ways; it does not pick a direction. ΔG, the free-energy change under the conditions inside the cell, does. When ΔG is close to zero, the step is **near equilibrium**: a two- or threefold change in concentrations tips it either way, so one enzyme can serve both pathways. When ΔG is large and negative, the step is **far from equilibrium** and in practice runs one way only. That is all "irreversible" means here.

Seven of glycolysis's ten steps are near equilibrium, and gluconeogenesis shares those seven enzymes. Three are far from it. These are classic estimates of their ΔG inside a cell (calculated from metabolite levels measured in red blood cells; textbooks differ by a few kJ/mol):

| Glycolytic step | What it does | ΔG in the cell |
|---|---|---|
| Hexokinase | ATP's phosphate onto glucose | about −33 kJ/mol |
| Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1) | ATP's phosphate onto fructose-6-phosphate | about −22 kJ/mol |
| Pyruvate kinase | PEP's phosphate onto ADP, making ATP | about −17 kJ/mol |

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only in principle. At body temperature, every 6 kJ/mol or so of ΔG is a tenfold change in the ratio of products to reactants. Just to bring pyruvate kinase to equilibrium the cell would have to shift that ratio about 650-fold, and for hexokinase about 400,000-fold; running either one backward would take more still. No cell can hold its ATP, ADP and sugar phosphates that far from normal.

</details>

So at each cliff, gluconeogenesis uses a different enzyme:

- **Where glycolysis put a phosphate on with ATP** (hexokinase, called glucokinase in liver, and PFK-1), gluconeogenesis does not hand the phosphate back to ADP; it cuts it off with water. These hydrolyses, by **glucose-6-phosphatase** and **fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (FBPase-1)**, are downhill (about −14 and −16 kJ/mol; these are standard values, unlike the table's in-cell ones).
- **Where glycolysis made ATP** (pyruvate kinase), there is no cheap trick: even with one ATP spent, turning pyruvate back into PEP is uphill by about 31 kJ/mol under standard conditions (about 17 in the cell, the table's number with its sign flipped). The cell spends two high-energy phosphates, an ATP at **pyruvate carboxylase** and a GTP at **PEP carboxykinase (PEPCK)**. The second phosphate is worth about 30.5, so with both the step is about even under standard conditions (+31 − 30.5 ≈ +1) and comfortably downhill in the liver.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Every step has to run downhill in the direction it is used, the near-equilibrium ones only slightly. Gluconeogenesis keeps glycolysis's seven near-equilibrium steps and swaps each of the three one-way steps for a different reaction that runs downhill toward glucose. With both pathways downhill, thermodynamics no longer picks the direction; the cell has to choose, by regulation.

This holds in the liver and kidney cortex, which carry all four bypass enzymes.

## Bypass 1: pyruvate to PEP, across two compartments

Inside the mitochondrion, **pyruvate carboxylase** adds CO₂ to pyruvate to make oxaloacetate, spending one ATP. It uses biotin, a vitamin that carries the CO₂ (see Going deeper), and is switched on by acetyl-CoA, the cell's way of saying *plenty of fuel here, make glucose rather than burn more pyruvate.*

Next, a shipping problem. PEPCK, the next enzyme, works in the cytosol in the route followed here (human liver also has a mitochondrial form), and oxaloacetate has no transporter across the inner mitochondrial membrane. The fix is the **malate shuttle**: oxaloacetate is reduced to malate, malate rides out on the malate–α-ketoglutarate carrier, and in the cytosol it is oxidized back to oxaloacetate.

The trip also moves reducing power, and where the NADH must come from decides whether oxaloacetate leaves as malate or as aspartate. Later, GAPDH runs in reverse and needs a cytosolic NADH for each three-carbon unit. From pyruvate or alanine, that NADH must come out of the mitochondrion, and malate brings it. From lactate, lactate dehydrogenase already made the NADH in the cytosol, so oxaloacetate can leave as aspartate instead.

Back in the cytosol, **PEPCK** spends a GTP and removes the very CO₂ that pyruvate carboxylase added, producing phosphoenolpyruvate.

**Predict.** Why would a cell add a CO₂ only to remove it one step later?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

PEP needs about two high-energy phosphates' worth of energy, and in animals no single enzyme turns pyruvate into PEP. The CO₂ is the handle that lets two enzymes, in two compartments, share the job. Pyruvate carboxylase spends ATP to attach it, storing energy in oxaloacetate. PEPCK pulls the same CO₂ off; that decarboxylation is downhill, and with a GTP it pays for PEP. That is what "CO₂ as a thermodynamic lever" means.

</details>

## Why fat can't become sugar

Every PEP made from pyruvate uses up one oxaloacetate, so gluconeogenesis from anything but glycerol (which enters at DHAP, above PEP) needs a net source of oxaloacetate or of something that becomes it, such as pyruvate or citric acid cycle intermediates. Even-chain fatty acids supply none. Their carbons enter the citric acid cycle as acetyl-CoA, which joins one oxaloacetate to get in; over a turn, two carbons leave as CO₂ and exactly one oxaloacetate comes back. Net oxaloacetate gain is zero, so net glucose from fatty acids is zero. (Carbon label from a fatty acid can still turn up in glucose, because the cycle shuffles atoms; see the isotope-tracing technique above.)

Why not turn acetyl-CoA back into pyruvate? Pyruvate dehydrogenase releases CO₂ in a step so far downhill that it runs one way only (next chapter). Plants and bacteria have a way around: two glyoxylate-cycle enzymes let acetyl-CoA skip the cycle's two CO₂-releasing steps, giving them a net gain of oxaloacetate. Humans and other vertebrates lack them. The one exception is odd-chain fatty acids, whose final three-carbon propionyl-CoA can be routed through succinyl-CoA to oxaloacetate. Of the fat you actually carry, only the glycerol backbone can become sugar.

## Bypasses 2 and 3, and why muscle can't share

From PEP, the shared enzymes run in reverse up to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. There **FBPase-1** hydrolyzes off a phosphate, a clean snip past the PFK-1 cliff. It is a major regulated step, inhibited by AMP and by fructose-2,6-bisphosphate and activated by citrate.

**Predict.** FBPase-1 makes no ATP and spends none. How can it run downhill, and what did it cost?

Cutting the phosphate off with water is downhill on its own (the −16 above). Handing it back to ADP, the reverse of PFK-1, would be uphill by about 14, since PFK-1 is downhill by about 14 under standard conditions. The gap, about 30 kJ/mol, is one ATP's worth: the ATP that PFK-1 spent, which gluconeogenesis never gets back. The cost is a refund given up, not a payment.

</details>

The last cliff, **glucose-6-phosphatase**, works the same way, and it hides in a revealing place: the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum. Glucose-6-phosphate is carried in by the T1 transporter to be dephosphorylated (why there is unsettled; Going deeper). Only liver, kidney cortex and, to a lesser degree, the small intestine express the glucose-releasing form, G6Pase-α. Muscle does not, so it cannot send its glycogen into the blood as glucose; it uses that glycogen locally and, in hard work, ships out the lactate it makes faster than it can use.

## Downhill both ways: the switch at each pair

The catch: if both pathways ran at full speed in one cell, glucose would be broken down and rebuilt with no product: a **futile cycle** that burns ATP as heat. Each turn wastes four high-energy phosphates: one at the glucose pair, one at the middle pair, and two at the pyruvate pair (lifting each pyruvate to PEP costs an ATP and a GTP, and pyruvate kinase returns one ATP). That matches the bill: six spent, two made.

The one idea, in short: **downhill both ways, so the cell has to choose.** As the film puts it, regulation, not the energy cost, holds the cycle in check, and each of the three pairs has its own controls.

**The middle pair, PFK-1 and FBPase-1.** The best-known switch, **fructose-2,6-bisphosphate** (F-2,6-BP), works here, activating PFK-1 and inhibiting FBPase-1. *1,6 is the pathway; 2,6 is the regulator.*

| Enzyme | What it acts on | Wins in the liver when you are |
|---|---|---|
| PFK-1 | makes fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (glycolysis) | fed |
| FBPase-1 | breaks fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (gluconeogenesis) | fasting |
| PFK-2/FBPase-2 | one protein; its two domains make (PFK-2) or break (FBPase-2) fructose-2,6-bisphosphate | fed: PFK-2; fasting: FBPase-2 |

In the fed liver, insulin keeps this **bifunctional enzyme** dephosphorylated, the kinase wins, F-2,6-BP rises, and glycolysis runs.

## When a bypass breaks

**Pyruvate carboxylase deficiency** breaks Bypass 1: pyruvate cannot be carboxylated to start gluconeogenesis, so it piles up and is converted to lactate, and lactic acidosis is the constant finding. In the severe newborn form hypoglycemia often joins it, with high ammonia, because the same oxaloacetate also feeds the citric acid cycle and, as aspartate, the urea cycle.

**Von Gierke disease** (glycogen storage disease type I) breaks Bypass 3. Without glucose-6-phosphatase or its T1 transporter, the liver can build glucose-6-phosphate but cannot release free glucose through its usual route: severe fasting hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis, and a liver swollen with glycogen it cannot cash out.

In **type 2 diabetes** no enzyme is missing. Insulin no longer holds the liver's glucose output down, and fasting blood sugar rises; much of the cause is extra fuel arriving from fat.

The **alanine cycle**, beside the Cori cycle, carries muscle nitrogen to the liver packaged on alanine, because muscle lacks the urea-cycle machinery to dispose of ammonia. A rising serum ALT, the enzyme that runs that cycle, flags liver damage.

In the next chapter we follow pyruvate the other way, into the mitochondrion, where the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex turns it irreversibly into acetyl-CoA, which the citric acid cycle can burn completely.

## Going deeper

### The swinging biotin arm of pyruvate carboxylase

Pyruvate carboxylase uses a biotin cofactor on a flexible lysine tether, carried on a mobile domain that swings the carboxyl group between two active sites. At the first site the enzyme spends its ATP to load CO₂ onto biotin; at the second, biotin hands the carboxyl to pyruvate, making oxaloacetate.

### Why glucose-6-phosphatase sits in the ER lumen

The reason is not settled. Hexokinase (glucokinase, in liver) sits in the cytosol running the opposite reaction, but both enzymes draw on the same pool of glucose-6-phosphate, so the separate room does not by itself stop a futile cycle; regulation of the two enzymes does most of that work.

### Type 2 diabetes, PEPCK and metformin

Much of the liver's lost insulin response is indirect. Insulin-resistant fat releases more glycerol and fatty acids, the fatty acids raise liver acetyl-CoA and so pyruvate carboxylase flux, and glucagon stays high. PEPCK levels alone control surprisingly little of the flux. Metformin lowers glucose output, by mechanisms still debated (AMPK activation is one candidate, a drop in liver energy state another).

### Fasting across cultures

Fasting itself is old and human. The Teton Sioux fasted to invite a spiritual awakening, and cultures across the world still fast for clarity or devotion. Every such fast runs on the handover this lesson opened with: glycogen and gluconeogenesis first, then, after several days, ketones taking over part of the brain's load.
