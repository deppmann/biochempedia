techniques:
  - name: "In vitro transcription with purified components"
    blurb: >-
      You mix a DNA template, purified polymerase, chosen factors and labeled nucleotides, then
      measure the RNA made. Adding or removing one component tells you what it is needed for, as
      when sigma restored transcription of phage DNA or general factors let Pol II start at a promoter.
  - name: "Column chromatography of enzyme activity"
    blurb: >-
      A cell extract is passed through a resin that holds proteins by charge, and fractions are
      collected and assayed. Separate peaks of activity show separate enzymes or separable parts,
      which is how sigma was split from core and how Pol I, II and III were first resolved.
  - name: "Nuclear run-on assay"
    blurb: >-
      Nuclei are isolated and given labeled nucleotides, so polymerases already engaged on genes
      extend their RNAs a short distance. Measuring labeled RNA from a given gene reports how much
      it was being transcribed, and adding inhibitors such as alpha-amanitin shows which
      polymerase does it.
  - name: "X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM"
    blurb: >-
      Crystals or frozen particles of a polymerase complex are imaged to build an atomic model.
      The models show where the template, the RNA and each factor sit, which turns a list of
      steps into a physical mechanism.

The first film showed a promoter choosing the template; here is the chemistry behind it.

> **The one idea.** The start site and its orientation determine which strand is read.
>
> *Scope:* transcription from double-stranded DNA. A promoter and its initiation machinery set a start site and the direction the polymerase faces there; RNA polymerase then reads that one template 3′ to 5′ while extending RNA 5′ to 3′. Promoter sequence alone does not determine how often initiation occurs, and where the RNA ends is set separately, by termination.

## The reaction fixes the direction

**Transcription** is the copying of a stretch of DNA into RNA. **RNA polymerase** opens a short stretch of the double helix and uses one DNA strand, the **template strand** (antisense or noncoding strand), to line up the ribonucleoside triphosphates ATP, GTP, CTP and UTP by base pairing: RNA A opposite DNA T, U opposite A, G opposite C and C opposite G. It joins each nucleotide to the 3′ hydroxyl of the growing RNA, releasing pyrophosphate. That chemistry fixes the directions:

- RNA grows **5′ to 3′**, as DNA does in [DNA replication](/lessons/dna-replication/).
- The polymerase therefore reads its antiparallel template **3′ to 5′**.
- RNA matches the other DNA strand, the **coding strand** (nontemplate strand), with U in place of T.

Unlike DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase needs no primer; it can join the first two nucleotides itself. A **promoter** is the DNA site where polymerase and its helpers assemble. The first transcribed base is **+1**. On the coding strand, bases toward its 5′ end before +1 are **upstream** and have negative numbers; positions after +1 are **downstream**. A **terminator** is a signal that ends transcription. Each gene uses one strand as its template, but neighbouring genes can face opposite ways and use opposite strands.

top:     5′-TACGGATCCATGAAACGT-3′

The **top is the template**; the RNA pairs with it antiparallel. At the right end, the RNA's A pairs with the top strand's T, then C with G.

The polymerase reads the top template 3′ to 5′. Its 3′ end is on the right, so the polymerase moves **right to left**.

## Bacteria: sigma places one core enzyme

The second film showed sigma docking onto core. In *E. coli*, one kind of **core enzyme**, two alpha subunits, one beta, one beta prime and one omega, makes nearly all cellular RNA. Bound to a **sigma factor**, it becomes the **holoenzyme**.

The housekeeping factor sigma 70 recognizes two coding-strand elements. A **consensus** gives the most common base at each position across many promoters:

> **−35 element: 5′-TTGACA-3′ ... about 17 bp ... −10 element: 5′-TATAAT-3′**

Real promoters differ from that sequence.

**Predict.** A mutation makes one −10 element closer to consensus. Does that guarantee more transcription?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. A closer match usually raises sigma-dependent initiation, but the promoter's spacing, other elements and bound proteins can outweigh that one change.

</details>

Holoenzyme bound to still double-stranded promoter DNA is the **closed complex**. Melting about a turn of the helix around the −10 element and +1 makes the **open complex**, with template DNA in the active site. Making and releasing short RNAs at the promoter is **abortive initiation**; once one RNA grows long enough, the polymerase leaves for productive elongation, **promoter escape**. Sigma commonly leaves at escape, but in single-molecule work with purified *E. coli* enzyme, a substantial share of elongating complexes kept sigma 70.

Other sigmas redirect the same core. In *E. coli*, sigma 32 directs transcription of heat-shock genes, so a temperature rise changes which promoters are used.

**Predict.** If sigma 32 rises while the amount of core enzyme stays fixed, which promoter set should gain access to core?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Promoters recognized by sigma 32 should gain access, increasing transcription of heat-shock genes. The catalytic core has not changed; its promoter-selecting partner has.

</details>

Because bacteria have no nucleus, ribosomes can translate an mRNA while its 3′ end is still being made. Many bacterial mRNAs are **polycistronic**, carrying several coding sequences from one promoter in an **operon**, though not all are ([the lac operon](/lessons/lac-operon/)).

## Eukaryotic nuclei divide the work

All eukaryotes share three main nuclear RNA polymerases; plants have two more, Pol IV and Pol V. In mammalian cells:

- **Pol I** makes the large 45S or 47S rRNA precursor, later cut into 28S, 18S and 5.8S rRNA.
- **Pol II** makes pre-mRNAs, most small nuclear RNAs and microRNA precursors.
- **Pol III** makes tRNAs, 5S rRNA, U6 snRNA and other small RNAs.

The death-cap toxin alpha-amanitin separates their activities: very low doses block Pol II while the other two keep working, and much higher doses inhibit Pol III.

### Placing Pol II

The second film's Pol II platform is built from the **general transcription factors**, which assemble with Pol II into the **preinitiation complex**. In the classic stepwise picture, **TFIID** recognizes the core promoter first. It contains **TBP**, the TATA-binding protein, and other subunits. **TFIIA** and **TFIIB** stabilize TBP on the DNA; TFIIB also helps position the start and bridge to Pol II. **TFIIF** brings Pol II, **TFIIE** helps recruit **TFIIH**, and TFIIH's XPB translocase helps open DNA. In living cells these factors exchange rapidly, so the order is less fixed.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/transcription/pol-ii-preinitiation-assembly.webp" width={1200} height={518} alt="AI-generated illustration: three steps of Pol II preinitiation complex assembly on a promoter. TFIID with TBP binds the TATA box first, TFIIA and TFIIB steady it, then Pol II with TFIIF arrives, followed by TFIIE and TFIIH." caption="General transcription factors recognize a Pol II core promoter, position the polymerase and help open the template; a TATA box is one route, not a requirement at every human promoter." />

In animal promoters that have one, a **TATA box** with consensus TATAAA lies about 25 to 30 bp upstream of +1. Most human promoters lack one. At those promoters, other TFIID subunits recognize other core elements, including an **initiator** spanning the start site.

TFIIH also contains the kinase CDK7. The largest Pol II subunit ends in the **CTD**, a carboxy-terminal tail of repeated seven-amino-acid units, YSPTSPS. As Pol II starts, CDK7 phosphorylates serine 5; other kinases phosphorylate serine 2 during elongation. The phosphorylated CTD recruits enzymes that cap the RNA and later those that splice and cut its 3′ end ([RNA processing](/lessons/rna-processing/)).

**Predict.** Suppose XPB remains active but CDK7 kinase activity is inhibited. Which listed task can still occur: DNA opening or CTD serine-5 phosphorylation?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

XPB can still help open DNA; inhibited CDK7 cannot phosphorylate CTD serine 5.

</details>

Regulators at the ends of [signaling pathways to the nucleus](/lessons/signaling-to-the-nucleus/) and [nuclear receptor](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/) pathways help or hinder assembly and alter promoter chromatin. Their specific contacts follow the principles in [protein binding and allostery](/lessons/protein-binding-allostery/). Many animal genes also pause Pol II just beyond the start; CDK9-triggered release into the gene is another regulated step.

## Stopping transcription

The start site and its orientation chose the strand; a stop only has to release the RNA and the enzyme.

An **intrinsic terminator** encodes a GC-rich RNA hairpin followed by a U tract. The hairpin forms near the RNA exit channel while weak A-U pairs hold the RNA to the DNA template in that tract, and together they destabilize the complex. A **Rho-dependent terminator** needs **Rho**, an ATP-driven protein that binds the nascent RNA at a Rho utilization (*rut*) site. These classes are useful, but in *Bacillus subtilis* Rho can assist some intrinsic terminators.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/transcription/intrinsic-vs-rho-termination.webp" width={1200} height={604} alt="AI-generated illustration: two panels compare bacterial termination. On the left the RNA folds into a hairpin above a run of U residues beside the polymerase; on the right a Rho ring sits at the rut site on the RNA and moves toward the polymerase." caption="An intrinsic hairpin and U tract can destabilize a bacterial elongation complex, whereas Rho binds nascent RNA and advances toward polymerase to promote release." />

Eukaryotic polymerases do not share one termination pathway. At the second film's Pol II stop, CPSF73 cuts the RNA and XRN2 follows, degrading the RNA still attached to Pol II from 5′ to 3′. In the **torpedo model**, XRN2 catches the polymerase and knocks it off the DNA; in the **allosteric model**, copying the signal changes the complex so it is ready to stop. Rapid factor-removal experiments in cultured human cells support a combination: slowing beyond the poly(A) site lets XRN2 catch up. Pol III stops at a T run in the nontemplate strand, although other features can assist in budding yeast.

## Design problem: identify the polymerase

A newly found mammalian gene makes an RNA of unknown type. Design an experiment to decide which nuclear RNA polymerase transcribes it.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Run a **nuclear run-on assay**: isolate nuclei, supply labeled nucleotides and measure extension by polymerases already on genes. Titrate alpha-amanitin from none through roughly 1 µg/mL to a few hundred µg/mL; these are orders of magnitude for mammalian cells and must be checked for the cell type.

Measure labeled RNA from the unknown gene beside an rRNA control for Pol I, a protein-coding control such as actin for Pol II, and a tRNA or 5S rRNA control for Pol III. The Pol II control should fall around 1 µg/mL or less, the Pol III control only at roughly 100 to 1000 times more, and the Pol I control should resist across the range. Match the unknown curve to a control. The same titration can be done by in vitro transcription in nuclear extract with the gene as template. Then confirm it independently (Going deeper: Confirming a polymerase assignment). Measure RNA, not protein, because Pol I and Pol III products are not translated.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Confirming a polymerase assignment

Map the start with a method designed for 5′ ends of newly made RNA; ordinary RNA-seq is not precise enough, and mature tRNA and rRNA ends may have been trimmed. Inspect upstream DNA, and the gene body for a suspected Pol III gene, for matching promoter elements. Then place a tagged copy with its upstream region and any internal promoter elements into cells and test whether its RNA has the same alpha-amanitin response.

### Initiation details and exceptions

The −10 element is the Pribnow box, named for David Pribnow, who in 1975 reported the sequence of a polymerase-binding site at an early phage T7 promoter. Budding-yeast start sites lie farther from a TATA box than animal start sites, about 40 to 120 bp downstream. Human Pol II has 52 CTD repeats and budding yeast 26.

### Termination details and overlap

In an atlas of *Bacillus subtilis* terminators, Rho stimulated about 10% of intrinsic terminators. In budding yeast, a T run is not always sufficient for Pol III termination; RNA structure and the helicase Sen1 can assist.
