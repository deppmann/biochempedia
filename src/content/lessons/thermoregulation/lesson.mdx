techniques:
  - name: "Indirect calorimetry (respirometry)"
    blurb: >-
      Place the animal in a chamber with a known airflow and measure how much oxygen it takes up
      and how much carbon dioxide it gives off. Oxygen use converts to energy use, about 20 kJ per
      litre of oxygen depending on the fuel, so this gives metabolic rate without measuring heat
      directly.
  - name: "Implanted temperature telemetry"
    blurb: >-
      Implant a small transmitter that reports internal body temperature by radio while the animal
      moves freely. Recorded at the same time as calorimetry, it shows whether metabolism and body
      temperature change together or apart, the evidence behind the mouse thermoneutral-point and
      bear hibernation studies.
  - name: "Holding infected animals at fixed temperatures"
    blurb: >-
      Infect animals in the same way, then hold groups at different constant temperatures so they
      cannot choose their own, and compare survival. It tests whether a raised temperature changes
      the outcome, though by itself it does not show which process in host or microbe changed.
  - name: "Whole-body thermal conductance from calorimetry"
    blurb: >-
      Divide an endotherm's measured heat production by the difference between its body and air
      temperatures, below its thermoneutral zone and with body temperature steady. The result, in watts per kelvin, is how readily
      heat leaks out, and it lets you compare insulation across animals or predict the cost of
      colder air.

      - "Heat made plus heat gained equals the heat each one loses"
      - "Each loses heat only by evaporation from its skin or mouth"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "A steady temperature means no heat is being stored or drawn down, so heat in, made by metabolism plus gained from sun, rock or air, matches heat out. Making most of its heat from its own metabolism describes the mouse, an endotherm, but not the iguana, which takes much of its heat from sun and rock. A basking iguana can be warmer than the air, and a mouse is warmer than the air in its cage, so neither body temperature equals the air's. Evaporation is only one of four routes; radiation, conduction and convection also move heat, and they can run either way."

      - "0.30 W"
      - "0.74 W"
      - "0.20 W"
      - "0.44 W"
    answer: 0
    rationale: "Heat production equals conductance times the body-to-air difference: 0.020 W per K x (37 - 22) K = 0.020 x 15 = 0.30 W.       - "Daily torpor, a controlled bout of low metabolism lasting under a day"
      - "Passive cooling, since its thermostat has briefly stopped working"
      - "Hibernation, since its temperature falls well below the normal value"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "Daily torpor is a controlled reduction of metabolic rate and body temperature that conventionally lasts under 24 hours, as here. Here is the first film's tank rule in a form you can calculate with.

> **The one idea.** At a steady body temperature, heat in equals heat out: **heat made (M) + heat gained (G) = conductance (C) × (body temperature − air temperature) + heat evaporated (E)**, where conductance is how readily heat leaks out per degree of body-to-air difference. So body temperature sits above the air by (M + G − E) ÷ C. An animal steers its temperature by changing one of those terms, and a controller in the brain decides which temperature to aim for.
>
> *Scope, for the whole lesson:* a simple model. Steady state means body temperature is not changing, so no heat is stored. Radiation, conduction and convection are lumped into one dry leak, C × (Tb − Ta): Tb is body temperature, and Ta, "the air" for short, is one effective temperature for the surroundings the animal exchanges dry heat with. Each heat load counts once: absorbed sunlight goes in G, and in this lesson so does a warm rock underfoot, so Ta stands for the rest of the surroundings. A chamber has one temperature only if its air and walls are held at the same temperature. Real conductance changes with posture, fur and blood flow; where later numbers hold C, Tb or E fixed, that is the model, not a measurement.

C is in watts per kelvin (a kelvin of difference equals a degree Celsius). **Metabolic rate** is the energy an animal uses per unit time, in watts (joules per second) or kilojoules per day; nearly all of it ends up as heat, so at rest it is M.

## Routes and dials the first film left out

Of the four routes the first film named, radiation (electromagnetic energy), conduction (surfaces in contact), convection (moving air or water) and evaporation, it left out that each one's share depends on species, size, posture and weather; evaporation's water cost is in [osmoregulation](/lessons/osmoregulation/).

Three dials the first film did not show:

- **Skin blood flow.** Narrowing the small arteries to the skin (**vasoconstriction**) keeps warm blood in the core; widening them (**vasodilation**) delivers heat to the surface (see [blood vessels, blood and lymph](/lessons/blood-and-vessels/)). Both change C, as do posture, huddling, fur and feathers, which trap a layer of still air, and blubber in many marine mammals.
- **Countercurrent exchange.** In a bird's leg, or a whale's or seal's flipper, warm blood going out passes its heat to cold blood coming back, so the heat returns to the core instead of leaking from the foot (figure).
- **Moving.** Seeking shade or burrowing changes Ta and G at once.

## Endotherms, ectotherms and a second axis

An **endotherm**, such as a mammal or bird, warms itself mainly with M, held in by a low C; an **ectotherm**, like most fish, amphibians, reptiles and invertebrates, takes its temperature mainly from Ta and G. Either can use both sources, and the mix shifts with activity and weather.

Constancy is a separate axis. A **homeotherm** holds a nearly constant body temperature, and a **heterotherm** lets it vary widely. A deep-sea fish in steady cold is a nearly constant ectotherm; a hibernating ground squirrel, whose temperature can fall to near freezing, is a heterothermic endotherm.

**The desert iguana, Dipsosaurus dorsalis**, lives in the deserts of the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico. It regulates its temperature by behaviour, shuttling between sun and shade or retreating into a burrow, and when active it can hold a body temperature near or above 37 degrees C, often near 40 degrees C. **The laboratory mouse, Mus musculus**, is a small endotherm that loses heat fast for its mass.

**Predict.** Night falls and the sun is gone. An iguana and a mouse sit in the same 30 degree C air. Using the balance, which stays well above the air, and why?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The mouse. With G gone, the iguana's only inflow is its small M, so (M + G − E) ÷ C shrinks to almost nothing. In invented numbers, with E = 0, C = 0.05 W per K and M = 0.02 W, sunshine worth G = 0.48 W holds it at 40 degrees C in 30 degree surroundings; with no sun it settles toward 30.4 degrees C. The mouse's large M, held in by fur, keeps it near 37 degrees C.

</details>

Not every fish is an ectotherm. Tunas and lamnid sharks keep some muscles warm, and **the opah, Lampris guttatus**, a disc-shaped ocean fish, warms its whole body (figure; Going deeper).

Some insects are partly endothermic too: bumblebees and many moths warm their flight muscles before take-off by a kind of shivering.

## Sensing and control: what the controller does

Behind the first film's controller: in mammals, temperature-sensing nerve cells in the skin report the surroundings, and those in the brain, spinal cord and abdominal organs report the **core**, the part whose temperature is defended. The controller, centred in the **preoptic area** of the [hypothalamus](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/), weighs these signals against its **set point**, the temperature it aims for ([tissues, organs and homeostasis](/lessons/tissues-and-homeostasis/) introduced set points).

**Feedback** responses start when the core moves away from its defended range, as when exercise heats the body. **Feed-forward** responses start from the skin before the core has changed, like pulling your sleeves down the moment cold air touches your arms.

Behaviour tends to come before the costlier physiological responses. In the cold, a mammal seeks warmth or huddles, then narrows its skin arteries and makes more heat. In the warm, it widens its skin vessels, then turns heat production down and evaporates water: humans and horses sweat, dogs and many birds pant, and rodents spread saliva on their fur.

Two responses raise M. **Shivering** is involuntary, rhythmic contraction of opposing muscles at the same time, so little external work is done and the energy appears as heat. Many mammals also have **brown adipose tissue**, whose mitochondria carry the protein UCP1, which lets protons leak back across the inner membrane so food energy becomes heat instead of ATP (see [the proton-motive force](/lessons/proton-motive-force/)). The same controller switches on both. Ectotherms run the same kind of loop mainly through behaviour, changing G.

Shivering and brown fat burn fuel. As the air cools, when does a mouse start paying, and how much? The second film measures it.

## The cost of keeping warm: numbers on the curve

The second film drew the curve; here are its numbers, the hot end and how real conductance drifts. One reminder of the rule: with no sun (G = 0) and evaporation neglected, though breath and skin always lose some water, heat made = C × (Tb − Ta).

The second film's oxygen gauge is **indirect calorimetry**: each litre of oxygen corresponds to roughly 20 kJ, depending on the fuel (see [integration of metabolism](/lessons/integration-of-metabolism/)). The flat level is the **basal metabolic rate (BMR)**, measured in an endotherm that is awake, resting, **postabsorptive** (not digesting a meal) and within its **thermoneutral zone**, the range of air temperatures over which it spends no extra energy on heating or cooling. That flat zone is the textbook picture; mouse measurements complicate it (Going deeper).

Take an invented mouse-like endotherm: body 37 degrees C, BMR 0.25 W, and a conductance it can set anywhere from 0.10 W per K (vessels open, fur flat) down to a minimum of 0.035 W per K. For each air temperature, ask: what conductance would leak exactly the basal heat, BMR ÷ (Tb − Ta)? Inside the animal's range, it stays basal; below its minimum, it must make more heat.

| Air (degrees C) | Conductance that would shed exactly BMR (W per K) | Heat made (W) |
|---|---|---|
| 35 | 0.125, more than its widest 0.10 | 0.25 if evaporating cost nothing; evaporation must remove at least 0.05 W |
| 33 | 0.063 | 0.25, basal |
| 30 | 0.036, just inside its range | 0.25, basal |
| 25 | 0.021, below its minimum | 0.42 |
| 21 | 0.016, below its minimum | 0.56, about 2.2 × BMR |

The bend falls where the needed conductance reaches the minimum: lower critical temperature = Tb − BMR ÷ Cmin = 37 − 0.25 ÷ 0.035, about 29.9 degrees C, close to the measured mouse thermoneutral point of 29 to 33 degrees C (see Going deeper), so animal rooms near 20 to 22 degrees C are cool for a mouse. At the warm end, even the widest C, 0.10 W per K, can shed BMR only across a gap of 2.5 K, so above about 34.5 degrees C dry loss alone falls short: evaporation must grow, or body temperature must rise. That is a threshold of the dry model, not the upper critical temperature, where active heat loss such as panting starts to raise metabolic rate; the model leaves out that cost.

**Predict.** The second film showed that thicker fur moves the bend to colder air. By how much? Suppose, in round illustrative numbers, an arctic fox and a tropical mammal have the same basal rate and a body temperature near 37 degrees C, and the fox's curve bends near −40 degrees C while the tropical animal's bends near +27 degrees C. How many times lower must the fox's minimum conductance be?

The bend sits where the smallest C can just carry away the basal heat: BMR = Cmin × (Tb − lower critical temperature). The gaps are about 77 K and 10 K, so the fox's Cmin is roughly 7 to 8 times lower.

</details>

The second film's straight cold limb, steepness C, aimed at zero at Tb, is the model; exam problems use it unless told otherwise. Real animals keep lowering conductance a little as the air cools, by curling up, fluffing fur and narrowing skin vessels, and body temperature can drift, so measured cold limbs are often shallower and need not reach zero exactly at Tb. Steepness equals conductance only if both stay roughly constant.

**Predict.** In a shaded chamber, the air and walls reach 40 degrees C, warmer than the mouse's 37. Which terms of the balance can still carry heat out?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only evaporation. With the whole surroundings at 40 degrees C, the dry leak has turned negative: radiation, conduction and convection bring heat in, so E must remove the metabolic heat plus everything the surroundings add. (If only the air were hot, the animal could still lose heat to a cooler sky or ground.) Above the **upper critical temperature**, metabolic rate rises again, partly because panting takes work and probably because a warmer body runs its chemistry faster.

</details>

**Worked problem.** The numbers here are invented for practice. A small endotherm holds its body temperature at 37 degrees C with a constant conductance C = 0.020 W per K. Assume steady state (no heat stored), no external work, and air temperatures below its lower critical temperature, so heat production equals heat loss:

> **heat production (W) = C (W per K) x (body temperature - air temperature) (K)**

1. At 27 degrees C the difference is 10 K. Heat production = 0.020 W per K x 10 K = 0.20 W.
2. At 17 degrees C the difference is 20 K. Heat production = 0.020 W per K x 20 K = 0.40 W.
3. Extra power: 0.40 W - 0.20 W = 0.20 W. Check: 0.020 W per K x (27 - 17) K = 0.20 W.
4. Extra energy in a day: 0.20 J per s x 86,400 s = 17,280 J, or 17.28 kJ.
5. Assume fat yields about 39 kJ per g. Then 17.28 kJ / 39 kJ per g = about 0.44 g of fat per day.

The cost doubled only because, with C fixed, these numbers double the gap; 10 degrees of cooling does not generally double metabolism. Fluff the fur so C falls to 0.010 W per K, and the cost at 17 degrees C drops back to 0.20 W.

## Fever, torpor and hibernation: changing the target

**Predict.** At the start of a fever the target rises, say from 37 to 39 degrees C, but the body is still at 37. Does the patient feel hot or cold, and what does the body do?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Cold. Body temperature is now below the target, so the controller acts as if the body were chilled: it conserves heat and makes more, for example by shivering, until it reaches the new level.

</details>

**Fever** is regulation of body temperature at a raised level. In mammals, signals from an infection lead the cells lining brain blood vessels to make prostaglandin, which acts on the brain's temperature-control region and raises the temperature it defends. [Innate immunity](/lessons/innate-immunity/) follows the immune side.

Ectotherms have fever too, by behaviour: infected desert iguanas offered a choice of temperatures settled about 2 degrees C warmer. Does that help? In 1975 Matthew Kluger and colleagues held infected iguanas each at one fixed temperature, so they could not choose, and survival was greater at the higher temperatures than at the lowest: a benefit of warmth in this lizard with this infection, though survival alone does not show what the warmth changed.

**Torpor** is a controlled drop in metabolic rate and body temperature in a bird or mammal. **Daily torpor** conventionally lasts under 24 hours; fasted laboratory mice housed at 22 degrees C enter it, cooling for hours and then rewarming. **Hibernation** is prolonged, usually seasonal torpor. In small hibernators such as ground squirrels it is a series of torpor bouts, each lasting days to several weeks, broken by periodic arousals that are costly in energy, and body temperature can fall to near freezing. Black bears hibernate differently: body temperature stays at roughly 30 to 36 degrees C for months, with no periodic arousals.

Siniša Hrvatin and colleagues (2020) found that reactivating neurons in the hypothalamic preoptic area brought on key features of torpor in mice that were not food-restricted, and that inhibiting an identified group of them disrupted natural torpor. In black bears, metabolism falls to about a quarter of basal at those temperatures, far more of a fall than that modest cooling explains, and it stays low after body temperature recovers (worked through in Going deeper). That points to active metabolic suppression.

### Design problem

Does the body wait for its core to cool? A mouse is moved from warm air into cool air. Within minutes its skin blood vessels narrow and it starts making extra heat, before a thermometer deep in its body shows any fall. Which sensors could have started the response? Design an experiment that would tell sensing in the skin apart from sensing in the core and brain.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The skin. The core had not changed, so core feedback cannot have started the response; skin sensors drove a feed-forward response through the preoptic area.

To test it, change the temperature of one site while holding the others steady, and record skin blood flow and heat production. Cool the skin while keeping the core warm: if skin sensing drives the response, the skin vessels narrow and heat production rises anyway. Then warm or cool a small region of the preoptic area alone while the skin and air stay steady. Experiments of this kind have shown that local cooling or warming of the preoptic area brings on coordinated cold-defence or heat-defence responses, and that the skin, spinal cord, abdominal organs and brain all provide input that the controller sums.

One caution. Removing a single kind of sensor may not stop regulation. Mice with the gene for the cold sensor TRPM8 disrupted largely compensate and keep core temperature in a broadly normal range, because the system has several overlapping inputs. A normal body temperature after a knockout does not show that the sensor plays no part.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Measuring metabolic rate: conditions and scaling

**Standard metabolic rate (SMR)** is the ectotherm's equivalent of BMR: resting, postabsorptive, and at a stated temperature, because its rate changes with temperature. In threespine sticklebacks acclimated to 5, 12 or 20 degrees C for 7 to 21 weeks, SMR rose with acclimation temperature. **Daily energy expenditure** is everything over 24 hours: activity, digestion, heating, growth. The work of exercise adds to it but is not part of BMR, which by definition is measured at rest. Daily expenditure can be far above the resting rate: 3.1 times the thermoneutral resting rate, on average, in single-housed male mice kept at 21 degrees C (Fischer, Cannon and Nedergaard, 2018).

Across mammals and birds, whole-body BMR rises more slowly than body mass, so a gram of mouse spends far more than a gram of elephant. The rise is often written as roughly the 3/4 power of mass (Kleiber's relation). That exponent is a fitted value, some analyses find nearer 2/3, and its cause is unsettled; see [size and scaling](/lessons/size-and-scaling/).

### The mouse's thermoneutral point

Škop and colleagues (2020), recording metabolic rate and body temperature together in laboratory mice, found no range of air temperature where both stayed constant, as the textbook flat zone assumes, and placed the mouse's thermoneutral point near 29 degrees C in the light phase and 33 degrees C in the dark.

### Where the balance stops: stored heat

A lizard warming on a morning rock is storing heat, so the steady-state balance gives only where its warming curve levels off, not how fast it gets there.

### Who described the opah

In 2015 Nicholas Wegner and colleagues showed that heat from its pectoral-fin muscles is kept by countercurrent exchangers in its gills, so blood leaving the gills is not cooled all the way to water temperature as in most fish. There the heat passes between the arteries carrying blood into and out of the gills.

### Fever: the evidence in detail

The vessel-lining source of the prostaglandin was traced in mice given bacterial lipopolysaccharide into a vein.

In the 1975 lizard study, Matthew Kluger, D. H. Ringler and M. R. Anver held each infected iguana at one of five air temperatures, 34, 36, 38, 40 or 42 degrees C. The result does not transfer automatically to mammals or other infections. The paper's own test of bacterial growth in culture found it similar from 34 to 40 degrees C, which pointed to the host's defences rather than direct harm to the bacteria, but the host change was not identified.

### Is hibernation just cooling? A worked estimate

Use illustrative numbers in the pattern of Tøien and colleagues (2011): a black bear's metabolic rate falls to 25% of basal while its body temperature falls from 37 to 33 degrees C. Assume a **Q10** (the factor by which a rate changes per 10 degree C change in temperature) of 2.5. How much of the fall could cooling explain, and what measurement would separate cooling from active suppression?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Passive cooling predicts:

> **predicted fraction of basal = Q10 ^ ((T - T0) / 10) = 2.5 ^ (-4 / 10) = 2.5 ^ (-0.4)**

ln 2.5 = 0.916; times -0.4 gives -0.367; e ^ (-0.367) = 0.69. Cooling alone predicts 69% of basal. The bear is at 25%, so a further factor of 0.25 / 0.69 = 0.36 is left unexplained by temperature: most of the suppression is something other than cooling.

To separate the two, record oxygen consumption and body temperature together, continuously, through hibernation and emergence. If metabolism simply followed temperature, it would recover with it. In the real bears it stayed low for up to three weeks after body temperature had returned to normal and they had emerged, which separates the two in time.

The Q10 of 2.5 is an assumption; there is no universal Q10. And a bear's answer does not transfer to a ground squirrel that cools near 0 degrees C, where the much larger temperature fall may account for more of the drop, though small hibernators also suppress metabolism actively.

</details>
