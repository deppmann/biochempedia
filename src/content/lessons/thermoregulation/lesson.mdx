techniques:
  - name: "Indirect calorimetry (respirometry)"
    blurb: >-
      Place the animal in a chamber with a known airflow and measure how much oxygen it takes up
      and how much carbon dioxide it gives off. Oxygen use converts to energy use, about 20 kJ per
      litre of oxygen depending on the fuel, so this gives metabolic rate without measuring heat
      directly.
  - name: "Implanted temperature telemetry"
    blurb: >-
      Implant a small transmitter that reports internal body temperature by radio while the animal
      moves freely. Recorded at the same time as calorimetry, it shows whether metabolism and body
      temperature change together or apart, the evidence behind the mouse thermoneutral-point and
      bear hibernation studies.
  - name: "Holding infected animals at fixed temperatures"
    blurb: >-
      Infect animals in the same way, then hold groups at different constant temperatures so they
      cannot choose their own, and compare survival. It tests whether a raised temperature changes
      the outcome, though by itself it does not show which process in host or microbe changed.
  - name: "Whole-body thermal conductance from calorimetry"
    blurb: >-
      Divide an endotherm's measured heat production by the difference between its body and air
      temperatures, below its thermoneutral zone and with body temperature steady. The result, in watts per kelvin, is how readily
      heat leaks out, and it lets you compare insulation across animals or predict the cost of
      colder air.

**An animal's body temperature is set by a balance: heat made by its own metabolism, plus heat gained from the sun, a rock, air or water, against heat lost to its surroundings.** Animals do not simply accept that balance. They sense temperature in the skin and deep in the body, and a controller, in mammals centred in the hypothalamus, adjusts heat production, heat loss and behaviour to hold the temperature it defends. That is how a desert iguana basking on a rock can end up warmer than you are: "cold-blooded" and "warm-blooded" describe where the heat comes from, not how warm the animal is. And because the controller can move the temperature it defends, fever, torpor and hibernation are regulated changes, not a broken thermostat.

## Heat in, heat out: the balance that sets body temperature

**Metabolic rate** is the energy an animal uses per unit time, in watts (joules per second) or kilojoules per day. Nearly all of that energy ends up as heat. That metabolic heat is one input. The other is heat gained from outside: sunlight, a warm rock, warm air or water. Against both runs heat loss. When heat made plus heat gained equals heat lost, body temperature holds steady. When they do not match, it rises or falls.

Heat moves by four routes.

- **Radiation**: emission and absorption of electromagnetic energy, as when a lizard absorbs sunlight.
- **Conduction**: transfer between surfaces in direct contact, as from a sun-warmed rock into a lizard's belly.
- **Convection**: transfer by moving air or water, as wind carries heat from skin warmer than the air.
- **Evaporation**: water turning to vapour takes heat with it, as in sweating in humans and horses or panting in dogs and many birds.

The first three run either way, depending on which side is warmer. Evaporation only removes heat, and it costs water (see [osmoregulation](/lessons/osmoregulation/)). Each route's share depends on species, size, posture and weather.

Animals adjust these routes with a few dials.

- **Insulation.** Fur and feathers trap still air; many marine mammals rely on blubber.
- **Skin blood flow.** Narrowing the small arteries to the skin (vasoconstriction) keeps warm blood in the core; widening them (vasodilation) delivers heat to the surface. (See [blood vessels, blood and lymph](/lessons/blood-and-vessels/).)
- **Countercurrent exchange.** When an artery carrying warm blood out to a limb runs alongside the vein bringing cool blood back, heat passes across, so much of it returns to the core. The legs of many birds and the flippers of whales and seals work this way. The opah, a fish met later in this lesson, uses the same principle in its gills, but there the heat passes between the arteries carrying blood into and out of the gills.
- **Evaporative cooling**, by sweating, panting or licking.
- **Behaviour**: basking, seeking shade, huddling, burrowing.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/thermoregulation/countercurrent-limb-exchange.webp" width={1200} height={529} alt="AI-generated illustration: a schematic limb with two tubes side by side. The artery carries warm blood from the body core toward the tip; the vein carries cooler blood back toward the core in the opposite direction. Short gold arrows cross from artery to vein showing heat passing across." caption="In countercurrent exchange, an artery carrying warm blood out to a limb runs alongside the vein bringing cool blood back, so heat passes across and much of it returns to the core." />

Every one of these dials changes heat gained or heat lost. The next question is what turns them.

## Sensing and control: how animals steer the balance

Animals do not wait for heat to balance by chance. They measure temperature and respond. In mammals, temperature-sensing nerve cells sit in two kinds of place. Sensors in the skin report the temperature of the surroundings. Sensors deep in the body, in the brain, the spinal cord and the abdominal organs, report the temperature of the **core**, the part whose temperature is defended. Both feed a controller centred in the **preoptic area** of the [hypothalamus](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/), which weighs the signals against the temperature it defends and drives the responses.

**Feedback** responses start when the core moves away from its defended range, as when exercise heats the body. **Feed-forward** responses start from the skin: cold air on the skin can trigger heat-saving responses before the core has cooled at all.

In the cold, a mammal usually changes its behaviour first, seeking warmth or huddling. It narrows the arteries to its skin, keeping warm blood in the core, and it makes more heat. In the warm, it widens its skin blood vessels, turns heat production down, and loses heat by evaporation: humans sweat, dogs pant, and rodents spread saliva on their fur. Behaviour tends to come before the costlier physiological responses, and in the heat, widening of skin vessels starts before sweating.

Two responses make extra heat. **Shivering** is rapid, unsynchronized muscle contraction that releases heat. Many mammals also have **brown adipose tissue**, whose mitochondria carry the protein UCP1, which lets protons leak back across the inner membrane so food energy becomes heat instead of ATP (see [the proton-motive force](/lessons/proton-motive-force/)). Both are switched on by the same controller.

Ectotherms run the same kind of loop mainly through behaviour, as when a lizard shuttles between sun and shade. The rest of this lesson is this loop at work: what it costs in the cold, and what happens when the defended temperature itself moves.

## Where the heat comes from: endotherms and ectotherms

An **endotherm**, such as a mammal or bird, warms itself mainly with heat from its own metabolism, held in by insulation. An **ectotherm** takes its body temperature mainly from outside: sun, warm rock, water. Most fish, amphibians, reptiles and invertebrates are ectotherms. A **homeotherm** holds a nearly constant body temperature, and a **heterotherm** lets it vary widely. Where the heat comes from and how constant the temperature is are separate questions: a deep-sea fish in steady cold is a nearly constant ectotherm, and a hibernating ground squirrel a heterothermic endotherm.

**The desert iguana, Dipsosaurus dorsalis**, is an ectotherm of the deserts of the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico. It regulates its temperature by behaviour, shuttling between sun and shade or retreating into a burrow, and through the active day it can hold a body temperature near or above 37 degrees C. Ectotherm names the heat source, not a cold animal.

**The laboratory mouse, Mus musculus**, is a small endotherm that makes its own heat and holds it in with fur. Being small, it loses heat fast relative to its mass, which matters for the cost of cold in the next section.

**The opah, Lampris guttatus**, shows that not every fish is an ectotherm. Tunas and lamnid sharks keep some muscles warm, and the opah, a disc-shaped ocean fish, warms its whole body with heat from continuous movement of its pectoral fins and limits heat loss with countercurrent heat exchangers in its gills. In most fish, blood leaves the gills at water temperature.

Some insects are partly endothermic too: bumblebees and many moths warm their flight muscles before take-off by a kind of shivering.

## The cost of keeping warm: metabolic rate and the thermoneutral zone

In **indirect calorimetry**, or respirometry, an animal sits in a chamber with a known airflow, and you record the oxygen it takes up and the carbon dioxide it gives off. Each litre of oxygen consumed corresponds to roughly 20 kJ, depending on the fuel burned (see [integration of metabolism](/lessons/integration-of-metabolism/)).

A rate means little without its conditions.

- **Basal metabolic rate (BMR)** is measured in an endotherm that is awake, resting, **postabsorptive** (not digesting a meal) and within its **thermoneutral zone**, the range of air temperatures over which it spends no extra energy on heating or cooling.
- **Standard metabolic rate (SMR)** is the equivalent for an ectotherm: resting, postabsorptive, and at a stated temperature, because its rate changes with temperature.
- **Daily energy expenditure** is everything over 24 hours: activity, digestion, heating, growth.

The work of exercise adds to daily expenditure but is not part of BMR, which by definition is measured at rest.

Across mammals and birds, whole-body BMR rises more slowly than body mass, so a gram of mouse spends far more than a gram of elephant; see [size and scaling](/lessons/size-and-scaling/).

Now plot an endotherm's resting metabolic rate against air temperature. In the textbook plot, the line is flat within the thermoneutral zone, at BMR (measurements in mice complicate this; see Going deeper). Below the **lower critical temperature** it climbs. Above the upper critical temperature it rises again, as panting and sweating take work. If body temperature and conductance stay roughly constant, the steepness of the cold limb (the size of its slope) equals the animal's **thermal conductance**, how readily heat leaks out per degree of difference. In 1950 Per Scholander, Laurence Irving and colleagues found that thick-furred arctic mammals stay on the flat part into much colder air than tropical ones, mainly because their insulation lowers conductance.

The climb below the lower critical temperature is the control loop at work. As the air cools, heat leaks out faster, and the controller raises heat production, by shivering and brown fat, to match it.

A small endotherm such as the laboratory mouse loses heat fast relative to its mass, so below its thermoneutral zone its expenditure rises steeply as the air cools. Its thermoneutral point lies near 29 to 33 degrees C (see Going deeper), so animal rooms near 20 to 22 degrees C are cool for a mouse.

**Worked problem.** The numbers here are invented for practice. A small endotherm holds its body temperature at 37 degrees C with a constant conductance C = 0.020 W per K. Assume steady state (no heat stored), no external work, and air temperatures below its lower critical temperature, so heat production equals heat loss:

> **heat production (W) = C (W per K) x (body temperature - air temperature) (K)**

1. At 27 degrees C the difference is 10 K. Heat production = 0.020 W per K x 10 K = 0.20 W.
2. At 17 degrees C the difference is 20 K. Heat production = 0.020 W per K x 20 K = 0.40 W.
3. Extra power: 0.40 W - 0.20 W = 0.20 W. Check: 0.020 W per K x (27 - 17) K = 0.20 W.
4. Extra energy in a day: 0.20 J per s x 86,400 s = 17,280 J, or 17.28 kJ.
5. Assume fat yields about 39 kJ per g. Then 17.28 kJ / 39 kJ per g = about 0.44 g of fat per day.

The cost doubled only because these numbers double the gradient with C fixed; 10 degrees of cooling does not generally double metabolism. Fluff the fur so C falls to 0.010 W per K, and the cost at 17 degrees C drops back to 0.20 W.

## Fever, torpor and hibernation are regulated

A **set point** is the value a control system defends, and in fever, torpor and hibernation the defended value shifts ([tissues, organs and homeostasis](/lessons/tissues-and-homeostasis/) introduced set points).

**Fever** is regulation of body temperature at a raised level. In mammals, signals from an infection lead the cells lining brain blood vessels to make prostaglandin, which acts on the brain's temperature-control region and raises the temperature it defends. [Innate immunity](/lessons/innate-immunity/) follows the immune side. The body then conserves heat and makes more, for example by shivering, until it reaches the new level.

Ectotherms have fever too, by behaviour: infected desert iguanas offered a choice of temperatures settled about 2 degrees C warmer. Does that help? In 1975 Matthew Kluger and colleagues injected desert iguanas with live bacteria and held each lizard at one fixed air temperature, so it could not choose. If fever were a harmless side effect, survival should not depend on temperature. Instead survival was greater at the higher temperatures than at the lowest. That shows a survival benefit of warmth in this lizard with this infection, though survival alone does not show what the warmth changed.

**Torpor** is a controlled drop in metabolic rate and body temperature in a bird or mammal. **Daily torpor** conventionally lasts under 24 hours; fasted laboratory mice housed at 22 degrees C enter it, cooling for hours and then rewarming. **Hibernation** is prolonged, usually seasonal torpor. In small hibernators such as ground squirrels it is a series of torpor bouts, each lasting days to several weeks, broken by periodic arousals that are costly in energy, and body temperature can fall to near freezing. Black bears hibernate differently: body temperature stays at roughly 30 to 36 degrees C for months, with no periodic arousals.

Torpor is controlled. In mice, Siniša Hrvatin and colleagues (2020) found that reactivating neurons in the hypothalamic preoptic area brought on key features of torpor in mice that were not food-restricted, and that inhibiting an identified group of them disrupted natural torpor. In black bears, metabolism falls to about a quarter of basal while body temperature stays in that 30 to 36 degree range, far more of a fall than that modest cooling explains (worked through in Going deeper).

### Design problem

Does the body wait for its core to cool? A mouse is moved from warm air into cool air. Within minutes its skin blood vessels narrow and it starts making extra heat, before a thermometer deep in its body shows any fall. Which sensors could have started the response? Design an experiment that would tell sensing in the skin apart from sensing in the core and brain.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The timing points to the skin. The core had not changed, so a feedback signal from core sensors cannot have started the response. Skin sensors detect the cooler air and drive a feed-forward response through the preoptic area.

To test it, change the temperature of one site while holding the others steady, and record skin blood flow and heat production. Cool the skin while keeping the core warm: if skin sensing drives the response, the skin vessels narrow and heat production rises anyway. Then warm or cool a small region of the preoptic area alone while the skin and air stay steady. Experiments of this kind have shown that local cooling or warming of the preoptic area brings on coordinated cold-defence or heat-defence responses, and that the skin, spinal cord, abdominal organs and brain all provide input that the controller sums.

One caution. Removing a single kind of sensor may not stop regulation. Mice with the gene for the cold sensor TRPM8 disrupted largely compensate and keep core temperature in a broadly normal range, because the system has several overlapping inputs. A normal body temperature after a knockout does not show that the sensor plays no part.

## Going deeper

### Measuring metabolic rate: conditions and scaling

A standard metabolic rate must name its temperature. In threespine sticklebacks acclimated to 5, 12 or 20 degrees C for 7 to 21 weeks, SMR rose with acclimation temperature. Daily expenditure can be far above the resting rate: in single-housed male laboratory mice (Fischer, Cannon and Nedergaard, 2018), mean 24-hour expenditure was 3.1 times the thermoneutral resting rate in mice kept at 21 degrees C.

The rise of BMR with body mass is often written as roughly the 3/4 power of mass (Kleiber's relation). That exponent is a fitted value, some analyses find nearer 2/3, and its cause is unsettled; see [size and scaling](/lessons/size-and-scaling/).

### The mouse's thermoneutral point

The textbook plot has a flat zone where metabolism is basal and body temperature constant. Škop and colleagues (2020), recording metabolic rate and body temperature together in laboratory mice, found no range of air temperature where both stayed constant, and placed the mouse's thermoneutral point near 29 degrees C in the light phase and 33 degrees C in the dark. Either way, ordinary animal rooms are below it.

### Who described the opah

The opah's whole-body endothermy was reported in 2015 by Nicholas Wegner and colleagues, who showed that heat from its pectoral-fin muscles is kept by countercurrent exchangers in its gills, so blood leaving the gills is not cooled all the way to water temperature as in most fish.

### Fever: the evidence in detail

The prostaglandin signal was traced in mice given bacterial lipopolysaccharide into a vein: prostaglandin made by the cells lining brain blood vessels supplied the fever signal.

In the 1975 lizard study, Matthew Kluger, D. H. Ringler and M. R. Anver held each infected iguana at one of five air temperatures, 34, 36, 38, 40 or 42 degrees C. The result applies to this lizard with this infection, not automatically to mammals or other infections. The paper's own test of bacterial growth in culture found it similar from 34 to 40 degrees C, which pointed to the host's defences rather than direct harm to the bacteria, but the host change was not identified.

### Is hibernation just cooling? A worked estimate

Is hibernation just cooling? Use illustrative numbers in the pattern of Tøien and colleagues (2011): a black bear's metabolic rate falls to 25% of basal while its body temperature falls from 37 to 33 degrees C. Assume a **Q10** (the factor by which a rate changes per 10 degree C change in temperature) of 2.5. How much of the fall could cooling explain, and what measurement would separate cooling from active suppression?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Passive cooling predicts:

> **predicted fraction of basal = Q10 ^ ((T - T0) / 10) = 2.5 ^ (-4 / 10) = 2.5 ^ (-0.4)**

ln 2.5 = 0.916; times -0.4 gives -0.367; e ^ (-0.367) = 0.69. Cooling alone predicts 69% of basal. The bear is at 25%, so a further factor of 0.25 / 0.69 = 0.36 is left unexplained by temperature: most of the suppression is something other than cooling.

To separate the two, record oxygen consumption and body temperature together, continuously, through hibernation and emergence. If metabolism simply followed temperature, it would recover when temperature does. In the real bears it stayed low for up to three weeks after body temperature had returned to normal and they had emerged, which separates the two in time.

The Q10 of 2.5 is an assumption; there is no universal Q10. And a bear's answer does not transfer to a ground squirrel that cools near 0 degrees C, where the much larger temperature fall may account for more of the drop, though small hibernators also suppress metabolism actively.

</details>
