## Explain: ATP carries energy to where it is used

> **The one idea.** A cell's jobs, from a muscle pulling to a nerve cell pumping charged particles to building proteins, run on ATP, not on glucose directly. The cell rebuilds ATP from ADP with energy from taking glucose apart. A glucose taken all the way down to carbon dioxide and water, with oxygen, rebuilds about 30 ATP. When oxygen-using respiration can't keep up, glucose that stops partway, at lactate or ethanol, rebuilds only 2. Most of its energy stays locked in those leftovers.
>
> *Scope:* glucose in plant, animal and fungal cells such as muscle and yeast, with classroom yields of about 30 and 2 ATP per glucose. ATP is the main carrier; a few jobs use others. Bacteria come up only briefly; how they make ATP without mitochondria is in Going deeper.

That difference is the energy the cell can use. Think of a ball on a shelf. Pushing it over the edge takes a nudge, but it ends lower than it started, and the drop is what you get.

ATP is like a rechargeable battery, drained by work and charged by respiration. Unlike a battery, though, ATP really changes into ADP and a loose phosphate, and is then rebuilt.

## Explain: where ATP is rebuilt

The first film built the respiration equation but skipped where the energy sits and whether the atoms add up. Glucose, the sugar from [Capturing Sunlight](/lessons/photosynthesis-energy-capture/), holds energy in how its atoms are bonded. Rearranged with oxygen into carbon dioxide and water, the same atoms sit lower in energy, like the ball on the floor. **Cellular respiration** is cells breaking down glucose and other food molecules and using the released energy to build ATP, in plants, animals, fungi and many microbes.

> C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy (much of it captured in ATP)

Check the atoms: 6 carbons, 12 hydrogens and 18 oxygens on each side. Matter is rearranged, not created or lost.

The real process runs as many small steps, each sped up by an enzyme, usually a protein (see [Enzymes at Work](/lessons/enzymes-at-work/)). The first film placed the first steps in the **cytoplasm** and the rest in the **mitochondria** (one is a mitochondrion), organelles found in plant, animal and fungal cells.

For an animal cell, modern estimates say about 30 to 32 ATP per glucose; real cells vary (see Going deeper). No energy is made or destroyed: it moves from glucose into ATP and heat.

## Explain: with oxygen and without it

The second film showed glucose stopping partway for 2 ATP. It left out why a cell bothers with a last step that adds no ATP. **Aerobic** means using oxygen; **anaerobic** means without it. The first steps, which rebuild the 2 ATP, use up a small helper molecule (Biology II calls it NAD+). Turning the halves into lactate or ethanol hands that helper back, so the first steps can keep running. It is not all or nothing, as the second film showed.

**What to call it.** Biologists call this route **fermentation**: making a small amount of ATP from glucose without oxygen. The SOL calls it anaerobic respiration, a name biologists also use for something different that some microbes do (see Going deeper).

**Predict.** If lactate still holds most of glucose's energy, does your body throw it away?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. **Lactate**, the muscle's leftover, is not just waste: the heart, other muscles and the liver use it as fuel or to rebuild glucose.

</details>

Whether fermentation is really faster for the same amount of enzyme is still debated (see Going deeper). About the burn and the breathing:
- The burn is not caused by lactate alone; experiments point to a mix of acid, lactate and other chemicals.
- Soreness the next day is a different effect, and lactate is not its cause.
- You keep breathing hard after a sprint partly because your cells use extra oxygen while they recover.

Respiration and photosynthesis mirror each other. Photosynthesis uses light energy to build glucose from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen; respiration breaks glucose back down with oxygen into carbon dioxide and water. Plants do both. The atoms go round and round between them, while energy arrives as light and leaves as heat (see [Matter Cycles](/lessons/matter-cycles/)).

## Elaborate: the Bay’s summer oxygen gap

Here the all-the-way-down route is the problem. In spring and summer, algae grow in the sunlit surface water, fed by nutrients that rivers carry in from the land, much of them from human activity. Many die and sink, and near the bottom bacteria break them down by aerobic respiration, using up the dissolved oxygen. In summer the warm surface layer does not mix well with the deep water, so little new oxygen reaches the bottom. Water with less than 2 milligrams of oxygen per liter (2 mg/L) is hypoxic; a large patch of it is often called a dead zone.

## Going deeper

### Is fermentation faster for the same amount of enzyme?

A 2024 analysis estimated that, for the same mass of enzyme, the fermentation route made ATP about 2.1 times as fast as respiration in yeast and 3.1 times as fast in mammal cells. In the bacterium *E. coli*, though, respiration was faster. Scientists still debate this result, because another 2024 study found respiration more efficient for the same amount of protein, in yeast, immune cells and cancer cells. The debate bears on a puzzle: baker's yeast given plenty of sugar ferments even when oxygen is present.

### How big is the ATP yield from one glucose?

Older textbooks say 36 to 38 ATP per glucose. The modern estimate of about 30 to 32 is lower mainly because each ATP turned out to cost more of the energy the mitochondrion stores across its inner membrane than older counts assumed; the total also depends on which shuttle passes electrons from the helper molecule in the cytoplasm to the electron transport steps in the mitochondrion. Real cells can get less, because some stored energy leaks away, and some, such as baker's yeast, get fewer.

### The first seconds of a sprint

For the first few seconds of a sprint, a store called creatine phosphate refills ATP; then fermentation adds ATP and lactate builds up.

### Bacteria

Bacteria have no mitochondria. In those that use oxygen, the electron transport steps, which make most of the ATP, run on the cell membrane.

### Anaerobic respiration in a stricter sense

Some microbes carry out anaerobic respiration in a stricter sense, using another substance such as nitrate or sulfate in place of oxygen.
