## Explain: competing for what you need

<WordBank terms={['population', 'community', 'competition', 'cooperation', 'limiting factor', 'biotic factor', 'abiotic factor', 'interdependent']} />

Every living thing needs matter and energy. Animals get both from food. Plants capture energy from sunlight and take in water, carbon dioxide and minerals, as you saw in [that lesson](/lessons/photosynthesis-feeds-the-world/). Most of these things are limited.

A **population** is all the members of one species living in one area at the same time. A **community** is all the populations in an area that interact with each other.

**Competition** happens when living things need the same resource and there is not enough for all of them. Animals compete for:

- food, which gives them matter and energy, and water
- shelter and territory, the area an animal uses and keeps others out of
- mates

Members of one population often compete hard, because they need many of the same things. Two male deer may push with their antlers in the breeding season. Robins on one lawn hunt the same worms. Different species compete too, like the two kinds of paramecium.

Competition is often quiet. Nobody has to fight. A bird that finds the seeds first eats them, and they are gone for the next bird. Over many generations, individuals with inherited traits that help them get food may leave more young. In this way, competition can shape these helpful traits, called adaptations, as you will see in [that lesson](/lessons/natural-selection-in-action/). Kinds that use different foods or places compete less, which you will meet in [that lesson](/lessons/habitat-and-niche/).

Plants compete too. They need light, water and minerals from the soil. A tall plant can shade a short one. Some plants grow taller, spread longer roots, or turn their leaves toward the light. How a plant responds depends on the plant and on its neighbors.

## Explain: working together

**Cooperation** happens when members of a population work together to meet their needs. Here are three examples.

- **Honeybees.** A colony can hold tens of thousands of bees. Worker bees do different jobs, such as caring for young, guarding the hive and collecting food. They also pass liquid food from one bee to another. The shares are not always equal.
- **Wolves.** A pack hunts as a group and can catch large prey, such as elk. You can read more in [that lesson](/lessons/predators-and-prey/).
- **Schools of fish.** Many small fish swim close together. A tight school can make it harder for a predator to catch any one fish.

## Explain: limiting factors and linked lives

A **limiting factor** is anything in the environment that limits which species, or how many of a species, can live in an ecosystem. A **biotic factor** is a living part of the environment. An **abiotic factor** is a nonliving part, such as water or temperature.

| Limiting factor | Biotic or abiotic | How it can limit a population |
|---|---|---|
| Food | Biotic | Less food means fewer young survive. |
| Water | Abiotic | In a dry summer, some animals and plants die. |
| Shelter | Either | Too few dens or nest holes leave some animals exposed. |
| Disease | Biotic | Sickness can spread faster in a crowded group. |
| Predation | Biotic | Predators eat some members of the population. |
| Cold weather | Abiotic | A hard freeze can kill many animals at once. |

Shelter can be living, like a hollow tree, or nonliving, like a gap in rocks.

Populations in a community depend on each other, so we call the community **interdependent**. A change in one population can affect the others linked to it. Think about Gause’s tubes. Every day, Gause gave the paramecia a fresh, measured amount of bacteria to eat. Once a tube was crowded, the paramecia ate the day’s bacteria until the liquid cleared. Running out of food helped stop their growth, though Gause wrote that other conditions in the liquid played a part too. The two kinds of paramecium were linked as well: when they shared a tube, each kind ended up with fewer than when it grew alone. If a disease killed many rabbits in a forest, the foxes that eat rabbits would find less food.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Living and nonliving limiting factors set how many of a species can live in a place, and a change in one population can affect others.</Support>

### Design problem

Plan a test of whether crowding makes plants compete. You can use radish seeds, pots, potting soil, water and a balance.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** How does the number of plants in a pot affect how much each plant grows?
- **Independent variable:** plants per pot, such as 1, 4, 8 and 16.
- **Dependent variable:** mean mass of each plant in grams after three weeks. Pull the plants, rinse off the soil, blot them dry and weigh them, then divide each pot’s total plant mass by the number of plants in it.
- **Control:** pots with one plant, which has no neighbors to compete with.
- **Constants:** pot size, the same soil, the same water in mL each day, the same light and the same temperature.
- **Repeated trials:** at least three pots for each crowding level.
- **What results would mean:** if mean mass per plant falls as crowding rises, the plants were probably competing for light, water or minerals.
- **Safety:** wear gloves or wash your hands after handling soil. Do not eat the plants or seeds.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How Gause took his counts

Gause’s tubes held 5 mL of liquid, and the counts are means for a 0.5 mL sample. The counts come from Table 3 of his 1934 book The Struggle for Existence (pages 144 to 145). From day 20 he took samples from similar tubes together.

### Plants and the shade of neighbors

In a 2017 greenhouse study, plants changed how they grew when green filters copied the shade of crowded neighbors. Under short neighbors they grew most upright, which can help a plant get above the others.
