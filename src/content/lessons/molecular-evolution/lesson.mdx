      change to the simple neutral picture. Between mutations that are strictly neutral and those
      that are clearly harmful, she argued, lies a large class of slightly deleterious mutations,
      whose effects on fitness are so small that drift and selection are of similar strength. The
      fate of such a mutation depends on the effective population size, Ne, which measures how
      strongly chance acts in a population. In a small population, drift can carry a slightly

      simple neutral model predicts that the substitution rate depends only on the neutral
      mutation rate; Ohta's model predicts that it also depends on Ne. That prediction gave
      researchers something new to test, by comparing species whose populations differ greatly
      in size, and the nearly neutral theory is now a standard framework for reading genome-wide
      patterns of constraint.

techniques:
  - name: "Fossil-calibrated molecular dating"
    blurb: >-
      Branch lengths estimated from sequences are converted to time with external age
      information: a fossil that sets a minimum age for a node, or sequences sampled at known,
      sufficiently different dates. Bayesian relaxed-clock methods let rates vary among branches
      and represent calibration uncertainty as a prior distribution on node ages.
  - name: "Codon-model estimation of dN/dS"
    blurb: >-
      Aligned coding sequences are analysed on a phylogeny with a model that separates
      nonsynonymous from synonymous substitutions, each counted per available site. The ratio
      omega = dN/dS, estimated by maximum likelihood for a gene, a branch or a class of sites,
      measures how amino-acid changes have fared relative to silent ones.
  - name: "McDonald-Kreitman test"
    blurb: >-
      Changes at one locus are sorted into a two-by-two table: replacement or synonymous, and
      polymorphic within species or fixed between species. Under neutrality the replacement to
      synonymous ratio is expected to be the same in both rows; an excess of fixed replacements
      suggests adaptive fixation, and an excess of replacement polymorphisms suggests weakly
      harmful variants.
  - name: "Parent-offspring trio sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      The genomes of two parents and their offspring are sequenced at high coverage, and sites
      where both parents are homozygous for the reference base and the offspring is heterozygous are taken as
      candidate new mutations. Correcting for the callable part of the genome and for missed
      mutations gives a per-generation germline mutation rate.

**Two living species may have left no fossil of the moment they split. Their genes can still tell you, roughly, when it happened, and whether selection or chance shaped what changed.**

This lesson reads DNA and protein sequences the way a geologist reads layers of rock: as a record laid down over time. You will date a split from sequence differences, see why the neutral theory holds that many of those differences were fixed by chance, and learn to tell, with caution, when selection has been at work.

## The idea: sequences as a record of time

When one ancestral population splits into two lineages, each keeps acquiring [mutations](/lessons/mutation/). Some spread until every member of the lineage carries them. A mutation that has reached fixation in a lineage is a **substitution**: the rate of substitution is the rate at which mutations arise and then fix. Compare the same gene in two species and you see the substitutions that built up on both branches since their common ancestor.

The gene must be **orthologous**: two copies descended from one gene in the common ancestor by speciation, rather than by duplication within a genome (the gene-family copies traced in [How Signaling Evolved](/lessons/how-signaling-evolved/) are mostly the duplicated kind). How the sequences are obtained belongs to [sequencing and genomes](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/); here you start from an alignment.

Counting differences is like counting the scratches on two copies of a record that have been played for years. A new scratch over an old one goes unseen. In the same way, a site can change twice, or change and change back, so the observed proportion of differing sites, p, underestimates the true number of substitutions as sequences diverge. A **corrected distance**, d, estimates the real number. The simplest correction (Jukes and Cantor, assuming all four bases interchange at one rate) is:

> **d = -(3/4) x ln(1 - (4/3) x p)**

If 30% of aligned sites differ (p = 0.30), then 1 - 0.40 = 0.60, ln 0.60 = -0.511, and d = 0.383 substitutions per site. More than a quarter more change happened than you can see. For small p the correction is tiny: p = 0.012 gives d = 0.0121.

The tree that relates the sequences is itself an inference with a stated support, as in [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/).

## The clock and its calibration

In the early 1960s Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling noticed that amino-acid differences between the hemoglobins of different mammals were roughly proportional to the time since their lineages split, as judged from fossils. That is the **molecular clock**: the expected number of changes on a branch is its rate times its duration.

Sequences alone cannot separate rate from time. A distance of 0.02 could mean a fast rate over a short time or a slow rate over a long one. You need a **calibration**: an age from outside the sequences. A fossil is the commonest. The oldest fossil that can be correctly assigned to a group shows the group existed by then, so it gives a **minimum** age; the actual split was earlier by an unknown margin. Sequences sampled at sufficiently different known dates, such as virus samples or ancient DNA, can also calibrate a tree.

**Worked problem (illustrative data).** Two species known from fossils to have split 3.0 million years ago differ by a corrected 0.012 substitutions per site in a gene. A second pair differs by 0.024 in the same gene. When did the second pair split? Assume both lineages in each pair evolve at the same constant rate, the calibration date is exact, and variation already present in the ancestral population adds negligible time.

1. *Set up the model.* Substitutions accumulate along both branches from the common ancestor, so the distance between two tips is D = rt + rt = 2rt, where r is the rate per lineage and t the time since the split.
2. *Calibrate.* r = 0.012 / (2 x 3,000,000 years) = 2.0 x 10^-9 substitutions per site per year.
3. *Date the target.* t = 0.024 / (2 x 2.0 x 10^-9) = 0.024 / (4.0 x 10^-9) = 6.0 million years. Check: 2 x 2.0 x 10^-9 x 6,000,000 = 0.024 substitutions per site, the stated distance.

An easy error is forgetting the factor 2 in step 3, which gives 0.024 / 2.0 x 10^-9 = 12 million years. And since a real fossil calibration is only a minimum, both splits may be older than these figures.

<ClockCalibrationLine />

Real lineages do not run at one rate. **Relaxed clocks** let branch rates vary: in Drummond and colleagues' 2006 method, each branch draws its own rate, and calibration uncertainty enters as a probability distribution on node ages. In their marsupial analysis the fastest branch of the most probable tree was estimated to evolve 2.7 times faster than the slowest. Even with abundant data, the dates such methods return stay sensitive to the assumptions about rates and times put in.

## The neutral theory and what it predicts

Why would a protein change at a roughly steady rate? In 1968 Motoo Kimura argued that the substitution rate implied by protein comparisons was too high to be explained if each substitution had been driven by selection. His **neutral theory** holds that most substitutions that fix are neutral or nearly neutral, fixed by [random drift](/lessons/drift-gene-flow-explanations/), while most strongly harmful mutations are removed by selection before they spread. It does not say that all mutations are neutral, or that selection is unimportant for the traits organisms show.

The model makes a clean prediction. In an ideal diploid population of N individuals there are 2N copies of each site. If neutral mutations arise at rate mu per copy per generation:

> **new neutral mutations per generation = 2N x mu; fixation probability of each = 1/2N; so K = 2N x mu x 1/2N = mu**

The neutral substitution rate K equals the neutral mutation rate and does not depend on population size: a large population makes more mutations, but each has a smaller chance of fixing. The derivation of the fixation probability belongs to [population genetics](/lessons/population-genetics/). Note that mu is the neutral mutation rate, not the total; mutations removed by selection never become substitutions.

In 1973 Tomoko Ohta proposed the **nearly neutral theory**. Many mutations are slightly deleterious, with effects on fitness of the order of 1/Ne, where Ne, the effective population size, measures how strongly drift acts. In a small population drift can fix them; in a large one selection removes them more reliably. So for this class the substitution rate does depend on Ne.

Mutation input also varies. Bergeron and colleagues in 2023 sequenced 151 parent-offspring trios from 68 vertebrate species and found per-generation germline mutation rates differing 40-fold across species. The 36 mammals sampled averaged 7.97 x 10^-9 mutations per site per generation, and the four classes sampled did not differ significantly overall. That is a per-generation mutation rate; the clock rate above is a per-year substitution rate. Linking them takes assumptions about neutrality and generation time.

## Reading selection in coding sequences

In a coding gene, a **nonsynonymous** change alters the amino acid and a **synonymous** change does not, because the [genetic code](/lessons/genetic-code-and-translation/) has several codons for most amino acids. **dN** is nonsynonymous substitutions per nonsynonymous site; **dS** is synonymous substitutions per synonymous site. If synonymous changes are neutral, dS is a built-in control and their ratio omega = dN/dS reads selection on the protein:

- omega below 1: amino-acid changes fixed more slowly than silent ones, because selection removed some (purifying selection).
- omega near 1: consistent with neutrality.
- omega above 1: amino-acid changes fixed faster than silent ones (positive selection).

**Worked problem, step 4.** In a different gene from the second pair, dN = 0.004 and dS = 0.040 substitutions per site. Omega = 0.004 / 0.040 = 0.10. Amino-acid changes fixed at a tenth the rate of silent ones, so most were removed by purifying selection, even though the gene is still evolving.

A second signal comes from polymorphism. Under neutrality, variants within a species are transient stages on their way to fixation or loss, so the ratio of replacement to synonymous changes should be the same among polymorphisms as among fixed differences between species. In 1991 John McDonald and Martin Kreitman applied this to the Adh gene in three Drosophila species and found an excess of fixed replacement differences, which they suggested reflected adaptive fixation. The **McDonald-Kreitman test** uses each class of change as the control for the other, so it needs no clock.

## When the signal misleads

Each of these tests is a model inference, and each model can fail.

A gene-wide omega averages over sites. If most codons are strongly constrained, a few positively selected sites barely lift the average, so the test has little power. Within a single population, omega near 1 can arise under selection and does not establish neutrality. Synonymous sites are not always neutral: Rahman and colleagues in 2021 found evidence of non-neutral synonymous substitutions in Enterobacterales, so treating every synonymous change as neutral inflated omega: corrected values averaged 80% of the standard estimates. And in 2018 Venkat, Hahn and Thornton showed with simulations based on mammal and fly alignments, containing no positive selection, that mutations changing several adjacent nucleotides at once can make the branch-site test report positive selection on a lineage.

### Design problem

A branch-site test flags positive selection in an immune gene on one primate lineage. Before you believe it, what would you check?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with the data. Inspect the alignment around the flagged codons for errors, and confirm that every sequence is a true ortholog rather than a duplicated paralog or a misassembled copy.

Then test the model. Simulate alignments on the same tree with no positive selection but with realistic multinucleotide mutations and the observed base composition, and see how often the test fires; if it fires often, the empirical result is weak. Check codon-usage bias, since selection on synonymous sites changes dS.

Seek a second line of evidence. Build a McDonald-Kreitman table from polymorphism within the species and fixed differences from a close relative, with an outgroup to place the fixed changes on this lineage: replacement and synonymous changes, polymorphic and fixed. An excess of fixed replacements would support the inference with partly independent data, though this test also treats synonymous changes as neutral.

Finally, test function. Reconstruct the ancestral and derived proteins, express both, and measure the property that matters, such as binding to a pathogen protein. Even then, report the result as a model inference supported by evidence, not as an observation of adaptation.

</details>
