scientists:
  - name: "Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling"
    contribution: >-
      Compared hemoglobin sequences across animals and proposed in the early 1960s that the number
      of amino-acid differences between two species grows roughly in proportion to the time since
      their lineages split, the idea later called the molecular clock.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Zuckerkandl & Pauling, Evolutionary divergence and convergence in proteins, in Bryson & Vogel (eds), Evolving Genes and Proteins, Academic Press (1965); Zuckerkandl & Pauling, Molecules as documents of evolutionary history, J. Theor. Biol. 8:357 (1965)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In the late 1950s Emile Zuckerkandl joined Linus Pauling's laboratory at the California
      Institute of Technology, where Pauling had already shown that sickle-cell hemoglobin differs
      from normal hemoglobin as a molecule. Zuckerkandl studied hemoglobins from different
      animals. As partial amino-acid sequences of hemoglobin chains from several species became
      available, the two of them compared them and noticed a regularity: the more distantly two
      species were related, judging by the fossil record, the more amino-acid differences their
      hemoglobins showed, and the count rose roughly in proportion to the estimated time since the
      split. In papers of 1962 and 1965 they proposed that protein differences could be used to
      estimate when lineages diverged, and they argued that sequences are documents of
      evolutionary history. Other laboratories, comparing cytochrome c across species, were
      reaching related conclusions in the same years. The work made it possible to put dates on
      branches that have no fossil record, and it raised the question the neutral theory later
      tried to answer: why should a protein change at a roughly steady rate at all?
  - name: "Motoo Kimura"
    contribution: >-
      Argued in 1968 that the rate of molecular substitution inferred from protein comparisons was
      so high that many of the substitutions were probably neutral mutations fixed by random
      genetic drift, the founding argument of the neutral theory of molecular evolution.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Kimura, Evolutionary rate at the molecular level, Nature 217:624 (1968); Kimura, PNAS 63:1181 (1969)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Kimura was a population geneticist at the National Institute of Genetics in Mishima, Japan,
      and much of his earlier work was mathematics: how allele frequencies wander by chance in a
      finite population. In a short paper in Nature in February 1968 he turned that mathematics on
      the new protein data. He took published estimates of how fast amino acids are replaced in
      proteins such as hemoglobin, scaled them up to the whole genome of a mammal, and argued that
      the implied rate of nucleotide substitution was so high that it would be hard to explain if
      every substitution had been driven by selection. His alternative was that many of these
      substitutions were mutations with no effect on fitness that had reached fixation by drift.
      In a 1969 paper in PNAS he put the hypothesis more fully: a majority of the amino-acid
      substitutions in the homologous proteins examined, including mammal and carp hemoglobins,
      resulted from random fixation of neutral or nearly neutral mutations. He did not say that
      all mutations are neutral; harmful ones are removed. What the argument made possible was a
      null model for sequence evolution, against which selection can be tested.
  - name: "Tomoko Ohta"
    contribution: >-
      Proposed in 1973 that many substitutions are slightly deleterious mutations whose fate
      depends on the balance between weak selection and drift, so that effective population size
      affects the substitution rate (the nearly neutral theory).
    profileSourceAnchor: "Ohta, Slightly deleterious mutant substitutions in evolution, Nature 246:96 (1973)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Ohta worked at the National Institute of Genetics in Mishima, where she collaborated with
      Kimura on the mathematics of neutral evolution. In a Nature paper in 1973 she proposed a
      change to the simple neutral picture. Between mutations that are strictly neutral and those
      that are clearly harmful, she argued, lies a large class of slightly deleterious mutations,
      whose effects on fitness are so small that drift and selection are of similar strength. The
      fate of such a mutation depends on the effective population size, Ne, which measures how
      strongly chance acts in a population. In a small population, drift can carry a slightly
      deleterious mutation to fixation; in a large one, selection removes it more reliably. The
      simple neutral model predicts that the substitution rate depends only on the neutral
      mutation rate; Ohta's model predicts that it also depends on Ne. That prediction gave
      researchers something new to test, by comparing species whose populations differ greatly
      in size, and the nearly neutral theory is now a standard framework for reading genome-wide
      patterns of constraint.
  - name: "John H. McDonald and Martin Kreitman"
    contribution: >-
      Devised a test that compares the ratio of amino-acid-changing to silent differences among
      polymorphisms within species with the same ratio among fixed differences between species,
      and found an excess of fixed amino-acid replacements at the Adh locus of Drosophila.
    profileSourceAnchor: "McDonald & Kreitman, Adaptive protein evolution at the Adh locus in Drosophila, Nature 351:652 (1991)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The gene for alcohol dehydrogenase, Adh, had become one of the best-studied genes in
      Drosophila population genetics; in 1983 Kreitman had published a survey of its DNA sequence
      variation in D. melanogaster. In 1991 McDonald and Kreitman used Adh sequences to test a
      simple consequence of the neutral theory. If amino-acid changes and silent changes are both
      neutral, then the polymorphisms seen within a species are transient stages on the way to
      fixation or loss, and the ratio of replacement to synonymous changes among those
      polymorphisms should match the ratio among the differences that have become fixed between
      species. They compared Adh sequences from three species of the D. melanogaster subgroup and
      found more fixed amino-acid replacements between species than the polymorphism ratio
      predicted, which they suggested reflected adaptive fixation. The test needs no assumption
      about the substitution rate or the dates of the splits, because each class of change acts
      as the control for the other. It has since been extended to many genes at once to estimate
      what fraction of amino-acid substitutions might have been fixed by positive selection.

techniques:
  - name: "Fossil-calibrated molecular dating"
    blurb: >-
      Branch lengths estimated from sequences are converted to time with external age
      information: a fossil that sets a minimum age for a node, or sequences sampled at known,
      sufficiently different dates. Bayesian relaxed-clock methods let rates vary among branches
      and represent calibration uncertainty as a prior distribution on node ages.
  - name: "Codon-model estimation of dN/dS"
    blurb: >-
      Aligned coding sequences are analysed on a phylogeny with a model that separates
      nonsynonymous from synonymous substitutions, each counted per available site. The ratio
      omega = dN/dS, estimated by maximum likelihood for a gene, a branch or a class of sites,
      measures how amino-acid changes have fared relative to silent ones.
  - name: "McDonald-Kreitman test"
    blurb: >-
      Changes at one locus are sorted into a two-by-two table: replacement or synonymous, and
      polymorphic within species or fixed between species. Under neutrality the replacement to
      synonymous ratio is expected to be the same in both rows; an excess of fixed replacements
      suggests adaptive fixation, and an excess of replacement polymorphisms suggests weakly
      harmful variants.
  - name: "Parent-offspring trio sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      The genomes of two parents and their offspring are sequenced at high coverage, and sites
      where both parents are homozygous for the reference base and the offspring is heterozygous are taken as
      candidate new mutations. Correcting for the callable part of the genome and for missed
      mutations gives a per-generation germline mutation rate.

    choices:
      - "None occurred during the divergence"
      - "They fixed at the neutral expectation"
      - "Most were fixed by positive selection"
      - "Most were removed by purifying selection"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Omega = 0.006 / 0.030 = 0.2, well below 1, so amino-acid changes have been fixed at a
      fifth of the rate of silent ones, the signature of purifying selection removing most of
      them. Because most amino-acid changes that arose were never fixed, most cannot have been fixed by positive selection, though omega alone cannot rule out a few adaptive changes hidden in the average. The neutral expectation
      is omega near 1, which this gene is far from. A dN of 0.006 is not zero, so some
      amino-acid substitutions did occur; the gene is evolving, only under constraint.
  - stem: "In the simple neutral model, a diploid population of N individuals receives new neutral mutations at a rate mu per gene copy per generation. What is the expected neutral substitution rate K?"
    choices:
      - "mu per generation, whatever N is"
      - "1/2N per generation, whatever mu is"
      - "mu divided by 2N per generation"
      - "2N mu per generation, rising with N"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      New neutral mutations arise at 2N mu per generation (2N copies, each mutating at rate mu),
      and each fixes with probability 1/2N, so K = 2N mu x 1/2N = mu, independent of population
      size. The rate 2N mu counts mutations arising, not mutations fixing, and leaves out the
      fixation probability. Dividing mu by 2N applies the fixation probability twice. The value
      1/2N is the fixation probability of one copy, not a rate of substitution, and it ignores
      how often mutations arise.
  - stem: "The oldest fossil that can be confidently assigned to a clade is 50 million years old. How should this fossil be used to calibrate a molecular clock for that clade?"
    choices:
      - "As the rate of change along each branch"
      - "As a minimum age for the clade's origin"
      - "As the exact date of the clade's origin"
      - "As a maximum age for the clade's origin"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The clade must be at least as old as its oldest correctly assigned fossil, and probably
      somewhat older, because early members may have been rare, may lack the features that identify them, and may not have fossilised or been found;
      so the fossil sets a minimum. Treating it as the exact origin date ignores that gap.
      Treating it as a maximum reverses the logic, since the clade existed by then and may have
      existed earlier. A fossil gives an age, not a substitution rate; the rate is inferred by
      combining that age with sequence distances.
  - stem: "A calibrating pair of species split 10 million years ago and differ by a corrected 0.040 substitutions per site. A second pair differs by 0.060. Assuming equal, constant rates on all lineages, when did the second pair split?"
    choices:
      - "7.5 million years ago"
      - "10 million years ago"
      - "15 million years ago"
      - "30 million years ago"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Distance accumulates on both lineages, so D = 2rt and r = 0.040 / (2 x 10,000,000) = 2.0 x
      10^-9 per site per year; then t = 0.060 / (2 x 2.0 x 10^-9) = 15 million years (check: the
      ratio 0.060/0.040 = 1.5 times 10 million). Thirty million comes from computing the
      per-lineage rate correctly and then forgetting the factor 2 in the second step. Dividing by
      4r gives 7.5 million. Ten million assumes the pairs split together despite different
      distances.
  - stem: "Sequences of a gene from many individuals of one bacterial species give dN/dS close to 1. A student concludes the gene is evolving neutrally. What is the best response?"
    choices:
      - "Wrong, since omega near 1 proves positive selection"
      - "Not shown, since selection can also give this"
      - "Correct, provided the sample size is large"
      - "Correct, because omega near 1 defines neutrality"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Within a single population, many of the differences sampled are recent polymorphisms not yet
      sorted by selection, and population-genetic models show that omega near 1 can arise there
      under either positive or negative selection, so the value alone does not establish
      neutrality. Omega near 1 is consistent with neutrality but does not define it. It is not
      proof of positive selection either, which omega alone cannot show here, since within a population positive selection need not push it above 1.
      A larger sample does not fix the problem, which lies in what the ratio measures at this
      timescale, not in sampling error.

**Genes can roughly date a split that left no fossil, and show whether selection or chance shaped what changed.**

> **The one idea.** On average, neutral changes (no effect on survival or reproduction) become substitutions at the rate they arise, whatever the population size. So neutral differences between two species pile up steadily, and one dated split turns their count into years. A departure from that neutral count is a clue to selection, not proof.
>
> *Scope:* neutral sites in the same gene in both species, corrected for hidden changes; the dated split and the target must share a steady rate. Slightly harmful mutations break it: their rate depends on effective population size.

## Sequences as a record

The first film counted **substitutions**, [mutations](/lessons/mutation/) that have reached **fixation** (every member of the lineage carries them), assuming you compare the same gene and see every change.

The same gene means **orthologous**: both copies descend from one ancestral gene, split by speciation rather than by duplication (the gene-family copies in [How Signaling Evolved](/lessons/how-signaling-evolved/) are mostly duplicates). [Sequencing and genomes](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/) covers obtaining them. Call p the proportion of aligned sites that differ.

**Predict.** Two sequences differ at 30% of aligned sites (p = 0.30). Did fewer than 30 substitutions per 100 sites happen since they split, exactly 30, or more?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

More. A site can change twice, or change back (A to G, then G to A), hiding the first change. So p underestimates the true number of substitutions, more so as sequences diverge.

</details>

A **corrected distance**, D, estimates the real number of substitutions per site. The simplest correction (Jukes and Cantor) assumes all four bases replace one another at one rate; take its form as given (ln is the natural logarithm):

> **D = -(3/4) x ln(1 - (4/3) x p)**

For p = 0.30, 1 - 0.40 = 0.60, ln 0.60 = -0.511, and D = 0.383: over a quarter more change than you see. For small p the correction is tiny: p = 0.012 gives D = 0.0121. The tree relating the sequences is itself an inference with a stated support, as in [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/).

## Why the clock ticks

Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling saw in the early 1960s that hemoglobin differences grew roughly in proportion to fossil-dated time since a split. The first film showed why; here is the rate.

<EquationCard
  purpose="The rate at which neutral mutations become substitutions in one lineage."
  eq="K = (2N × mu) × (1/2N) = mu"
  say="New neutral mutations arise at two N mu per generation; each fixes with probability one over two N; so the substitution rate K equals the neutral mutation rate mu."
  symbols={[
    ["N", "N", "diploid individuals, so 2N copies of each site", ""],
    ["mu", "mu", "neutral mutations per site, per copy, per generation", ""],
    ["K", "K", "neutral substitutions per site, per generation, in one lineage", ""],
  ]}
  check="N = 100, mu = 10^-8: 2 × 10^-6 new mutations per generation × 1/200 = 10^-8 = mu."
/>

The fixation chance of one new neutral copy is 1/2N only in an ideal population (constant size, random mating); [population genetics](/lessons/population-genetics/) derives it. K = mu is an average; real substitutions arrive irregularly. Two steps link K to measured distance:

- **Per year**, the clock rate r is K divided by generation time, so at the same per-generation mutation rate, long generations mean fewer substitutions per year.
- **Between two tips**, D = 2rt after a time t, if both branches share the rate r.

Motoo Kimura reached the **neutral theory** in 1968 from the other direction, arguing that protein comparisons implied too many substitutions for selection to pay for each in selective deaths. He proposed that most substitutions are neutral or nearly neutral mutations fixed by [random drift](/lessons/drift-gene-flow-explanations/), while most strongly harmful mutations are removed before they spread. The theory does not say all mutations are neutral, or that selection is unimportant for traits.

## Reading time: calibration

The **molecular clock** says the expected number of changes on a branch is its rate times its duration. The first film's outside date is a **calibration**; besides a fossil, sequences sampled at sufficiently different known dates, such as virus samples or ancient DNA, can calibrate a tree.

**Worked problem (illustrative data).** Two species that fossils show split 3.0 million years ago differ by a corrected 0.012 substitutions per site in a gene. A second pair differs by 0.024 in the same gene. When did the second pair split? Assume one constant rate on all four lineages, an exact calibration date, and negligible variation in the ancestral population (see Going deeper).

1. *Set up the model.* D = rt + rt = 2rt, with r the rate per lineage and t the time since the split.
2. *Calibrate.* r = 0.012 / (2 x 3,000,000 years) = 2.0 x 10^-9 substitutions per site per year.
3. *Date the target.* t = 0.024 / (2 x 2.0 x 10^-9) = 0.024 / (4.0 x 10^-9) = 6.0 million years. Check: 2 x 2.0 x 10^-9 x 6,000,000 = 0.024.

Forgetting the factor 2 in step 3 gives 0.024 / 2.0 x 10^-9 = 12 million years. Since a real fossil calibration is only a minimum, both splits may be older. Try it on the line below.

## When the tick rate varies

**Rates differ between branches**: in Drummond and colleagues' 2006 marsupial analysis, the fastest branch of the most probable tree was estimated to evolve 2.7 times faster than the slowest. **Relaxed clocks** let each branch have its own rate. Bayesian dating commonly treats a fossil calibration as a range of possible ages, not a point; even with abundant data, the dates stay sensitive to the rate model and calibration ranges put in.

**The mutation rate itself varies.** Bergeron and colleagues in 2023 sequenced parents and offspring from 68 vertebrate species and found per-generation germline mutation rates differing 40-fold, around 10^-8 per site per generation in mammals. So mu is no constant, and turning it into a per-year rate takes assumptions about neutrality and generation time.

**Some mutations are not quite neutral.** For these, the **effective population size**, Ne, matters: the size of an ideal population that drifts as strongly as the real one (the smaller Ne, the stronger drift).

**Predict.** A new mutation lowers fitness by 1 part in 10,000 for each copy carried. Is it more likely to become a substitution when Ne is 1,000 or 100,000?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

When Ne is 1,000: chance swings dwarf so small a cost, and it fixes at about 80% of the neutral rate (simple diploid model, constant Ne; other dominance or demographic assumptions shift it). At 100,000, selection wins and it almost never fixes. This is Tomoko Ohta's 1973 **nearly neutral theory**: many mutations are slightly deleterious, with costs of the order of 1/Ne, and their substitution rate depends on Ne.

</details>

## Reading selection in coding sequences

Where selection acts, substitutions depart from the neutral count: purifying selection can make amino-acid changes lag, and positive selection can make them run ahead. The second film shows two ways to catch that in a real gene.

Why count per site? Given the [genetic code](/lessons/genetic-code-and-translation/), most single-base changes in a codon alter the amino acid, so a gene offers roughly three nonsynonymous sites for every synonymous one, and raw counts would overstate amino-acid change about threefold.

**Worked problem, step 4.** For the second pair, a different gene has dN = 0.004 and dS = 0.040 substitutions per site. Omega = 0.004 / 0.040 = 0.10: the signature of **purifying selection** removing most amino-acid changes, though the gene still evolves; omega alone cannot rule out a few adaptive changes hidden in the average.

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Probably not. A gene-wide omega averages over all sites, so 297 constrained codons usually hold it below 1 unless the three change extremely fast. Codon models can instead estimate omega for a branch or a class of sites.

A **polymorphism** is a variant that still varies within a species. The second film's Adh counts, from three *Drosophila* species (McDonald and Kreitman, 1991):

| Fixed between species | 7 | 17 | 0.41 |

## When the signal misleads

Besides the power problem of gene-wide omega, each test's model can fail:

- **McDonald-Kreitman.** It needs no clock but treats synonymous changes as neutral. Slightly harmful replacement variants linger as polymorphisms but rarely fix, inflating the variable row and hiding adaptation; a change in population size can mimic the signal.
- **Omega within one population.** The differences sampled are mostly recent polymorphisms not yet sorted by selection, so omega near 1 can arise under selection and does not establish neutrality.
- **The synonymous control.** In Enterobacterales, the bacterial group that includes *E. coli*, Rahman and colleagues (2021) found non-neutral synonymous substitutions; treating all as neutral inflated omega, and corrected values averaged 80% of standard estimates.
- **Branch-site tests**, which ask whether some codons changed faster on one chosen branch, assume one base changes at a time, so one mutation hitting adjacent bases looks like rapid amino-acid change. Venkat, Hahn and Thornton (2018) simulated mammal- and fly-based alignments without positive selection; such multinucleotide mutations can make the test report it.

### Design problem

A branch-site test flags positive selection in an immune gene on one primate lineage. Before you believe it, what would you check?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

1. *The data.* Check the alignment around the flagged codons; confirm every sequence is a true ortholog, not a **paralog** (a duplicated copy) or misassembled.
2. *The model.* Simulate alignments on the same tree with no positive selection but realistic multinucleotide mutations and base composition; if the test often fires, the result is weak. Check codon-usage bias, since selection on synonymous sites changes dS.
3. *A second line of evidence.* Build a McDonald-Kreitman table from polymorphism within the species and fixed differences from a close relative, with an **outgroup** (a more distant species) to place the fixed changes on this lineage. An excess of fixed replacements adds partly independent support, under the same synonymous-neutrality assumption.
4. *Function.* Reconstruct and express the ancestral and derived proteins, and measure the property that matters, such as binding to a pathogen protein. Even then, report a model inference supported by evidence, not an observation of adaptation.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The mutation-rate numbers

Bergeron and colleagues' survey used 151 trios. The 36 mammals sampled averaged 7.97 x 10^-9 mutations per site per generation, and the four classes sampled did not differ significantly overall.

### Variation already in the ancestor

In a simple model (neutral autosomal genes, a randomly mating diploid ancestral population of constant size, no gene flow after the split), two species' gene copies trace back to an ancestral copy on average about 2Ne generations older than the species split: with Ne = 10,000 and 20-year generations, about 0.4 million years, 13% of the 3-million-year calibration. So a raw gene divergence runs old. A calibrated date can err the other way: the calibrating gene split is older too, so the rate comes out too high and the second date too young (6.0 million years, where with the same offset the species split was about 6.4).
