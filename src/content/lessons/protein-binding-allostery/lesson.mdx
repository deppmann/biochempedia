summary: >-
  Blood has two opposite jobs: grab oxygen in the lungs (~100 torr) and let go of it in
  hard-working muscle (~20 torr). Hemoglobin unloads about 66% of its oxygen across that gap,
  while myoglobin, a high-affinity, non-cooperative carrier, would manage about 7%. One rule
  carries the lesson: hemoglobin is always a mix of a weak-binding T shape and a tight-binding R
  shape, and anything that binds one shape better shifts the mix toward it. Starting from the
  dissociation constant Kd and the single-site hyperbola, you build the S-shaped curve as a
  weighted average of the two shapes, then trace the structural switch, in which O₂ binding
  pulls an iron atom 0.4 Å into the heme plane and that twitch is coupled to a ~15° rotation of
  one αβ dimer against the other. The same rule explains how H⁺ (the Bohr effect), CO₂ and
  2,3-BPG tune delivery, why fetal hemoglobin runs at higher affinity and why CO poisons, and
  one amino-acid swap (Glu6→Val) turns the machinery into sickle-cell disease.
hook: "Your blood has to grab oxygen in the lungs and let go of about two-thirds of it in hard-working muscle. A tighter grip than hemoglobin's would make it worse. What does hemoglobin do instead?"

techniques:
  - name: "The oxygen-binding (saturation) curve"
    blurb: >-
      Fractional saturation Y plotted against the partial pressure of O₂. A hyperbola
      signals independent, non-interacting sites (myoglobin has just one); an S-shaped sigmoid signals cooperative
      sites whose affinity rises as the molecule fills (hemoglobin). P50, the pO₂ at half-saturation, is
      read straight off the x-axis.
  - name: "The Hill coefficient (n_H)"
    blurb: >-
      A number for cooperativity, taken from the slope of a linearized binding plot:
      n_H = 1 means identical, independent sites, n_H > 1 means positive cooperativity, n_H < 1
      means negative cooperativity or a mix of sites with different affinities. Hemoglobin's n_H ≈ 2.8 (out of a maximum of 4). The Hill
      equation is an empirical curve fit; the two-state picture explains the
      cooperativity it measures.
  - name: "X-ray crystallography of T and R states"
    blurb: >-
      Solving deoxy- and oxyhemoglobin side by side reveals the tense (T, low-affinity)
      and relaxed (R, high-affinity) quaternary states and the sub-ångström iron
      movement that triggers the switch. The classic Perutz structures made allostery a
      thing you could see.
  - name: "Protein electrophoresis"
    blurb: >-
      Separating proteins by charge in an electric field. Pauling used it in 1949 to
      show that sickle-cell and normal hemoglobin migrate differently, pinning a human
      disease to a charge change in one molecule.
  - name: "BOLD / functional MRI"
    blurb: >-
      Imaging that reads the magnetic difference between paramagnetic deoxyhemoglobin
      and diamagnetic oxyhemoglobin. Active brain regions draw more oxygenated blood,
      changing the signal — the same iron chemistry that carries oxygen also lets a
      scanner track blood oxygenation, an indirect readout of brain activity.

  - stem: "A hypothetical O₂ carrier has the same P50 as hemoglobin but a Hill coefficient of 1.0. Compared with real hemoglobin, this carrier would:"
    choices:
      - "Deliver less O₂ between lung pO₂ (~100 torr) and working-muscle pO₂ (~20 torr)."
      - "Deliver far more O₂ across the same pO₂ gap."
      - "Be unable to bind O₂ at all in the lungs."
      - "Show a steeper curve in the physiological window."
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      An n_H of 1 gives a hyperbola, which is less steep through the physiological
      window than hemoglobin's sigmoid, so it gives up a smaller fraction of its O₂
      between lungs and tissue. (The headline ~66% vs ~7% contrast is hemoglobin against
      high-affinity myoglobin, whose very low P50 keeps it nearly saturated even in tissue;
      a same-P50 hyperbola delivers about 36%, against hemoglobin's about two-thirds.) The
      carrier would still bind O₂ in the lungs, and its curve would be less steep, not
      more, so it could not deliver more O₂ across the gap.
  - stem: "Myoglobin and hemoglobin both use heme to bind O₂, yet only hemoglobin shows a sigmoidal binding curve. The best explanation is that:"
    choices:
      - "Myoglobin's heme iron is Fe³⁺ while hemoglobin's is Fe²⁺."
      - "Myoglobin binds O₂ with much lower affinity than hemoglobin at every pO₂."
      - "Hemoglobin lacks a proximal histidine, so its iron can move freely."
      - "Hemoglobin has multiple subunits whose binding sites influence one another, while myoglobin has a single independent site."
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Cooperativity requires communication between sites, which needs more than one
      binding site; myoglobin's lone site cannot cooperate, so its curve is hyperbolic
      (n_H = 1). The others are false on the facts: both proteins use functional
      Fe²⁺, myoglobin actually has higher affinity at low pO₂, and both have a
      proximal histidine.
  - stem: "During intense exercise, working muscle becomes warmer, more acidic, and richer in CO₂. The net effect on hemoglobin in those tissues is to:"
    choices:
      - "Left-shift the O₂ curve and increase O₂ affinity, holding O₂ in the blood."
      - "Right-shift the O₂ curve and decrease O₂ affinity, releasing more O₂ to tissue."
      - "Abolish cooperativity, converting the sigmoid to a hyperbola."
      - "Convert hemoglobin to methemoglobin, blocking O₂ transport."
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Lower pH (Bohr effect) and higher CO₂ (carbamate formation) both stabilize the T
      state, which lowers O₂ affinity and right-shifts the curve, dumping more oxygen
      exactly where the working muscle needs it. The shifts do not abolish cooperativity
      or oxidize the iron, and they decrease rather than increase affinity.
  - stem: "Fetal hemoglobin (HbF, α₂γ₂) has higher O₂ affinity than adult HbA, which lets the fetus extract O₂ from maternal blood. The molecular reason is that HbF:"
    choices:
      - "Has four γ chains instead of two, doubling its O₂ capacity."
      - "Lacks heme groups, so it cannot be stabilized in the T state."
      - "Binds 2,3-BPG weakly because the γ chain has serine instead of histidine at position 143."
      - "Carries the Glu6→Val substitution that locks it in the R state."
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      2,3-BPG stabilizes the T (low-affinity) state by binding a β-cleft pocket; HbF's γ
      chains have serine at 143 instead of the histidine in adult β chains, so they bind
      BPG weakly, stay more relaxed, and run at higher affinity — pulling O₂ across the
      placenta. The other statements fail on the facts: HbF is still a two-α, two-γ
      tetramer with a full set of hemes, and the Glu6→Val substitution is the sickle
      mutation, which HbF does not carry.
  - stem: "A protein binds ligand A with Kd = 5 nM and ligand B with Kd = 50 µM. Which statement is correct?"
    choices:
      - "The protein binds A more tightly than B, because a smaller Kd means tighter binding."
      - "The protein binds B more tightly than A, because a larger Kd means more bound complex."
      - "The two ligands bind equally well, because Kd does not depend on the ligand."
      - "B reaches half-saturation at a lower free concentration than A."
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Kd is the free-ligand concentration at half-saturation, so a smaller Kd means
      half-occupancy is reached with less ligand — i.e., tighter binding. A (5 nM)
      binds far more tightly than B (50 µM). Saying B binds more tightly inverts the
      relationship; the equal-binding option fails because Kd is specific to the
      protein–ligand pair; and it is A, with the lower Kd, that reaches half-saturation
      at lower concentration.

  - stem: "A researcher measures the O₂ saturation of two purified O₂-binding proteins (made-up data). Protein X: 98% saturated at 100 torr and 32% at 20 torr. Protein Y: 99% saturated at 100 torr and 92% at 20 torr. Which conclusion is best supported?"
    choices:
      - "X releases about 66% of its O₂ between 100 and 20 torr, consistent with a cooperative, sigmoidal curve whose steep part lies between those pressures."
      - "Y is the better O₂ carrier for tissues, because it is more fully saturated in the lungs."
      - "X must have a lower P50 than Y, because it gives up its O₂ more readily."
      - "If X shows the Bohr effect like hemoglobin, lowering the pH would make X hold more of its O₂ at 20 torr."
    answer: 0
    rationale: "X drops from 98% to 32% saturation across the gap, so it unloads about 66% of its O₂, the pattern of a cooperative carrier like hemoglobin whose steep sigmoid sits between lung and tissue pressures. Y drops only from 99% to 92%, about 7%, like high-affinity myoglobin, which is still nearly saturated at tissue pressures. Saturation in the lungs is not delivery, so Y is the worse carrier. Releasing O₂ more readily means lower affinity and a higher P50, not a lower one. Lower pH right-shifts the curve through the Bohr effect, so X would hold less O₂ at 20 torr, not more."
  - stem: "In deoxyhemoglobin, the heme iron sits about 0.4 Å out of the porphyrin plane; on O₂ binding it moves into the plane. Which sequence correctly describes how this triggers the quaternary transition?"
    choices:
      - "O₂ binding → iron becomes high-spin and larger → it rises out of the plane → tetramer relaxes to T."
      - "O₂ binding → iron becomes low-spin and smaller → it moves into the plane → proximal His and F helix move → tetramer rotates toward R."
      - "O₂ binding → distal His forms a salt bridge → β-cleft opens → 2,3-BPG binds → R state."
      - "O₂ binding → Fe²⁺ oxidizes to Fe³⁺ → porphyrin flattens → T state is stabilized."
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      O₂ binding converts high-spin Fe²⁺ to a smaller low-spin form that fits into the
      porphyrin plane; because the iron is tethered to the proximal histidine on the F
      helix, that drop is levered into the quaternary T→R rotation. The
      high-spin-and-larger sequence reverses the spin/size change; the distal-histidine
      route gets the trigger wrong, and 2,3-BPG binding stabilizes T, so it cannot drive
      the shift to R; and O₂ binding does not oxidize the iron (that would give
      nonfunctional methemoglobin).
  - stem: "Sickle-cell crises are precipitated by exertion, cold, and dehydration. The main molecular reason these triggers promote sickling is that they:"
    choices:
      - "Raise tissue pO₂, driving hemoglobin into the R state where Val6 is exposed."
      - "Oxidize Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺, which makes HbS fibers form."
      - "Lower tissue pO₂, driving hemoglobin into the T (deoxy) state where the Val6 contact that polymerizes HbS becomes available."
      - "Increase fetal hemoglobin, which co-polymerizes with HbS."
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      HbS, with its Glu6→Val substitution, polymerizes only in the deoxy/T state. Val6
      is on the surface in every state, but the hydrophobic pocket it docks into
      (Phe85/Leu88) on a neighboring HbS molecule is open only in T. Exertion raises oxygen use and cold slows flow through small vessels, so more hemoglobin spends longer in T, and
      dehydration concentrates HbS so fibers form faster. A rise
      in pO₂ has it backwards, iron oxidation is a different pathology (methemoglobin),
      and fetal Hb actually inhibits sickling.

    text: |
      Your blood is full of tiny trucks called hemoglobin that carry oxygen from your lungs
      to the rest of you. The clever part is that the trucks are good at grabbing oxygen
      where there's lots of it (your lungs) and good at dropping it off where there's almost
      none (your busy muscles). Each truck can take a loose-grip shape or a tight-grip shape,
      and grabbing oxygen tips it toward the tight-grip shape, so its other three seats fill
      more easily. That teamwork is why you can run around
      and not run out of air.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: |
      Binding is how proteins recognize specific molecules, and Kd measures how tightly they
      hold on (smaller Kd, tighter grip). Myoglobin stores oxygen in muscle with one binding
      site and a smooth hyperbolic curve. Hemoglobin has four sites that cooperate: the first
      oxygen is hard to load, but it changes the protein's shape and makes the next three
      easier, giving an S-shaped curve. That S-shape is the point, because it lets hemoglobin
      pick up oxygen in the lungs and let go of much of it in hard-working tissues. Things like
      2,3-BPG, acid, and CO₂ nudge the curve so you unload even more oxygen when you exercise.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: |
      Write binding as RL ⇌ R + L so Kd = [R][L]/[RL], with units of molarity; at
      half-saturation [R] = [RL], so Kd = L½. Myoglobin (one chain, one heme, n_H = 1, P50 ≈
      2 torr) is the non-cooperative reference; hemoglobin (α₂β₂, n_H ≈ 2.8, P50 ≈ 26 torr)
      is cooperative. The mechanism: O₂ binding flips heme iron from high-spin to low-spin,
      moving it ~0.4 Å into the porphyrin plane, pulling the proximal His and F helix and
      rotating one αβ dimer ~15° against the other, from the T state toward R. Each binding
      event shifts the T↔R equilibrium, so affinity climbs with occupancy. Allosteric
      effectors (2,3-BPG, H⁺ via the Bohr effect, CO₂ as carbamate) stabilize T and
      right-shift the curve; the steep sigmoid sitting in the physiological pO₂ window is why
      Hb delivers ~66% of its O₂ between 100 and 20 torr versus ~7% for myoglobin (non-cooperative, P50 ≈ 2 torr).
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: |
      Hemoglobin is the canonical homotropic/heterotropic allosteric system. The MWC
      concerted model (a pre-existing T↔R equilibrium with state-specific affinities, ligand

      high-affinity R-like states, and HbS polymerization, which depends on deoxy T-state
      Val6 contacts.

**Your blood has to grab oxygen in the lungs and let go of it in working muscle, and a protein that gripped oxygen harder than hemoglobin does would deliver less, not more.**

Hemoglobin delivers about two-thirds of its oxygen to hard-working muscle. Myoglobin, which uses the same heme (the iron-holding ring that binds oxygen) and the same iron, would deliver about 7%. The difference is not simply that one grips harder. Hemoglobin can change its own grip, oxygen is what changes it, and its grip is tuned so the change happens between the lungs and the muscle.

## The delivery problem: load in the lungs, unload in the tissues

Start with the pressures. In the lungs the partial pressure of oxygen, pO₂ (the share of the gas pressure due to oxygen), is near 100 torr (torr is a unit of pressure; one atmosphere is 760 torr). Blood leaving resting tissue is near 40 torr, and blood leaving hard-working muscle is near 20 torr.

So blood has two opposite jobs. It must load oxygen where oxygen is plentiful and unload it where oxygen is scarce.

**Predict.** Would a protein that binds oxygen more tightly make a better carrier?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. A tight binder fills up in the lungs, but it is still nearly full when it reaches the muscle, and oxygen that stays aboard is not delivered. Myoglobin, the muscle's high-affinity storage protein, is that kind of binder: across the gap from 100 to 20 torr it would unload only about 7%.

</details>

Hemoglobin does both jobs: it gives up about 66% of its oxygen between the lungs and hard-working muscle, but only about a fifth at resting tissue pressures, so blood leaving resting tissue is still mostly full.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/protein-binding-allostery/lungs-to-muscle-delivery.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: red blood cells leave the lungs full of oxygen; after resting tissue they still carry most of it, after hard-working muscle they carry about a third. An inset shows a tight-gripping carrier leaving muscle still nearly full." caption="Hemoglobin loads fully in the lungs (pO2 near 100 torr), gives up only about a fifth in resting tissue (near 40 torr) and about two-thirds in hard-working muscle (near 20 torr). The difference: myoglobin has one binding site, while hemoglobin has four that cooperate, and its grip is set for the pressures between the lungs and the muscle. This lesson is about what that cooperation is.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Hemoglobin is always a mix of two shapes: T, which holds oxygen weakly, and R, which holds it tightly. How full hemoglobin is at any pO₂ is the average of how full each shape would be, weighted by how much of each shape is present. Anything that binds one shape better than the other shifts the mix toward that shape. Oxygen and CO favor R; H⁺, CO₂ and 2,3-BPG favor T. Cooperativity is oxygen shifting the mix toward the shape that binds oxygen best.

This is the two-state picture. It explains the S-shaped curve, the regulators and CO poisoning. It does not explain sickle-cell disease on its own (there the T shape itself turns sticky), and real hemoglobin is a little more complicated than two shapes (Going deeper, "The two-state fit used in this lesson").

To use the rule you need one tool first: a number for how tightly a single site holds its ligand.

## One site: Kd and the hyperbola

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

**Most of life is molecules bumping into each other, and once in a while two of them agree to stick.** The sticking is never permanent. Binding is an equilibrium, a partnership that keeps forming and breaking. A receptor is a protein that binds a specific ligand far more tightly than molecules that look almost right, and that discrimination is what lets a signal be a signal.

To measure how tightly a protein holds a ligand, write the binding reaction in the direction it comes apart:

> RL ⇌ R + L

The dissociation constant is the ratio of what is loose to what is bound:

> **Kd = [R][L] / [RL]**

Kd has units of concentration: in [R][L] / [RL], two concentrations on top and one below leave one. Read it as a stickiness rating with the scale running backwards: a **small Kd means tight binding**, and a large Kd means loose. The most common student error is flipping it. Kd is not Ka, the association constant, which is its reciprocal (Ka = 1/Kd, so a bigger Ka means tighter binding).

### Why Kd is the concentration at half-saturation

Set the free ligand concentration to the value where exactly half the receptor is bound, and call it L½. At that point the bound and free receptor populations are equal, [R] = [RL], and they cancel:

> Kd = [R][L] / [RL] = [L] when [R] = [RL], so **Kd = L½**

So Kd is just the ligand concentration that gets you to half occupancy. That is why a small Kd means tight binding: very little ligand fills half the sites. The same definition gives the whole curve.

Plotted against L, this is a hyperbola. The clean result holds for a single binding site, with no cooperativity and ligand in excess.

### From Kd to P50

For oxygen there is one more step. Oxygen is a gas, and the concentration of a dissolved gas is proportional to its partial pressure. So for a single site, **P50**, the pO₂ at half-saturation, is just Kd measured in torr, and the curve is Y = pO₂ / (P50 + pO₂).

**Myoglobin** is the test case. It is the storage protein in muscle, one polypeptide chain wrapped around one heme. Its binding curve is a plain hyperbola, the same shape as the Kd plot, with a P50 of about 2 torr. There is no cooperativity, because there is nothing for a lone site to cooperate with.

**Predict.** Myoglobin is about 98% full at 100 torr, in the lungs. How full is it at 20 torr, in hard-working muscle?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Still about 91% full: 20 / (2 + 20) ≈ 0.91. Twenty torr is far above its P50, so the site is still nearly always occupied. Myoglobin hands over only about 7% (98 − 91), a sponge that grips oxygen and barely lets go. That suits a storage protein, which holds its oxygen and releases it only when oxygen gets truly scarce.

</details>

## Two shapes: building the S

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Myoglobin has one site and one shape, so its curve is the hyperbola you just built. Hemoglobin has four sites, and the molecule can take either of two overall shapes.

- **T (tense)** is held together by extra salt bridges (attractions between oppositely charged side chains) between the subunits. In this shape each heme holds oxygen weakly. In one illustrative fit of hemoglobin's curve, a T site is half full only at about 110 torr.
- **R (relaxed)** has fewer of those bridges, and each heme holds oxygen tightly. In the same fit, an R site is half full at about 0.37 torr.

A molecule does not have to wait for oxygen to change shape. Even with no oxygen at all, it flickers between T and R, but T is so much more stable that R molecules are almost absent.

So at any moment, a sample of hemoglobin is a *mix* of the two shapes. How full is the mix? Ask how full each shape would be on its own, then average, counting each shape in proportion to how much of it there is.

That example hides the key move. In the same fit, f_R is about 0.2% at 5 torr; by 20 and 100 torr it has climbed to a fifth and 96%. **Oxygen changed the mix.** Why?

Compare a T molecule and an R molecule that each pick up one oxygen. R holds oxygen far more tightly, so every oxygen bound makes R a little more favorable compared with T. It is Le Chatelier's principle applied to shape: oxygen is a reactant that R uses far better, so adding oxygen pulls the T ⇌ R balance toward R.

Follow one molecule as it fills. The numbers are from the same illustrative fit; the pattern, not the exact values, is the point.

| Oxygens bound | Odds that the molecule is R |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | T wins by millions to one |
| 1 | T still wins by tens of thousands to one |
| 2 | T wins by a few hundred to one |
| 3 | roughly even: R slightly ahead |
| 4 | R wins by hundreds to one |

Each oxygen multiplies the odds of R by the same large factor, about 300 in this fit (110 / 0.37), and that factor is how much more tightly R holds oxygen than T does. That is where the S comes from.

- **At low pO₂** almost every molecule is T, so the curve follows T's weak hyperbola and barely rises. The first oxygens are hard to load.
- **Through the middle** each bound oxygen tips more molecules into R, and an R molecule's empty sites are suddenly easy to fill. Fullness climbs faster than either shape alone would allow. This is the steep part.
- **At high pO₂** nearly everything is R, so the curve flattens onto R's hyperbola near full.

So binding gets easier as the molecule fills, and most of the gain comes late, as the mix tips to R (Going deeper, "Step by step").

**Predict.** Suppose T and R held oxygen equally well. What shape would the curve have?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A plain hyperbola. If both shapes bind equally, oxygen gives neither an advantage, so the mix never shifts, and the curve is just one single-site curve. The S needs two shapes that *differ* in affinity, plus oxygen that shifts the mix between them. In the model, setting the two affinities equal gives a Hill coefficient of exactly 1.

</details>

This is **cooperativity**. In the two-state picture no separate message has to pass from subunit to subunit: the four sites are coupled because they share one overall shape, carried across the contacts between the subunits, so whatever shifts the shape changes all four at once. That shared shape is how the subunits talk.

The **Hill coefficient**, n_H, scores how steep the S is at half-saturation: it is the slope there of a Hill plot, log[Y/(1 − Y)] against log pO₂. For identical, independent sites it is 1, as for myoglobin. It can be no larger than the number of sites, so 4 for hemoglobin. Human hemoglobin's is about 2.8: strong cooperation that stops short of the limit. It is a measure of steepness, not a count of anything.

Steepness alone does not make a good carrier. Where the steep part sits matters just as much, and P50 sets that. Hemoglobin's P50 is about 26 torr, against myoglobin's 2. Compare four curves between 100 and 20 torr:

| Carrier | P50 | n_H | Oxygen delivered, 100 → 20 torr |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Myoglobin | 2 torr | 1 | about 7% |
| A hyperbola with hemoglobin's P50 | 26 torr | 1 | about 36% |
| A cooperative carrier with myoglobin's P50 | 2 torr | 2.8 | almost none |
| Hemoglobin | 26 torr | 2.8 | about two-thirds |

The third row is the surprise: a steep curve in the wrong place delivers almost nothing, because it is still nearly full at 20 torr. Hemoglobin needs both: an S, and an S whose steep part sits between the lungs and the muscle.

## The switch inside the molecule

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

Why does R hold oxygen better than T? The answer starts with a movement smaller than an atom.

Hemoglobin is four chains, two α and two β, arranged as a pair of αβ dimers (α₂β₂). Each chain cradles one heme with a single iron atom. Heme is protoporphyrin IX with an iron(II) at its center. The iron makes four bonds to the porphyrin's nitrogen atoms, holds a fifth bond to a histidine reaching up from the protein (the **proximal histidine**), and saves its sixth coordination site for oxygen. The iron must stay Fe²⁺; oxidize it to Fe³⁺ and you get methemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen at all. Across the pocket sits the **distal histidine**, which hydrogen-bonds to bound oxygen and steadies it.

T and R are hemoglobin's two classic quaternary states (arrangements of the four chains): T is the deoxy form, R the oxy form, and the molecule also visits other relaxed shapes. In Max Perutz's classic account, four linked movements lead from T to R.

1. **The iron moves into the ring.** In the deoxy T state the iron is high-spin and slightly too big to fit the porphyrin hole, so it sits about 0.4 Å out of the ring plane, on the side of the proximal histidine. That is about a seventh the width of a water molecule. (High-spin means some of the iron's electrons sit in orbitals that point at the ring's nitrogens, which makes the ion effectively bigger.) When oxygen binds on the other face, the iron flips to low-spin, pulls those electrons in, shrinks and moves into the plane.
2. **The histidine follows.** The iron is bonded to the proximal histidine, so the histidine is pulled along.
3. **The helix shifts.** The histidine sits on a helix, the F helix, and that helix presses against the neighboring subunit.
4. **The dimers rotate.** The twitch at the iron is levered into roughly a 15° rotation of one αβ dimer against the other, and the salt bridges that held the T state break.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/protein-binding-allostery/perutz-switch-steps.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: four steps. Oxygen binds and the heme iron moves from just below the flat heme into its plane; the proximal histidine beneath it moves up; the F helix carrying the histidine tilts against a neighbouring subunit; one alpha-beta dimer rotates against the other and the salt bridges between them break." caption="Perutz's chain from T to R. Oxygen binding lets the iron, about 0.4 Å out of the heme plane in deoxy hemoglobin, move into the plane. The proximal histidine and the F helix follow, and the movement is levered into a roughly 15° rotation of one αβ dimer against the other that breaks the T-state salt bridges. The dashed arrow marks the hedge: each iron shift raises the odds of the switch rather than forcing it. Sizes and movements are exaggerated." />

Read backwards, the same chain says why T is the weak binder: in T the salt bridges resist these movements, so binding oxygen is less favorable there. **Structure talks to structure.** A protein takes a sub-ångström event at one iron and broadcasts it across the whole molecule.

One caveat matters for the rule. Later work shows the coupling is looser than a gear train: each iron shift raises the odds of the switch rather than forcing it. That is just what the two-state picture needs, since each oxygen bound makes R more likely, not certain. Perutz saw the two end points by solving deoxy- and oxyhemoglobin side by side; open 2HHB and 1HHO in the structures panel to compare them.

Whether the four subunits switch all together (the concerted model) or one at a time (the sequential model) is a long-running question, and real hemoglobin fits neither perfectly.

## Tuning the mix: allosteric regulators

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

Cooperativity sets the baseline. The rule then predicts what any other molecule will do. If it binds T better than R, it shifts the mix toward T, so it takes more oxygen to tip the population to R and the curve moves right, toward higher pO₂ on the x-axis: a higher P50 and lower affinity. Three regulators work this way.

**2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG)** is a small, ferociously charged anion (around four to five negative charges), present in red cells at nearly 1:1 with hemoglobin. It wedges into a central pocket between the β chains that is wide open only in the T state (relaxed forms narrow it and bind BPG far more weakly).

**Predict.** Add 2,3-BPG to purified hemoglobin. Which way does P50 move?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Up: the curve shifts right. BPG binds T and acts as a mechanical doorstop that holds the molecule tense, so more oxygen is needed to tip the mix to R. At a given tissue pO₂, more of the oxygen is let go.

</details>

BPG is the slower dial, compared with the fast H⁺ and CO₂ signals below. It rises in your red cells over hours to days at high altitude, which is part of how you acclimatize, and its response to exercise varies.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/protein-binding-allostery/bpg-doorstop-t-vs-r.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: in the T shape, a wedge of 2,3-BPG sits in a wide pocket between hemoglobin's two beta chains; in the R shape the pocket narrows and the wedge is excluded. An inset shows fetal hemoglobin, whose gamma chains hold the wedge only loosely." caption="2,3-BPG is a doorstop for the T shape. It binds the wide central pocket between the β chains in T, while R narrows that pocket and binds it far more weakly, so BPG shifts the mix toward T and moves the curve right. Fetal hemoglobin's γ chains (serine instead of histidine at position 143) grip BPG weakly, so HbF stays at higher oxygen affinity. Schematic." />

**Hydrogen ions** act through the Bohr effect. Protonating certain groups on hemoglobin, chiefly a histidine near the end of each β chain, lets them form salt bridges that steady the T state, so low pH lowers oxygen affinity. Lung blood runs slightly alkaline (pH ~7.4) and favors loading; respiring tissue is more acidic (venous blood ~7.36, lower in working muscle) and favors unloading. At rest that gap is small. The effect is large where it counts: working muscle makes acid, and the acid tips hemoglobin toward tense exactly where a straining muscle needs the oxygen.

**Carbon dioxide** binds as carbamate groups (CO₂ bonded to an amino group) on hemoglobin's N-termini, which form their own T-stabilizing salt bridges. Carbonic anhydrase, the enzyme that turns most CO₂ into bicarbonate, also supplies the Bohr protons.

During exercise, three fast signals arrive at once in working muscle: falling pO₂, falling pH and rising CO₂.

**Does a right shift spoil loading in the lungs?** H⁺ and CO₂ are high in tissue, not in the lungs, so their shift happens where unloading happens. BPG shifts the curve everywhere, but the lung end sits on the flat top of the curve, so a modest shift costs little loading. A very large shift would start to cost loading (Going deeper, "How big a shift helps").

Fetal hemoglobin runs the rule the other way. Fetal hemoglobin (α₂γ₂) has a serine where the adult β chain has a histidine at position 143, so it binds 2,3-BPG weakly. With less BPG holding it in T, it stays at higher oxygen affinity and pulls oxygen across the placenta from the mother's blood.

Evolution can also tune the shapes themselves. The bar-headed goose, which flies over the Himalayas, carries a hemoglobin with higher oxygen affinity that loads oxygen better from thin air.

## When the shapes betray you: hemoglobin pathology

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

The rule explains one failure and sets up the other: in CO poisoning the mix is locked toward R, and in sickle-cell disease the rule's T shape is the one that turns dangerous.

**Carbon monoxide** is the R-state lock. It binds the heme about 250-fold more tightly than oxygen does, and like oxygen it favors R.

**Predict.** One subunit of a hemoglobin carries CO, and the other three carry oxygen. Is the oxygen on those three held more or less tightly than usual?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

More tightly. CO holds hemoglobin in the high-affinity R state, so the oxygen still aboard the other subunits is released far less readily, and the curve shifts left. That is double damage: fewer sites carry oxygen, and the oxygen that is carried stays put in the tissues.

</details>

CO-hemoglobin is bright red, though the classic cherry-red skin is rarely seen in living patients. A pulse oximeter, which cannot tell O₂-hemoglobin from CO-hemoglobin, reads falsely normal while tissues suffocate. The fix is to flood the system with high-concentration oxygen to compete the CO back off; hyperbaric oxygen is reserved for selected severe cases.

**Sickle-cell disease** is the textbook molecular disease, and here the T shape itself turns sticky. **One amino acid. Glutamate to valine, at position 6 of the β chain.** That valine sits on the surface in every state, but the hydrophobic pocket it docks into (Phe85, Leu88) on a neighboring hemoglobin is open only in the deoxy T shape. So in T, the valine of one molecule plugs into the pocket of the next, which plugs into the next. The molecules polymerize into stiff fibers that deform the red cell into a crescent.

So the trigger is deoxygenation. Crises come with exertion, which raises oxygen use, and with cold, which slows blood through small vessels. Exertion and cold leave more hemoglobin in T for longer. Dehydration concentrates HbS, so fibers form faster. Carriers (one copy) are usually healthy and are partly protected against severe falciparum malaria, which is why the mutation persists.

One of the newest treatments, Casgevy, uses CRISPR to switch fetal hemoglobin back on in red-cell precursors. HbF does not join the sickle polymer and dilutes the HbS around it, so cells sickle far less.

The thalassemias fail differently again: the problem is mostly not a broken chain but the wrong amounts of chain.

That is the lesson in one line. Hemoglobin is a mix of a weak shape and a tight shape, and oxygen shifting that mix is why blood loads in the lungs and gives up about two-thirds of its oxygen in hard-working muscle. A 0.4 Å iron twitch, levered into a ~15° dimer rotation, makes the switch; acid, CO₂ and BPG tune the mix; CO locks it; and a single wrong amino acid makes the T shape stick. Sickle-cell anemia is what happens when structure talks, and structure lies.

## Going deeper

The main line above does not depend on these details.

### The two-state fit used in this lesson

The numbers marked "one illustrative fit" come from fitting a two-state (concerted) model to human hemoglobin's curve: P50 = 26 torr, a Hill coefficient of 2.8 at P50, and 97.5% full at 100 torr. One fit puts an R site half full at about 0.37 torr and a T site half full at about 110 torr, with T outnumbering R about 1.7 × 10⁷ to one when no oxygen is bound. Fitted two-state parameters are not unique, so other fits give other numbers with the same pattern.

The picture has limits. Real hemoglobin also changes shape within each quaternary state (tertiary T and R), so its Hill coefficient of about 2.8 is not a clean two-state parameter.

One trap: the rule averages how full each shape is, not their Kd values. Averaging the two shapes' half-full pressures by population at P50 gives about 67 torr, far from the true 26.

### Step by step: the measured binding constants

The old summary, "the second oxygen comes a little easier, the third easier still, the fourth easiest of all", is right as a trend, but measured step-by-step (Adair) constants do not rise evenly: the last oxygen binds on the order of a hundred times more tightly than the first.

### How big a shift helps

In the two-state fit, tripling T's advantage, as a T-favoring effector would, moves P50 from 26 to about 35 torr and raises delivery between 100 and 20 torr from about 64% to about 71%. A tenfold increase moves P50 to about 48 torr, and delivery falls back from 71% to about 66%, because the curve no longer fills fully at 100 torr. A tenfold shift toward R moves P50 to about 14 torr and cuts delivery to about 24%, the direction CO pushes. (For normal hemoglobin the two-state fit gives about 64% and the Hill curve used in the opening and the sandbox about 65%, only because they are different idealized curves fitted to the same P50 and steepness; the lesson rounds both to two-thirds.) The sandbox's BPG preset uses the Hill equation with the Hill coefficient held at 2.8, so it shows a somewhat larger gain than the two-state model.

### Inside the red blood cell

The hemoglobin in this lesson does its work packed inside red blood cells, which also hold 2,3-BPG at nearly 1:1 with it. About 5 million red blood cells sit in a microliter of your blood.

### Pressure bookkeeping

Where does the lesson's lung value of about 100 torr come from? Dry sea-level air is 21% oxygen, so about 159 torr of the 760, and by the time air reaches the alveoli, humidified and mixed with CO₂, pO₂ has fallen to roughly 100 torr.

### How picky binding gets

A receptor earns the name by binding its specific ligand far more tightly than molecules that look almost right, and Kd is how that pickiness is scored. Inside the cell, the estrogen receptor binds estradiol far more tightly than testosterone, a steroid with the same four-ring core that differs in only a few chemical groups.

You will mostly interpret Kd values, not compute them. Three ranges are worth memorizing. Enzymes often hold their metabolites loosely, in the micromolar to millimolar range, which suits molecules that must be released after each reaction. Hormones and their receptors bind in the nanomolar range. Antibodies, refined by your immune system, can reach picomolar, and that refinement is not metaphorical: booster shots can drive further rounds of selection for B cells that make lower-Kd antibodies, so antibody binding often gets stickier over time.

### Myoglobin in the clinic

Myoglobin, the single-site example from earlier in this lesson, is the oxygen-storage protein of muscle. When myoglobin leaks into the blood after a heart attack or crush injury, clinicians read that escaped storage protein as a marker of muscle damage.

### Magnetic hemoglobin and functional MRI

The iron switch has a clinical echo. Deoxyhemoglobin is paramagnetic (its iron carries unpaired electrons) and oxyhemoglobin is diamagnetic. Functional MRI uses that difference: active brain regions pull in more oxygenated blood, which lowers the local amount of paramagnetic deoxyhemoglobin, and the magnetic signal shifts. The same change in the iron's electrons that lets it slide into the ring is what the scanner picks up, summed over vast numbers of molecules.

### CO and the distal histidine

Oxygen binds bent, carbon monoxide prefers to bind straight, and the distal histidine favors oxygen, mostly by hydrogen-bonding to it, cutting CO's advantage roughly 100-fold. That discrimination is a big part of why we tolerate the trace CO our own cells produce. CO's 250-fold edge in hemoglobin is what is left after that cut; on bare heme the gap is far larger.

### Concerted or sequential?

There are two textbook models for how four subunits switch. In the concerted (MWC) model all four flip together, and ligand binding only nudges the T↔R balance. In the sequential (KNF) model each subunit changes one at a time. Hemoglobin's quaternary switch looks concerted, but each subunit's own shape also shifts as it binds, and how the two levels combine is still studied.

### Which groups carry the Bohr effect

Protonating histidine β146 (favored at low pH) lets it form a salt bridge with aspartate β94 that steadies the T state; this is the biggest single part of the Bohr effect, and other groups, such as the α-chain N-termini, add the rest.

### How CO₂ travels

Carbon dioxide travels three ways: under a tenth dissolved, about 70% as bicarbonate (made by carbonic anhydrase), and the rest as carbamate groups bound to hemoglobin's N-termini. Together with more blood flow to working muscle, the three exercise signals help push whole-body oxygen extraction from about 25% at rest to about 70% in heavy exercise.

### The thalassemias

The thalassemias are the quantitative cousins of sickle-cell disease, and they can be just as severe. Too few β chains and the leftover α chains precipitate and wreck the cell; too few α chains and the surplus β chains assemble into β₄ tetramers (HbH; before birth, surplus γ chains form γ₄, Hb Bart's) that bind oxygen tightly but with no cooperativity, a delivery truck that forgot how to open its doors. Matched α-to-β synthesis matters because imbalance is toxic either way.

### How the sickle-cell gene edit works

Casgevy, approved in late 2023, uses CRISPR to disrupt the erythroid enhancer of BCL11A, a repressor that silences the fetal γ-globin genes after birth. Less BCL11A in red-cell precursors switches fetal hemoglobin back on.
