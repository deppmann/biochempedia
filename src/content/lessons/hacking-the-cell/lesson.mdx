techniques:
  - name: "Genetically encoded fluorescent reporters"
    blurb: >-
      You join the gene for a fluorescent protein to the gene for a detector, such as calmodulin or
      a PH domain, and the cell makes the reporter itself. A fluorescence microscope then records
      the reporter's brightness, position or color over time in living cells, which tells you when
      and where the signal happened.
  - name: "Optogenetic stimulation"
    blurb: >-
      You express a microbial opsin such as channelrhodopsin-2 in chosen cells and deliver light,
      in a dish from a lamp or laser and in an animal through an optical fiber. Recording the
      cell's voltage or the animal's behavior during the light tells you whether activity in those
      cells is enough to cause the effect.
  - name: "Gene targeting and Cre/lox conditional genetics"
    blurb: >-
      You change one chosen gene in embryonic stem cells by homologous recombination, make chimeric
      mice from those cells, and breed the change into a line. With loxP sites placed around a
      piece of DNA, Cre recombinase removes that piece in the cells that make Cre and in their descendants, so a gene
      can be removed or switched on in largely one cell type.
  - name: "Single-molecule localization microscopy (PALM)"
    blurb: >-
      You switch on a few photoactivatable fluorescent proteins at a time, find the center of each
      separate blur, and repeat for thousands of camera frames. The summed positions give an image
      with detail of tens of nanometers, well below the diffraction limit of about 200 nanometers.

  - stem: "Which statement correctly separates a reporter from an actuator?"
    choices:
      - "A reporter starts a signal on command; an actuator shows the signal as it happens"
      - "A reporter is always a small dye; an actuator is always a fluorescent protein"
      - "A reporter works only in fixed cells; an actuator works only in living cells"
      - "A reporter shows a signal as it happens; an actuator starts a signal on command"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      A reporter, such as GFP joined to a detector, changes its light when a signal happens, and an
      actuator, such as channelrhodopsin, starts a signal when you supply light or a drug. The
      choice in which the reporter starts the signal has the two words swapped. The choice about
      fixed cells is wrong because both tools are made to work in living cells. The choice about
      dyes is wrong because the reporters in this lesson are proteins made from a gene, and an
      actuator need not be fluorescent at all.
  - stem: "In 1992, E. coli carrying only the protein-coding sequence of the jellyfish GFP gene fluoresced green. Which idea about GFP did this result rule out?"
    choices:
      - "That GFP must have calcium bound before it can give off any light"
      - "That GFP must be finished by a jellyfish enzyme before it glows"
      - "That GFP must have oxygen before its light-emitting group can form"
      - "That GFP must absorb light of a shorter wavelength than it emits"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Bacteria have no jellyfish enzymes, so if the gene alone gives green light, no jellyfish enzyme
      can be needed (later work showed that the protein forms its own light-emitting group with only oxygen). The oxygen idea was not ruled out and is true: the
      bacteria grew in air, and the group needs oxygen to form. Calcium is what triggers aequorin,
      a different jellyfish protein, and the experiment did not test it. The wavelength idea
      describes how fluorescence ordinarily works and held for the glowing bacteria too.
  - stem: "In a GCaMP calcium reporter, what does calmodulin contribute?"
    choices:
      - "It absorbs blue light and then hands the energy on to the attached GFP"
      - "It carries the reporter to the membrane, where calcium channels open"
      - "It binds calcium and changes shape, which brightens the attached GFP"
      - "It pumps calcium out of the cytosol, which then dims the attached GFP"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Calmodulin is the detector. It binds calcium and wraps around the neighboring peptide, and
      that movement changes the surroundings of the light-emitting group so that GFP brightens.
      Calmodulin does not absorb visible light, so it cannot feed energy to GFP; that choice
      confuses a detector with a FRET partner. Calmodulin has no membrane anchor, and GCaMP reports
      calcium wherever the reporter happens to be. Calmodulin is not a pump: it binds calcium and
      does not move it across a membrane.
  - stem: "Channelrhodopsin and the rhodopsin of your retina both hold retinal among seven membrane-crossing helices. What does channelrhodopsin do that rhodopsin does not?"
    choices:
      - "It activates a G protein, which then closes a cation channel"
      - "It opens a pore of its own, which then lets cations through"
      - "It pumps chloride inward, which then holds the neuron silent"
      - "It absorbs a photon, which then changes the shape of its retinal"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Channelrhodopsin is itself the ion channel, so light leads to current with no relay.
      Activating a G protein that leads to the closing of a channel is what rhodopsin does in a rod
      cell, so it is the reverse of what was asked. Absorbing a photon that changes retinal is
      common to both proteins. Pumping chloride inward describes halorhodopsin, a different
      microbial opsin that is used to silence neurons.
  - stem: "You need one opsin in neuron type A and a different opsin in neuron type B of the same mouse. Which design does this?"
    choices:
      - "Cre in type A only, with both opsin genes behind strong viral promoters"
      - "Flp in both types, with each opsin gene placed between two loxP sites"
      - "Cre in both types, with both opsin genes behind a single lox-stop-lox"
      - "Cre in type A and Flp in type B, each switching on one opsin gene"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Cre acts only at lox sites and Flp only at its own sites, so two enzymes made in two cell
      types can each remove the stop in front of a different opsin gene. Cre in both types would
      switch on the same genes in both, so the two types would not differ. Opsin genes behind
      strong promoters do not wait for Cre, so every cell that received them would make both. Flp
      does not recognize loxP sites, and a gene placed between two sites would be removed by the
      matching enzyme instead of switched on.

**Two engineered protein classes let you work on a signal in a living cell: a reporter watches where and when it happens, and an actuator pushes or stops it on command.** The first film paired a calcium reporter with a light-gated channel; here we examine why GFP needs no jellyfish enzyme, expand the toolkit to lipids, pH, kinases and drugs, and then turn to genetic targeting and super-resolution microscopy.

<Support>**The one idea.** Reporters turn signals into light, and actuators control them on command; promoters and recombinases place each tool in chosen cells. **Scope:** Applies to genetically encoded optical and chemical tools, including simple fluorescent tags that mark location. Reporters can buffer or blur fast signals, actuators can shift extra ions or need drug controls, Cre cuts are permanent across descendants and can leak, and resolving molecules closer than about 200 nanometers requires super-resolution methods such as PALM.</Support>

## GFP and the reporter toolkit

A **reporter** is an engineered protein whose light changes with a signal; an **actuator** changes a cell's activity on command, starting or stopping a signal (like a voltmeter versus a switch, or an electrode). Using light and a gene to control cells is called **optogenetics**, a word often stretched to cover reporters used alongside it.

In **fluorescence**, absorbing a photon lifts an electron; some energy leaves as heat before a longer-wavelength photon is emitted (blue in, green out). In 1992, moving only the protein-coding stretch of jellyfish **green fluorescent protein (GFP)** into *E. coli* made the bacteria glow green—ruling out the assumption that a jellyfish enzyme had to finish it.

Structure explains why: GFP folds into a barrel with a central helix whose three neighboring amino acids react, lose water, and are oxidized by oxygen from the air into a rigid ring absorbing violet and blue light and emitting green. Because the recipe is the sequence itself, many cells with the gene and oxygen build their own lamp—though the first version folded poorly in warm cells—and fusing another gene to GFP tags that protein directly.

**Predict.** You express the GFP gene in human cells kept at 37 °C in an oxygen-free chamber. Will they glow green?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. No jellyfish enzyme is needed, but forming GFP's light-emitting ring requires molecular oxygen—and the original jellyfish protein also folded poorly in warm mammalian cells before improved variants were engineered.

</details>

### Four ways to wire a reporter

The simplest fusion marks **location**: GFP fused to gephyrin, a scaffold under inhibitory synapses, lights up a neuron's dendrites in rows of bright dots.

To track an active **signal**, fuse GFP to a signaling detector so the signal alters **location**, **brightness**, or **color ratio**:

- **Location — lipid reporter.** A PH domain binding PIP3 (see the lesson on lipid anchors and addresses), fused to GFP, shifts from cytosol to membrane when PI 3-kinase makes PIP3.
- **Brightness — pH reporter.** An acid-dimming GFP facing inside a synaptic vesicle (pH about 5.5) brightens on fusion when it meets extracellular fluid at pH 7.4 (a roughly 80-fold drop in proton concentration).
- **Brightness — calcium reporter (GCaMP).** Expanding on the first film: engineers join GFP's original ends and cut new ends beside the chromophore so water dims the barrel. Calmodulin (from the calcium lesson) on one new end and its target peptide on the other wrap together when calcium rises, plugging the gap and shutting water out.
- **Color ratio — kinase reporter (FRET).** Place a kinase substrate peptide and a phospho-binding domain (an SH2 domain for a tyrosine kinase) between cyan (donor) and yellow (acceptor) fluorescent proteins. Phosphorylation folds the chain so the pair sit within a few nanometers, letting cyan pass energy to yellow without a photon (**Förster resonance energy transfer, FRET**) and raising the yellow-to-cyan ratio; phosphatases reverse it.

**Predict.** In a cell with a tyrosine-kinase FRET reporter, a drug blocks phosphatases while basal kinase activity continues. Does the yellow-to-cyan ratio rise or fall?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It rises. The reporter tracks the kinase-phosphatase balance; blocking phosphatases lets phosphorylated, folded molecules accumulate, keeping donor and acceptor close and increasing transfer to yellow.

</details>

A reporter also perturbs what it reads: a calcium reporter buffers some of the calcium it measures, and its binding and shape changes take time, so in slower variants a fast spike train can blur into one slow rise.

## Actuators: light or drugs in, signal out

In the lesson on the senses and cGMP, retinal **rhodopsin** uses seven helices and retinal to trigger a G protein. As the first film showed, green-algal **channelrhodopsin-2** skips that relay—it is itself a cation channel opened within milliseconds by blue light. Already shown to work in mammalian cells, it was used in 2005 by Edward Boyden, Feng Zhang, Ernst Bamberg, Georg Nagel and Karl Deisseroth to make cultured hippocampal neurons fire action potentials in step with millisecond blue flashes; other laboratories reported similar results about the same time.

| Actuator | Mechanism | Light | Effect and key limit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Channelrhodopsin-2** (and relatives) | Direct non-selective cation channel | Blue (other variants use other colors) | Cations enter, firing the neuron; some relatives pass anions instead. |
| **Halorhodopsins** | Chloride pump (1 Cl⁻/photon) | Yellow | Drives Cl⁻ in to suppress firing; needs bright light, and Cl⁻ loading makes the chloride gradient shallower, weakening inhibition. Light-gated Cl⁻ channels are now used more often for silencing. |
| **OptoXRs** | Chimeric GPCR (inner loops from another GPCR) | Green | Starts that chosen receptor's G-protein pathway instead of opening a pore. |

A virus can deliver an opsin to one brain region—though the **adeno-associated virus (AAV)** vectors used most hold only a short promoter—with light supplied through an optical fiber.

For wireless control over minutes to hours without a fiber, **chemogenetics** substitutes a drug:

- **DREADDs (Designer Receptors Exclusively Activated by Designer Drugs):** engineered GPCRs that barely answer their natural ligand and answer a synthetic drug instead, so in the main only cells with the receptor react. A drug control is essential: **clozapine-N-oxide (CNO)**, used most, converts into clozapine in the body, which acts on the animal's own receptors.
- **TRPV1** (from the lesson on ion channels): expressing TRPV1 in one neuron type of a TRPV1 knock-out mouse lets capsaicin open a cation channel in those neurons alone (Güler and colleagues, 2012).

**Predict.** To silence one neuron population for two hours in a freely moving mouse without an implanted fiber, which actuator fits best, and what control does CNO require?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

An inhibitory Gi-coupled DREADD fits best: opsins require a fiber, and halorhodopsin also loads chloride over long exposures. Give the same CNO dose to control mice lacking the DREADD to separate receptor effects from clozapine.

</details>

Every reporter, opsin, or DREADD above is a protein encoded by DNA, which raises the second half of the problem: how to put its gene into one chosen cell type in a living mouse.

## Genetic addressing: putting a tool into one cell type

The second film showed why a ubiquitous promoter lights up every cell and how Cre removes a stop block; here are the genome-editing routes that build those mice and the logic for targeting two cell types.

### Transgenes, gene targeting, and CRISPR

A **transgene** pairs a **promoter** (where and when a gene is read) with a **coding sequence** (what is made). Injected into a fertilized mouse egg, it sometimes integrates at a random chromosomal site and is inherited if it reaches the germ line. In 1982 Richard Palmiter and colleagues paired a metallothionein promoter with a growth hormone gene to produce giant mice; a ubiquitous promoter with GFP yields a green mouse.

To edit one chosen gene instead, work in **embryonic stem cells** (early-embryo cells able to form any tissue): flanking DNA swaps your edit into the target locus by homologous recombination. Destroying the gene is a **knock-out**; inserting something new (such as GFP or Cre) is a **knock-in**. Altered cells injected into an early embryo form a chimera whose germ-line offspring can inherit the edit (see Going deeper for selection and breeding). **CRISPR** now shortens this path: an RNA guide directs a DNA-cutting enzyme to a matching sequence in a fertilized egg, so many knock-outs and knock-ins no longer need stem cells or chimeras.

### Conditional control with Cre, loxP, and Flp

**Cre**, from a bacterial virus, cuts and rejoins DNA at a 34-base-pair **loxP** site. Two same-direction loxP sites turn that single cutting reaction into an **off** or **on** switch:

- **Conditional knock-out (off):** Flank an essential part of a gene with loxP sites. Cre removes it where Cre is made—so the gene is lost there and, in the main, nowhere else.
- **Conditional expression (on):** Place a stop signal between two loxP sites ahead of the tool. Cre cuts the stop out permanently, so that cell and its descendants read the gene wherever the tool's promoter is active (**lineage tracing**). Give Cre several fluorescent proteins to pick among at random, and neighboring cells take different colors.

**Predict.** You flank a coding exon of a kinase gene (not a stop block) with two same-direction loxP sites and express Cre in neuron type A. What happens in type A versus other cells?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Cre excises the exon, knocking out the kinase in neuron type A and its descendants, while cells without Cre keep the exon and make the kinase normally.

</details>

Because the cut is permanent, **every Cre line must be checked with a reporter**: brief developmental or germ-line expression can cut DNA in unintended cells too.

For two tools in two neuron types of one mouse, pair Cre with yeast **Flp**, which cuts only its own sites:

| Cell type | Recombinase | Tool 1 (loxP-stop-loxP) | Tool 2 (Flp-flanked stop) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Neuron type A | Cre only | **On** (stop cut) | Off |
| Neuron type B | Flp only | Off | **On** (stop cut) |
| Other cells | Neither | Off | Off |

## Past the limit of light

Even with a reporter in the right cell type, a lens cannot focus light to a point: one molecule spreads into a blur, and two molecules closer than about half a wavelength merge. Ernst Abbe wrote the limit in the 1870s:

> **smallest separable distance ≈ wavelength ÷ (2 × numerical aperture)**

**Numerical aperture** measures how wide a light cone the lens collects—about 1.4 for a very good lens—giving a limit near 200 nanometers for green light, compared with about 40 nanometers across a synaptic vesicle.

**Predict.** Using 520 nm green light and a lens of numerical aperture 1.4, can a standard microscope separate two GFP-tagged proteins 50 nm apart on a synaptic vesicle?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No: 520 ÷ (2 × 1.4) ≈ 186 nm, far wider than 50 nm, so their blurs merge.

</details>

One bypass came through Jennifer Lippincott-Schwartz's NIH laboratory. In 2002 postdoc George Patterson built a photoactivatable GFP that starts dark and switches on with violet light. In 2005 physicist Eric Betzig proposed a microscope needing such a probe, and she gave Betzig and Harald Hess lab space. Their **PALM** method (published in 2006 alongside Xiaowei Zhuang's **STORM** and Samuel Hess's **FPALM**) turns on a sparse few molecules so blurs never overlap, locates each blur's center, lets them bleach, and repeats thousands of times.

In the shot-noise approximation (neglecting background and pixel size), **center uncertainty ≈ blur standard deviation ÷ √(photons collected)**: a 100 nm blur giving 100 photons is centered to about 100 ÷ 10 = 10 nm, yielding tens-of-nanometers detail. Other routes include **STED** (Stefan Hell, proposed 1994, shown 1999: a ring beam switches off fluorescence around the rim) and **structured illumination** (computing finer detail from striped lighting).

### Design problem

Put both halves of the rule to work: pick a reporter and an actuator, then use promoters and recombinases to place them in chosen cells. First, how can you make a green animal? Second, design a mouse in which only one neuron type glows when calcium rises and can be switched on with light—naming the reporter, actuator, and targeting strategy.

<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The green animal is a transgene: a ubiquitous promoter joined to GFP, injected into fertilized eggs.

1. **Address:** Cre knocked into a gene read only by your neuron type.
2. **Reporter:** A calcium reporter behind a lox-flanked stop.

A red-shifted channelrhodopsin does not fix this, because retinal itself still absorbs blue light. </details>

## Going deeper

### How GFP became a tool

From 1961 Osamu Shimomura and Frank Johnson extracted and purified aequorin, a jellyfish protein that gives off blue light when calcium binds it. Traces of a second protein, green and fluorescent, came along in the extract. In 1971 Morin and Hastings renamed it green fluorescent protein, GFP.

Until 1992 nobody knew whether the GFP gene alone would be enough. The common guess was that a jellyfish enzyme had to finish the protein. That year Douglas Prasher published the cloned gene, and in Martin Chalfie's laboratory at Columbia the rotation student Ghia Euskirchen put its coding stretch into *E. coli*. The bacteria fluoresced green.

Once the protein folds, serine 65, tyrosine 66 and glycine 67 on the central helix react with one another, lose water, and are oxidized by oxygen from the air.

### Who showed channelrhodopsins are channels

Georg Nagel, Peter Hegemann, Ernst Bamberg and colleagues had shown in 2002 and 2003 that channelrhodopsins from the green alga *Chlamydomonas* are light-gated channels, and that channelrhodopsin-2 passes cations and works in mammalian cells.

### Selecting and breeding targeted mice

Drugs select the rare correct cells (random insertions keep an added herpes thymidine-kinase gene, and ganciclovir kills the cells that keep it). The chosen cells, from a strain with a brown 'agouti' coat, are injected into an early embryo of a black strain. The pup is a chimera, a patchwork of both, and if the stem cells formed its sperm or eggs, its agouti offspring can carry the change.
