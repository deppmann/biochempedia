techniques:
  - name: "Genetically encoded fluorescent reporters"
    blurb: >-
      You join the gene for a fluorescent protein to the gene for a detector, such as calmodulin or
      a PH domain, and the cell makes the reporter itself. A fluorescence microscope then records
      the reporter's brightness, position or color over time in living cells, which tells you when
      and where the signal happened.
  - name: "Optogenetic stimulation"
    blurb: >-
      You express a microbial opsin such as channelrhodopsin-2 in chosen cells and deliver light,
      in a dish from a lamp or laser and in an animal through an optical fiber. Recording the
      cell's voltage or the animal's behavior during the light tells you whether activity in those
      cells is enough to cause the effect.
  - name: "Gene targeting and Cre/lox conditional genetics"
    blurb: >-
      You change one chosen gene in embryonic stem cells by homologous recombination, make chimeric
      mice from those cells, and breed the change into a line. With loxP sites placed around a
      piece of DNA, Cre recombinase removes that piece only in the cells that make Cre, so a gene
      can be removed or switched on in one cell type.
  - name: "Single-molecule localization microscopy (PALM)"
    blurb: >-
      You switch on a few photoactivatable fluorescent proteins at a time, find the center of each
      separate blur, and repeat for thousands of camera frames. The summed positions give an image
      with detail of tens of nanometers, well below the diffraction limit of about 200 nanometers.

      - "A reporter works only in fixed cells; an actuator works only in living cells"
      - "A reporter shows a signal as it happens; an actuator starts a signal on command"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      A reporter, such as GFP joined to a detector, changes its light when a signal happens, and an
      actuator, such as channelrhodopsin, starts a signal when you supply light or a drug. The
      choice in which the reporter starts the signal has the two words swapped. The choice about
      fixed cells is wrong because both tools are made to work in living cells. The choice about
      dyes is wrong because the reporters in this lesson are proteins made from a gene, and an
      actuator need not be fluorescent at all.

    choices:
      - "It absorbs blue light and then hands the energy on to the attached GFP"
      - "It carries the reporter to the membrane, where calcium channels open"
      - "It binds calcium and changes shape, which brightens the attached GFP"
      - "It pumps calcium out of the cytosol, which then dims the attached GFP"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Calmodulin is the detector. It binds calcium and wraps around the neighboring peptide, and
      that movement changes the surroundings of the light-emitting group so that GFP brightens.
      Calmodulin does not absorb visible light, so it cannot feed energy to GFP; that choice

**A gene copied out of a jellyfish and put into gut bacteria in 1992 made the bacteria glow green, with nothing else from the jellyfish.** A cell that builds its own light can be watched while it is alive. The tools that followed let you see a signal as it happens or start one on command, and a set of genetic methods puts either kind into one cell type in a mouse.

## The idea: watch a signal, or push one

Two words sort the tools. A **reporter** is an engineered protein that changes its light when a signal happens, so you can watch it in a living cell. An **actuator** is an engineered protein that starts a signal on command, usually with a flash of light. Using light and a gene to control cells is called optogenetics, and the word is often stretched to cover reporters used alongside it.

Think of a voltmeter and a switch. A reporter can show that calcium rises in a neuron each time a mouse turns left. Only an actuator can tell you whether firing that neuron is enough to turn the mouse.

From 1961 Osamu Shimomura and Frank Johnson extracted and purified aequorin, a jellyfish protein that gives off blue light when calcium binds it. Traces of a second protein, green and fluorescent, came along in the extract. In 1971 Morin and Hastings renamed it green fluorescent protein, GFP.

"How does fluorescence work?" A molecule absorbs a photon, which lifts an electron to a higher energy level. A little of that energy is lost as heat before the electron falls back and emits a photon. So the light that comes out has a longer wavelength than the light that went in: blue in, green out.

Until 1992 nobody knew whether the GFP gene alone would be enough. The common guess was that a jellyfish enzyme had to finish the protein. That year Douglas Prasher published the cloned gene, and in Martin Chalfie's laboratory at Columbia the rotation student Ghia Euskirchen put its coding stretch into *E. coli*. The bacteria fluoresced green.

The structure explains why. GFP is a barrel with a helix through its middle. Once the protein folds, three neighboring amino acids on that helix (serine 65, tyrosine 66 and glycine 67) react with one another, lose water, and are oxidized by oxygen from the air. That leaves a rigid ring system that absorbs violet and blue light and emits green. The recipe is the sequence itself, so many kinds of cell with the gene and oxygen make their own lamp, though the first version folded poorly in warm cells, and a gene joined to it gives a protein with the lamp attached.

## Building a reporter: a glowing protein joined to a detector

"What would a synaptic protein look like?" Fuse GFP to gephyrin, a scaffold protein found under inhibitory synapses, and a neuron's dendrites show rows of bright dots. That is the simplest reporter: it tells you where a protein is.

To make the light depend on a signal, join GFP to a detector you have already met.

- **A lipid reporter.** A PH domain that binds PIP3 (see the lesson on lipid anchors and addresses), fused to GFP, moves from the cytosol to the membrane when PI 3-kinase makes PIP3.
- **A pH reporter.** A GFP that dims in acid, placed inside a synaptic vesicle (pH about 5.5), brightens when the vesicle fuses and it meets fluid at pH 7.4.
- **A calcium reporter.** In the GCaMP family, GFP's original ends are joined and new ends are cut beside the light-emitting group. Calmodulin (from the calcium lesson) is attached to one new end and a peptide that it grips to the other. When calcium rises, calmodulin wraps around the peptide and plugs the gap in the barrel, which shuts water out from the light-emitting group, and the GFP brightens.
- **A kinase reporter.** Put a peptide that the kinase phosphorylates, and a domain that binds the phosphorylated peptide (an SH2 domain, for a tyrosine kinase), between a cyan and a yellow fluorescent protein. Phosphorylation folds the chain. Two fluorescent proteins within a few nanometers pass energy from one to the other without a photon (FRET), so the balance of cyan and yellow light changes as the kinase works.

A reporter also takes part in what it reports. A calcium reporter soaks up some calcium and lets go slowly, so a fast train of spikes can blur into one slow rise.

## An actuator: light in, current out

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

In the lesson on the senses and cGMP you met rhodopsin: seven helices holding retinal, which changes shape when it absorbs a photon and sets off a G protein. Some microbes have opsins on a similar plan that skip the relay. **Channelrhodopsin-2**, from a green alga, is itself an ion channel. Blue light changes its retinal, the pore opens within milliseconds, and cations flow into the neuron. That depolarizes the membrane, and at threshold the neuron fires.

Georg Nagel, Peter Hegemann, Ernst Bamberg and colleagues had shown in 2002 and 2003 that channelrhodopsins from the green alga *Chlamydomonas* are light-gated channels, and that channelrhodopsin-2 passes cations and works in mammalian cells. In 2005 Edward Boyden, Feng Zhang, Ernst Bamberg, Georg Nagel and Karl Deisseroth reported that hippocampal neurons in culture, given the channelrhodopsin-2 gene, fired action potentials in step with millisecond flashes of blue light. Other laboratories reported similar results at about the same time.

Opsins are sorted by what light does through them:

- **Channelrhodopsins**: blue light opens a channel for cations, with little choice among them. The neuron switches on.
- **Halorhodopsins**: yellow light drives chloride into the cell. The neuron switches off.
- **OptoXRs**: green light activates a rhodopsin whose inner loops come from another G-protein-coupled receptor, so light starts that receptor's pathway.

A virus can carry the gene into one brain region, though it has room for only a short promoter, and light arrives through an optical fiber.

A drug can do the job of light. In chemogenetics the cell gets an engineered G-protein-coupled receptor that ignores its natural ligand and responds to a synthetic drug, so only cells with the receptor react. A channel from the lesson on ion channels works too: put the TRPV1 gene into one neuron type of a mouse whose own TRPV1 gene has been knocked out, and capsaicin opens a cation channel in those neurons alone (Güler and colleagues, 2012).

## How can we make a green animal?

Start with a **transgene**: DNA built from a promoter (the stretch that sets where and when a gene is read) and a coding sequence. Injected into a fertilized mouse egg, it sometimes joins a chromosome at a random place and is inherited from then on. In 1982 Palmiter and colleagues did this with a metallothionein promoter joined to a growth hormone gene, and the mice grew much larger than their littermates. Use a promoter active in every cell and the coding sequence for GFP, and you have a green mouse.

To change one chosen gene you work in **embryonic stem cells**, cells from an early embryo that can still become any tissue. Give them DNA that matches the target gene on both sides of your change, and in a few cells the matching stretches swap your version into the gene's own place. Destroying the gene this way is a **knock-out**; writing something new into it, such as GFP, is a **knock-in**. Drugs select the rare correct cells (ganciclovir kills cells that took the DNA up at a random site). The chosen cells, from a strain with a brown "agouti" coat, are injected into an early embryo of a black strain. The pup is a chimera, a patchwork of both, and if the stem cells formed its sperm or eggs, its agouti offspring can carry the change.

"How do you get expression in 2 different neuron types?" The tool is **Cre and lox**. Cre is an enzyme from a bacterial virus that cuts and rejoins DNA at a 34-base-pair site called loxP. Put two loxP sites around part of a gene, pointing the same way, and Cre removes what lies between them. Make Cre from a promoter that is active in one cell type, and the gene is lost there and nowhere else. To switch a gene on, put a stop signal between two loxP sites in front of it. Where Cre is made the stop is cut out, and the gene is read from then on in that cell and all its descendants, which is how cell lineages are traced. With several fluorescent proteins for Cre to pick among at random, neighboring cells take different colors. For the second neuron type, add a second enzyme of the same kind with its own sites (Flp, from yeast): Cre switches on one gene in one type and Flp another gene in the other.

CRISPR shortens the path: an RNA guide brings a DNA-cutting enzyme to a matching sequence in a fertilized egg, so many knock-outs and knock-ins no longer need stem cells or chimeras.

### Design problem

"How can I make a mouse that does this?" First, how can we make a green animal? Then go one step further: design a mouse in which only one type of neuron glows when its calcium rises, and in which you can switch that same type of neuron on with light. Say which reporter, which actuator, and how you make sure that both are made in that cell type and nowhere else.

<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The green animal is a transgene: a promoter active in every cell, joined to the GFP coding sequence and injected into fertilized eggs.

For the second mouse, breed together three parts.

1. **An address.** Cre knocked into a gene that only your neuron type reads.
2. **A reporter.** A GCaMP gene behind a lox-flanked stop.
3. **An actuator.** A channelrhodopsin gene behind its own lox-flanked stop.

Only cells that make Cre remove the stops, so only they make both proteins.

One trap: GCaMP is read with blue light and channelrhodopsin-2 is opened by blue light, so looking would also be pushing. Use a channelrhodopsin variant that opens with red light.

## Past the limit of light

A lens cannot focus light to a point. The glow of one molecule spreads into a blur, and two molecules closer than about half a wavelength merge. Ernst Abbe put the limit as a formula in the 1870s:

> **smallest separable distance ≈ wavelength ÷ (2 × numerical aperture)**

Numerical aperture measures how wide a cone of light the lens collects, about 1.4 for a very good lens. For green light the limit is about 200 nanometers; a synaptic vesicle is about 40 across.

One way past it came through Jennifer Lippincott-Schwartz's laboratory at the NIH. In 2002 her postdoc George Patterson made a GFP that starts dark and switches on after a flash of violet light. In 2005 the physicist Eric Betzig described to her a microscope that needed such a molecule, and she gave him and Harald Hess room in her laboratory. The method is **PALM**. Switch on a few scattered molecules, so that no two blurs overlap, and find the center of each blur, which can be done far more precisely than the blur is wide. Let those molecules bleach and repeat thousands of times. The stacked positions make a picture with detail down to tens of nanometers. The paper appeared in 2006, as did two related methods, STORM from Xiaowei Zhuang and FPALM from Samuel Hess.

Other methods take other routes: STED (proposed by Stefan Hell in 1994, first shown to work in 1999) switches off fluorescence around the rim of the spot with a ring-shaped second beam, and structured illumination computes finer detail from striped lighting.
