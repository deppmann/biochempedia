techniques:
  - name: "Terminal patch clamp with single-fibre recording"
    blurb: >-
      A pipette is sealed onto the afferent terminal inside an intact corpuscle while a second
      electrode records spikes from the same fibre. You see the transduction current itself and
      can ask whether its timing, rather than the capsule's, sets adaptation. So far this has been
      done in ex vivo duck bill skin.
  - name: "Cre and lineage genetics with operant psychophysics"
    blurb: >-
      Genetic labels (Ret, a TrkB lineage tracer) mark afferent subtypes, a conditional deletion
      removes an end organ, and trained mice report whether they felt a calibrated push. The
      result shows what that end organ contributes to detection, within the limits of a
      developmental rather than acute loss.
  - name: "Optogenetics in non-neuronal cells"
    blurb: >-
      A light-gated channel or pump expressed in lamellar Schwann cells lets you activate or
      inhibit them while recording the afferent or testing behaviour. Activation shows what the
      cells can do; inhibition tests how much the afferent and the behaviour depend on them, both under artificial drive.
  - name: "DRG recording in freely moving mice"
    blurb: >-
      A stabilized electrode in the lumbar dorsal root ganglion records single sensory neurons for a few hours after the mouse wakes from anaesthesia,
      while the mouse moves about freely. It asks what an afferent reports in natural life
      instead of during imposed sine waves.

  - Flutter with Meissner-type afferents, and hum with Pacinian afferents
  - Both percepts with slowly adapting afferents that fire through a hold
  - Flutter with Meissner-type afferents, and hum with Merkel cell afferents
  answer: 1
  rationale: Flutter matched the rapidly adapting afferents now linked to Meissner corpuscles, which detect movement across the skin and low-frequency vibration, and the hum matched Pacinian afferents, which respond to higher-frequency vibration. Swapping the two reverses the division of labour. Slowly adapting afferents fire through a steady press, but both percepts here are of changing stimuli, carried by rapidly adapting afferents. Merkel cell afferents are slowly adapting (SA I), not the Pacinian (RA II) class. The link was drawn across species, human percepts against monkey recordings, and the boundary describes two percepts rather than a sharp receptor cut-off.

- stem: A lab finds that optogenetically inhibiting a mouse corpuscle's lamellar cells raises the vibration detection threshold fivefold. A student concludes that lamellar cells transmit every vibration cycle to the afferent by fast chemical synapses. What is the strongest objection?
  choices:
  - The result shows a contribution to detection but not the coupling mechanism
  - Vibration detection in mice does not depend on Pacinian corpuscles at all
  - A fivefold threshold rise proves that coupling between the cells is electrical
  - Inhibiting lamellar cells cannot change behaviour, so the result is artefact
  answer: 0
  rationale: A raised threshold after inhibition shows the lamellar cells contribute to detection under this manipulation; it says nothing about whether coupling is chemical, electrical or mechanical, and latencies of 20 ms to seconds after lamellar activation in duck Grandry (Meissner-type) corpuscles argue against a fast cycle-by-cycle synapse there, though that result may not carry over to Pacinian cells. Dismissing the behavioural effect as impossible ignores the data. Mouse vibration responses studied here do depend on Pacinian input, so denying that contradicts the work. A threshold change cannot identify electrical coupling any more than chemical coupling.
- stem: In Loewenstein and Mendelson's 1965 experiments on isolated cat Pacinian corpuscles, what changed after most of the lamellae were removed and the ending was held compressed?
  choices:
  - Both generator potential and impulses became shorter than before
  - The generator potential lasted longer but impulses stayed brief
  - The ending switched to sustained firing for the whole hold
  - The generator potential vanished while impulses continued
  answer: 1
  rationale: The decapsulated ending gave a prolonged generator potential yet typically still fired one impulse, which separated a mechanical stage from a spike-generator stage of adaptation. Sustained firing is the common misreading of the experiment; the impulses did not become tonic. Loss of the generator potential with spikes continuing would make no sense, because the generator potential drives the spikes. Shortening of both is the opposite of what was seen for the generator potential.
- stem: Which statement about the afferents of mouse Meissner corpuscles matches Neubarth and colleagues (2020)?

  - TrkB protein is high in adult terminals and defines the less sensitive type

  - Most corpuscles hold a Ret-positive and a TrkB-lineage afferent together

  answer: 2

**Meissner and Pacinian corpuscles are the skin's two detectors of change. Both typically fire when contact starts, stops or moves and fall silent during a steady press, but they split the work: Meissner afferents report low-frequency flutter and movement across the skin, and Pacinian afferents report high-frequency vibration. Each is also more than a nerve ending. It is a multicellular organ in which the axon, the lamellar cells wrapped around it and the capsule together set what it detects.**

The [touch and pain lesson](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) introduced Meissner and Pacinian afferents as rapidly adapting, with small and large receptive fields, and [the LTMR lesson](/lessons/ltmr-classes/) set them in the full class table. This lesson builds on that division of labour and asks how each organ achieves it: where force becomes current, what the cells around the nerve ending add, why the response is so brief, and what the brain and the animal do with the signal. Two terms recur. A **lamellar cell** is a flattened, Schwann-cell-like cell wrapped around the nerve terminal inside a corpuscle. In the Pacinian corpuscle, lamellar Schwann cells hug the terminal in the **inner core**, and many concentric layers, the onion, make up the **outer core**. The outer-core layers are made by a different, non-glial cell type, though papers sometimes call them lamellar cells too.

## Two detectors of change: flutter and hum

Reviews of these end organs open with the same division of labour. Meissner corpuscles, the rapidly adapting type I (RA I) organs, detect movement across the skin and low-frequency vibration. Pacinian corpuscles, the rapidly adapting type II (RA II) organs, respond to higher-frequency vibration. The difference in tuning is thought to arise from the different structures of the two end organs (Handler et al. 2023), which is why the rest of this lesson looks inside them.

Put a vibrating probe on your fingertip and turn up the frequency. The percept changes kind. Talbot, Darian-Smith, Kornhuber and Mountcastle (1968, Journal of Neurophysiology) asked healthy adults to describe sinusoidal stimulation of the hand. At about 5 to 40 Hz they reported a light **flutter**. From around 60 to 80 Hz upward the sensation became a deeper, diffuse **hum** of vibration.

The same paper recorded single afferents from the median nerve of anaesthetized rhesus monkeys. That is a second preparation, not a measurement of the human subjects. The comparison tied flutter to the rapidly adapting afferents now linked to Meissner corpuscles and vibration to Pacinian afferents. Read the boundary between them as a description of two percepts, not a sharp receptor cut-off.

Frequency preferences also depend on species. Prsa and colleagues (2021, Nature Communications) found that behavioural vibration sensitivity peaked near 240 Hz at the human index fingertip and near 1000 Hz in their mouse forepaw preparation. "Pacinian tuning around 250 Hz" is therefore a human figure. Behavioural sensitivity is also not identical to the tuning of a single isolated afferent.

## A multicellular sensor: axon, Piezo2 and lamellar cells

A corpuscle is built from three elements: the terminal of an Aβ afferent, non-myelinating glial cells (the lamellar cells), and connective-tissue sheaths that form the capsule. Reviews now treat the three as one sensor. The older picture made the cells around the ending passive padding; the current evidence is that they help set what the afferent feels.

Start with the step that is settled. Force opens mechanically gated channels in the afferent terminal, and the current that flows is the generator current that drives spikes. In mammals the established channel is **Piezo2**. Mice lacking Piezo2 have an almost complete loss of light touch, and people with PIEZO2 mutations lose touch perception selectively (reviewed by Cobo and colleagues 2020 and Suazo and colleagues 2022). In mouse Meissner and Pacinian corpuscles, Piezo2 sits in the sensory axon membrane, including many small protrusions that tether the axon to the surrounding non-neuronal cells, and it is needed for their light-touch responses, while deleting it from lamellar cells left those responses intact (Handler et al. 2023). The authors' model is that a stimulus stretches these protrusions across the end organ and opens axonal Piezo2. [The mechanotransduction channels lesson](/lessons/mechanotransduction-channels/) sets out what proof a transducer claim needs.

The lamellar cells feel force too. Working on bill skin from late-stage duck embryos, ex vivo, the Bagriantsev and Gracheva laboratories showed that lamellar cells of Grandry corpuscles (the avian Meissner type) and Herbst corpuscles (the avian Pacinian type) carry mechanically activated currents (2020), that the Grandry terminal is a transducer in its own right (2023), and that depolarizing one Grandry lamellar cell could make its afferent fire, with calcium influx required (2023). So the corpuscle is bicellular in the authors' sense: two kinds of mechanosensitive cell, the afferent and the lamellar cells, each able to influence the spike.

The mouse supplies the behavioural test. Chen and colleagues (2025, Science Advances; Huber laboratory) expressed optogenetic tools in mouse Pacinian lamellar Schwann cells. Activating them drove time-locked afferent spiking, and inhibiting them raised the hindpaw vibrotactile detection threshold of awake mice about fivefold. The genetic line used also reaches some other cell types, and the authors note that optogenetic drive may not reproduce normal physiology, so the result shows that lamellar cells contribute to detection, not how they normally do it. Meissner lamellar cells matter in mice too: they carry USH2A, a large membrane protein, and without it the Meissner (RA I) afferents lose much of their vibration sensitivity (Schwaller et al. 2021, reviewed by Suazo and colleagues 2022).

What remains open is the coupling. In duck Grandry corpuscles, the Meissner-type organ, the slow, variable delays between lamellar-cell activation and afferent firing argue against lamellar cells carrying a fast signal for each cycle. Whether Pacinian lamellar cells signal fast enough to follow each cycle of a 200 Hz vibration, or instead set the terminal's sensitivity, is open. The channel behind the duck terminal currents, the lamellar-cell transducer and the signal from lamellar cell to terminal remain unidentified. [Merkel cells](/lessons/merkel-cells/) give a parallel case of a non-neuronal partner cell in touch.

## Why the response is brief: capsule, terminal and spike

Why does a Pacinian afferent fire at the start and end of a press but not during it? The classical answer was mechanical. Loewenstein and Mendelson (1965, Journal of Physiology) dissected Pacinian corpuscles from the cat mesentery and removed most of the lamellae. After decapsulation, the generator potential decayed much more slowly (see Going deeper for the compression conditions). The impulses did not persist: the ending typically still fired a single spike. So adaptation had two stages. One lay in the mechanics around the terminal; another lay in the terminal's spike-generating membrane, which stopped firing despite a lasting depolarization.

Loewenstein and Skalak (1966) then modelled the capsule. Think of a stack of water balloons: press slowly and the fluid flows sideways before the centre feels much; tap fast and the force reaches the core. In their model, fluid between the lamellae supplies viscosity and the lamellae supply elasticity, so the capsule suppresses low-frequency input and passes transients. For decades that **high-pass filter** was the textbook explanation of rapid adaptation.

The duck bill tested the filter directly. Ziolkowski and colleagues (2025, Science Advances) patch-clamped the afferent terminal inside Herbst corpuscles in bill skin from late-stage duck embryos, ex vivo. Removing the outer core, while leaving the inner-core lamellar cells in place, left the rapid kinetics of the mechanotransduction current intact and did not significantly change the afferent's frequency tuning. In this preparation, then, fast adaptation and tuning do not need the onion. It is one bird preparation, not a cat or a human hand, and decapsulation also increased the terminal's current amplitude, so the outer core may still damp the stimulus.

The current view is that adaptation is set jointly: the capsule shapes the force that arrives, the terminal's channels set how fast the current falls, and the spike-generating membrane sets how many impulses follow. The same duck study found that depolarizing an inner-core lamellar Schwann cell by current injection lowered the afferent's mechanical threshold, which points back to the cells that touch the terminal.

## Two afferents in one Meissner corpuscle

The Meissner corpuscle sits in a dermal papilla of glabrous skin, a stack of lamellar cells with Aβ axons coiled between them. The usual picture has one physiological type of afferent per corpuscle, although several distinct endings per corpuscle had been described before. Neubarth, Emanuel, Ginty and colleagues (2020, Science) found that most corpuscles in adult mouse paw pads hold two genetically distinct Aβ afferents, intermingled among the lamellar cells, and that losing the Meissner system makes gentle touch harder to detect.

The two kinds were marked by developmental genetic labels, a **Ret** label and a **TrkB lineage** label. TrkB protein was undetectable in adult terminals, so the TrkB label marks a developmental lineage, not a current receptor. Each kind tiled the skin with its own kind, its fields offset from the other's.

The two differed in physiology. TrkB-lineage afferents were the more sensitive. Ret-positive afferents were less sensitive, and some of them also fired during maintained indentation, so not every unit in this pair is purely rapidly adapting.

Then the behaviour. Deleting TrkB from sensory neurons during development produced adult mice without the Meissner system. Mice were trained in an operant task to report a step indentation of the forepaw.

**Worked problem, part (a): what does a doubled threshold mean?** Adult control mice detected the push at a threshold of about 10 mN; mice lacking Meissner corpuscles, at about 20 mN (Neubarth et al. 2020).

1. Ratio: 20 mN ÷ 10 mN = 2.0, a twofold rise.
2. The mutants still had a measurable threshold, so they still detected touch.
3. Conclusion: Meissner afferents contribute to gentle-touch detection, lowering the threshold by about half, but other afferents can support detection without them.

Two limits apply. The loss was developmental and TrkB was deleted in all sensory neurons, not only Meissner afferents, so other afferents may have compensated or contributed, and an acute silencing could give a larger or smaller effect. And the task measured detection only, not discrimination of flutter or slip.

## Vibration in the brain and in the wild

What does cortex do with a Pacinian signal? Prsa, Morandell, Cuenu and Huber (2019, Nature) delivered vibrations to the mouse forelimb and recorded neurons in forelimb primary somatosensory cortex (S1). The responses depended on deep Pacinian input, were tuned to a feature combining frequency and amplitude, and did not follow the vibration cycle by cycle. That is a rate code for a composite feature. The authors likened it to pitch coding in auditory cortex and contrasted it with primate S1, where low-frequency flutter entrains spiking cycle by cycle. The afferents themselves do phase-lock, much as auditory nerve fibres do in [hearing and balance](/lessons/hearing-and-balance/); the loss of entrainment is a cortical result.

Prsa and colleagues (2021) then measured the percept. Trained mice and adult human volunteers compared vibrations, and in both species a change in amplitude shifted perceived pitch. The direction depended on where the frequency sat relative to the frequency of best sensitivity (about 1000 Hz in mice, about 240 Hz in humans): a change in amplitude pulled pitch toward that frequency. Near 440 Hz, below the mouse peak but above the human one, the two species therefore went opposite ways. In mice a louder vibration felt higher; in humans it felt lower (fitted exponent about −0.24). For mice near a 450 Hz reference, the equal-pitch data were fitted with a power law:

> **P = f × (A / Aref)^0.32**, with Aref = 5.6 µm

This is a local mouse fit; it does not apply to humans at this frequency, where the sign reverses. Try it in the matcher below; Going deeper works the fit through for a doubled amplitude.

Finally, natural behaviour. Turecek and Ginty (2024, Neuron) recorded single Pacinian neurons in the L4 dorsal root ganglion of awake, freely moving mice. The neurons fired during the mouse's own movements as well as during external vibration, and sensitivity varied widely between neurons. The most sensitive responded to substrate vibrations generated 2.5 m away, the furthest point tested on that surface, not a known limit. Whether the brain separates self-generated from external vibration was not tested. ### Design problem

A mouse Pacinian afferent fires briefly at the onset and offset of a press and is most sensitive to high-frequency vibration. Design an experiment that decides how much of that behaviour comes from the outer core, from the inner-core lamellar cells and from the terminal itself.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

1. **Measure the right signal.** Voltage-clamp the terminal inside one corpuscle and record the transduction current, and record spikes from the same fibre separately, so generator current and spiking are not confused.
2. **Remove the outer core in the same corpuscle.** Record before and after. Deliver step indentations and sinusoids from 10 to 1000 Hz at matched displacement at the terminal (measured, for example, by imaging the inner core), so that any change in tuning can be assigned to mechanics or to the membrane.
3. **Control for damage.** Track time after dissection and the terminal's leak current. If adaptation slows only as leak rises, deterioration, not loss of a filter, explains the change. Include sham-handled corpuscles kept for the same time.
4. **Test the lamellar cells.** With the outer core on and off, optogenetically inhibit the lamellar Schwann cells (as Chen and colleagues did in 2025) during the same stimuli, and measure the afferent's threshold and the animal's behavioural detection threshold. Allow for the genetic line also reaching other cell types.
5. **Interpret.** If adaptation and tuning survive outer-core removal but change when lamellar cells are inhibited, the inner core shapes them; if neither manipulation changes them, the terminal's own channels and membrane set them; if they change only in healthy decapsulated terminals, the outer core shapes adaptation, and the displacement measurements in step 2 say whether it does so mechanically. Duck results do not transfer automatically, and human corpuscles have not been patch-clamped this way, so human answers would need other methods, such as microneurography and psychophysics.

</details>

## Going deeper

These notes hold the methods, tallies and exact numbers behind the main line.

### The 1965 and 2025 decapsulation experiments in detail

Later reviewers note that the slower decay of the generator potential after decapsulation in 1965 appeared mainly under intense compression. The 2025 duck recordings used bill skin from embryonic day 25 to 27 duck embryos. In those recordings, slower inactivation appeared only as decapsulated terminals deteriorated. That offers one reading of 1965: a damaged, slowly dying terminal can look like a terminal that has lost its filter. Besides increasing the terminal's current amplitude, decapsulation slightly lowered its current threshold, and the authors propose that the outer core protects the inner core.

### Duck bill recordings, step by step

Three papers from the Bagriantsev and Gracheva laboratories built the lamellar-cell case in steps, all in duck bill skin ex vivo.

- **2020** (Nikolaev and colleagues, Science Advances; embryonic day 24 to 26). Lamellar cells of both Grandry and Herbst corpuscles produced mechanically activated currents. Grandry lamellar cells fired action potentials, predominantly through R-type calcium channels. Herbst outer-core cells were mechanosensitive but not excitable.
- **January 2023** (Ziolkowski, Gracheva and Bagriantsev, eLife). The Grandry terminal itself carried transduction currents at both the onset and the offset of indentation. The onset current inactivated in 8.95 ± 1.82 ms (mean ± SEM). Tetrodotoxin blocked the afferent's action potentials. The terminal is a transducer in its own right.
- **Later 2023** (Nikolaev, Ziolkowski and colleagues, Science Advances). Selectively depolarizing one Grandry lamellar cell could trigger firing in its afferent, and calcium influx was required. Latencies ran from 20 ms to about 9 s. Chemical transmission was proposed but not shown.

Grandry lamellar cells lack functional gap junctions with surrounding cells. In duck Herbst corpuscles, the Pacinian type, the inner-core lamellar Schwann cells are coupled to one another by gap junctions, and depolarizing one evoked an inward current in the terminal (Ziolkowski et al. 2025), although dye did not pass from the terminal into them.

### Mouse lamellar-cell optogenetics: details

Chen and colleagues activated the lamellar Schwann cells in anaesthetized adult mice and saw first-spike latencies up to about 40 ms; the shortest latencies, under intense light, were very short, which the authors read as tight coupling between the two cells. Electron microscopy showed lamellar cells linked to one another by many desmosomes and gap junctions and anchored to the axon by desmosomes. The Etv1-CreER line marks inner-core lamellar cells but is also expressed in other cell types, so the manipulation is not perfectly cell-specific.

### Meissner afferent labels, tallies and wrapping

The Ret label used Ret-CreER given tamoxifen in early embryos. The TrkB lineage label marked cells that expressed TrkB during development: an inducible Cre driver, TrkB-CreER, given tamoxifen, switched on a permanent reporter in them. Both afferent types conducted in the Aβ range. Of 10 recorded Ret-positive afferents, 4 also fired during maintained indentation, so "two types of rapidly adapting afferent" overstates the result: one subtype included units with a sustained response. By electron microscopy, TrkB-lineage endings had more lamellar-cell wrappings than Ret-positive endings, and the authors proposed that the extent of wrapping helps set an afferent's force threshold and kinetics. The behavioural deletion used Advillin-Cre, which removes TrkB from all sensory neurons.

### The mouse equal-pitch fit, worked

The 2021 mice were C57BL/6, awake and head-fixed, and the equal-pitch fit near 450 Hz came from four mice.

**Worked problem, part (b): the frequency that keeps pitch constant.** Reference: 450 Hz at 5.6 µm. Amplitude doubles to 11.2 µm. What frequency feels the same?

1. Set pitches equal: 450 Hz × (5.6/5.6)^0.32 = f2 × (11.2/5.6)^0.32.
2. Left side: 450 Hz × 1 = 450 Hz. Right side: f2 × 2^0.32.
3. 2^0.32 = e^(0.32 × 0.693) = e^0.2218 = 1.248.
4. f2 = 450 Hz ÷ 1.248 = 360.5 Hz.
5. Change: (450 − 360.5) ÷ 450 = 0.199, a 19.9% decrease. Check: 360.5 × 1.248 = 449.9 Hz.

The amplitude ratio has no units, so the answer stays in hertz. This is a prediction of a local mouse fit, assumed to hold at double amplitude; it is not a new measurement.
