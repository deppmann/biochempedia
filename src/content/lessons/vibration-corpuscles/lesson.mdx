    story: >-
      By the 1960s the Pacinian corpuscle was a favourite preparation for asking how a sense
      organ turns pressure into nerve impulses, because a single large corpuscle with one axon
      could be dissected out of the cat mesentery and studied on its own. The puzzle was why its
      response was so brief. In a 1965 paper in the Journal of Physiology, Loewenstein and
      Mendelson stripped away most of the onion-like lamellae and pressed on what remained.
      The generator potential, the graded depolarization of the nerve ending, could now last
      much longer during a held compression. The impulses did not: the decapsulated ending still
      typically fired a single spike. So adaptation had at least two stages, one in the
      mechanics around the ending and one in the ending's own spike-generating membrane. The
      following year, with R. Skalak, Loewenstein built a mechanical model of
      the capsule in which fluid between the lamellae supplies viscosity and the lamellae supply
      elasticity, so that slow pressure is dissipated before it reaches the core. That model
      gave the field a testable claim about the capsule, which direct terminal recordings could
      later put to the test.

techniques:
  - name: "Terminal patch clamp with single-fibre recording"
    blurb: >-
      A pipette is sealed onto the afferent terminal inside an intact corpuscle while a second
      electrode records spikes from the same fibre. You see the transduction current itself and
      can ask whether its timing, rather than the capsule's, sets adaptation. So far this has been
      done in ex vivo duck bill skin.
  - name: "Cre and lineage genetics with operant psychophysics"
    blurb: >-
      Genetic labels (Ret, a TrkB lineage tracer) mark afferent subtypes, a conditional deletion
      removes an end organ, and trained mice report whether they felt a calibrated push. The
      result shows what that end organ contributes to detection, within the limits of a
      developmental rather than acute loss.
  - name: "Optogenetics in non-neuronal cells"
    blurb: >-
      A light-gated channel or pump expressed in lamellar Schwann cells lets you activate or
      inhibit them while recording the afferent or testing behaviour. Activation shows what the
      cells can do; inhibition tests how much the afferent and the behaviour depend on them, both under artificial drive.
  - name: "DRG recording in freely moving mice"
    blurb: >-
      A stabilized electrode in the lumbar dorsal root ganglion records single sensory neurons for a few hours after the mouse wakes from anaesthesia,
      while the mouse moves about freely. It asks what an afferent reports in natural life
      instead of during imposed sine waves.

- stem: Talbot and colleagues (1968) found that vibration of the hand at about 5 to 40 Hz felt like a light flutter, and from around 60 to 80 Hz upward like a deeper hum. Which pairing of percept and afferent did their comparison with single-afferent recordings in monkeys support?
  choices:
  - Flutter with Pacinian afferents, and hum with Meissner-type afferents
  - Flutter with Meissner-type afferents, and hum with Pacinian afferents
  - Both percepts with slowly adapting afferents that fire through a hold
  - Flutter with Meissner-type afferents, and hum with Merkel cell afferents
  answer: 1
  rationale: Flutter matched the rapidly adapting afferents now linked to Meissner corpuscles, which detect movement across the skin and low-frequency vibration, and the hum matched Pacinian afferents, which respond to higher-frequency vibration. Swapping the two reverses the division of labour. Slowly adapting afferents fire through a steady press, but both percepts here are of changing stimuli, carried by rapidly adapting afferents. Merkel cell afferents are slowly adapting (SA I), not the Pacinian (RA II) class. The link was drawn across species, human percepts against monkey recordings, and the boundary describes two percepts rather than a sharp receptor cut-off.
- stem: Prsa and colleagues (2021) found that behavioural vibration sensitivity peaked near 240 Hz at the human fingertip. Near what frequency did it peak in their mouse forepaw preparation?
  choices:
  - About 240 Hz, the same as the human peak
  - About 100 Hz, less than half the human peak
  - About 1000 Hz, well above the human peak
  - About 40 Hz, in the flutter range of humans
  answer: 2
  rationale: The study reported a mouse forepaw sensitivity peak near 1000 Hz, far above the human value, so a Pacinian optimum near 250 Hz is a human figure. Assuming the same peak in both species is the error the comparison corrects. A peak near 40 Hz would put the mouse optimum in the human flutter band, which Meissner-type afferents serve in humans. A peak near 100 Hz has no support in the paper and moves in the wrong direction.
- stem: A lab finds that optogenetically inhibiting a mouse corpuscle's lamellar cells raises the vibration detection threshold fivefold. A student concludes that lamellar cells transmit every vibration cycle to the afferent by fast chemical synapses. What is the strongest objection?
  choices:
  - The result shows a contribution to detection but not the coupling mechanism
  - Vibration detection in mice does not depend on Pacinian corpuscles at all
  - A fivefold threshold rise proves that coupling between the cells is electrical
  - Inhibiting lamellar cells cannot change behaviour, so the result is artefact
  answer: 0
  rationale: A raised threshold after inhibition shows the lamellar cells contribute to detection under this manipulation; it says nothing about whether coupling is chemical, electrical or mechanical, and latencies of 20 ms to seconds after lamellar activation in duck Grandry (Meissner-type) corpuscles suggest a slower, modulating role there rather than a fast cycle-by-cycle synapse, though they did not measure timing during vibration and may not carry over to Pacinian cells. Dismissing the behavioural effect as impossible ignores the data. Mouse vibration responses studied here do depend on Pacinian input, so denying that contradicts the work. A threshold change cannot identify electrical coupling any more than chemical coupling.
- stem: In Loewenstein and Mendelson's 1965 experiments on isolated cat Pacinian corpuscles, what changed after most of the lamellae were removed and the ending was held compressed?
  choices:
  - Both generator potential and impulses became shorter than before
  - The generator potential lasted longer but impulses stayed brief
  - The ending switched to sustained firing for the whole hold
  - The generator potential vanished while impulses continued
  answer: 1
  rationale: The decapsulated ending gave a prolonged generator potential yet typically still fired one impulse, which separated a mechanical stage from a spike-generator stage of adaptation. Sustained firing is the common misreading of the experiment; the impulses did not become tonic. Loss of the generator potential with spikes continuing would make no sense, because the generator potential drives the spikes. Shortening of both is the opposite of what was seen for the generator potential.
- stem: Which statement about the afferents of mouse Meissner corpuscles matches Neubarth and colleagues (2020)?
  choices:
  - TrkB protein is high in adult terminals and defines the less sensitive type
  - All recorded Ret-positive afferents were silent during sustained indentation
  - Most corpuscles hold a Ret-positive and a TrkB-lineage afferent together
  - Each corpuscle holds one afferent, and Ret and TrkB mark the same cells
  answer: 2
  rationale: Most mouse Meissner corpuscles were innervated by two afferents, one Ret-positive and one from the TrkB lineage, intertwined in the same corpuscle. One afferent with overlapping markers matches neither result; the paper's advance was that the two afferents are molecularly distinct types. TrkB protein was undetectable in adult terminals, so the label is developmental lineage, and the TrkB-lineage afferents were the more sensitive ones, not the less. Four of ten recorded Ret-positive afferents fired during maintained indentation, so they were not all silent.

- stem: Researchers record a single afferent terminal inside an isolated duck Grandry corpuscle. Indentation evokes an inward current at onset that inactivates with a time constant of about 9 ms, and a second current when the probe is withdrawn. Tetrodotoxin abolishes the afferent's spikes but not these currents. What are these currents?
  choices:
  - The currents are sodium action potentials propagated from the axon
  - The currents arise from the lamellar cells and reach the terminal through gap junctions
  - The currents reflect slow capsule mechanics with a time constant of seconds
  - The currents are transduction currents generated in the terminal membrane itself
  answer: 3
  rationale: Currents that survive tetrodotoxin and appear at indentation onset and offset in a voltage- clamped terminal are mechanotransduction currents in the terminal, while tetrodotoxin removes only the spikes they trigger. Propagated sodium action potentials are exactly what tetrodotoxin blocks, so that reading contradicts the data. Gap-junction coupling from lamellar cells is a hypothesis this recording does not test. A time constant near 9 ms is far faster than the seconds-long time constant this choice proposes, and capsule mechanics would shape the stimulus, not appear as a current in a voltage-clamped terminal.
- stem: Adult control mice trained to report a forepaw push detect it at about 10 mN. Mice in which TrkB was deleted from sensory neurons during development, and which lack Meissner corpuscles as adults, detect it at about 20 mN. Both groups learn the task. Which conclusion do these data best support?
  choices:
  - Meissner afferents matter only acutely, so the developmental loss is irrelevant
  - Meissner afferents help set the threshold, but other afferents still detect touch
  - Meissner afferents are required for any detection of gentle forepaw touch
  - Meissner afferents raise the threshold, since the mutants detect smaller forces
  answer: 1
  rationale: The threshold doubled, 20 mN divided by 10 mN, but did not become unmeasurable, so Meissner afferents contribute to gentle-touch detection while other afferents can still support it. Calling them required for any detection ignores that the mutants still performed the task. The mutants needed larger, not smaller, forces, so the system lowers the threshold. A developmental deletion cannot isolate acute function, but it is the evidence here; it is a limit on interpretation, not a reason to discard the result.

**Press a fingertip on a surface and hold it still. Many nerve fibres from that skin fire as the press begins, go quiet while you hold, and often fire again as you let go. Where in the sense organ does a held press become two brief events?**

Those fibres are afferents, sensory neurons whose axons carry the signal inward, and they end in Meissner and Pacinian corpuscles, the skin's two detectors of change. Slide a fingertip across cloth and Meissner afferents report the movement; hold a buzzing phone and Pacinian afferents report the hum. Neither organ is a bare nerve ending. Each is a nerve ending and the cells that help it feel. A **lamellar cell** is a flattened, Schwann-cell-like cell wrapped around the nerve terminal inside a corpuscle. In the Pacinian corpuscle the lamellar cells form the **inner core**, and many concentric layers outside them, the onion or **outer core**, form the capsule.

The [touch and pain lesson](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) introduced Meissner and Pacinian afferents as rapidly adapting, with small and large receptive fields, and [the LTMR lesson](/lessons/ltmr-classes/) set them in the full class table. This lesson takes the response itself, then the one settled step, where force becomes current, then the central question: why the response is so brief, and what the cells around the ending add. Last come the two afferents of the Meissner corpuscle and what the brain and the animal do with the signal.

## The typical response: a burst at each end

Push a probe into the skin, hold it still for a second, then lift it, and record from one afferent at a time.

**Predict.** A slowly adapting afferent, such as the Merkel cell afferent (SA I), keeps firing while the press is held. What should a Meissner or Pacinian afferent do during the hold, and what should it do when the probe lifts?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It typically falls silent during the hold and fires again at the release.

| Phase of the press | Typical Meissner or Pacinian afferent (rapidly adapting) | Slowly adapting afferent, for comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Probe pushes in | a burst of spikes | starts firing |
| Probe held still | typically silent | keeps firing |
| Probe lifts | often a second burst | firing ends |

</details>

**Rapidly adapting** means that the firing dies away while a press is held. The release burst is common in classical units but is not part of the definition. The names label afferent classes, not organs. Meissner corpuscles carry the rapidly adapting type I (RA I) afferents, which detect movement across the skin and low-frequency vibration. Pacinian corpuscles carry the rapidly adapting type II (RA II) afferents, which respond to higher-frequency vibration.

### Flutter and hum

Vibration is change that repeats, so it drives these afferents strongly. Put a vibrating probe on your fingertip and turn up the frequency, and the percept changes kind. Talbot, Darian-Smith, Kornhuber and Mountcastle (1968, Journal of Neurophysiology) asked healthy adults to describe sinusoidal stimulation of the hand. At about 5 to 40 Hz they reported a light **flutter**. From around 60 to 80 Hz upward the sensation became a deeper, diffuse **hum** of vibration.

The same paper recorded single afferents from the median nerve of anaesthetized rhesus monkeys, a second preparation, not a measurement of the human subjects. The comparison tied flutter to the rapidly adapting afferents now linked to Meissner corpuscles and the hum to Pacinian afferents. Read the boundary between them as a description of two percepts, not a sharp receptor cut-off. Why the two organs prefer different frequencies is thought to come from their different structures (Handler et al. 2023), which is why this lesson looks inside them.

Frequency preferences also depend on species. Prsa and colleagues (2021, Nature Communications) found that behavioural vibration sensitivity peaked near 240 Hz at the human index fingertip and near 1000 Hz in their mouse forepaw preparation. "Pacinian tuning around 250 Hz" is therefore a human figure. Behavioural sensitivity is also not identical to the tuning of a single isolated afferent.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A corpuscle reports change, not level. Its afferent fires when the skin starts to move, stops or keeps moving, and goes quiet while a press is held. Compare two pushes of the same size: the faster its response dies away during a hold, the more it discounts slow change, so a push that builds over seconds gives a smaller response than one that arrives in milliseconds. That dying-away can happen in three places: the capsule shapes the force that reaches the ending, the terminal's channels set how fast the current falls, and the spike generator sets how many impulses follow. Because more than one place can make the response brief, removing one need not abolish it, and an unchanged response does not prove the removed part did nothing.

The idea covers rapidly adapting afferents: Meissner and Pacinian corpuscles in mammals and their counterparts in the duck bill. It does not cover slowly adapting afferents, or the minority of Ret-positive Meissner afferents that also fired during maintained indentation (later in this lesson). The rule's push comparison is a simple linear filter model; real firing also depends on amplitude, threshold and recovery. It explains the low-frequency side of an afferent's band, a graded roll-off rather than a hard detection limit, not its best frequency, which also depends on what fails at high frequencies. And it does not settle how lamellar cells act in mammals: in mice, manipulating them changes detection and the afferent's vibration responses, but under artificial drive, so their normal coupling is unresolved.

## Where force becomes current: Piezo2 in the axon

A corpuscle is built from three elements: the terminal of an Aβ afferent (a large, fast-conducting, myelinated fibre), the lamellar cells (non-myelinating glia), and connective-tissue sheaths that form the capsule. Start with the step that is settled. Force opens mechanically gated channels in the afferent terminal, and the current that flows, the transduction current, is what drives spikes. In mammals the established channel is **Piezo2**. Mice lacking Piezo2 have an almost complete loss of light touch, and people with PIEZO2 mutations lose touch perception selectively (reviewed by Cobo and colleagues 2020 and Suazo and colleagues 2022). In mouse Meissner and Pacinian corpuscles, Piezo2 sits in the sensory axon membrane, including many small protrusions that tether the axon to the surrounding non-neuronal cells, and it is needed for their light-touch responses, while deleting it from lamellar cells left those responses intact (Handler et al. 2023). The authors' model is that a stimulus stretches these protrusions across the end organ and opens axonal Piezo2. [The mechanotransduction channels lesson](/lessons/mechanotransduction-channels/) sets out what proof a transducer claim needs.

## Why the response is brief: capsule, terminal and spike

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Something between the skin and the axon turns a held Pacinian press into two brief events. There are three candidates, and this section tests them.

- **The capsule.** The onion of concentric layers could absorb a held press before it reaches the nerve ending.
- **The terminal's channels.** The mechanically gated channels in the ending could open at the press and then close, or inactivate, even while the press is held.
- **The spike generator.** The membrane where impulses start could stop firing even while the ending stays depolarized.

Two terms come first. The **generator potential** is the graded depolarization of the nerve ending driven by the transduction current. The **impulses** are the all-or-none action potentials it triggers further along the axon.

### The classical answer: the capsule

Loewenstein and Mendelson (1965, Journal of Physiology) dissected Pacinian corpuscles from the cat mesentery, removed most of the capsule's layers (the lamellae) and pressed on what remained.

**Predict.** If the capsule alone made the response brief, what should the bare ending do during a held press? What happens to the generator potential, and what happens to the impulses?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If the capsule did all the work, both should now last through the hold. Half of that happened: after decapsulation the generator potential decayed much more slowly, an effect that later reviewers note appeared mainly under intense compression. The impulses did not persist: the ending typically still fired a single spike. So adaptation had two stages. One lay in the mechanics around the terminal; another lay in the terminal's spike-generating membrane, which stopped firing despite a lasting depolarization.

</details>

Loewenstein and Skalak (1966) then modelled the capsule. In their model, fluid between the lamellae supplies viscosity and the lamellae supply elasticity. Follow one press through time. At the instant of the press the fluid cannot move fast enough, so the force passes straight to the core and the ending is squeezed. During the hold the fluid flows and the stretched layers take up the load, so the core relaxes although the skin is still pressed. At the release the layers spring back and jolt the core a second time. The capsule thus suppresses slow or steady input and passes transients, at both ends of a press. For decades that **high-pass filter** was the textbook explanation of rapid adaptation.

### Testing the filter at the terminal itself

A filter is best tested by recording at the place it is supposed to protect. Ziolkowski and colleagues (2025, Science Advances) patch-clamped the afferent terminal inside Herbst corpuscles, the bird counterpart of the Pacinian corpuscle, in bill skin from late-stage duck embryos, ex vivo. They then removed the outer core and kept the inner core.

**Predict.** If the onion sets the brief response and the frequency tuning, what should happen to the terminal's transduction current, and to the afferent's tuning, once the outer core is gone?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The current should become slow and sustained, and the tuning should change. Neither happened. Removing the outer core left the rapid kinetics of the mechanotransduction current intact and did not significantly change the afferent's frequency tuning. In this preparation, fast adaptation and tuning do not need the onion. It is one bird preparation, not a cat or a human hand. Decapsulation also increased the terminal's current amplitude, so the outer core may still damp the stimulus.

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/vibration-corpuscles/duck-decapsulation.webp" width={1200} height={802} alt="AI-generated illustration: two copies of a layered Herbst corpuscle with a patch pipette on the central nerve terminal. On the left the corpuscle is intact; on the right the outer layers are removed and only the inner core cells and terminal remain." caption="Ziolkowski and colleagues patch-clamped the terminal inside duck Herbst corpuscles, with and without the outer core. Removing it left the rapid kinetics of the transduction current intact in this ex vivo embryo preparation." />

So in the duck, the terminal can adapt without the onion. An earlier duck study had already shown that the terminal inside a Grandry corpuscle, the bird counterpart of the Meissner corpuscle, is a transducer in its own right, with transduction currents at both the onset and the offset of indentation (Ziolkowski, Gracheva and Bagriantsev 2023).

How do 1965 and 2025 fit together? The duck recordings offer one reading. Slower inactivation appeared only as decapsulated terminals deteriorated, so a damaged, slowly dying terminal can look like a terminal that has lost its filter.

| Experiment | What was removed | Signal at the terminal during a held press | Afferent output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cat Pacinian, 1965 | most of the capsule | generator potential decayed much more slowly, mainly under intense compression | typically still one spike |
| Duck Herbst, 2025 | outer core; inner core kept | transduction current kept its rapid kinetics, and grew larger | frequency tuning not significantly changed |

The verdict on the three suspects, so far. The capsule can shape the signal (the cat generator potential; the duck current's size), but the duck terminal did not need it to adapt. In the duck, fast current decay persisted without the outer core, which supports a contribution from the terminal's channels, though the retained inner-core cells and mechanics may also shape that decay. The spike generator adds its own brevity (the cat's single spike). Adaptation is set jointly, the one idea of this lesson at work.

Adaptation explains only one side of the frequency band. A stage that discards slow change sets a graded low-frequency edge: well below it, the response largely dies away during each slow push. On its own it has no best frequency. A peak, such as the human peak sensitivity near 240 Hz, also needs something that fails at high frequencies, such as the mechanics, the channel kinetics or the timing of spikes, and that side of the band is less well understood. So, on a simple filter model, neither the capsule nor the terminal "makes" the peak by adapting.

That leaves one more candidate still wrapped around the ending once the onion has gone: the inner-core lamellar cells.

## What is left once the onion is gone: the lamellar cells

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

The older picture made the cells around the ending passive padding. The current evidence is that they help set what the afferent feels. Three findings build the case.

1. **They feel force.** In duck bill skin, the Bagriantsev and Gracheva laboratories showed that lamellar cells of Grandry and Herbst corpuscles carry mechanically activated currents (in Herbst corpuscles these recordings were from outer-core cells).
2. **They can make the afferent fire.** Depolarizing one Grandry lamellar cell could make its afferent fire, with calcium influx required, and in Herbst corpuscles depolarizing an inner-core cell by current injection lowered the afferent's mechanical threshold (Ziolkowski et al. 2025). So in the authors' sense the corpuscle is **bicellular**: two kinds of cell, the afferent and the lamellar cells, each able to feel force and to influence the spike.
3. **In mice they matter for detection.** Chen and colleagues (2025, Science Advances; Huber laboratory) expressed optogenetic tools in mouse Pacinian lamellar Schwann cells. Activating them drove time-locked afferent spiking, and inhibiting them raised the hindpaw vibrotactile detection threshold of awake mice about fivefold: a vibration had to be about five times larger to be detected. Inhibition also lowered the afferent's spike rate and phase locking and shifted its preferred frequency downward. The genetic line used also reaches some other cell types, and the authors note that optogenetic drive may not reproduce normal physiology, so the result shows that lamellar cells contribute to detection, not how they normally do it.

Meissner lamellar cells matter in mice too: without USH2A, a large membrane protein they carry, Meissner (RA I) afferents lose much of their vibration sensitivity (Schwaller et al. 2021, reviewed by Suazo and colleagues 2022).

What remains open is the coupling. Does a lamellar cell pass on each cycle of a vibration, or does it set how sensitive the terminal is?

**Predict.** To pass on each cycle, a lamellar cell's signal would have to keep time to within a few milliseconds. A 200 Hz vibration repeats every 5 ms (1 ÷ 200 Hz = 0.005 s). In duck Grandry corpuscles, the delay between depolarizing one lamellar cell and afferent firing ran from 20 ms to about 9 s. Could the lamellar cells there be carrying each cycle?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

These data cannot settle it. The shortest delay, 20 ms, spans 20 ms ÷ 5 ms = 4 cycles, but a steady delay would only shift every cycle by the same amount. Nor do first-spike delays measure cycle-to-cycle precision: they followed electrical depolarization of one lamellar cell, not vibration. In duck Grandry corpuscles, the Meissner-type organ, the delayed, irregular firing suggests a complementary, modulating role rather than the fast primary response; testing cycle following would need repeated mechanical stimulation and timing measured in the same cell–afferent pair. In mouse Pacinian corpuscles the shortest latencies under intense light were very short, which the authors read as tight coupling. Whether Pacinian lamellar cells signal fast enough to follow each cycle of a 200 Hz vibration, or instead set the terminal's sensitivity, is open.

</details>

The channel behind the duck terminal currents, the lamellar-cell transducer and the signal from lamellar cell to terminal remain unidentified. [Merkel cells](/lessons/merkel-cells/) give a parallel case of a non-neuronal partner cell in touch.

## Two afferents in one Meissner corpuscle

The Meissner corpuscle sits in a dermal papilla of glabrous skin, a stack of lamellar cells with Aβ axons coiled between them. The usual picture has one physiological type of afferent per corpuscle, although several distinct endings per corpuscle had been described before. Neubarth, Emanuel, Ginty and colleagues (2020, Science) found that most corpuscles in adult mouse paw pads hold two genetically distinct Aβ afferents, intermingled among the lamellar cells.

The two kinds were marked by developmental genetic labels, a **Ret** label and a **TrkB lineage** label. TrkB protein was undetectable in adult terminals, so the TrkB label marks a developmental lineage, not a current receptor. Receptive fields of the same kind fitted together across the skin like floor tiles, and the two kinds' tilings were offset from each other.

The two differed in physiology. TrkB-lineage afferents were the more sensitive. Ret-positive afferents were less sensitive and rarely fired at the release, and 4 of 10 recorded also fired during maintained indentation, so "two types of rapidly adapting afferent" overstates the result: one subtype included units with a sustained response.

Then the behaviour. Deleting TrkB from sensory neurons during development produced adult mice without the Meissner system. Mice were trained in an operant task to report a step indentation of the forepaw.

### Worked problem: a doubled threshold

Adult control mice detected the push at a threshold of about 10 mN; mice lacking Meissner corpuscles, at about 20 mN (Neubarth et al. 2020).

**Predict.** If Meissner afferents were required for detecting a gentle push, what would the mutants' threshold look like? What does a measurable but higher threshold say instead?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If no detection were possible without Meissner afferents, the mutants would never reach the detection criterion over the forces tested, and no threshold could be measured.

1. Ratio: 20 mN ÷ 10 mN = 2.0, a twofold rise.
2. The mutants still had a measurable threshold, so they still detected larger pushes; their normal sensitivity was impaired.
3. Conclusion: Meissner afferents contribute to gentle-touch detection, lowering the threshold by about half, but other afferents can support detection without them.

</details>

Two limits apply. The loss was developmental and TrkB was deleted in all sensory neurons, not only Meissner afferents, so other afferents may have compensated or contributed, and an acute silencing could give a larger or smaller effect. And the task measured detection only, not discrimination of flutter or slip.

## Vibration in the brain and in the wild

What does cortex do with a Pacinian signal? Prsa, Morandell, Cuenu and Huber (2019, Nature) delivered vibrations to the mouse forelimb and recorded neurons in forelimb primary somatosensory cortex (S1). The responses depended on deep Pacinian input. The neurons did not fire in step with each cycle; their firing rates were tuned to a feature combining frequency and amplitude. That is a rate code for a composite feature. The authors likened this to pitch coding in auditory cortex and contrasted it with primate S1, where low-frequency flutter entrains spiking cycle by cycle. The afferents themselves do phase-lock, much as auditory nerve fibres do in [hearing and balance](/lessons/hearing-and-balance/); the loss of entrainment is a cortical result.

Prsa and colleagues (2021) then measured the percept. Trained mice and adult human volunteers compared vibrations, and in both species a change in amplitude pulled perceived pitch toward the frequency of best sensitivity (about 1000 Hz in mice, about 240 Hz in humans). Near 440 Hz, below the mouse peak but above the human one, the two species therefore went opposite ways: in mice a louder vibration felt higher, and in humans it felt lower. For mice near a 450 Hz reference the equal-pitch data were fitted with a power law, which the matcher below uses: in that fit, a 360 Hz vibration at double amplitude matched the 450 Hz reference. It is a local mouse fit and does not apply to humans at this frequency, where the fitted exponent is about −0.24, so the sign reverses.

<EqualPitchMatcher />

Finally, natural behaviour. Turecek and Ginty (2024, Neuron) recorded single Pacinian neurons in the L4 dorsal root ganglion of awake, freely moving mice. The neurons fired during the mouse's own movements as well as during external vibration, and sensitivity varied widely between neurons. The most sensitive responded to substrate vibrations generated 2.5 m away, the furthest point tested on that surface, not a known limit. Whether the brain separates self-generated from external vibration was not tested. ### Design problem

A mouse Pacinian afferent fires briefly at the onset and offset of a press and is most sensitive to high-frequency vibration. Design an experiment that decides how much of that behaviour comes from the outer core, from the inner-core lamellar cells and from the terminal itself.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

1. **Measure the right signal.** Voltage-clamp the terminal inside one corpuscle and record the transduction current, and record spikes from the same fibre separately, so generator current and spiking are not confused.
2. **Remove the outer core in the same corpuscle.** Record before and after. Deliver step indentations and sinusoids from 10 to 1000 Hz at matched displacement at the terminal (measured, for example, by imaging the inner core), so that any change in tuning can be assigned to mechanics or to the membrane.
3. **Control for damage.** Track time after dissection and the terminal's leak current. If adaptation slows only as leak rises, deterioration, not loss of a filter, explains the change. Include sham-handled corpuscles kept for the same time.
4. **Test the lamellar cells.** With the outer core on and off, optogenetically inhibit the lamellar Schwann cells (as Chen and colleagues did in 2025) during the same stimuli, and measure the afferent's threshold and the animal's behavioural detection threshold. Allow for the genetic line also reaching other cell types.
5. **Interpret.** If adaptation and tuning survive outer-core removal but change when lamellar cells are inhibited, the inner core shapes them; if neither manipulation measurably changes them, the terminal's own mechanisms become the leading candidate, once incomplete inhibition, retained mechanics, redundant contributions and assay sensitivity are excluded; if they change only in healthy decapsulated terminals, the outer core shapes adaptation, and the displacement measurements in step 2 say whether it does so mechanically. Duck results do not transfer automatically, and human corpuscles have not been patch-clamped this way, so human answers would need other methods, such as microneurography and psychophysics.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Anatomy, terms and framing

The outer-core layers of the Pacinian corpuscle are made by a different, non-glial cell type from the inner-core lamellar Schwann cells, though papers sometimes call them lamellar cells too. Reviews now treat the afferent terminal, the lamellar cells and the capsule as one sensor.

### The 1965 and 2025 decapsulation experiments in detail

The 2025 duck recordings used bill skin from embryonic day 25 to 27 duck embryos, ex vivo. Decapsulation also slightly lowered the terminal's current threshold, and the authors propose that the outer core protects the inner core.

### A simple filter model of adaptation

This is an illustrative model, not a measurement. Treat adaptation as one first-order high-pass stage with time constant τ, the Loewenstein and Skalak idea in its simplest form.

1. **A press, hold and release.** Run a step of force through the stage. For a stage normalized to pass fast change fully, the output jumps to +1 of the step just after the onset, decays toward zero during the hold (as e^(−t/τ)), and jumps the opposite way at the release, to e^(−T/τ) − 1 for a hold of length T: about −0.99 for a hold of 5τ. A transducer that responds to deformation in either direction then fires at both ends, much as the duck Grandry terminal shows onset and offset currents; the model is an analogy, not an account of how those currents arise.
2. **Two stages in series.** Put a capsule stage and a terminal stage one after the other. With equal time constants the step response is e^(−t/τ)(1 − t/τ): it starts at +1, crosses zero at τ, dips to about −0.14 at 2τ and settles to zero during a long hold. Remove either stage and the output is still transient, though its shape changes: the guiding rule in miniature.
3. **No peak on its own.** The gain of a high-pass stage, |H| = ωτ/√(1 + (ωτ)²), rises with frequency all the way up; it sets a corner frequency of 1/(2πτ), a graded low edge rather than a cut-off (at a tenth of the corner the gain is still about 0.0995), and has no best frequency. For stages with the same high-frequency gain and inputs of the same amplitude, a shorter τ attenuates slow change more strongly. With τ = 9 ms, the onset inactivation time constant of the duck Grandry terminal current, the corner is 1/(2π × 0.009 s) ≈ 17.7 Hz. That falls inside the human flutter range, but it is a cross-species coincidence of order of magnitude, not evidence.
4. **A peak needs a second, low-pass element.** For example (illustrative values, not measured), a high-pass time constant of 2.5 ms combined with a low-pass time constant of 0.25 ms peaks near 200 Hz, at 1/(2π × √(τ_high × τ_low)).

### Duck bill recordings, step by step

Three papers built the lamellar-cell case in steps, all in duck bill skin ex vivo.

- **2020** (Nikolaev and colleagues, Science Advances; embryonic day 24 to 26). Grandry lamellar cells fired action potentials, predominantly through R-type calcium channels. Herbst outer-core cells were mechanosensitive but not excitable.
- **January 2023** (Ziolkowski, Gracheva and Bagriantsev, eLife). The onset current in the Grandry terminal inactivated in 8.95 ± 1.82 ms (mean ± SEM), and tetrodotoxin blocked the afferent's action potentials.
- **Later 2023** (Nikolaev, Ziolkowski and colleagues, Science Advances). Latencies from lamellar-cell depolarization to afferent firing ran from 20 ms to about 9 s. Chemical transmission was proposed but not shown.

Grandry lamellar cells lack functional gap junctions with surrounding cells. In duck Herbst corpuscles, the Pacinian type, the inner-core lamellar Schwann cells are coupled to one another by gap junctions, and depolarizing one evoked an inward current in the terminal (Ziolkowski et al. 2025), although dye did not pass from the terminal into them.

### Mouse lamellar cells: optogenetics

Chen and colleagues activated the lamellar Schwann cells in anaesthetized adult mice and saw first-spike latencies up to about 40 ms. Electron microscopy showed lamellar cells linked to one another by many desmosomes and gap junctions and anchored to the axon by desmosomes. The Etv1-CreER line marks inner-core lamellar cells but is also expressed in other cell types, so the manipulation is not perfectly cell-specific.

### Meissner afferent labels, tallies and wrapping

The Ret label used Ret-CreER given tamoxifen in early embryos. The TrkB lineage label marked cells that expressed TrkB during development: an inducible Cre driver, TrkB-CreER, given tamoxifen, switched on a permanent reporter in them. Both afferent types conducted in the Aβ range. By electron microscopy, TrkB-lineage endings had more lamellar-cell wrappings than Ret-positive endings, and the authors proposed that the extent of wrapping helps set an afferent's force threshold and kinetics. The behavioural deletion used Advillin-Cre.

### Worked problem: the equal-pitch fit

The 2021 mice were C57BL/6, awake and head-fixed, and the equal-pitch fit near 450 Hz came from four mice:

> **P = f × (A / Aref)^0.32**, with Aref = 5.6 µm

**The frequency that keeps pitch constant.** Reference: 450 Hz at 5.6 µm. Amplitude doubles to 11.2 µm. What frequency feels the same?

1. Set pitches equal: 450 Hz × (5.6/5.6)^0.32 = f2 × (11.2/5.6)^0.32.
2. Left side: 450 Hz × 1 = 450 Hz. Right side: f2 × 2^0.32.
3. 2^0.32 = e^(0.32 × 0.693) = e^0.2218 = 1.248.
4. f2 = 450 Hz ÷ 1.248 = 360.5 Hz.
5. Change: (450 − 360.5) ÷ 450 = 0.199, a 19.9% decrease. Check: 360.5 × 1.248 = 449.9 Hz.

The amplitude ratio has no units, so the answer stays in hertz. This is a prediction of a local mouse fit, assumed to hold at double amplitude; it is not a new measurement.
