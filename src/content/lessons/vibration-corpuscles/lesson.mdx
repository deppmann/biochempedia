techniques:
  - name: "Terminal patch clamp with single-fibre recording"
    blurb: >-
      A pipette is sealed onto the afferent terminal inside an intact corpuscle while a second
      electrode records spikes from the same fibre. You see the transduction current itself and
      can ask whether its timing, rather than the capsule's, sets adaptation. So far this has been
      done in ex vivo duck bill skin.
  - name: "Cre and lineage genetics with operant psychophysics"
    blurb: >-
      Genetic labels (Ret, a TrkB lineage tracer) mark afferent subtypes, a conditional deletion
      removes an end organ, and trained mice report whether they felt a calibrated push. The
      result shows what that end organ contributes to detection, within the limits of a
      developmental rather than acute loss.
  - name: "Optogenetics in non-neuronal cells"
    blurb: >-
      A light-gated channel or pump expressed in lamellar Schwann cells lets you activate or
      inhibit them while recording the afferent or testing behaviour. Activation shows what the
      cells can do; inhibition shows what the behaviour needs, both under artificial drive.
  - name: "Chronic DRG recording in freely moving mice"
    blurb: >-
      A stabilized electrode in the lumbar dorsal root ganglion records single sensory neurons
      while the mouse moves about freely. It asks what an afferent reports in natural life
      instead of during imposed sine waves.

**Strip away the onion layers of a Pacinian corpuscle and you might expect the nerve ending inside to keep firing during a steady press. In isolated cat corpuscles in 1965 it mostly did not, and in duck corpuscles in 2025 the terminal, with its inner-core cells left in place after the outer layers were removed, still adapted in milliseconds.**

So where does the brevity of a vibration receptor come from, what tunes it, and what does the brain make of the signal? The [touch and pain lesson](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) introduced Meissner and Pacinian afferents as rapidly adapting, with small and large receptive fields, and [the LTMR lesson](/lessons/ltmr-classes/) set them in the full class table. This lesson starts at mechanism. Two terms recur. A **lamellar cell** is a flattened, Schwann-cell-like cell wrapped around the nerve terminal inside a corpuscle. In the Pacinian corpuscle, lamellar Schwann cells hug the terminal in the **inner core**, and many concentric layers, the onion, make up the **outer core**.

## Flutter and hum: what human hands report

Put a vibrating probe on your fingertip and turn up the frequency. The percept changes kind. Talbot, Darian-Smith, Kornhuber and Mountcastle (1968, Journal of Neurophysiology) asked healthy adults to describe sinusoidal stimulation of the hand. At about 5 to 40 Hz they reported a light **flutter**. From around 60 to 80 Hz upward the sensation became a deeper, diffuse **hum** of vibration.

The same paper recorded single afferents from the median nerve of anaesthetized rhesus monkeys. That is a second preparation, not a measurement of the human subjects. The comparison tied flutter to the rapidly adapting afferents now linked to Meissner corpuscles and vibration to Pacinian afferents. Read the boundary between them as a description of two percepts, not a sharp receptor cut-off.

Frequency preferences also depend on species. Prsa and colleagues (2021, Nature Communications) found that behavioural vibration sensitivity peaked near 240 Hz at the human index fingertip and near 1000 Hz in their mouse forepaw preparation. "Pacinian tuning around 250 Hz" is therefore a human figure. Behavioural sensitivity is also not identical to the tuning of a single isolated afferent.

## The onion that was not the filter: decapsulation then and now

Why does a Pacinian afferent fire at the start and end of a press but not during it? The classical answer was mechanical. Loewenstein and Mendelson (1965, Journal of Physiology) dissected Pacinian corpuscles from the cat mesentery and removed most of the lamellae. After decapsulation, the generator potential decayed much more slowly (later reviewers note this appeared mainly under intense compression). The impulses did not persist: the ending typically still fired a single spike. So adaptation had two stages. One lay in the mechanics around the terminal; another lay in the terminal's spike-generating membrane, which stopped firing despite a lasting depolarization.

Loewenstein and Skalak (1966) then modelled the capsule. Think of a stack of water balloons: press slowly and the fluid flows sideways before the centre feels much; tap fast and the force reaches the core. In their model, fluid between the lamellae supplies viscosity and the lamellae supply elasticity, so the capsule suppresses low-frequency input and passes transients. For decades that **high-pass filter** was the textbook explanation of rapid adaptation.

The duck bill tested it directly. Ziolkowski and colleagues (2025, Science Advances) patch-clamped the afferent terminal inside Herbst corpuscles, the avian Pacinian, in bill skin from embryonic day 25 to 27 duck embryos. Removing the outer core left the rapid kinetics of the mechanotransduction current intact and did not significantly change the afferent's frequency tuning. Slower inactivation appeared only as decapsulated terminals deteriorated. That last result offers one reading of 1965: a damaged, slowly dying terminal can look like a terminal that has lost its filter.

Keep the scope. This is a bird corpuscle, ex vivo, from late embryos. It shows the outer core is not needed for fast currents and tuning in that preparation; it does not show the capsule does nothing in a cat or a human hand. The same study found that depolarizing an inner-core lamellar Schwann cell by current injection lowered the afferent's mechanical threshold, which moves attention inward, toward the cells that touch the terminal.

## Two afferents in one Meissner corpuscle

The Meissner corpuscle sits in a dermal papilla of glabrous skin, a stack of lamellar cells with Aβ axons coiled between them. The usual cartoon has one axon per corpuscle. Neubarth, Emanuel, Ginty and colleagues (2020, Science) labelled mouse Meissner afferents by two genetic routes: **Ret** expression and a **TrkB lineage** label (cells that expressed TrkB during development, marked when an inducible Cre driver, TrkB-CreER, given tamoxifen, switched on a permanent reporter in them). In adult mice, most corpuscles in the paw pads held one afferent of each kind, intermingled. Both conducted in the Aβ range. Each kind tiled the skin with its own kind, its fields offset from the other's.

The two differed in physiology. TrkB-lineage afferents were the more sensitive. Ret-positive afferents were less sensitive, and 4 of 10 recorded Ret-positive afferents also fired during maintained indentation. So "two types of rapidly adapting afferent" overstates the result: one subtype included units with a sustained response. And because TrkB protein was undetectable in adult terminals, the TrkB label marks a developmental lineage, not a current receptor.

Then the behaviour. Deleting TrkB from sensory neurons during development produced adult mice without the Meissner system. Mice were trained in an operant task to report a step indentation of the forepaw.

**Worked problem, part (a): what does a doubled threshold mean?** Adult control mice detected the push at a threshold of about 10 mN; mice lacking Meissner corpuscles, at about 20 mN (Neubarth et al. 2020).

1. Ratio: 20 mN ÷ 10 mN = 2.0, a twofold rise.
2. The mutants still had a measurable threshold, so they still detected touch.
3. Conclusion: Meissner afferents contribute to gentle-touch detection, lowering the threshold by about half, but other afferents can support detection without them.

Two limits apply. The loss was developmental and TrkB was deleted in all sensory neurons (Advillin-Cre), not only Meissner afferents, so other afferents may have compensated or contributed, and an acute silencing could give a larger or smaller effect. And the task measured detection only, not discrimination of flutter or slip.

## Accessory cells that feel force

If the terminal adapts without the outer layers, what are the lamellar cells for? Three papers from the Bagriantsev and Gracheva laboratories built the answer in steps, all in duck bill skin ex vivo.

- **2020** (Nikolaev and colleagues, Science Advances; embryonic day 24 to 26). Lamellar cells of both Grandry and Herbst corpuscles produced mechanically activated currents. Grandry lamellar cells fired action potentials, predominantly through R-type calcium channels. Herbst outer-core cells were mechanosensitive but not excitable.
- **January 2023** (Ziolkowski, Gracheva and Bagriantsev, eLife). The Grandry terminal itself carried transduction currents at both the onset and the offset of indentation. The onset current inactivated in 8.95 ± 1.82 ms (mean ± SEM). Tetrodotoxin blocked the afferent's action potentials. The terminal is a transducer in its own right.
- **Later 2023** (Nikolaev, Ziolkowski and colleagues, Science Advances). Selectively depolarizing one Grandry lamellar cell could trigger firing in its afferent, and calcium influx was required. Latencies ran from 20 ms to about 9 s. Chemical transmission was proposed but not shown.

So the corpuscle is bicellular: two mechanosensitive cells, each able to influence the spike. In duck the latencies are the problem. A 200 Hz vibration has a 5 ms cycle, so a signal that takes 20 ms or more cannot be what follows each cycle. Lamellar cells may set the terminal's sensitivity rather than carry each cycle.

The mouse supplies the behavioural test. Chen and colleagues (2025, Science Advances; Huber laboratory) expressed optogenetic tools in mouse Pacinian lamellar Schwann cells. Activating them drove time-locked afferent spiking in anaesthetized adult mice, with first-spike latencies up to about 40 ms. The Etv1-CreER line marks inner-core lamellar cells but is also expressed in other cell types, so the manipulation is not perfectly cell-specific. Inhibiting them raised the hindpaw vibrotactile detection threshold of awake mice about fivefold. The authors note that optogenetic drive may not reproduce normal physiology. The channel in either cell is unproven (Piezo2 is the expected candidate in the terminal) and the signal from lamellar cell to terminal remains unidentified; [the mechanotransduction channels lesson](/lessons/mechanotransduction-channels/) sets out what proof a transducer claim needs, and [Merkel cells](/lessons/merkel-cells/) give a parallel case of a non-neuronal partner cell in touch.

## Vibration in the brain and in the wild

What does cortex do with a Pacinian signal? Prsa, Morandell, Cuenu and Huber (2019, Nature) delivered vibrations to the mouse forelimb and recorded neurons in forelimb primary somatosensory cortex (S1). The responses depended on deep Pacinian input, were tuned to a feature combining frequency and amplitude, and did not follow the vibration cycle by cycle. That is a rate code for a composite feature, unlike the phase-locking of auditory nerve fibres described in [hearing and balance](/lessons/hearing-and-balance/).

Prsa and colleagues (2021) then measured the percept. Trained mice (C57BL/6, awake, head-fixed) and adult human volunteers compared vibrations, and in both species a change in amplitude shifted perceived pitch. For mice near a 450 Hz reference, the equal-pitch data were fitted with a power law (a fit to four mice):

> **P = f × (A / Aref)^0.32**, with Aref = 5.6 µm

**Worked problem, part (b): the frequency that keeps pitch constant.** Reference: 450 Hz at 5.6 µm. Amplitude doubles to 11.2 µm. What frequency feels the same?

1. Set pitches equal: 450 Hz × (5.6/5.6)^0.32 = f2 × (11.2/5.6)^0.32.
2. Left side: 450 Hz × 1 = 450 Hz. Right side: f2 × 2^0.32.
3. 2^0.32 = e^(0.32 × 0.693) = e^0.2218 = 1.248.
4. f2 = 450 Hz ÷ 1.248 = 360.5 Hz.
5. Change: (450 − 360.5) ÷ 450 = 0.199, a 19.9% decrease. Check: 360.5 × 1.248 = 449.9 Hz.

The amplitude ratio has no units, so the answer stays in hertz. This is a prediction of a local fit, assumed to hold at double amplitude; it is not a new measurement.

Finally, natural behaviour. Turecek and Ginty (2024, Neuron) recorded single Pacinian neurons in the L4 dorsal root ganglion of awake, freely moving mice. The neurons fired during the mouse's own movements as well as during external vibration, and sensitivity varied widely between neurons. The most sensitive responded to substrate vibrations generated 2.5 m away, the furthest point tested on that surface, not a known limit. Whether the brain separates self-generated from external vibration was not tested. The [dorsal horn lesson](/lessons/dorsal-horn-touch/) follows these signals into the spinal cord and brainstem, and [prediction](/lessons/multisensory-and-prediction/) returns to how the brain discounts its own movements.

### Design problem

Decapsulation of cat Pacinian corpuscles (1965) and duck Herbst corpuscles (2025) seem to give different answers about the outer core. Design an experiment that decides whether the lamellae filter the stimulus or the terminal itself adapts.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

1. **Measure the right signal.** Voltage-clamp the terminal inside one corpuscle and record the transduction current before and after removing the outer core, in the same corpuscle. Record spikes from the same fibre separately, so generator current and spiking are not confused.
2. **Control for damage.** Track time after dissection and the terminal's leak current. If adaptation slows only as leak rises, deterioration, not loss of a filter, explains the change. Include sham-handled corpuscles kept for the same time.
3. **Probe the filter directly.** Deliver step indentations and sinusoids from 10 to 1000 Hz at matched displacement at the terminal (measured, for example, by imaging the inner core), so that any change in tuning can be assigned to mechanics or to the membrane.
4. **Test in a mammal.** Duck results do not transfer automatically. In mouse Pacinian corpuscles, add optogenetic silencing of lamellar Schwann cells (as Chen and colleagues did in 2025) with an afferent recording and a behavioural detection threshold.
5. **Interpret.** If the terminal adapts at the same speed and keeps its tuning once the outer core is gone, adaptation does not need the outer core (the inner-core cells are still present, so a separate test is needed to rule them out); if it slows only in healthy decapsulated terminals, the capsule filters. Human corpuscles cannot be patch-clamped this way, so human answers would need other methods, such as microneurography and psychophysics.

</details>
