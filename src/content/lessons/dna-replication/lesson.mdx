scientists:
  - name: "Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl"
    contribution: >-
      Followed nitrogen-15-labelled DNA in E. coli through rounds of replication in ordinary
      nitrogen, using caesium chloride density gradients, and found the hybrid-then-hybrid-plus-light
      pattern expected if each daughter molecule receives one parental subunit.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Meselson & Stahl, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 44:671 (1958); Meselson, Stahl & Vinograd, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 43:581 (1957)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Meselson and Stahl worked at the California Institute of Technology in Pasadena. The
      replication experiment needed a method first. In 1957, with Jerome Vinograd, they described
      equilibrium density-gradient centrifugation: DNA spun for many hours in concentrated caesium
      chloride settles where the salt solution matches its own buoyant density. In 1958 they
      applied it to E. coli B. The bacteria were grown for many generations on ammonium chloride
      made with heavy nitrogen-15, then a tenfold excess of ordinary ammonium chloride was added to
      the growing culture, and samples were taken as it doubled. After one generation they saw only
      DNA of hybrid density. After two, hybrid and light DNA were present in equal amounts. Heating
      hybrid DNA split it into two species that matched heated heavy and heated light DNA. They
      concluded that each daughter molecule receives one parental subunit, and stated plainly that
      they had not shown each subunit to be a single chain. The result made semiconservative
      copying the foundation for later studies of the machinery.
  - name: "Arthur Kornberg"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory purified from E. coli the first enzyme shown to make DNA on a DNA template,
      now called DNA polymerase I, which established that a polymerase copies a template by
      extending a primer with deoxynucleoside triphosphates.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Lehman, Bessman, Simms & Kornberg, J. Biol. Chem. 233:163 (1958); Nobel Lecture, 11 December 1959; De Lucia & Cairns, Nature 224:1164 (1969)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Kornberg's laboratory set out to find the enzyme that makes DNA by grinding up E. coli and
      looking for an activity that put radioactive nucleotides into acid-insoluble DNA. In 1958,
      with I. Robert Lehman, Maurice Bessman and Ernest Simms, he described the partial purification
      of such an enzyme and the substrates it needed: all four deoxynucleoside triphosphates and a
      DNA template, which the enzyme copied. Kornberg shared the 1959 Nobel Prize in Physiology or
      Medicine with Severo Ochoa. For a decade the enzyme, now DNA polymerase I, was the obvious
      candidate for the cell's copying enzyme. In 1969 Paula De Lucia and John Cairns reported an
      E. coli mutant, polA, with very little of this activity that still grew. A cell that copies its chromosome without much Pol I polymerase activity
      must use another enzyme, and the search in polA extracts led to DNA polymerases II and III.
      The episode made the case for testing a purified enzyme's role with a mutant, and Pol I
      found its real job in removing primers and filling gaps.
  - name: "Reiji Okazaki and Tsuneko Okazaki"
    contribution: >-
      With Kiwako Sakabe, Kazunori Sugimoto and Akio Sugino, labelled newly made DNA in E. coli for
      a few seconds and found the label first in short chains that later appeared in long DNA,
      evidence that at least part of the DNA is made discontinuously.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Okazaki, Okazaki, Sakabe, Sugimoto & Sugino, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 59:598 (1968)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The 1968 paper that gave Okazaki fragments their name has five authors: Reiji Okazaki,
      Tsuneko Okazaki (printed as Tuneko), Kiwako Sakabe, Kazunori Sugimoto and Akio Sugino. The
      question was how a fork can copy both antiparallel strands when known polymerases extend
      chains in only one direction. Their approach was to catch DNA in the act. They grew E. coli B
      at 20°C, which slows replication, gave very short pulses of radioactive thymidine, broke the
      cells open, separated the strands in alkali and sized the labelled chains on alkaline sucrose
      gradients. After a five-second pulse most of the label was in a slowly sedimenting component
      of about 11S; with longer labelling, label moved into faster-sedimenting long DNA. They
      suggested units of about 1,000 to 2,000 nucleotides and examined phage-infected cells as well.
      The paper left the direction of synthesis and the joining of the pieces by ligase for further
      proof. Those later tests turned the short chains into the standard picture of the lagging
      strand.

    contribution: >-
      Found in extracts of the ciliate Tetrahymena an activity, telomerase, that adds TTGGGG
      repeats to telomere-like primers, and later found that its RNA contains the sequence that
      templates those repeats.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Greider & Blackburn, Cell 43:405 (1985); Greider & Blackburn, Nature 337:331 (1989); Nobel Lectures, 7 December 2009."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Blackburn had sequenced the ends of small linear DNA molecules in Tetrahymena as a
      postdoctoral fellow in Joseph Gall's laboratory at Yale, finding a short repeat many times
      over. Ciliates suit this work because their macronucleus carries a very large number of small
      chromosomes, so an extract holds many ends. Greider joined Blackburn's laboratory as a
      graduate student and looked for an enzyme that could lengthen such ends. In 1985 they reported
      that Tetrahymena cell-free extracts added tandem TTGGGG repeats to synthetic telomeric
      primers, and that the activity was destroyed by heat and by proteinase K, pointing to a
      protein component. In 1989 they reported that the cloned RNA of the enzyme contains the
      sequence CAACCCCAA and suggested that it templates the repeats. An enzyme that carries its
      own template could rebuild ends that ordinary polymerases cannot finish. Blackburn and
      Greider shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Jack Szostak, and their
      assay made it possible to ask which cells make telomerase and what happens when it is lost.

techniques:
  - name: "Equilibrium density-gradient centrifugation in CsCl"
    blurb: >-
      DNA is spun for many hours in concentrated caesium chloride until a density gradient forms
      and each DNA species settles where the solution matches its buoyant density. DNA made with
      heavy nitrogen-15 bands apart from ordinary DNA, so the bands report how parental and new
      material are shared among molecules.
  - name: "Pulse labelling and alkaline sucrose gradients"
    blurb: >-
      Cells are given radioactive thymidine for seconds, then the DNA is denatured in alkali and
      sized by sedimentation. The size of the newest chains shows whether DNA is made continuously
      or as short pieces that are joined later; a chase in unlabelled thymidine shows where the
      label goes next.
  - name: "Cell-free primer-extension assay"
    blurb: >-
      A cell extract or purified enzyme is given a short synthetic primer and labelled
      nucleotides, and the products are sized on a gel. A ladder of added repeats, lost after heat
      or protease treatment, identified the telomerase activity in Tetrahymena.
  - name: "Reconstitution with purified proteins"
    blurb: >-
      Replication is rebuilt in a tube from purified proteins and origin DNA, then single
      components are left out or added. In budding yeast this showed which polymerase and clamp
      each strand needs in that defined reaction.

  - stem: "Why is one new strand at a replication fork made as short pieces?"
    choices:
      - "Polymerases extend only a 3' end, and on one template that means growing away from the fork"
      - "Polymerases extend only a 5' end, and on one template that means growing toward the fork"
      - "The helicase on one template stops at intervals, and each stop ends one fragment"
      - "Primase on one template makes DNA, and polymerase cannot extend DNA it did not start"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Every DNA polymerase adds nucleotides to a free 3'-OH, so chains grow 5' to 3'. The two
      template strands are antiparallel, so on one of them the new strand must grow away from the
      opening fork and is restarted on new primers. Polymerases do not extend 5' ends. Helicase
      pausing does not define the fragments. Primase makes RNA primers, not DNA, and polymerase
      extends them readily.
  - stem: "Each daughter molecule keeps one old strand and builds one new strand. E. coli grown for many generations in 15N medium are moved to 14N; with complete synchronous rounds and no label recycling, what DNA bands appear after three generations?"
    choices:
      - "50% hybrid, 50% light"
      - "12.5% heavy, 87.5% light"
      - "25% hybrid, 75% light"
      - "One band, 1/8 of the way to heavy"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      One heavy duplex gives 2 hybrids after one round, 2 hybrids and 2 light after two, and 2
      hybrids and 6 light after three: 25% hybrid and 75% light. The two original heavy strands
      never multiply. Half hybrid and half light is the two-generation answer. Heavy plus light at
      12.5% heavy is the conservative prediction. A single band at one-eighth is the uniform
      dispersive prediction.
  - stem: "Why does E. coli need no telomerase to finish copying its chromosome?"
    choices:
      - "Its Pol III can start new chains without any primer"
      - "Its chromosome is circular, so every gap has a 3' end behind it"
      - "Its primase makes DNA primers that never need removal"
      - "Its Pol I adds repeated sequences to the chromosome ends"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      On a circle there is no end: every gap left when an RNA primer is removed has DNA upstream
      whose 3' end Pol I can extend, and ligase seals the nick. Pol III, like the other replicative DNA polymerases,
      needs a primer. DnaG primase makes RNA primers, which are removed. Pol I fills primer gaps
      and does not add telomere repeats; adding repeats is telomerase's job in eukaryotes.

**One generation after Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl switched heavy-nitrogen *E. coli* to ordinary nitrogen, the extracted DNA formed one band halfway between heavy and light. Why halfway?**

This lesson builds on [DNA as the genetic material](/lessons/dna-the-genetic-material/) and the dNTPs from [nucleotide metabolism](/lessons/nucleotide-metabolism/).

> **The one idea.** Each daughter molecule keeps one old strand and builds one new strand.
>
> *Scope:* chromosome replication. Meselson and Stahl showed in *E. coli* that each daughter inherits one parental subunit, not which enzymes copy it. Heating fitted those subunits being the two strands, but their experiment alone did not prove that.

## Three models, one experiment

The first film ran three models: **conservative replication** keeps the parental duplex whole, **semiconservative** gives each daughter one parental and one new strand, and **dispersive** mixes old and new segments through both strands.

Meselson and Stahl grew *E. coli* B for many generations with heavy nitrogen-15, then added a tenfold excess of ordinary nitrogen-14 ammonium chloride. **Equilibrium density-gradient centrifugation** in concentrated caesium chloride then separates heavy, hybrid and light DNA by buoyant density.

**Predict.** Start with one heavy-heavy duplex. Assume complete synchronous rounds, equal-length molecules, fully light new strands and no recycled heavy nitrogen. Under semiconservative replication, what molecules appear after a third round in light medium?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Each original heavy strand stays in one hybrid; the other six molecules are light: 25% hybrid and 75% light.

</details>

| Model | After generation 1 | After generation 2 | After generation 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | 1 heavy + 1 light (50% heavy) | 1 heavy + 3 light (25% heavy) | 1 heavy + 7 light (12.5% heavy, 87.5% light) |
| Semiconservative | 2 hybrid | 2 hybrid + 2 light | 2 hybrid + 6 light |
| Dispersive, even mixing | one band 1/2 of the way from light to heavy | one band 1/4 of the way | one band 1/8 of the way |

**Observed.** A single hybrid band after one generation rules out conservative copying, though not dispersive. Hybrid and light bands after two generations, in equal amounts, rule out uniform dispersive copying.

**Predict.** Does the average heavy fraction per generation differ among the three models?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. In every model it is one-half, one-quarter, then one-eighth. The distribution of label among molecules, not its average, distinguishes the models.

</details>

Autoradiography of bean (*Vicia faba*) chromosomes by J. Herbert Taylor and colleagues in 1957 was separate evidence.

Copying happens at a Y-shaped **replication fork**. Every DNA polymerase extends only a free 3'-OH, whose hydroxyl attacks the incoming dNTP and releases pyrophosphate (see [enzyme action](/lessons/enzyme-action-basics/)). The replicative DNA polymerases also cannot start a chain: each needs a **primer**, a short paired stretch with a 3' end to extend. The templates are antiparallel, so how can one fork extend 3' ends on both? The second film answers.

## Testing the pieces

The second film named the **leading strand** and the **lagging strand**'s **Okazaki fragments**, not the evidence for them.

The upper new strand is one long piece growing toward the fork; the lower new strand is three short primed Okazaki fragments growing away from the fork. An inset shows a primer being extended from its 3' end." caption="Both new strands grow 5' to 3', so one is made continuously toward the fork and the other in short primed pieces growing away from it." />

In 1968 Reiji Okazaki, Tsuneko Okazaki, Kiwako Sakabe, Kazunori Sugimoto and Akio Sugino tested for pieces. They grew *E. coli* B at 20°C to slow replication, gave radioactive-thymidine pulses as short as five seconds, denatured the DNA and sized the labelled chains on alkaline sucrose gradients. After a five-second pulse most label was in a slow component of about 11S; with longer labelling it moved into long DNA. They suggested pieces of about 1,000 to 2,000 nucleotides, leaving their direction and joining for further proof. Eukaryotic Okazaki fragments are shorter, about 100 to 200 nucleotides.

## The fork's jobs, organism by organism

The table adds what the second film skipped: origin opening, helicase direction and primer removal.

| Job | *E. coli* (one origin, **oriC**, circular chromosome) | Eukaryotes (many origins, linear chromosomes) |
|---|---|---|
| Open the origin | DnaA binds oriC and opens a short AT-rich stretch | In G1, inactive MCM2-7 rings load at origins |
| Unwind | DnaB ring (loaded by DnaC, ATP-driven) moves 5' to 3' on the lagging-strand template; SSB coats single strands; gyrase, a topoisomerase, relieves overwinding | In S phase, Cdc45 and GINS join MCM as the active **CMG helicase**, which moves 3' to 5' on the leading-strand template |
| Prime | DnaG primase makes short RNA primers | **Pol α-primase** makes RNA and extends it briefly with DNA |
| Extend | Pol III holoenzyme; its β sliding clamp keeps it on DNA for thousands of nucleotides | In the usual summary, **Pol ε** most leading-strand and **Pol δ** most lagging-strand synthesis, both on the PCNA clamp |
| Remove primers | Pol I removes each primer with its 5' to 3' exonuclease while filling the gap | Pol δ displaces primer material as a 5' flap; FEN1 cuts short flaps, Dna2 helps with longer ones, RNase H has a smaller part |
| Seal | DNA ligase | DNA ligase I |

Arthur Kornberg's laboratory purified Pol I first, but a 1969 mutant with very little Pol I polymerase activity still grew; Pol III is the replicative polymerase.

**Predict.** The *E. coli* K-12 chromosome is about 4.6 million base pairs. Two forks each move at about 1,000 nucleotides per second. How long does one round take, and how can cells divide about every 20 minutes in rich medium?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Each fork copies half: (4.6 × 10^6 / 2) / 1,000 = 2,300 seconds, about 38 minutes. Fork speed rises only modestly with growth rate, far too little to close the gap, so new rounds begin at oriC before earlier rounds finish, and each division still receives a finished chromosome.

</details>

## Three layers of accuracy

Polymerases first **select** bases: the active site closes well around a correct pair and poorly around a mispair, leaving roughly one error per 10^4 to 10^5 nucleotides. They then **proofread**: a 3' to 5' exonuclease removes a mispaired 3' end. In *E. coli* Pol III that activity is in the ε subunit. Proofreading improves accuracy about 100-fold, and up to 1,000-fold depending on polymerase and mispair. Not every polymerase proofreads: eukaryotic Pol α lacks a functional proofreading exonuclease. **Mismatch repair** then fixes errors after the fork ([DNA repair and recombination](/lessons/dna-repair-and-recombination/)). Together they bring the *E. coli* rate to roughly one error per 10^9 to 10^10 base pairs per round.

**Predict.** At that rate, about how often does copying a 4.6-million-base-pair chromosome add an error?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

About one copy in 200 to 2,000. These are orders of magnitude, not fixed constants; assay and sequence matter.

</details>

## Linear chromosomes need finishing work

**Predict.** When an RNA primer is removed, why can a circular chromosome always fill the gap, while the gap left by the last primer at a linear tip may not be filled?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A circle has DNA behind every gap, supplying a 3' end for extension. At a linear tip, no upstream 3' end may remain. This is the classical **end-replication problem**.

</details>

By the mid-1990s it was clear that real telomeres end in a single-stranded 3' overhang of the G-rich strand, which leading-strand synthesis cannot regenerate and telomerase makes up; the lagging strand has its own finishing problem (Going deeper).

<LessonFigure src="/figures/dna-replication/end-replication-problem.webp" width={1200} height={689} alt="AI-generated illustration: a linear chromosome with a telomere at each end, and two close-ups of a lagging-strand tip. In this classical model, with the last RNA primer in place the new strand reaches the end; once it is removed, the new strand stops short of the template end." caption="Removing a terminal primer can leave no 3' end to extend; real telomeres also require overhang restoration and C-strand fill-in." />

**Telomeres** are repeated sequences at chromosome ends: TTGGGG in the ciliate *Tetrahymena* and TTAGGG in humans. In 1985 Carol Greider and Elizabeth Blackburn found that *Tetrahymena* extracts added TTGGGG repeats to synthetic telomeric primers; heat and protease destroyed the activity. In 1989 they reported that the enzyme's RNA contains CAACCCCAA, suggested as the template.

**Telomerase** is a reverse transcriptase (it makes DNA from an RNA template) that carries its own RNA and extends the G-rich 3' strand. The C strand is filled in separately; in engineered human cells that required the CST complex with Pol α-primase. Most human somatic cells make little telomerase, so their telomeres shorten as they divide; germline cells and most cancer cells keep it active. Blackburn, Greider and Jack Szostak shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

### Design problem

*E. coli* are given a 5-second pulse of tritiated thymidine, and their DNA is denatured and sized. Right after the pulse, most label is in pieces of about 1,000 to 2,000 nucleotides. After a 60-second chase in unlabelled thymidine, the label is in long DNA. In a ligase-defective mutant, the short pieces persist after the chase. What do you conclude, and what control would you add?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Conclusion.** At least some new DNA is made as short pieces later joined into long strands. The chase shows the same labelled molecules moving from short to long, a precursor becoming a product, and the mutant shows that joining needs ligase.

**Controls.** Long-labelled DNA handled the same way should stay long (not extraction breakage); repair mutants should still make the pieces (not repair patches). Hybridizing pulse-labelled pieces to separated strands of a region with known fork direction tests the semidiscontinuous prediction: pieces mostly from the lagging strand. Early data could not exclude pieces on both strands, and later *E. coli* work by Tye and colleagues in 1977 suggested that excising misincorporated uracil also makes short fragments, so a uracil-excision-deficient strain gives a cleaner test.

</details>

## Going deeper

In a replisome rebuilt from purified budding-yeast proteins, Pol δ helped establish leading strands before handing off to Pol ε, so the usual division is dynamic.

Work published in 2024 added a second problem on the lagging strand: in budding-yeast replisomes rebuilt on linear DNA, no primers were laid on the overhang, and lagging-strand synthesis stopped in a zone more than 26 nucleotides from the template end. CST with Pol α-primase supplies the C-strand fill-in. This refines, rather than overturns, the classical last-primer model.
