summary: >-
  One epinephrine molecule can mobilize millions of glucose units. That single fact
  captures a core logic of cell signaling: a tiny extracellular signal is converted
  at the membrane into an internal messenger, then amplified down a relay of enzymes
  until it changes what the cell does. This lesson builds every pathway from one
  repeating template: each switch is pushed ON by one reaction and pulled OFF by
  another. The gains multiply down the chain, the OFF reactions set how big and how
  long the response is, and disease follows when a switch sticks ON or its ON arm
  weakens. The beta-adrenergic cAMP cascade is the worked paradigm; insulin is the
  deep dive; cholera, pertussis, Cushing's and CML are the cautionary tales that
  prove the wiring.
hook: "One epinephrine molecule can mobilize millions of glucose units, and the same relay has to stop on time."

techniques:
  - name: "The cell-fractionation paradox (Sutherland's method)"
    blurb: >-
      Grind tissue, spin it, and split it into a membrane pellet and a soluble supernatant.
      Sutherland found adrenaline needed the membrane fraction to act but the response
      (a phosphorylase-activating factor) showed up in the supernatant. The hormone could not be in two places at once. The
      resolution was a diffusible second messenger made at the membrane.
  - name: "Reconstitution with a broken cell (Gilman's cyc⁻ assay)"
    blurb: >-
      Start with membranes from a mutant cell that cannot raise cAMP, add back a protein extract from normal
      cells, and watch signaling return. Gilman's cyc⁻ S49 lymphoma restored cAMP only
      when GTP was present, which is how the G-protein transducer was first caught; Gilman
      purified Gs from rabbit liver some years later.
  - name: "Radioligand receptor binding"
    blurb: >-
      Tag a hormone with a radioactive label and count how much sticks to membranes at
      equilibrium. Lefkowitz tagged the hormone ACTH with radioactive iodine, an early handle on
      a hormone receptor as a countable molecule. Receptor pharmacology itself was decades older.
  - name: "Western blot readout of kinase inhibition"
    blurb: >-
      Run cell lysate on a gel, transfer it, and probe with a phospho-specific antibody to
      see whether a kinase is firing. Druker used this to show STI-571 (Gleevec) shut down
      Bcr-Abl in CML cells.

  - stem: "A peptide hormone binds a receptor on the outside of a liver cell and, seconds later, glycogen breakdown begins inside. In the five-component scheme of a signaling pathway, the molecule that carries the message across that gap — generated inside the cell only after the receptor is activated — is called the:"
    choices:
      - "second messenger"
      - "first (primary) messenger"
      - "effector"
      - "physiological response"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The second messenger (for example cAMP) is the intracellular signal made after
      receptor activation; it bridges the outside-bound hormone and the inside response.
      The first messenger is the extracellular hormone itself, which binds the receptor but
      does not cross the membrane. The effector is the downstream enzyme the second messenger
      acts on (such as PKA), not the messenger. The physiological response is the end output
      (glucose release), not the carrier of the message.
  - stem: "Cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates Gs-α so that it can no longer hydrolyze GTP. Compared with a healthy cell, the affected intestinal cell will have:"
    choices:
      - "lower cAMP, because Gs can no longer be activated"
      - "unchanged cAMP, because adenylate cyclase is the real target"
      - "persistently high cAMP, because Gs is locked in its active GTP-bound form"
      - "high IP3 and DAG, because the toxin switches the cell to the Gq branch"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Blocking GTP hydrolysis traps Gs-α in the GTP-bound ON state, so it continuously
      stimulates adenylate cyclase and cAMP stays high — driving the chloride and water
      efflux that causes the diarrhea. cAMP does not fall, because the lesion locks Gs ON,
      not OFF. Adenylate cyclase is downstream and over-stimulated, not bypassed. And the
      toxin acts on Gs, not Gq, so it does not switch the cell into the IP3/DAG branch.
  - stem: "PIP2 is a substrate in two different pathways with different outcomes. Which pairing correctly states what each enzyme does to PIP2?"
    choices:
      - "PLC phosphorylates PIP2 to PIP3; PI3K cleaves PIP2 into IP3 + DAG"
      - "Both enzymes cleave PIP2, but PLC works in the membrane and PI3K in the cytosol"
      - "Both enzymes phosphorylate PIP2, but at different positions on the inositol ring"
      - "PLC cleaves PIP2 into IP3 + DAG; PI3K phosphorylates PIP2 to PIP3"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      PLC hydrolyzes (cleaves) PIP2 into soluble IP3 and membrane-bound DAG; PI3K is a lipid
      kinase that phosphorylates PIP2 to make PIP3. Swapping the two enzymes is wrong. PI3K
      does not cleave PIP2, so "both cleave" fails; PLC does not phosphorylate PIP2, so "both
      phosphorylate" fails. Same substrate, different chemistry, different messengers is the whole
      point of the contrast.
  - stem: "In the beta-adrenergic cascade, one active receptor turns on ~10 G proteins, each adenylate cyclase makes ~100 cAMP, and so on down the chain. Why does one epinephrine molecule end up mobilizing millions of glucose units?"
    choices:
      - "The per-step gains add together, and many small additions reach a large total"
      - "The per-step gains multiply, so the output is the product of all the gains"
      - "Epinephrine is consumed and regenerated, re-firing the receptor millions of times"
      - "The receptor stays bound permanently, so the signal never has to be amplified"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Amplification is multiplicative: the final output is the product of the per-step gains,
      which grows steeply down the cascade (10 × 100 × … reaches millions from one input).
      Adding the gains would give only a small sum. Epinephrine is not consumed and
      regenerated to re-fire; the amplification comes from the enzyme relay, not from reusing
      the ligand. The receptor does not bind permanently — ligand dissociation is part of
      termination — and amplification is exactly what the cascade is for.
  - stem: "Gleevec (imatinib) and cetuximab are both anticancer drugs that target signaling, but in opposite ways. Which statement is correct?"
    choices:
      - "Gleevec wedges into the Bcr-Abl ATP pocket inside the cell; cetuximab is an antibody that blocks EGF from binding EGFR outside"
      - "Gleevec is an antibody that blocks ligand binding outside; cetuximab wedges into an ATP pocket inside"
      - "Both act outside the cell at the ligand-binding site, so a downstream KRAS mutation defeats both"
      - "Both act inside at the ATP pocket, so neither is affected by mutations in the receptor's ligand site"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Gleevec is a small-molecule kinase inhibitor that occupies the Bcr-Abl ATP-binding
      pocket (acting inside, "gum in a lock"); cetuximab is a monoclonal antibody that blocks
      EGF from binding EGFR and prevents dimerization (acting outside, "covering the
      doorbell"). Swapping the two is wrong. A downstream KRAS mutation defeats cetuximab
      specifically, because it leaves the pathway stuck ON below the receptor; it is not a
      problem for both. And they do not both act at the ATP pocket — one is an antibody.

  - stem: "A researcher treats cells with a high concentration of caffeine, which at that dose inhibits phosphodiesterase, while stimulating the beta-adrenergic receptor with a fixed dose of agonist. Compared with agonist alone, caffeine co-treatment will most likely:"
    choices:
      - "lower cAMP, because phosphodiesterase normally makes cAMP"
      - "block Gs activation, because phosphodiesterase is required for GDP–GTP exchange"
      - "raise cAMP, because the enzyme that degrades cAMP is blocked"
      - "have no effect, because phosphodiesterase acts only on GTP"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Phosphodiesterase degrades cAMP to AMP. Inhibiting it weakens cAMP's OFF arm while the
      cyclase keeps making cAMP, so cAMP rises (it also persists somewhat longer, by how much
      depending on whether PDE was the slowest OFF step). "Lower cAMP" is wrong because the
      enzyme destroys cAMP rather than making it. "No effect" is wrong because it acts on cAMP,
      not GTP. And "block Gs activation" is wrong because GDP–GTP exchange on Gs is catalyzed by
      the activated receptor, not by phosphodiesterase.
  - stem: "A tumor has lost both copies of PTEN, a lipid phosphatase that converts PIP3 back to PIP2. The most direct signaling consequence is:"
    choices:
      - "loss of PIP3, shutting down the Akt arm of insulin signaling"
      - "increased PLC activity, raising IP3 and intracellular calcium"
      - "failure to activate Ras, because PTEN is its GTPase-activating protein"
      - "accumulation of PIP3 and constitutive Akt signaling, promoting growth and survival"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      PTEN reverses PI3K by dephosphorylating PIP3 to PIP2; without it, PIP3 accumulates and
      Akt is chronically activated, driving growth and survival — which is why PTEN is a tumor
      suppressor. Losing PTEN raises PIP3, it does not lower it. PTEN acts on the PI3K/PIP3
      lipid arm, not on PLC/IP3, so it does not directly raise calcium. And PTEN is a lipid
      phosphatase, not a Ras GAP, so it has nothing to do with Ras GTP hydrolysis.
  - stem: "An oncogenic point mutation in Ras prevents hydrolysis of GTP but leaves nucleotide exchange intact. Functionally, this mutant Ras behaves most like:"
    choices:
      - "a Ras that is permanently in the inactive GDP-bound state"
      - "a Ras that is constitutively active because it stays GTP-bound"
      - "a Ras that can no longer bind guanine nucleotides at all"
      - "a Gi protein locked in its inhibitory conformation"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Ras is ON when GTP-bound and OFF once it hydrolyzes GTP to GDP; a mutant that cannot
      hydrolyze GTP is stuck in the GTP-bound active state, firing the Raf-MEK-ERK
      proliferation cascade continuously (mutations of this kind appear in roughly one in five
      human cancers). It is
      not locked in the inactive GDP state — that is the opposite of blocked hydrolysis. It
      still binds nucleotide, since exchange is intact. And Ras is a monomeric small GTPase,
      not a heterotrimeric Gi, which would inhibit adenylate cyclase rather than drive
      proliferation.

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm 5"
    text: |
      Imagine you whisper a secret to one friend, who tells ten friends, who each tell a
      hundred more. By the end, a whole crowd knows, even though you only whispered once.
      Cells work like that. A tiny signal touches the outside of a cell, and the cell passes
      it inward, getting louder at every step, until the message is big enough to make the
      cell do something, like let in sugar. The cell also has an OFF button for the message.
      When that button breaks and the message never stops, the person gets sick.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: |
      A signal molecule (a hormone) arrives at a cell. If it is small and oily it can slip
      through the membrane; if it is big it has to knock on a receptor that sticks through the
      membrane. The receptor passes the message to the inside by making a "second messenger"
      like cAMP. That kicks off a chain of enzymes, and at each step one enzyme switches on
      many of the next, so the message gets amplified. One adrenaline molecule can end up
      releasing millions of glucose molecules from storage. Every pathway has an ON phase, an
      amplification phase, and an OFF phase. Diseases like cholera and many cancers happen
      when the OFF phase fails and a switch gets stuck in the ON position.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: |
      Five components define a pathway: first messenger, receptor, second messenger,
      effectors, response, plus active termination. The beta-adrenergic paradigm runs
      epinephrine to GPCR to Gs to adenylate cyclase to cAMP to PKA to phosphorylase kinase to
      glycogen breakdown. The G protein is a heterotrimer; receptor activation catalyzes
      GDP-to-GTP exchange on alpha, which separates or rearranges away from beta-gamma; both can act on effectors. Three logics:
      Gs raises cAMP, Gi lowers it, Gq activates PLC (IP3 plus DAG, then Ca²⁺ and PKC). PKA is
      an R2C2 tetramer freed by cAMP. Amplification is the product of per-step gains, not the
      sum. Termination is active: intrinsic GTPase, phosphodiesterase (high-dose caffeine inhibits it),
      ligand dissociation, desensitization, with AKAP scaffolds positioning PKA and
      co-locating phosphatases. Other receptor classes: receptor dimers with attached JAK (growth
      hormone to JAK2 to STAT5) and RTKs that are kinases (EGFR to Grb2/SOS to Ras to
      Raf-MEK-ERK). Insulin's RTK is pre-dimerized and routes through IRS to PI3K, which
      phosphorylates PIP2 to PIP3 (contrast: PLC cleaves PIP2), then Akt to GLUT4. PTEN
      reverses PIP3 to PIP2 and is a tumor suppressor. Disease is mostly a stuck switch: cholera
      locks Gs ON, pertussis locks Gi OFF, Cushing's frees PKA, stuck-ON Ras keeps a kinase cascade running, and Bcr-Abl is a kinase stuck ON.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: |
      Read the cascade as a set of coupled GTPase and phosphorylation cycles whose duty cycles
      set signal duration. The heterotrimeric Gα cycle (GEF = the activated receptor, intrinsic
      and RGS-accelerated GTP hydrolysis as the timer) is mechanistically homologous to the
      monomeric Ras cycle (GEF = SOS, GAP-accelerated hydrolysis as the timer); oncogenic Ras
      mutations (e.g. G12, Q61) impair the GAP-stimulated transition state for hydrolysis (some, such as Q61L and G13D, also speed
      nucleotide exchange), which is why they lock GTP-on. The cAMP node illustrates that
      steady-state second-messenger concentration is set by a synthesis/degradation balance
      (adenylate cyclase vs phosphodiesterase), so methylxanthine PDE inhibition shifts the
      setpoint without touching the receptor. Spatial computation is real: AKAP complexes that hold PKA
      beside cyclases, phosphodiesterases and phosphatases help create local cAMP microdomains and steep phospho-
      gradients, so the same kinase yields target-specific outputs. The PIP2 fork is a clean
      lesson in hydrolysis vs covalent modification: PLC hydrolyzes the glycerophosphodiester
      bond (yielding soluble IP3 and membrane DAG, a coincidence input for conventional PKC),
      whereas class I PI3K phosphorylates the 3-OH of the inositol ring to make PIP3, a
      PH-domain docking platform for PDK1 and Akt; PTEN is the 3-phosphatase that opposes it,
      and its loss mimics activating PI3K mutations at the level of PIP3. The recurring design principle is
      that signaling specificity and reversibility are enforced kinetically by opposing enzyme
      pairs, and many oncogenic lesions disable the negative arm.

**One epinephrine molecule, arriving at the outside of a liver or muscle cell, can mobilize millions of glucose units from the glycogen inside** (a teaching estimate; see Going deeper, "Where the glucose goes").

The size is only half the puzzle. The relay that turns a few molecules into millions must also stop on time, or the cell keeps answering a signal that has gone. This lesson is about how one design does both.

The short film above covers the ideas up to PKA; the text covers them all.

## The parts: messengers, receptors, effectors

A phone computes with billions of transistors; a cell computes with thousands of signaling proteins wired together, sensing the outside, weighing competing inputs and committing to an action.

Strip a membrane-receptor pathway down and the same five components appear in order: a **primary (first) messenger** arrives from outside, binds a **receptor**, which generates or activates a **second messenger** inside the cell, which drives **effector molecules**, which produce the **physiological response**. A sixth feature is easy to forget: **active termination**; the pathway has to be switched off on purpose. In this lesson's example, epinephrine is the first messenger, the beta-adrenergic receptor is the receptor, cAMP is the second messenger, PKA and the glycogen enzymes are the effectors, and glucose release is the response.

First and second messengers sit on opposite sides of the membrane. A first messenger is the extracellular signal: epinephrine, insulin, a growth factor. A second messenger is made or released *after* the receptor fires and relays the order inside the cell: cAMP, IP3, DAG, calcium, PIP3. The split exists because of a physical constraint. Small, lipophilic (fat-soluble) signals find receptors inside the cell (steroids slip straight through the membrane; thyroid hormone enters through transporters such as MCT8). Large or polar signals, such as peptide hormones and growth factors, cannot cross, so they must be read by a receptor that spans the membrane.

## One switch first: the G-protein cycle

Epinephrine's receptor here is the **beta-adrenergic receptor**, a **GPCR** (G-protein-coupled receptor): a seven-transmembrane protein. The ligand (the signal molecule, here epinephrine) binds on the outside and never passes through. Binding changes the receptor's shape on the inside, and that activates a G protein.

The **heterotrimeric G protein** has three subunits: alpha, beta, gamma. At rest, alpha holds GDP and the trimer is intact. The activated receptor pries GDP off alpha and lets GTP take its place. That makes the receptor alpha's **GEF** (guanine-nucleotide exchange factor): it pushes the switch ON. Alpha-GTP moves away from, or rearranges relative to, beta-gamma and carries the signal to its target enzyme (for Gs, adenylate cyclase). Alpha is itself a **GTPase**, an enzyme that slowly cuts its own GTP back to GDP: a built-in timer. Once it has, alpha rejoins beta-gamma and goes quiet. That pulls the switch OFF.

With an extracellular ligand bound, GTP-bound alpha is drawn apart from beta-gamma with an arrow toward adenylate cyclase. There are three logics, one per type of alpha. **Gs** stimulates adenylate cyclase and raises cAMP. **Gi** inhibits it and lowers cAMP. **Gq** activates phospholipase C and launches the calcium branch. So the same epinephrine can speed your heart, mobilize glucose and constrict a vessel, because the response is written into the *receptor, its G protein and the machinery of that cell*, not into the ligand.

## The cAMP relay: a chain of switches

Earl Sutherland found the next switch through a contradiction. Adrenaline needed the membrane fraction of broken liver cells to work, yet the factor that switched on phosphorylase appeared in the soluble fraction. The membrane, it turned out, converts the hormone's arrival into a small, water-soluble messenger that carries the command inward: **cyclic AMP**, the first second messenger ever identified.

Adenylate cyclase makes cAMP from ATP by clipping off two phosphates and cyclizing the rest. Think of cAMP as used ATP with a circular handle. The cyclic phosphate is the message; cAMP is not the same molecule as AMP.

The whole relay, epinephrine to glucose, is a row of switches. Each one has its own ON arm and OFF arm:

| Switch | Pushed ON by | Pulled OFF by | Acts on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Receptor | epinephrine binding | epinephrine leaving; desensitization and internalization under sustained stimulation | Gs (as its GEF) |
| Gs-alpha | the receptor swapping GDP for GTP | its own GTPase | adenylate cyclase |
| cAMP | adenylate cyclase, from ATP | **phosphodiesterase (PDE)**, to AMP | PKA's regulatory subunits |
| PKA | cAMP freeing its catalytic subunits | regulatory subunits clamping it again as cAMP falls | phosphorylase kinase, glycogen synthase |
| Phosphorylase kinase, phosphorylase | phosphorylation | **protein phosphatases** | glycogen, cut into glucose 1-phosphate |

PKA sits as an **R2C2 tetramer**: two regulatory subunits clamping two catalytic subunits silent. cAMP binds the regulatory subunits, they let go, and the catalytic subunits come free and active.

Free PKA then uses the cell's universal toggle: reversible phosphorylation. Kinases add a phosphate using ATP; phosphatases take it off. The phosphate brings two negative charges and some bulk, which can change the protein's shape or create a docking site for a partner, activating or inhibiting depending on where it lands. PKA uses both signs at once. It switches on breakdown (phosphorylase kinase, then phosphorylase) and switches off synthesis (glycogen synthase), so stored glucose is released and not rebuilt. Glucagon uses the same Gs → cAMP → PKA route in liver. The film also names lipolysis, the breakdown of stored fat, as a cAMP output; this lesson follows the glycogen branch.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Every switch in a pathway is pushed ON by one reaction and pulled OFF by another, and how much of it is ON at any moment is set by the contest between the two. An active enzyme's gain is how fast it works times how long it stays ON, and its OFF reaction sets that lifetime (binding steps just pass the signal on at a fixed ratio). So the pathway's output is the product of its gains; once the signal is removed, the response dies away at about the pace of its slowest OFF step (the faster ones add a little); and a switch sticks ON when it loses its OFF reaction or stops needing its ON signal.

The rule covers membrane-receptor pathways: GPCRs, receptors that recruit a kinase, and receptors that are kinases. It does not cover nuclear receptors or ligand-gated channels. The film gives it in four beats: a switch turns on, the signal amplifies, the switch turns off, a stuck switch is disease.

## How big: speed times lifetime, multiplied down the chain

An active enzyme does not make one product and stop; it keeps working until its own OFF reaction catches it. So the gain of an enzyme step is two numbers multiplied:

> **gain = how fast the active form works × how long it stays active**

Take the cyclase. While Gs-alpha holds GTP, the cyclase it touches keeps turning ATP into cAMP. When alpha's GTPase hydrolyzes that GTP, the cyclase stops. In round numbers, a cyclase making about 10 cAMP a second, switched on by a Gs-alpha that stays ON for about 10 seconds, makes about 100 cAMP. Slow the GTPase and the same cyclase makes more. The OFF arm sets the gain.

Now run the whole relay, in round teaching values; real gains differ between cell types.

| Step | What one active molecule does | Gain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Receptor → Gs | activates G proteins one after another, until the hormone leaves | ×10 |
| Gs → cyclase | one Gs-alpha switches on one cyclase | ×1 |
| Cyclase → cAMP | makes cAMP until its Gs-alpha switches off | ×100 |
| cAMP → PKA | four cAMP free the two catalytic subunits of one PKA | ×½ |
| PKA → phosphorylase kinase | one catalytic subunit phosphorylates many | ×10 |
| Phosphorylase kinase → phosphorylase | one kinase phosphorylates many | ×10 |
| Phosphorylase → glucose 1-phosphate | clips glucose units off glycogen, one after another | ×1,000 |

Multiply down the column: 10 × 1 × 100 × ½ × 10 × 10 × 1,000 = 50,000,000. One hormone molecule, tens of millions of glucose units. One row does not amplify (×1) and one loses half (×½). Both are binding steps, not enzymes: one Gs-alpha binds one cyclase, and four cAMP free two catalytic subunits. (The ×½ is a teaching conversion; in the cell, PKA follows the cAMP level, which PDE keeps trimming.) The total is still enormous, because the other steps multiply.

The product rule has a limit. It holds while each step has plenty of the next enzyme waiting to be switched on. Push in a big enough signal and the downstream enzyme is all ON already, so the output stops rising. The ceiling is set by whichever step runs out of enzyme first (Going deeper, "When the product rule stops working").

## How long: the slowest OFF step

A signal that cannot be turned off is not a signal; it is a stuck cell. Every switch in the relay table has its own OFF arm. Weaken it and that switch's steady level rises, just as if you had strengthened the ON arm, though it now also takes longer to switch off.

**Predict.** At high concentrations, caffeine blocks phosphodiesterase. Add it while the hormone is present. What happens to cAMP, and will it last much longer after the hormone leaves?

cAMP rises, as the OFF-arm rule predicts. In a simple model of this relay, halving PDE activity doubles the cAMP level. But cAMP lasts only a little longer after the hormone leaves, because cyclase keeps making cAMP until Gs-alpha switches off, and in that model the G protein is the slowest OFF step on the way to cAMP. So blocking PDE mostly makes cAMP bigger, not longer. The glycogen response can last longer still, because phosphatases must also undo the phosphorylations (Going deeper, "Size and duration in a model relay"; at coffee doses caffeine acts mainly elsewhere, see "cAMP and caffeine outside this relay").

OFF arms are also placed in space. **AKAP scaffold proteins** tether PKA next to the specific targets it should phosphorylate, and they also recruit phosphatases, building the OFF switch right beside the ON switch. The scaffold is a surgical tray with every instrument pre-arranged.

## Broken switches: four ways to break the contest

If each switch's level is a contest, disease can come from either side of it. Cholera, some forms of Cushing's syndrome and many cancers involve, at least in part, a switch that will not turn off, but not every break leaves a switch stuck ON:

| What broke | Example | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lost its OFF arm | cholera (Gs GTPase); oncogenic Ras (GAP-assisted GTPase); PTEN loss | the switch can turn ON but not OFF |
| Freed from its ON signal | Cushing's (PKA catalytic subunit cannot bind its regulatory subunit); Bcr-Abl (an always-ON fusion kinase) | the switch is ON with no signal at all |
| A brake cut | pertussis (Gi cannot be switched ON) | the brake's target runs high |
| A weakened ON arm | insulin resistance | too little response, not too much |

In **cholera**, choleragen chemically modifies Gs-alpha by ADP-ribosylation (attaching an ADP-ribose group) so it can no longer hydrolyze its GTP. Gs is locked ON, cAMP never stops, and chloride and water pour into the intestine. Severe dehydration can kill within hours.

In some cases of **Cushing's syndrome**, a mutation leaves PKA's catalytic subunit unable to bind its regulatory subunit. PKA escapes its clamp, and cortisol production runs without a hormone telling it to. Nothing is wrong with PDE or the phosphatases here; the switch simply no longer needs cAMP. The clinical picture (muscle weakness, thin and easily bruised skin, osteoporosis) follows downstream.

## Same template, new parts

The rest of the chapter is the same rule with new parts: for each pathway, find the switch, its ON arm and its OFF arm. Learn the names in bold; the rest you only need to recognize.

### The Gq branch: one cut, two messengers

When Gq fires, it activates **phospholipase C (PLC)**, which cleaves the membrane lipid **PIP2** into two messengers at once. **IP3** is soluble; it floats to the endoplasmic reticulum and opens an IP3-gated calcium channel, releasing a flood of Ca²⁺. **DAG** stays in the membrane, and together with that Ca²⁺ it activates **protein kinase C (PKC)**. Conventional PKC needs *both* signals, which makes it a coincidence detector: it fires best when the two signals arrive together.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/signal-transduction/plc-one-cut-two-places.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: a cut-away of a cell with phospholipase C on the inner face of the plasma membrane. One arrow shows DAG staying in the membrane; another shows IP3 travelling through the cytosol to a channel in the endoplasmic reticulum, which releases calcium that, with DAG, reaches protein kinase C." caption="Phospholipase C makes a single cut in PIP2. DAG stays in the membrane; soluble IP3 floats to the endoplasmic reticulum and opens a calcium channel. Together, DAG and Ca²⁺ activate protein kinase C." />

Calcium is a ubiquitous second messenger, and its common reader is **calmodulin**, which carries four Ca²⁺-binding EF-hand motifs, each a helix-loop-helix fold. Ca²⁺-loaded calmodulin activates targets including CaM kinase and the plasma-membrane Ca²⁺ ATPase (PMCA). The OFF arms are pumps: the PMCA pushes calcium out of the cell, and the SERCA pump returns it to the endoplasmic reticulum. DAG has its own OFF arm, DAG kinase. Calcium also ties straight back to the glycogen relay: in muscle, calmodulin is built into phosphorylase kinase itself.

### Receptors that recruit a kinase: JAK-STAT

Not every receptor talks to a G protein. Growth hormone binds both chains of a receptor pair that is already assembled, with a **JAK2** kinase attached to each tail. Binding moves the two JAK2s into position, and they phosphorylate each other (cross-phosphorylation), then the receptor tails. **STAT5** docks there and is phosphorylated, and STAT5 dimers enter the nucleus and change gene expression. Too much growth hormone, usually from a pituitary tumor, overdrives this pathway and causes gigantism in children or acromegaly in adults.

### Receptors that are kinases: EGFR and Ras

**Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs)** *are* kinases. EGFR dimerizes on ligand binding, the two halves cross-phosphorylate, and the phospho-tyrosines become docking sites for the adaptor Grb2, which brings in SOS. SOS is **Ras**'s GEF: it pushes Ras ON. Ras is a monomeric small GTPase, a tiny version of G-alpha: GDP-off, GTP-on. Its OFF arm is its own GTPase, sped up by **GAPs** (GTPase-activating proteins). Active Ras drives the Raf-MEK-ERK cascade toward proliferation.

**Predict.** An oncogenic Ras mutation blocks the GAP-assisted GTPase but leaves SOS free to load GTP. Which switch earlier in this lesson does this mutant Ras resemble?

About one in five people with cancer (roughly 19% in an incidence-weighted estimate) has a tumor with a Ras mutation of this kind. It is the lost-OFF-arm story again, one tier down.

### Insulin: the lipid-kinase deep dive

Insulin's receptor is also an RTK, but it routes mainly through a lipid switch, in five steps:

1. **Receptor.** Unlike EGFR, the insulin receptor is a **pre-formed dimer**. Insulin binding triggers a quaternary rearrangement, and the halves cross-phosphorylate.
2. **IRS.** The receptor phosphorylates adaptor proteins called **IRS**, which recruit **PI3K**. Both EGFR and the insulin receptor can activate Ras and PI3K; the IRS adaptors steer insulin strongly toward PI3K.
3. **PIP3.** PI3K is a *lipid* kinase: it phosphorylates PIP2 to make **PIP3**, a membrane second messenger. Its OFF arm is the lipid phosphatase **PTEN**, which converts PIP3 back to PIP2.
4. **Akt.** PIP3 recruits the kinase **Akt** to the membrane, along with PDK1, which phosphorylates it; a second kinase, mTORC2, completes its activation. Protein phosphatases strip the activating phosphates.
5. **Output.** Akt drives the two outcomes that define the fed state: **GLUT4 translocation** to the membrane for glucose uptake in muscle and fat, and glycogen synthesis in muscle and liver.

A second inset shows a GLUT4-containing vesicle fusing with the plasma membrane in muscle or fat, placing GLUT4 in the membrane for glucose uptake." caption="Insulin activates its pre-formed receptor dimer, triggering cross-phosphorylation and IRS phosphorylation. Akt promotes GLUT4 translocation and glucose uptake in muscle and fat, and glycogen synthesis in muscle and liver." />

Hold the two PIP2 stories side by side. In the Gq branch, PLC **cleaves** PIP2 into IP3 and DAG. In insulin signaling, PI3K **phosphorylates** PIP2 into PIP3. Same substrate, different chemistry, different messengers.

Both sides of this contest cause disease. PTEN is a tumor suppressor: lose it, and PIP3 builds up and Akt stays active, which helps drive many cancers. On the ON side, a block at any node along insulin, receptor, IRS, PI3K, PIP3, Akt, GLUT4 reduces glucose uptake in muscle and fat. Weakened signaling along this chain (insulin resistance) is one half of type 2 diabetes; the other half is beta cells that can no longer make enough insulin to overcome it.

## Drugs on the wiring

Much of cancer is signaling gone wrong. **Proto-oncogenes** promote growth and become **oncogenes** when stuck ON; **tumor-suppressor genes** brake growth or guard the genome, and help cause cancer when lost.

Two drug strategies attack the wiring from opposite sides. **Gleevec (imatinib)** treats chronic myelogenous leukemia, where the Philadelphia chromosome fuses two genes into a constitutively active Bcr-Abl tyrosine kinase. Gleevec wedges into the kinase's ATP-binding pocket and shuts it off, turning a leukemia that usually killed within a few years into a manageable chronic condition. It acts from the inside, on the stuck switch itself. **Cetuximab** is an antibody that blocks EGF from binding EGFR, preventing dimerization from the outside. If Gleevec is like putting gum in a lock, cetuximab is like covering the doorbell so no one can ring it. But cetuximab only helps if the cancer is actually EGFR-driven.

**Predict.** A tumor carries a KRAS mutation that locks Ras ON. Will cetuximab, which blocks EGFR above Ras, help much?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Usually not. The KRAS mutation leaves the pathway stuck ON below the receptor. Weakening an ON arm upstream does little to a switch that has lost its OFF arm, because loaded Ras is not switched back off (mutant KRAS is largely GTP-loaded without help from EGFR). That is why precision medicine requires knowing the tumor's genetics, and why single-pathway drugs so often fail: pathways cross-talk, and the cell reroutes around a block.

</details>

Cells compute with molecular switches. Broken switches cause disease. Knowing the wiring is what makes a therapy targetable. That is signal transduction.

## Going deeper

### When the product rule stops working

In a simple model of the relay, the active G protein matches the linear prediction at small inputs, but at an input ten times the level that half-saturates it, it reaches about 0.9 of the total where the linear rule predicts ten times the total.

Saturation can also make a single switch sharp. When the kinase and phosphatase of one switch are both working near their maximum rate, the contest still decides the outcome, but abruptly: in one model where each enzyme's Km is about 1% of the substrate it acts on, an ON/OFF rate ratio of 0.9 leaves the switch 8% ON and a ratio of 1.1 leaves it 91% ON, where unsaturated enzymes would give 47% and 52%. This is zero-order ultrasensitivity. It does not break the rule; ON still wins when it outpaces OFF.

### Size and duration in a model relay

In a three-switch model (G-alpha, cAMP, a phosphorylated target) where the GTPase gives active G-alpha a half-life of about 7 seconds and PDE gives cAMP one of about 1.4 seconds, halving PDE lengthened cAMP's half-time after the hormone left only from about 9 to about 11 seconds. The faster PDE step still added a little: about 9 seconds, not 7. Slowing only the phosphatase (here giving the phosphate a half-life of about 80 seconds) stretched the target's half-time to about 90 seconds: the response can outlast its second messenger. With the GTPase blocked, as in cholera, cAMP stayed up after the hormone left, and even with no hormone, active G protein slowly built up from spontaneous GDP-to-GTP exchange.

### Where the glucose goes

The opening figure multiplies round gains and counts glucose 1-phosphate. In muscle, that glucose 1-phosphate is burned in the cell, not released as blood glucose. In human liver, adrenaline acts partly through alpha-1 receptors (Gq and Ca²⁺) as well as beta-2 receptors.

### The GPCR family

GPCRs are the largest receptor family in humans (roughly 800 of them) and the target of about 35% of approved drugs. Once alpha-GTP leaves or rearranges, beta-gamma can signal to its own targets too. Light, which the film lists among first messengers, reads through one of them: in vision, light switches on rhodopsin, a GPCR, directly.

### cAMP and caffeine outside this relay

In the glycogen cascade, cAMP's job is to free PKA; in other settings it also acts on Epac and some ion channels. Caffeine's PDE block needs high concentrations; at the doses in a cup of coffee its main action is blocking adenosine receptors.

### Kinases and phosphatases

The human genome encodes more kinases than phosphatases (roughly 500 versus roughly 200). That does not mean OFF control matters less. Phosphatase catalytic subunits such as PP1 and PP2A get their specificity from many regulatory subunits, so fewer catalytic genes still serve many targets.

### GLP-1 drugs

GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy), and tirzepatide (Mounjaro), which also activates the GIP receptor, act on a Gs-coupled GPCR. GLP-1 is released from gut L-cells after a meal; its receptor shares the seven-transmembrane core of the beta-adrenergic receptor but belongs to a different family (class B), with a large outer domain that grips the peptide. Its safety feature is built into the wiring: GLP-1 boosts insulin release when blood glucose is high, and the boost fades as glucose falls. That glucose-dependence means these drugs rarely cause hypoglycemia, unlike the older sulfonylureas, while still acting on pancreas, brain and stomach.

### Potency and efficacy

Every agonist, GLP-1 agonists included, is described in a given assay by two numbers. **Potency** is how much drug it takes to get half the maximal response (the EC₅₀); **efficacy** is how large that maximal response can get (Emax). ### The twist and the name

Growth hormone has two receptor-binding sites on one molecule. Because the two sites are not alike, binding twists the receptor chains relative to each other, and that twist is what brings the two JAK2s into position. JAK is named for Janus, the two-faced Roman god, because it carries two kinase-like domains though only one is a fully active kinase.

### EF hands

The calmodulin EF-hand motif is named for helices E and F of parvalbumin, where it was first seen. Your own hand is the mnemonic for the fold: stretch out your index finger and thumb as the two helices and curl your middle finger as the calcium-binding loop between them.
