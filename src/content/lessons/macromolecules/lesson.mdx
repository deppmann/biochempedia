## Explain: small blocks, big chains

<WordBank terms={['monomer', 'polymer', 'macromolecule', 'dehydration synthesis', 'hydrolysis', 'carbohydrate', 'protein', 'amino acid', 'nucleic acid', 'nucleotide', 'lipid']} />

A **monomer** is a small molecule that can join to others like it. A **polymer** is a large molecule of many monomers linked in a chain; each link is a chemical bond between two blocks. A **macromolecule** is a very large molecule in a living thing, such as a polymer. Starch, proteins and nucleic acids are polymers; lipids are not, as step 3 showed.

The first film counted the waters; here are the atoms. In the figure below, one block has a hydrogen (H) on its end and the next an oxygen and a hydrogen (OH). When the link forms, those three atoms leave the chain: one water molecule, H2O. Joining this way is **dehydration synthesis**, "making something by removing water." It takes energy, which the cell supplies. Run backward, **hydrolysis** ("splitting with water") puts a water molecule back: the H goes to one block and the OH to the other, and the link breaks.

> C6H12O6 + C6H12O6 → C12H22O11 + H2O (dehydration synthesis: two glucose molecules form maltose, a double sugar, and water; the small numbers count atoms)

> maltose + water → glucose + glucose (hydrolysis)

## Explain: four classes, four kinds of job

| Class | Building blocks | Main jobs | Elements |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrates | simple sugars | quick energy; short-term energy storage (starch, glycogen); plant cell walls | C, H, O |
| Proteins | amino acids | building tissues; running cell processes | C, H, O, N (most also S) |
| Nucleic acids | nucleotides | storing and passing on genetic information | C, H, O, N, P |
| Lipids | fats: fatty acids and glycerol; other lipids differ | fats: long-term energy storage and insulation; phospholipids and cholesterol: cell membranes | mostly C, H, O |

- A **carbohydrate** is a molecule made of simple sugars, such as glucose, starch or cellulose.
- A **protein** is a chain of amino acids folded into a shape. An **amino acid** is a small molecule containing nitrogen that is the building block of proteins; most proteins also contain some sulfur.
- A **nucleic acid** is a chain of nucleotides, such as DNA or RNA. A **nucleotide** is a building block made of a sugar, a phosphate group and a base containing nitrogen.
- A **lipid** is a molecule that mostly does not mix with water, such as a fat or oil. A **phospholipid** is a lipid with one end that does attract water.

Not every lipid is a fat: phospholipids and steroids such as cholesterol are lipids too.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Carbohydrates, proteins, nucleic acids and lipids each have their own building blocks and their own main jobs.</Support>

## Explain: shape decides the job

Beyond the second film’s starch, cellulose, enzyme and membrane:

- **Nucleic acid: DNA.** DNA has two strands whose bases pair in a fixed way, A with T and G with C, so each strand can guide the copying of the other, passing the information to new cells. [DNA: Structure and Copying](/lessons/dna-structure-and-copying/) shows how.
- **Carbohydrate: cellulose.** Much of the fiber in whole-wheat bread is cellulose and other cell-wall material; bacteria in your gut can break down some of it.
- **Protein: an enzyme.** An enzyme is usually a protein that speeds up a reaction. Its pocket works somewhat like a lock that opens only for a key of the right shape, although the analogy breaks because molecules bend and move.

**Predict.** If heat or acid changes the shape of an enzyme’s pocket, what happens to the enzyme’s work?

Together these molecules run life processes: proteins and lipids build new cells for growth and repair, DNA passes information on in reproduction, a protein in red blood cells carries oxygen for gas exchange, enzymes run metabolism (the chemical reactions in cells), and membrane proteins detect signals so cells can respond.

### Design problem

Many school labs use three tests. Iodine solution turns blue-black with starch. Biuret solution turns violet with protein. A drop of fat leaves a see-through spot on brown paper that stays after the paper dries. Plan an investigation to find which of these molecules are in bread, turkey and apple.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The independent variable is the food. The dependent variable is the color change or spot. Keep the amount of food, the amount of test solution and the waiting time the same. For each test, use water as a negative control (it should not react) and a known sample as a positive control (it should): starch for iodine, a known protein for biuret, oil for the spot test. Run each test three times. Based on the table, you might expect starch in the bread and protein in the turkey and bread. The apple has very little protein or fat, and a ripe apple may show little starch. Treat these as predictions to test. Wear goggles, never taste lab samples, and handle iodine and biuret solution with care.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The real steps in a cell

Joining takes energy. In cells, each kind of molecule is built by its own energy-using route, so the real steps usually release other products, not water. The one-water-per-link count describes atoms, not the cell’s steps. Digestion is different: hydrolysis does add one water for each link it breaks.

### Where plants get their nitrogen

Plants also build amino acids from sugar-derived carbon, but they take their nitrogen from inorganic nitrate or ammonium in the soil. In legumes such as peas, bacteria in root nodules also supply nitrogen.
