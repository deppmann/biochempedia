## Explain: small blocks, big chains

<WordBank terms={['monomer', 'polymer', 'macromolecule', 'dehydration synthesis', 'hydrolysis', 'carbohydrate', 'protein', 'amino acid', 'nucleic acid', 'nucleotide', 'lipid']} />

Think of a necklace of beads. Each bead is small, but a string of hundreds of them is long. Cells build their largest molecules the same way.

A **monomer** is a small molecule that can join to others like it. A **polymer** is a large molecule made of many monomers linked in a chain. A **macromolecule** is a very large molecule in a living thing, such as a polymer. The necklace analogy breaks in one place: real chains fold, coil and branch, and their shape matters for their job.

When a cell links two monomers, it removes a hydrogen from one and an oxygen and hydrogen from the other. Those atoms leave as one water molecule. This joining reaction is **dehydration synthesis**, which means "making something by removing water." That is the overall result. Joining needs energy, so in cells enzymes first attach each building block to an energy-carrying molecule, and the actual steps can release other small molecules instead of water. Breaking the link runs the other way. **Hydrolysis** splits a polymer into smaller pieces by adding water.

> simple sugar + simple sugar → double sugar + water (dehydration synthesis)

> C6H12O6 + C6H12O6 → C12H22O11 + H2O (two glucose molecules form maltose and water)

> double sugar + water → simple sugar + simple sugar (hydrolysis)

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/macromolecules/join-and-split-polymer.webp" width={1200} height={800} keyItems={["monomers", "water molecule", "link", "water added"]} given={[0, 2]} alt="AI-generated illustration: two separate blocks on the left and two blocks joined by a short bar on the right, with an arrow each way between them; a small bent block leaves above the top arrow and another arrives below the bottom arrow. Four numbered circles mark parts to name." caption="Joining two monomers releases water, and splitting the link takes water in. The same four parts appear in both directions." />

Digestion is mostly hydrolysis. Your gut breaks the bread’s starch into sugars and the turkey’s proteins into amino acids. Your cells then use dehydration synthesis to build their own polymers from those pieces. A turkey protein does not become a human protein; its amino acids are reused in new chains.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/macromolecules/sandwich-to-body.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: a turkey sandwich on whole-wheat bread beside a starch chain and a protein chain; the chains are cut into single beads in the gut, and the cell then joins the amino-acid beads into a new chain." caption="Digestion splits the bread's starch and the turkey's proteins into pieces by adding water; your cells then join those pieces into their own polymers." />

Most of these molecules share carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Cells can take the carbon, hydrogen and oxygen from sugar and join them with nitrogen to make amino acids. Plants make their amino acids in a similar way, using nitrogen they take up from the soil as nitrate or ammonium; in legumes such as peas, bacteria in root nodules also supply nitrogen. Your cells can make some amino acids, but about nine must come from food.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Cells use energy to build large polymers by joining small monomers, which removes water overall, and break them apart again by adding water.</Support>

## Explain: four classes, four kinds of job

Scientists sort the large molecules of life into four classes. Each has its own building blocks and main jobs.

A **carbohydrate** is a molecule made of simple sugars, such as glucose, starch or cellulose. A **protein** is a chain of amino acids folded into a shape. An **amino acid** is a small molecule containing nitrogen that is the building block of proteins. A **nucleic acid** is a chain of nucleotides, such as DNA or RNA. A **nucleotide** is a building block made of a sugar, a phosphate group and a base containing nitrogen. A **lipid** is a molecule that mostly does not mix with water, such as a fat, oil or phospholipid, which has one end that attracts water.

| Class | Building blocks | Main jobs | Elements |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrates | simple sugars | quick energy; short-term energy storage (starch, glycogen); plant cell walls | C, H, O |
| Proteins | amino acids | building tissues; running cell processes | C, H, O, N (often S) |
| Nucleic acids | nucleotides | storing and passing on genetic information | C, H, O, N, P |
| Lipids | fats: fatty acids and glycerol; other lipids differ | fats: long-term energy storage and insulation; phospholipids and cholesterol: cell membranes | mostly C, H, O |

Two cautions. Not every lipid is a fat: phospholipids and steroids such as cholesterol are lipids too. And fats are not true polymers. A common fat is one glycerol joined to three fatty acids, not a long chain of repeating blocks.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Carbohydrates, proteins, nucleic acids and lipids each have their own building blocks and their own main jobs.</Support>

## Explain: shape decides the job

A key only opens a lock its shape fits. Molecules work in a similar way, although the analogy breaks because molecules bend and move. Here is one example from each class.

- **Carbohydrate: cellulose.** Long chains of glucose lie side by side and hold together in strong fibers. Those fibers stiffen the walls of plant cells. Much of the fiber in whole-wheat bread comes from plant cell walls.
- **Protein: an enzyme.** An enzyme is usually a protein that speeds up a reaction. Its folded shape forms a pocket where one kind of molecule fits. If heat or acid changes that shape, the molecule may no longer fit. You will test this in [Enzymes at Work](/lessons/enzymes-at-work/).
- **Nucleic acid: DNA.** DNA has two strands whose bases pair in a fixed way. Each strand can therefore guide the copying of the other, so the information can be passed to new cells. [DNA: Structure and Copying](/lessons/dna-structure-and-copying/) shows how.
- **Lipid: a phospholipid.** Each phospholipid has a head attracted to water and two tails that avoid it. In water they form a double layer, heads out and tails in. That layer is the basic structure of the cell membrane.

Together these molecules carry out life processes. Proteins and lipids build new cells for growth and repair. DNA passes information on in reproduction. A protein in red blood cells carries oxygen for gas exchange. Enzymes run metabolism (the chemical reactions in cells), and proteins in membranes detect signals so cells can respond.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Each large molecule’s shape, from cellulose fibers to the membrane’s double layer, determines the job it can do.</Support>

### Design problem

Your teacher gives you three tests used in many school labs. Iodine solution turns blue-black with starch. Biuret solution turns violet with protein. A drop of fat leaves a see-through spot on brown paper that stays after the paper dries. Plan an investigation to find which of these molecules are in bread, turkey and apple.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The independent variable is the food. The dependent variable is the color change or spot. Keep the amount of food, the amount of test solution and the waiting time the same. Use water as a negative control, and a known starch, protein or oil sample as a positive control for each test. Run each test three times. Based on the table, you might expect starch in the bread and protein in the turkey and bread. The apple has very little protein or fat, and a ripe apple may show little starch. Treat these as predictions to test. Wear goggles, never taste lab samples, and handle iodine and biuret solution with care.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The sandwich holds carbohydrates, proteins and fats, and, because its ingredients came from cells, nucleic acids too. Your digestive system splits those large molecules into small pieces, such as sugars, amino acids, fatty acids and the parts of nucleotides. Your cells then use those pieces, along with building blocks they make themselves, to build new large molecules with shapes that fit your own body’s jobs.

**Science practices you used:** making and testing predictions with a model, naming where an analogy breaks, reading a data table on a fair 100-gram basis, calculating with energy factors, and planning a test with controls and repeated trials.
