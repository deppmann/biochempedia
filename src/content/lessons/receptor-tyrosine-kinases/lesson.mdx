    story: >-
      The expected picture of the EGF receptor was that two paired
      receptors switch each other on by adding phosphate to each other's activation loops, as
      most kinases of this kind do. John Kuriyan's crystallography laboratory at the University
      of California, Berkeley, had reasons to doubt it. The receptor's isolated kinase had
      already been crystallized by others, and those crystals had caught it in its active shape
      although its loop carried no phosphate. Kuriyan's group took that as a sign that the answer
      lay in how the molecules packed against one another. Their structures, published in Cell in
      June 2006 with Philip Cole of Johns Hopkins, showed a lopsided pair: the large lobe of one
      kinase pressed against the small lobe of its neighbour, much as a cyclin holds a
      cell-division kinase in its working shape. Left alone, the kinase folded into a self-blocked
      form like that of Src. The model explained how the four receptors of the EGF family can
      switch one another on in mixed pairs, even pairs with HER3, whose own kinase is nearly
      inactive.

    story: >-
      By the early 1990s the insulin receptor was known to be a tyrosine kinase that must
      phosphorylate three of its own tyrosines to switch on fully, but no one had seen how those
      phosphates switch the enzyme on. Stevan Hubbard, working in Wayne Hendrickson's laboratory
      at Columbia University, crystallized the receptor's kinase without its phosphates and
      solved the structure, which appeared in Nature in 1994. The surprise was the activation
      loop. It lay folded into the active site, and one of its own tyrosines, Tyr1162, sat in the
      spot where a substrate tyrosine belongs. The resting kinase was plugged by a piece of
      itself. Three years later, at New York University, Hubbard solved the same kinase carrying
      its three phosphates, with a peptide substrate and an ATP analogue bound. The loop had
      swung clear and the two lobes had closed around the nucleotide. The two structures, off and
      on, became the reference picture for how a receptor tyrosine kinase is held in check and
      released, and later kinase structures were read against them.

techniques:
  - name: "X-ray crystallography of receptor fragments"
    blurb: >-
      A soluble piece of the receptor, such as the ectodomain or the kinase domain, is
      crystallized, and its atomic structure is worked out from the way the crystal scatters
      X-rays. For these receptors the outside and the inside were solved as separate pieces. How
      the molecules pack against each other in the crystal gave the clue to the EGF receptor's
      lopsided kinase pair.
  - name: "Tyrosine-to-phenylalanine mutagenesis"
    blurb: >-
      Phenylalanine is tyrosine without its hydroxyl group, so it cannot accept a phosphate.
      Changing one tyrosine at a time, then asking which protein no longer docks or which
      response is lost, assigns a job to each phosphorylation site.
  - name: "Kinase assay at controlled density"
    blurb: >-
      A purified kinase domain is mixed with ATP and a peptide substrate, and the rate of
      phosphate transfer is measured. Comparing the kinase free in solution with the same kinase
      gathered on the surface of lipid vesicles tests whether closeness alone switches it on.
  - name: "Southern blotting for gene copy number"
    blurb: >-
      Tumour DNA is cut with a restriction enzyme, separated by size, and probed with a labelled
      piece of the gene of interest. The strength of the band, compared with a control gene
      present at the normal number, reports how many copies the tumour carries. The 1987 study of
      HER2 in breast tumours measured amplification this way.

      - "A pair of EGF molecules that bind to each other"
      - "A disulfide bond that links the two membrane helices"

    rationale: >-
      EGF holds each ectodomain in an extended shape that exposes a loop, the dimerization arm,
      and the two arms clasp. A single EGF bridging both receptors is the style of pairing used

      cannot copy. A pair of EGF molecules binding each other is ruled out by the structure,
      where the two EGFs sit on the outer faces and do not touch. A disulfide bond between

      not the helices.
  - stem: "In most receptor tyrosine kinases, what switches on each kinase once two receptors are held together?"
    choices:
      - "Phosphorylation of its activation loop by protein kinase A"
      - "Phosphorylation of its activation loop by the partner"
      - "Binding of an SH2 protein to a phosphotyrosine on its tail"
      - "Hydrolysis of the GTP held between its two kinase lobes"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      In most of these receptors the partner kinase phosphorylates the activation loop, which
      moves the loop out of the active site. Protein kinase A is a serine and threonine kinase of
      the cAMP pathway and plays no part here. An SH2 protein docks on the tail after the kinase
      is already on, so docking is a result of activation and not its cause. GTP hydrolysis is
      the timer of G proteins such as Ras; a kinase holds ATP between its lobes.
  - stem: "The tail of HER3 carries six tyrosines that are each followed, three residues later, by a methionine. When HER3 is the partner in an activated pair and these tyrosines are phosphorylated, which branch is most directly strengthened?"
    choices:
      - "The branch through Gs to cyclic AMP and PKA"
      - "The branch through PI 3-kinase and on to Akt"
      - "The branch through Grb2 and Ras to MAP kinase"
      - "The branch through PLC-gamma to IP3 and DAG"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A phosphotyrosine followed three residues later by methionine is the address read by the
      SH2 domains of PI 3-kinase, so HER3 strengthens the branch through PI 3-kinase to Akt. The
      SH2 domain of Grb2 prefers an asparagine two residues after the phosphotyrosine, so the
      branch through Grb2 and Ras is not the one these sites feed. PLC-gamma docks at other
      sites, such as phosphotyrosine 992 of the EGF receptor. The branch through Gs and cyclic
      AMP starts at G-protein-coupled receptors and does not use phosphotyrosine docking.
  - stem: "In a thyroid tumour, a RET receptor has lost one cysteine from its ectodomain, which leaves the cysteine it normally bonds with unpaired. The receptor signals with no ligand present. What is the most likely cause?"
    choices:
      - "A tail that has lost the tyrosine where Cbl would dock"
      - "A kinase that no longer needs a phosphate on its loop"
      - "A gene that is present in many extra copies per cell"
      - "A disulfide bond that joins two receptors into a pair"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Cysteines in an ectodomain normally form disulfide bonds within one chain. An unpaired
      cysteine can bond with the unpaired cysteine of a second receptor, and a covalently joined
      pair holds two kinases together with no ligand. A kinase that needs no loop phosphate would
      require a change inside the kinase, and this change is outside the cell. Extra gene copies
      are a change in amount, which losing one cysteine does not produce. A lost Cbl docking
      tyrosine is a change in the tail, and it would slow the receptor's removal rather than pair
      two receptors with no ligand.

      catches a growth factor. Its inside is an enzyme, a kinase, that attaches phosphate groups

  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      Receptor tyrosine kinases are single-pass membrane proteins: a ligand-binding ectodomain,
      one transmembrane helix, and a cytoplasmic tyrosine kinase domain followed by a tail.
      Ligand binding produces or rearranges a receptor dimer by one of three routes: a dimeric
      ligand such as NGF or PDGF, a monomeric ligand such as EGF that exposes a dimerization arm
      on each receptor, or a change of shape within the disulfide-linked insulin receptor. In
      most of these receptors the paired kinases then phosphorylate each other's activation loops
      (autophosphorylation in trans), which releases the loop from the active site. The EGF
      receptor family instead forms an asymmetric dimer in which one kinase activates the other
      allosterically. Phosphotyrosines outside the kinase core recruit SH2-domain proteins such
      as Grb2, PLC-gamma and the regulatory subunit of PI 3-kinase, each chosen by the residues
      that follow the phosphotyrosine. Signalling ends through tyrosine phosphatases and through

      bypass the ligand by overexpression, by mutation of the kinase domain, or by loss or
      replacement of the ectodomain.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      In the unphosphorylated insulin receptor kinase, Tyr1162 of the activation loop occupies
      the active site, and the loop also obstructs the nucleotide site (Hubbard and colleagues,
      1994). Phosphorylation in trans of Tyr1158, Tyr1162 and Tyr1163 stabilizes an open loop and
      lobe closure (Hubbard, 1997). In an FGF receptor kinase the sites are filled in a kinetic
      order, an activation-loop tyrosine first. The EGF receptor departs from this scheme.
      Phosphorylation of its activation loop is dispensable. The inactive kinase adopts a
      conformation like that of inactive Src and the cyclin-dependent kinases, and activation
      requires an asymmetric dimer in which the C-lobe of an activator kinase packs against the
      N-lobe of a receiver, as a cyclin does on its kinase (Zhang and colleagues, 2006). The
      receiver's juxtamembrane segment then latches onto the activator's C-lobe, and this latch
      is needed for activation (Jura and colleagues; Red Brewer and colleagues, 2009).

      asymmetry explains why a pair of HER2 and HER3 signals although the HER3 kinase is nearly

**Fifty-eight of your 518 protein kinases sit in the cell surface, with a ligand-binding half outside and the kinase inside.** The first film showed how bringing two receptors together answers the single-helix problem; here we examine the family's scale, the experiments behind both activation switches, the docking code, and cancer therapies.

## One plan, built 58 times

A **receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)** is a membrane receptor whose inner half adds phosphate to tyrosine side chains (from the receptor field guide). Each combines an outer ligand-binding **ectodomain**, a **single transmembrane helix** anchoring and joining the two halves, and an inner two-lobed **kinase domain** (holding ATP in its cleft, from the phosphorylation lesson) followed by a flexible tyrosine-carrying **tail**.

Of the human genome's 518 protein kinases, about 90 are tyrosine kinases and 58 are RTKs, sorted into about 20 families including the receptors for EGF, insulin, NGF, PDGF, and VEGF. The kinase fold is recognizably conserved across all 58; ectodomains and tails vary most, and each family holds a lone receptor's kinase off, or close to it, in its own way.

## Three ways to bring two kinases together

Growth factors are often **dimeric** (two chains), while most RTKs are **monomeric** (one chain). Cells use at least three structural routes to put two resting kinases side by side:

- **Dimeric ligand:** NGF (two identical chains) bridges two copies of its receptor, TrkA; PDGF and VEGF work the same way.
- **Exposed dimerization arm:** EGF is a single small chain, and one EGF binds one receptor, so it cannot be the bridge. At rest, the EGF receptor's ectodomain is mostly folded with a loop—the **dimerization arm**—tucked away. EGF binds between two regions of the ectodomain and holds it extended with the arm out, so two arms clasp; in the crystal structure, the two EGFs sit on the outer faces, far from the contact, so whatever holds the pair together belongs to the receptors themselves.
- **Pre-paired receptor:** The insulin receptor is already two disulfide-linked halves (each an outer alpha chain plus a membrane-spanning beta chain carrying the kinase). Pairing cannot be its signal, because the pair is permanent; insulin instead shifts the pair's shape so that the two kinase-bearing legs, held apart at rest, come together.

## Two ways paired kinases release self-inhibition

Once two kinases sit side by side, they release self-inhibition by one of two mechanisms.

### Activation-loop phosphorylation in trans

Recall the **activation loop**, the mobile strand beside the active site. In the resting insulin receptor kinase, this loop folds into the active site with one of its own tyrosines sitting where a substrate tyrosine belongs—plugged by a piece of itself. Held together by insulin, each kinase phosphorylates its partner's loop (**autophosphorylation in trans**). With three phosphates on the loop, it swings clear, the two lobes close on ATP, and the kinase runs. Most RTKs use this scheme (see Going deeper for ordered phosphorylation in an FGF receptor).

### Asymmetric allosteric contact in the EGF receptor

The EGF receptor is the best-studied exception (RET is another): its activation loop needs no phosphate (see Going deeper for that history). Two results reported in 2006 by Xuewu Zhang in John Kuriyan's laboratory pointed to what throws the switch instead. First, changing one activation-loop residue—leucine to arginine, called **L858R** in the clinic and found in many lung cancers whose EGF receptor is always on—made the isolated kinase about 20 times more active without needing a phosphate on the loop.

**Predict.** Second, Zhang took the normal kinase alone—no ectodomain, no growth factor—and crowded it by gathering it on the surface of lipid vesicles. If contact between two kinases is the switch, what should crowding do, and should the loop need a phosphate?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It switched on, again without needing the loop phosphate: crowding supplied the contact that a partner would.

</details>

So the resting kinase holds itself off, and contact with a second copy releases the hold. Kuriyan's 2006 crystal structures showed that lopsided (**asymmetric**) contact: one kinase's large lobe presses its neighbour's small lobe into the working shape, much as a cyclin activates a cell-division kinase. Only the pressed copy switches on; the pressing copy only supplies a surface.

## How phosphotyrosines branch and end the signal

The second film showed how SH2 domains read phosphotyrosines and how Cbl and phosphatases turn signaling off; here are the numbered docking addresses, the mutation test showing that each site recruits its own partner, and the lysosome–proteasome distinction.

### Docking addresses and proof of independence

In the EGF receptor, most docking sites sit on the two tails; other RTKs also use the juxtamembrane segment or a kinase insert, and the insulin receptor puts many on separate **IRS proteins**. An **SH2 domain** (from the interaction-modules lesson) binds a phosphotyrosine and checks the following residues, so local sequence largely decides which target binds:

- **Phosphotyrosine 1068 (EGF receptor):** asparagine two residues later recruits **Grb2** → Ras and MAP kinase.
- **Phosphotyrosine 992 (EGF receptor):** recruits **PLC-gamma** → IP3 and DAG.
- **Six tyrosines on HER3:** each has methionine three residues later, recruiting **PI 3-kinase** → Akt.

Swapping partners (such as HER2–HER3) thus shifts the pathway mix; the next three lessons follow each branch.

### Turning the signal off

A phosphotyrosine also recruits **Cbl**, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that tags the receptor with ubiquitin. **Endocytosis** (pinching-in of membrane as a vesicle) brings it inside, where ubiquitin routes it to the **lysosome** (the protein-digesting compartment) rather than the proteasome used for many cytosolic proteins. Meanwhile, **protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs)** strip phosphates directly (see Going deeper for their genome count).

Recall the guiding rule's first half: bringing two receptor kinases together releases self-inhibition and activates the pair. RTKs recur across human tumours—including the EGF receptor family, c-KIT, VEGF receptors, c-MET, and RET.

Tumours use at least three mechanisms:

- **Gene amplification:** Dennis Slamon and colleagues (1987) found extra *HER2* copies in 30 percent of 189 breast tumours, predicting earlier relapse and shorter survival (most strongly with lymph-node spread). HER2's dimerization arm is exposed without any ligand, so crowding alone makes pairs.
- **Kinase mutation:** **L858R** weakens self-inhibition inside an otherwise normal EGF receptor so it signals without growth factor, though it still needs a partner.
- **Lost or replaced ectodomain:** In some thyroid cancers, *RET* breaks and fuses its kinase half to a self-pairing protein, leaving no ectodomain at all.

### Design problem

A tumour cell must keep an RTK switched on with no growth factor present. Design three different ways to do it, each one changing a different part of the receptor or the amount of it. For each, predict whether an antibody against the outside of the receptor would still work, and whether a small molecule in the ATP pocket would.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

1. **Change the amount.** Amplify the gene, so that the membrane is crowded with normal receptors and pairs form without ligand. The ectodomain is present and normal, so an antibody can bind it, as trastuzumab binds HER2. The kinase is normal too, so a small molecule in the ATP pocket should also work.
2. **Change the kinase.** Put in a mutation such as L858R that releases the kinase's hold on itself. An antibody can still bind outside, and the mutant kinase still needs a partner, so cetuximab, which holds the receptor folded and unable to pair, can still act: it shrank mouse lung tumours driven by L858R. In patients, though, adding it to the kinase inhibitor afatinib as a first treatment did not help. A small molecule in the ATP pocket acts on the faulty part directly, as gefitinib and erlotinib do.
3. **Change the outside.** Delete the ectodomain, or swap it for a piece of protein that pairs by itself, as in the RET fusions. The antibody has nothing to bind and fails. The small molecule works as long as the ATP pocket is unchanged.

The antibody needs the outside to be present and to matter. The small molecule needs only the pocket.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How the EGF receptor's switch was uncovered

In an FGF receptor followed step by step, the sites were filled in order: an activation-loop tyrosine was phosphorylated first, which raised the kinase's rate, and the docking tyrosines followed. In 1987 Yosef Yarden and Joseph Schlessinger presented evidence from purified EGF receptor that activation comes from receptor contact, and by 1989 evidence showed one receptor adds phosphate to another, making loop phosphorylation the expected switch. Experiments from 1992 on showed that loop phosphate is not needed, leaving the switch unexplained until Zhang, Kuriyan, and colleagues published the 2006 vesicle-crowding and asymmetric-dimer results.

### Phosphatases balanced against tyrosine kinases

The human genome encodes 107 protein tyrosine phosphatase (PTP) genes, of which 38 are classical enzymes that, with a few exceptions, act only on phosphotyrosine—set against about 90 protein tyrosine kinases (PTKs).

### Drug approvals and the 2004 gefitinib papers

Trastuzumab was approved in 1998, imatinib in 2001, gefitinib in 2003, and erlotinib and cetuximab in 2004. That year three papers—by Lynch, Paez, Pao, and their colleagues—reported that most lung cancers that shrank on gefitinib carried EGF receptor kinase mutations, L858R among them.
