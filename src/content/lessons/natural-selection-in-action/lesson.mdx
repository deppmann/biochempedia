      Michael Majerus studied moths near Cambridge, England. His last experiment tested whether

## Explain: how natural selection works

<WordBank terms={['population', 'environment', 'generation', 'trait frequency', 'natural selection', 'adaptation', 'artificial selection', 'evolution']} />

A **population** is a group of the same kind of living thing in one area. The peppered moths in one wood are a population. An **environment** is everything around a living thing, such as bark, air, food and predators. A **generation** is one step from parents to their offspring.

Members of a population are not all the same. Some moths are pale and some are dark, and the colour is inherited. New variations first appear by chance, as you saw in [that lesson](/lessons/mutations-and-variation/).

**Natural selection** is the process in which individuals with traits that suit their environment are more likely to survive and reproduce. Here is how it works in moths.

1. Moths in the population vary in colour.
2. Birds spot more of the moths that stand out from the bark.
3. Moths that are not eaten live to reproduce.
4. Their offspring inherit their colour.
5. Over generations, the colour that matched the bark becomes more common.

**Trait frequency** is how common a trait is in a population, often given as a percent. Natural selection shifts trait frequency. It does not change any one moth. As an environment changes, some organisms survive and reproduce. Others may move to a new place or die.

{/* DIAGRAM: natural selection in moths on sooty bark, numbered: 1 a mixed population of pale and dark moths, 2 a bird eats a pale moth that stands out, 3 survivors, mostly dark, have young, 4 the next generation has a higher percent of dark moths */}

You may hear "survival of the fittest." In biology, fit means well suited to the environment and able to leave offspring. It does not mean strongest. A dark moth on sooty bark is fit because birds miss it more often.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Individuals with traits that suit their environment survive and reproduce more often, so those traits become more common over generations.</Support>

## Explain: adaptations, breeders and evolution

An **adaptation** is a structure, function or behaviour that comes from natural selection and helps an organism survive and reproduce. A dark colour on sooty bark was an adaptation. On clean, pale bark, the same colour was not. Whether a trait helps depends on the environment.

People can shift traits too. **Artificial selection** is when people choose which plants or animals breed, so the traits they want become more common. Dog breeds, from tiny Chihuahuas to huge Great Danes, all came from breeding one kind of animal. Corn came from a wild grass called teosinte, after farmers kept seeds from the plants they liked best. The idea is the same as natural selection: some individuals breed and others do not. The difference is who decides.

| | Natural selection | Artificial selection |
|---|---|---|
| What decides who breeds | The environment, such as predators or weather | People |
| Example | Dark moths on sooty bark | Dog breeds, corn |
| Time needed | Many generations | Many generations |

**Evolution** is a change in the inherited traits of a population over many generations. Natural selection drives evolution. If no individuals have traits that suit a changed environment, the population may die out, as you will see in [that lesson](/lessons/extinction-and-survival/). Scientists also have fossils and DNA as evidence for evolution, which [that lesson](/lessons/evidence-for-change/) explores.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Adaptations come from natural selection, people use the same idea in artificial selection, and natural selection drives evolution.</Support>

### Design problem

Plan a classroom model to test this question: does the colour of the background change which paper moths "predators" find first?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Cut 40 paper moths, each about 2 centimetres wide: 20 white and 20 black. The independent variable is the background: a sheet of white paper or a sheet of black paper, each 50 centimetres by 50 centimetres. The dependent variable is how many moths of each colour a classmate picks up.

Keep these the same: moth size and shape, the number of each colour, a picking time of 10 seconds, and a distance of 1 metre to the sheet. The picker looks away while you spread the moths. Run the white sheet and the black sheet as two cases to compare. Repeat with five different classmates and find the mean for each colour. If fewer black moths are picked on black paper, and fewer white moths on white paper, background matching helps the moths survive. Safety: use safety scissors, and do not use anything to eat.

</details>
