summary: >-
  BMP says skin: in early frog animal caps, turning its signal down far enough lets competent cells
  become neural, provided their other required inputs, including early FGF signaling, are intact. In
  the frog Xenopus laevis, a square of ectoderm left intact becomes skin, but the same cells pulled
  apart become neural tissue. You follow the default model through three
  animal-cap experiments, the organizer's secreted proteins Noggin, Chordin and Follistatin that catch
  BMP outside the cell, and the whole-embryo test. It is a strong model of the BMP side of the decision
  in the frog, not a full account of all neural induction: frog caps and embryos also need early FGF
  signaling, the tissue's competence matters, and in chick BMP inhibition alone does not induce neural
  tissue. Afterwards you can predict the fate of an animal cap under a
  given treatment, trace the same contest in the fly, and say where the model falls short.
hook: "Pull a square of frog skin-to-be apart into single cells, and it becomes neural. Why? Because BMP signaling, which makes skin, falls in those cells. In the embryo, the organizer's secreted proteins make it fall."

scientists:
  - name: "Ali Hemmati-Brivanlou"
    contribution: >-
      With Douglas Melton showed that a truncated activin receptor neuralizes Xenopus animal caps
      and that follistatin does the same, and with Paul Wilson showed that BMP4 turns dissociated
      cap cells to epidermis; with Melton he set out the default model of neural induction.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Hemmati-Brivanlou & Melton, Cell 77:273 (1994); Wilson & Hemmati-Brivanlou, Nature 376:331 (1995); Hemmati-Brivanlou & Melton, Cell 88:13 (1997)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Working in Douglas Melton's laboratory at Harvard, Hemmati-Brivanlou built a broken
      receptor. In 1992 he and Melton reported that RNA for an activin type II receptor lacking
      its kinase domain, injected into Xenopus embryos, blocked the formation of mesoderm. A
      receptor like this can still bind ligand and pair with normal partners, but it cannot pass
      the signal on, so it jams the normal receptors. In 1994 they reported a second effect:
      animal caps from injected embryos became neural tissue, with no mesoderm detected that could
      have induced it. In the same issue of Cell, with O. G. Kelly, they reported that follistatin,
      an activin-binding protein made in the organizer, also neuralized caps. In 1995, with Paul
      Wilson, he added BMP4 to dissociated cap cells, and the cells became epidermis instead of
      neural tissue. In a 1997 review Hemmati-Brivanlou and Melton set out the default model. It
      gave the field a simple test for any proposed neural inducer in the frog: does it lower BMP
      signaling?
  - name: "Richard Harland"
    contribution: >-
      With William Smith cloned noggin from Xenopus by its ability to restore dorsal structures;
      his laboratory then showed that Noggin protein neuralizes animal caps directly and that it
      binds BMP4 and blocks its access to receptors.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Smith & Harland, Cell 70:829 (1992); Lamb et al., Science 262:713 (1993); Zimmerman, De Jesús-Escobar & Harland, Cell 86:599 (1996)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Harland's laboratory at the University of California, Berkeley, found Noggin by asking what
      a damaged embryo was missing. Frog eggs irradiated with ultraviolet light develop without
      dorsal structures. In 1992 William Smith and Harland injected such embryos with RNA made
      from pools of cloned genes, then split the pools that worked into smaller pools until a
      single gene was left that restored dorsal development. They named it noggin. It encoded a
      secreted protein, and its RNA was found in the organizer. In 1993 Teresa Lamb, Harland and
      colleagues added Noggin protein to animal caps. The caps made neural markers without making
      dorsal mesoderm, so Noggin was acting on the ectoderm itself, and the markers were those of
      anterior tissue. In 1996 Lyle Zimmerman, José De Jesús-Escobar and Harland showed that Noggin
      binds BMP4 and keeps it from binding its receptors. The paper appeared beside the matching
      result for Chordin, and together the two showed that two of the organizer's neural inducers
      act outside the cell, by catching BMP.
  - name: "Eddy De Robertis"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory cloned chordin as an organizer gene, showed that Chordin binds BMP4 and
      blocks its receptor binding, and with Yoshiki Sasai argued that flies and vertebrates share
      an inverted dorsoventral plan built from a BMP and a Chordin-like antagonist.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Sasai et al., Cell 79:779 (1994); Piccolo et al., Cell 86:589 (1996); De Robertis & Sasai, Nature 380:37 (1996)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      De Robertis's laboratory at the University of California, Los Angeles, came to Chordin
      through the organizer's own genes. In 1994 Yoshiki Sasai and colleagues looked for genes
      switched on by organizer transcription factors such as goosecoid and found chordin, which
      encodes a secreted protein made in the organizer. Injected chordin RNA could restore axial
      structures to ventralized embryos. In 1995 the laboratory, with the fly groups of F. Michael
      Hoffmann and Edwin Ferguson, reported that chordin and the fly gene sog act alike when
      swapped between frog and fly. In 1996 Stefano Piccolo, Sasai, Bin Lu and De Robertis showed
      that Chordin binds BMP4 tightly and keeps it from its receptor. The same year De Robertis
      and Sasai argued that the common ancestor of flies and vertebrates already patterned its
      body with a BMP on one side and a Chordin-like antagonist on the other, and that the
      vertebrate plan is that ancestral plan turned upside down. Later work from the laboratory on
      the cutting of Chordin by Tolloid enzymes fed the shuttling model.
  - name: "Pieter Nieuwkoop"
    contribution: >-
      With a team of coauthors at the Hubrecht Laboratory in Utrecht, used folds of competent
      amphibian ectoderm to propose that neural induction has two steps: activation, which makes
      tissue neural and anterior, and transformation, which makes part of it posterior.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Nieuwkoop et al., J. Exp. Zool. 120:1–108 (1952, three parts); De Robertis, Int. J. Dev. Biol. 43:603 (1999)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The experiments that carry Nieuwkoop's name were a team effort at the Hubrecht Laboratory in
      Utrecht. They were published in 1952 as three papers in the Journal of
      Experimental Zoology. Parts I and II were written by Nieuwkoop with a team of coauthors. The team
      took strips of ectoderm from amphibian embryos while the tissue could still respond
      to induction, folded them, and planted one end in a host's neural plate at different levels
      along the head-to-tail axis. Near a posterior attachment a fold could form hindbrain or
      spinal cord, while tissue farther along resembled forebrain. In Part III, which he wrote
      alone, Nieuwkoop proposed two steps, activation and transformation. With Job Faber he also
      edited the Normal Table of Xenopus laevis, first published in 1956. His two-step scheme
      gave later molecular work a frame: Noggin and Chordin, when they arrived, looked like
      activators.

techniques:
  - name: "Animal-cap explant"
    blurb: >-
      The roof of the blastocoel is cut from a Xenopus blastula and cultured in salt solution.
      Left alone it becomes epidermis, so any treatment that makes it express neural or mesoderm
      genes reveals what that treatment can induce in competent ectoderm.
  - name: "Dissociation and reaggregation"
    blurb: >-
      Cap cells are separated in medium lacking calcium and magnesium, kept apart for hours, then
      allowed to clump again. On the classic reading, signals the cells normally pass to one another are diluted away
      while they are separate, so a change in fate points to a signal among the cells themselves. Later work found that separation also switches on MAPK signaling, which damps the BMP pathway inside the cell.
  - name: "Dominant-negative receptor"
    blurb: >-
      RNA for a receptor missing its kinase domain is injected into the egg. The broken receptor
      binds ligand and pairs with normal partners but cannot signal, so it blocks the pathway in
      cells that inherit enough of it. Its weakness is that it may block more than one ligand.
  - name: "Expression cloning"
    blurb: >-
      RNA made from pools of cloned genes is injected into embryos that lack a structure, and pools
      that restore it are divided again and again until one gene remains. Noggin was found this way
      by its ability to rescue dorsal development.

    choices:
      - "The dorsal mesoderm that involutes under the ectoderm"
      - "The epidermis on the ventral side of the embryo"
      - "The endoderm that forms the floor of the blastocoel"
      - "The animal-cap ectoderm that becomes neural plate"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The organizer and the dorsal mesoderm that involutes beneath the ectoderm during gastrulation
      make all three proteins, which diffuse up to the overlying ectoderm. The ventral epidermis is
      where BMP signaling stays high, so it is the tissue the antagonists reach least.
      The ectoderm that becomes neural plate is mainly the responding tissue, not the main source (part of it makes some Chordin and Noggin at the blastula stage). The endoderm
      has other roles in early patterning but is not where these three antagonists come from.
  - stem: "An intact Xenopus animal cap cultured alone in salt solution becomes epidermis. Which explanation fits the dissociation experiments best?"
    choices:
      - "Its cells were committed to skin before removal"
      - "Its cells receive Noggin from attached mesoderm"
      - "Its cells keep signaling to one another with BMPs"
      - "Its cells cannot switch on any neural genes at all"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      When the same cells are separated for hours they become neural, and adding BMP4 to separated
      cells restores epidermis, so the best explanation is that the intact cap stays epidermal because its cells keep signaling
      to one another with BMP. (Later work found that BMP receptors keep signaling in separated cells, and that sustained MAPK activity damps Smad1 downstream of them, so lower BMP pathway output need not mean that BMP or receptor activity has been lost.) They were not committed to skin, since separation changes their fate. They do not receive
      Noggin from mesoderm, which would make them neural, not epidermal. They can switch on neural
      genes, as the separated cells show.

      In the fly Dpp is made on the dorsal side and Sog in the ventral and lateral ectoderm that

  - stem: "Noggin protein added to Xenopus animal caps turns on markers of forebrain and cement gland but not of hindbrain or spinal cord. In Nieuwkoop's terms of activation (neural and anterior) and transformation (a shift toward hindbrain and spinal cord), what does Noggin supply?"
    choices:
      - "Neither, since caps need mesoderm"
      - "Activation, without any transformation"
      - "Transformation, without any activation"
      - "Both activation and transformation"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Activation makes ectoderm neural with an anterior character, and transformation shifts some
      of it toward hindbrain and spinal cord; Noggin gives neural tissue that is only anterior, so
      it supplies activation alone. Transformation without activation would leave the cap epidermal,
      since there would be no neural tissue to posteriorize. Supplying both would have produced
      posterior markers too. The claim that caps need mesoderm fails because Noggin neuralized caps
      in which no dorsal mesoderm was detected.
  - stem: "A Xenopus embryo is injected with RNA for a BMP type I receptor carrying a mutation that keeps its kinase active with no ligand bound. Animal caps from this embryo are then treated with a high dose of Noggin protein. What do the caps most likely become?"
    choices:
      - "Neural, because Noggin blocks the kinase in the receptor"
      - "Epidermis, because the receptor signals with no ligand"
      - "Neural, because Noggin removes the BMP from the fluid"
      - "Mesoderm, because blocking BMP turns ectoderm to muscle"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Noggin works only by catching BMP outside the cell, and this receptor signals inside the cell
      whether or not BMP is bound, so the BMP pathway stays on and the cells become epidermis.
      Removing BMP from the fluid does not matter to a receptor that needs no ligand. Noggin does not
      enter the cell or act on the receptor's kinase. Blocking BMP in caps gives neural tissue, not
      muscle, and here the pathway is not blocked anyway.

  - stem: "Xenopus animal-cap cells are separated in medium lacking calcium and magnesium and allowed to reaggregate at different times. Cells reaggregated at once express epidermal keratin and no neural marker. Cells kept apart for three hours mostly express a neural marker and little keratin. Cells kept apart for three hours in medium containing BMP4 express keratin and no neural marker. Which interpretation fits all three results?"
    choices:
      - "Separation turns BMP receptors on, and those receptors drive the neural program"
      - "Separation removes contact with mesoderm, which normally makes them epidermis"
      - "Separation stresses the cells, which then turn neural whatever signals reach them"
      - "Separation lowers a signal that keeps cells epidermal, and BMP4 can restore it"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Cells kept together, or kept apart with BMP4 supplied, stay epidermal, so epidermis needs a
      signal whose effect separated cells lose and that BMP4 restores. If damage from separation made cells
      neural whatever signals reach them, BMP4 should not reverse it, since the cells were separated just as long. Animal caps
      contain no mesoderm to lose, and the organizer's mesoderm signals push ectoderm toward neural fate, not
      epidermis. If separation turned BMP receptors on, the added BMP4 should have made the cells
      more neural, but it made them epidermal. Dilution is the classic reading of these data; later work found that separation also switches on MAPK signaling, which damps the BMP pathway inside the cell, so the keyed answer says the signal is lowered, not how.
  - stem: "In a simplified model of an animal cap, cells become epidermis when free BMP4 in the fluid between them is above 0.2 nM and neural when it is at or below. The cells release BMP4 to a total of 1.0 nM. Suppose one Noggin dimer binds one BMP4 dimer so tightly that binding is complete until Noggin runs out, and concentrations are given as dimers. What is the least Noggin that makes the cap neural?"
    choices:
      - "0.2 nM"
      - "1.0 nM"
      - "0.8 nM"
      - "1.2 nM"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Free BMP4 equals total BMP4 minus the BMP4 held by Noggin, so free BMP4 falls to 0.2 nM when
      1.0 − 0.2 = 0.8 nM of Noggin is bound, which is just enough to make the cap neural; any less leaves free BMP4 above 0.2 nM. The value 0.2 nM
      takes the threshold to be the amount of antagonist needed. The value 1.0 nM assumes all BMP4
      must be removed, when only the excess above the threshold matters. The value 1.2 nM adds the
      threshold instead of subtracting it. With a weaker binder, some Noggin would stay unbound at
      equilibrium and more than 0.8 nM would be needed; the question rules this out by making binding
      complete.
  - stem: "In a chick embryo, Chordin is misexpressed in epiblast outside the future neural plate. Chordin induces no early neural markers there. In cells that have already received neural-inducing signals, Chordin makes the early neural markers last longer than they otherwise do. Which conclusion do these results support best?"
    choices:
      - "Hensen's node induces neural tissue by adding extra BMP signaling"
      - "Chordin protein cannot bind any of the BMPs made by chick embryos"
      - "BMP inhibition is not needed for neural tissue anywhere in the chick"
      - "BMP inhibition is not sufficient to make neural tissue in this region"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Chordin alone does not induce neural markers outside the future neural plate, so
      in this tissue blocking BMP is not enough and another signal is also needed. The results
      do not show that BMP inhibition is unneeded: Chordin prolongs neural markers in cells already receiving neural-inducing signals, which
      shows it does act. Chordin cannot be inactive against chick BMPs for the same reason. Nothing in
      these results suggests that the node adds BMP signaling.

  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm five"
    text: >-
      Some cells in a frog egg would like to become brain. Their neighbors keep whispering "be
      skin, be skin" to each other, so they stay skin. Other cells nearby send out catchers that
      grab the "be skin" messages between cells. The cells that stop hearing "be skin" become brain
      instead.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      The outer layer of an early frog embryo, the ectoderm, can become skin or nervous system. Its
      cells release a signal protein called BMP4 that tells one another to become skin. Cut a piece
      of this layer out and keep it whole, and it becomes skin. Separate its cells, which weakens the signal
      inside them, and they become neural tissue. In the embryo, a region called the organizer and
      the tissue that folds in beneath the ectoderm release proteins (Noggin, Chordin, Follistatin)
      that catch BMP4 and stop it from signaling. The ectoderm above them becomes the nervous
      system. In an early frog animal cap, neural is what the cells become when BMP does not tell them
      otherwise, as long as they are still young enough to respond and receive the other signals
      they need, such as FGF. Those other signals matter in the cap and in the whole embryo.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      In Xenopus, ectoderm cells signal to one another with BMP4, a TGF-beta family ligand that
      acts through type I and type II serine/threonine kinase receptors and Smad1/5/8. Intact
      animal caps become epidermis; dissociated cells, in which BMP pathway output falls (classically put down to dilution, more likely to MAPK activation), become
      neural; BMP4 added to dissociated cells restores epidermis; and a dominant-negative receptor
      neuralizes intact caps. Hence the default model: in this tissue neural fate is the default,
      actively suppressed by BMP, although early FGF signaling is also required in caps and embryos. The organizer and involuting dorsal mesoderm secrete Noggin,
      Chordin and Follistatin, which bind BMPs outside the cell and block their signaling (Noggin and Chordin by blocking receptor binding).
      Tolloid proteases cut Chordin and release BMP, which may move BMP toward the ventral side. The
      fly uses Sog (the Chordin homolog) and Dpp (the BMP homolog) with the dorsoventral axis inverted. Tissue
      neuralized by Noggin is anterior; posterior identity needs further signals. In chick, BMP
      inhibition alone does not neuralize competent epiblast, and FGF and the state of Wnt
      signaling matter.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      The default model rests on gain- and loss-of-function work in Xenopus explants. Noggin and
      Chordin bind BMP dimers with high affinity and block receptor binding; the Noggin–BMP-7
      structure shows Noggin covering both the type I and type II receptor epitopes. Chordin cleavage by Xolloid-related
      metalloproteases releases bound BMP, and in the shuttling model a diffusible Chordin–BMP
      complex carries BMP away from the organizer and releases it ventrally, which can sharpen and
      scale the gradient; the fly Sog–Tsg–Dpp system concentrates Dpp at the dorsal midline in a
      comparable way. Depletion of antagonists in frog and compound mutants in mouse argue that
      BMP inhibition is needed for the neural plate in frog and for forebrain development in mouse, while
      chick explant and electroporation work argues that it is not sufficient: FGF signaling
      before gastrulation and attenuated Wnt signaling in the epiblast are required, and FGF may
      act partly by repressing BMP expression. Nieuwkoop's activation and transformation remain a
      useful frame for the brain, but mouse stem-cell work suggests that much of the spinal cord
      arises from progenitors that acquire a posterior identity before they become neural.


      An open question is whether "default" describes a cell-intrinsic program or only the outcome
      under the culture conditions tested, given that BMP receptors keep signaling in dissociated frog cells and it is sustained
      MAPK activation, which inhibits Smad1, that is required for their neuralization.

**Cut a small square from the top of a frog blastula, culture it in salt water, and it becomes skin. Pull the same square apart into single cells, keep them apart for a few hours, then let them clump again, and they become neural tissue.**

Both results come from the frog *Xenopus laevis*, and together they turned an old question around. For decades the question had been what positive signal makes ectoderm neural. The square suggested another answer. Perhaps the signal in the ectoderm is the one that makes skin, and the job of the embryo's organizer is to stop it. This lesson follows how that idea was tested, which molecules carry it, and where it falls short.

## The layout

Every experiment in this lesson happens on one map, which the opening film draws. (For how the organizer was discovered, see [the lesson on the organizer](/lessons/the-organizer/).)

- **The blastula** is the early embryo, a hollow ball of cells around a fluid-filled cavity, the blastocoel.
- **The animal cap** is the roof of that cavity, at the top of the ball. It is made of **ectoderm**, the outer germ layer (mesoderm and endoderm are the other two), which gives rise to both the skin and the nervous system. The outer layer of skin is the **epidermis**.
- **Gastrulation** is the set of cell movements that form the germ layers. It begins at the **blastopore**, a groove on the lower half of the embryo where cells start to move inside.
- **The organizer** is the tissue at the blastopore's **dorsal lip**. **Dorsal** is the side where the back and the neural plate will form; **ventral** is the belly side.
- **The graft that started it.** In the 1920s Hilde Mangold and Hans Spemann grafted an organizer into another embryo and got a second body axis. The organizer could change what nearby ectoderm became.
- **The animal-cap explant.** Cut out the cap and culture it alone in salt solution, and it becomes epidermis, making epidermal keratin, a skin protein scored as the readout. Any treatment that makes it switch on neural genes reveals what that treatment can induce in this tissue.
- **BMPs**, bone morphogenetic proteins, are secreted signals of the TGF-beta family. Inside a cell, their signal is carried to the nucleus by the proteins Smad1, Smad5 and Smad8.

The map raises three puzzles.

1. **What holds an intact cap as skin?** In the dish, no organizer tissue is attached to it.
2. **What does the organizer send**, and how does it change the ectoderm's fate?
3. **Does the answer hold outside the frog cap**: in the whole embryo, in a fly, in a chick?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** BMP says "skin." In early frog animal caps, turn its signal down far enough, by any of the routes in this lesson, and the cells become neural, provided they are still competent and their other required inputs, including early FGF signaling, are intact. BMP's effect comes down to one dial: the activity of Smad1 (with its close relatives Smad5 and Smad8) in the nucleus. The organizer turns the dial down from outside the cell, by catching BMP. A broken receptor turns it down at the membrane. MAPK signaling turns it down inside the cell.

**What the rule covers.** The BMP side of the choice between epidermis and neural tissue in standard *Xenopus* animal-cap cultures, while the ectoderm is **competent**, able to respond, early in development. Even there the dial is not the whole decision: early FGF signaling is needed too, in caps and in the whole embryo. The rule says nothing about where along the head-to-tail axis the neural tissue will lie. And in chick, turning the dial down is not enough on its own.

## Three cap experiments

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Each experiment starts from the intact cap, which becomes epidermis. The question is what holds it there.

### Break the receptor

BMPs signal through pairs of receptor kinases. A BMP dimer brings together a type II and a type I receptor; the type I receptor then phosphorylates Smad1, 5 or 8, which moves to the nucleus with Smad4 and turns on target genes (see [the lesson on TGF-beta and Smads](/lessons/tgf-beta-and-smads/)). A receptor missing its kinase domain still binds ligand and still pairs with normal partners, but it cannot pass the signal on, so it jams the complexes it joins. This is a **dominant negative**: a broken copy that blocks the working copies.

In 1994 Ali Hemmati-Brivanlou and Douglas Melton injected eggs with RNA for such a receptor. It had been built from an activin receptor, but it also blocks signaling by related ligands.

**Predict.** Cut animal caps from the injected embryos and culture them alone, with no other tissue. What do they become?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Neural tissue, with no mesoderm detected that could have induced it. Blocking a receptor, not adding a signal, made neural tissue.

</details>

Which blocked signal normally holds ectoderm as skin? The result was first read as a block of activin. But activin added to caps makes mesoderm, not skin, so it is a poor candidate for the skin signal. A relative fit better: BMP4, whose RNA is in the ectoderm early and is cleared from the dorsal side as the neural plate appears.

### Add the ligand back

To test BMP4 directly you need cap cells that would otherwise turn neural. In 1989 Horst Grunz and Lothar Tacke had found them. They separated animal-cap cells in medium lacking the calcium and magnesium the cells need to stick together, kept them apart for hours, then let them clump again. Instead of epidermis, the cells made neural tissue. Assume for now that separation lowers BMP signaling; the next section shows how.

**Predict.** In 1995 Paul Wilson and Hemmati-Brivanlou separated cap cells in the same way and added BMP4 protein to the medium. What do the cells become?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Epidermis, and their neural genes stayed off. The ligand alone was enough to restore skin.

</details>

## One dial

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Put the results in one table. The fourth column is the model's reading of each row; it was not measured directly in every case.

| Condition | What was changed | BMP receptors | Smad1 output (the dial) | Fate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Intact cap | nothing (the control) | working | high | epidermis |
| Intact cap, dominant-negative receptor | broken activin receptor, which also blocks BMP | jammed | low | neural |
| Separated cells | kept apart for hours | still signaling | low (MAPK; next section) | neural |
| Separated cells + BMP4 | ligand added | working | high enough | epidermis |

<BmpDefaultFate />

Read down the last two columns. In these four conditions, where the dial was high the cells made skin; where it was low, they made neural tissue. In a 1997 review Hemmati-Brivanlou and Melton called this the **default model**: neural is the fate frog ectoderm takes when BMP signaling is kept low, and epidermis the fate BMP instructs.

*Default* is an operational word. It names the outcome in these cultures, with the cells competent and their other inputs intact. It does not mean that no signals are acting, and the third row shows why that matters.

### Why separated cells turn neural

The first explanation of the third row was dilution. Separation spreads the cells through a large volume of fluid, so on this reading the BMP they normally pass to one another is diluted away. It was a fair guess, and the physics of diffusion supports it if BMP spreads freely (Going deeper works it). But later work found that BMP receptors keep signaling in separated cells. Something else had changed.

Separation switches on lasting signaling by **MAPK** (mitogen-activated protein kinase), a kinase cascade usually started by growth factors such as FGF. MAPK phosphorylates Smad1 in its middle, or linker, region. That can keep Smad1 out of the nucleus and mark it for destruction. The cells need this MAPK activity to turn neural. So the dial still goes down in separated cells, by a different hand, and dilution is probably not the reason.

That is why the rule names the dial, Smad1 in the nucleus, and not BMP in the fluid. It also reads the fourth row: the damping is partial, so enough added BMP4 can push the pathway's output back up. Keep MAPK in mind; it returns in the frog embryo and in the chick.

## The organizer's catchers

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

If BMP makes skin, the organizer should make something that blocks BMP. It makes at least three such proteins.

Chordin and Noggin are made early, at the blastula stage, in dorsal animal cells, so neural induction starts before gastrulation. Then, during gastrulation, the organizer and the dorsal mesoderm turn inward and spread beneath the ectoderm. This is **involution**. The involuting mesoderm secretes Chordin, Noggin and Follistatin, and the ectoderm above it, where BMP signaling is kept low, becomes the neural plate.

**Noggin.** Noggin was found in 1992 by William Smith and Richard Harland. It encodes a secreted protein expressed in the organizer, and it binds BMP4 and keeps it off its receptors (Zimmerman, De Jesús-Escobar and Harland, 1996).

**Predict.** In 1993 Teresa Lamb, Harland and colleagues added Noggin protein to intact animal caps. Using the dial, what should the caps become? Should they need to make dorsal mesoderm first?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Neural tissue, without dorsal mesoderm, so Noggin was acting on the ectoderm directly. The markers were all anterior: forebrain, which is neural, and cement gland, a mucus gland at the head end that forms from non-neural ectoderm. There were no hindbrain or spinal cord markers.

</details>

**Chordin.** Chordin was cloned in 1994 by Yoshiki Sasai and colleagues in Eddy De Robertis's laboratory as a gene switched on by organizer transcription factors. It neuralizes animal caps and BMP4 opposes the effect (Sasai, Lu, Steinbeisser and De Robertis, 1995), and it binds BMP4 tightly and blocks its receptor binding (Piccolo, Sasai, Lu and De Robertis, 1996).

**Follistatin.** Known as a protein that binds activin, follistatin is made in the organizer, and in 1994 Hemmati-Brivanlou, O. G. Kelly and Melton showed that it neuralizes caps. It was later found to bind some BMPs as well. It works differently from the other two: instead of blocking receptor binding, it can stay bound to BMP as BMP docks on its receptor, forming a three-part complex that does not signal.

None of the three needs to enter a cell; each grabs BMP outside it. The structure viewer shows how Noggin does it: in the crystal, a Noggin dimer clasps a BMP-7 dimer and covers the faces the receptors would bind.

Chordin does one more thing, which the course calls the shuttling mechanism. In the model, BMP held by Chordin travels farther than free BMP, so it is carried away from the organizer; Tolloid-family proteases (Xolloid in the frog) then cut Chordin and release the BMP toward the ventral side.

### The embryo test

Caps show what the antagonists *can* do. Does the embryo *need* them? Two groups asked by depleting proteins from whole frog embryos.

**Predict.** In 2005 Khokha and colleagues depleted Chordin, Noggin and Follistatin together from embryos of *Xenopus tropicalis*, a related frog. What happens to the neural plate?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The embryos lost their dorsal structures, the neural plate among them. With all three catchers gone, BMP signaling stays high, which fits the ectoderm not becoming neural.

</details>

The reverse test points the same way. Reversade and colleagues depleted BMP2, BMP4 and BMP7 in embryos whose organizer signals had been prevented, by ultraviolet treatment or by knocking down beta-catenin. The embryos formed massive amounts of brain, even without normal organizer signals. So in the frog, low BMP signaling is needed for the neural plate, not only able to make neural tissue in a dish.

The dial is not the whole story. Early FGF signaling is also needed for neural induction, in the embryo and in caps, in addition to BMP inhibition (Delaune and colleagues, 2005). FGF can turn the dial down through MAPK, the hand you met in separated cells, but it also does things that lowering BMP does not explain.

## The same contest in a fly, upside down

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

The fruit fly has the same pair. Its gene *decapentaplegic* (*dpp*) encodes a BMP2/4 relative, and *short gastrulation* (*sog*) encodes a relative of Chordin. In 1995 Holley and colleagues, a collaboration of fly and frog laboratories including De Robertis's, reported that the two antagonists act alike when swapped between species: frog Chordin works in the fly and fly Sog works in the frog. Tolloid itself was first named as a fly gene, and it cuts Sog.

The map is inverted. In the fly, Dpp is made on the dorsal side and Sog in broad lateral stripes of ectoderm on the ventral half, and the nerve cord forms from that ectoderm ventrally, where Sog keeps Dpp signaling low. In a vertebrate, BMP4 signaling is high on the ventral side, Chordin comes from the dorsal organizer, and the neural tube forms dorsally. In both animals, nervous system forms where the antagonist keeps the BMP signal low.

In 1996 De Robertis and Sasai argued that the common ancestor of flies and vertebrates already patterned its body this way, and that one lineage inverted the plan: turn a fly over, and its BMP and antagonist lie where a frog's do.

## Where the rule stops

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

Beyond FGF, three more findings mark the rule's edges.

**It says neural, not where.** The Noggin-treated caps made only anterior markers. This fits an older idea from Nieuwkoop: activation, which makes ectoderm neural and anterior, and transformation, which shifts part of it toward hindbrain and spinal cord. In molecular terms the BMP antagonists look like activators. Wnt signals (see [that lesson](/lessons/wnt-signaling/)), FGFs and retinoic acid are leading candidates for transformation. The two-step frame still serves for the brain. The spinal cord may follow a different order: lineage tracing in mouse embryos and stem-cell work suggest that much of it comes from progenitors that take on a posterior identity before they become neural.

**In chick, turning the dial down is not enough.** Read blindly, the rule predicts that blocking BMP anywhere in competent ectoderm should make it neural.

**Predict.** In 1998 Claudio Stern's laboratory misexpressed Chordin in chick epiblast, the top cell layer of the early chick embryo, outside the future neural plate. Do those cells switch on early neural markers?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Chordin did not induce early neural markers there, although it did stabilize early neural markers in cells that had already received neural-inducing signals.

</details>

What else is needed? In 2000 Andrea Streit and colleagues reported that FGF signaling before gastrulation is needed to begin neural induction in chick. In 2001 Sara Wilson and colleagues in Thomas Edlund's laboratory reported that in chick epiblast the state of Wnt signaling decides whether cells respond to FGF by becoming neural or epidermal. FGF can turn the dial through MAPK, but here, as in the frog, it does more than that.

**In mouse, the antagonists matter for the forebrain.** Embryos lacking both chordin and noggin show severe defects in forebrain development, yet they still form neural tissue. That shows the two antagonists are needed for the forebrain, not that they are needed for neural tissue as such.

None of this shows the frog results were wrong. All the animal-cap work is in *Xenopus laevis*, and the rule was written for that tissue.

### Design problem

You have Xenopus animal caps and five conditions. Predict the fate of each: (1) an intact cap; (2) an intact cap given Noggin protein; (3) cells separated for three hours, then reaggregated; (4) separated cells given BMP4; (5) an intact cap from an embryo injected with RNA for a dominant-negative BMP receptor. Then say which single result would have falsified the default model.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

(1) Epidermis: the cells keep exchanging BMP, so the dial stays high. (2) Neural, and anterior in character, since Noggin catches the BMP before it reaches the receptors and supplies no posteriorizing signal. (3) Neural: separation switches on MAPK, which damps the BMP pathway (the classic reading was dilution). (4) Epidermis: the damping is partial, so enough BMP4 pushes the pathway's output back up. (5) Neural, if enough of the broken receptor is made: the pathway is jammed, so BMP in the fluid has little effect.

The default model says that competent cap ectoderm with BMP signaling blocked becomes neural. What could break it is a cap with BMP signaling measured and shown to be blocked, for example by the dominant-negative receptor, that still fails to make neural markers, or makes keratin, while its competence, timing and other required inputs such as FGF are preserved. Then something other than BMP would be holding it as skin, or neural fate would need a further positive signal. A cap that was too old or lacked FGF signaling does not test the model, and nor does separation alone, since separated cells also switch on MAPK.

</details>

## Putting it together

In one line: neural induction involves inhibition of BMP signals, with FGF and competence alongside. That answers the three puzzles. An intact cap stays skin because its own BMP keeps the dial up. The organizer sends catchers, Noggin, Chordin and Follistatin, that hold BMP outside the cell, and the frog embryo needs them for its neural plate. Outside the frog cap, the fly runs the same contest upside down, the chick shows that turning the dial down is not always enough, and none of it says where along the head-to-tail axis the new tissue will lie.

The general lesson is that a cell's fate is set in part by the signals it is exposed to, and in part by how the cell reads them. You will meet the second half again in [netrin and DCC](/lessons/netrin-and-dcc/), where a growth cone's internal state helps decide its answer to a cue, as MAPK does here.

## Going deeper

### The shuttling mechanism, step by step

While bound to Chordin, BMP cannot reach its receptors, so it is silent as it travels. Where Chordin is cut, the freed BMP piles up on the ventral side. The antagonist thus both lowers BMP signaling dorsally and helps shape a gradient that peaks ventrally, which can sharpen and scale it. The fly's Sog, with its partner Tsg, concentrates Dpp at the dorsal midline in a comparable way.

### Why dilution was a good guess, and what would make it fail

The numbers here are illustrative, not measured for frog BMP4. Picture a cap as a ball of cells about 200 micrometres in radius, each cell secreting BMP at the same rate, and compare it with one isolated cell 15 to 25 micrometres in radius, both in a large bath kept near zero BMP, with no removal. For steady, free diffusion, the concentration a cell inside the ball sees is roughly (R/a)² times what an isolated cell sees, where R is the ball's radius and a the cell's: about 60 to 180-fold here. So if BMP diffused freely, dispersal should dilute it strongly. A real cap is a sheet a few cells thick, not a ball, so the true effect would be smaller.

Now suppose instead that cells remove BMP by uptake or breakdown, idealized as a uniform first-order sink with rate constant k, so each molecule lasts on average 1/k. The concentration then decays over a length √(D/k), where D is the diffusion coefficient: a characteristic scale, not a hard limit, and only an approximation to uptake at cell surfaces. Reversible binding alone would not do this. With D near 2 µm²/s and an average lifetime of 5 to 30 minutes, the decay length is about 25 to 60 micrometres, roughly half a cell diameter to two. Then much of each cell's BMP signal is its own or its near neighbors', and dispersal changes it far less. That is one physical reading consistent with BMP receptors still signaling after separation, not a measured explanation; the signal also depends on secretion, where removal happens and how many receptors are occupied.

### The truncated receptor and the whole axis

Noggin-treated caps are anterior, but caps neuralized by the truncated receptor expressed markers along the whole head-to-tail axis. This lesson does not explain the difference. It is a reminder that the broken receptor blocks more than one ligand and acts in every cell that inherits it, from the egg onward.

### Stem cells: between frog and chick

In mammals the closest test of the default idea is in embryonic stem cells: mouse cells grown alone at low density, and human cells given blockers of BMP and related signals, mostly become neural. These cells make some FGF of their own, so they sit between the frog and chick results.

### An inversion guessed from anatomy

Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire had proposed an inversion of the body plan between arthropods and vertebrates in the 1820s on anatomy alone, long before Dpp and Sog.

### How noggin and chordin were found

Noggin was found by expression cloning: RNA from pools of cloned genes was injected into ultraviolet-treated embryos that lack dorsal structures, and the pools were narrowed to the one gene that rescued them. Chordin's 1995 neuralizing paper appeared in the same issue of Nature as Wilson and Hemmati-Brivanlou's BMP4 result; the 1994 cloning paper was published separately, in Cell. The 1996 papers showing that Noggin and Chordin bind BMP4 appeared side by side.

### The Utrecht fold experiments of 1952

In 1952 Pieter Nieuwkoop and coauthors at the Hubrecht Laboratory in Utrecht reported experiments in which a fold of responsive amphibian ectoderm, planted with one end in a host's neural plate, could form posterior structures near the attachment and forebrain farther along it. These folds were the basis of his two steps.
