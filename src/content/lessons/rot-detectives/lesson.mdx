## The big idea

Every autumn, trees drop huge piles of leaves. A year later, most of them seem to be gone. Where did they go?

Living things called **decomposers** break down dead plants and animals. Most are far too small to see, like bacteria. Some, like **mold**, a kind of fungus, grow fuzzy patches you can see. Small animals such as earthworms and pill bugs help too, by eating dead leaves. As decomposers break things down, they return nutrients to the soil, and plants use them again.

In this activity, your child watches decomposers at work through the side of a sealed container. Some changes are fast, like fuzz on bread within a week. Some are slow, like a dry fallen leaf, which may barely change in a month. A bottle cap does not change at all, because plastic is not food for most decomposers.

Virginia’s kindergarten standard asks children to see that changes can be fast or slow and can be measured. Grades 3 and 4 add decomposers and their place in the food web.

<SafetyNote>
- An adult sets up and seals every container. Your child can watch, draw and point, but never opens a container.
- Never open, sniff or touch the inside of a container once it is sealed. Mold spores can set off allergies and asthma. Anyone with asthma, a mold allergy or a weakened immune system should not handle the containers. If that includes your child or anyone who lives with you, skip this activity or ask their doctor first. Keep the containers on a shelf in a room where no one sleeps.
- Use plastic containers or zip-top bags, not glass, so nothing breaks. If a lid bulges or a bag puffs up, do not open or squeeze it. Put it in a second bag, close that bag and put it in the outdoor trash.
- Use damp soil from a garden bed or a bag of potting soil. Open a bag slowly, away from your face. Do not use soil from within 10 feet of roads, driveways or old painted buildings, from lawns treated with chemicals, or from where cats use the soil as a toilet.
- Nobody eats any food used in the activity. The adult handles the bread and the cut apple. If your child has a wheat or other food allergy, leave the bread container out. Keep the containers, and the bottle cap or foil before it is sealed in, where pets and young children cannot reach them.
- Wash hands with soap and water after handling soil or food. At the end, the adult puts the sealed containers in a bag and throws them in the outdoor trash, unopened.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Set up.** Number five containers with the marker. Put 2 or 3 spoonfuls of damp soil in containers 1 to 4. The soil should feel like a wrung-out sponge. If it is dry, add a teaspoon of water. Pick out any worms or bugs and put them back outside.
2. **Add the things.** You put half of the apple slice in container 1, the leaf in container 2, the bread in container 3, and the bottle cap or foil in container 4. Container 5 gets the other half of the apple slice and no soil. Close each lid and tape it all the way around, or seal each bag and tape the top.
3. **Predict before you look.** Ask your child, "Which one will change first? Which one will never change?" Write their guesses on the sheet.
4. **Draw day one.** Your child draws each container as it looks now. Older children can note color, size and shape.
5. **Check twice a week.** Put the containers on a shelf out of direct sun. Look at them twice a week for 3 to 4 weeks. Look for fuzz, spots, color changes, liquid and shrinking. Bread often grows fuzzy mold within one to two weeks, sooner for plain bread without preservatives. Store bread with preservatives can take longer.
6. **Compare the apples.** Ask, "Is the apple with soil changing in a different way than the apple without soil?" Both usually rot, since mold spores are in the air and on the fruit. The two may look different.
7. **Wrap up.** After 3 or 4 weeks, line up the drawings from first to last. Ask, "What changed fast? What changed slowly? What did not change?" Then you bag the containers and throw them out, unopened.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder which one will get fuzzy first. Why do you think so?"
- "What do you think the fuzz is? Is it alive?"
- "What will happen to the bottle cap? Will it look different in a month?"
- "I wonder if the apple without soil will rot. What could make it rot with no soil?"
- "Where do you think the apple goes when it gets smaller?"
- "What would happen in the woods if nothing ever rotted?"

## Why it works

Bacteria and molds feed by releasing chemicals that break food down outside their bodies, then taking in the small pieces. Mold spores are everywhere in air and soil, so a slice of apple soon carries some. In a warm, damp, sealed space they grow fast.

Soft, wet foods like bread and apple are easy to break down. A leaf is tougher. It has stiff fibers and waxy layers, so on a forest floor it can take months to years to break down fully. Outdoors, bacteria, fungi, earthworms and pill bugs all work on it together. A sealed jar holds mostly the tiny ones.

Plastic is made of very long chains that most decomposers cannot use. It breaks into smaller pieces over many years, but it does not rot like food. Foil is a metal and does not rot either.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use three containers: bread, apple and bottle cap. Your child draws each one on day one and again at the end. Ask one question each visit: "Fast, slow or no change?" Let your child sort the final drawings into those three groups. Use the words "rot" and "tiny living things" before you introduce mold and decomposers.

### Ages 8 to 10
Use all five containers. On each check, measure the widest patch of mold in centimeters through the side with a ruler and record it in a table. Plot the bread mold size on a line graph. Then plan a fair test with one thing changed, such as damp versus dry bread, keeping everything else the same.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If nothing grows after two weeks, the soil may be too dry or the room too cold. Set up new containers with damper soil in a warmer spot. Store bread may take three weeks to show mold. If one container leaks or the tape comes loose, bag it and throw it away. Do not reseal it.
