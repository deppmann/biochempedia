## Explain: what an adaptation is

<WordBank terms={['adaptation', 'physical adaptation', 'behavioral adaptation', 'camouflage', 'migration', 'hibernation', 'trade-off', 'variation']} />

Look at a snowshoe hare’s back feet. They are large, and fur covers the bottoms. Big feet help the hare move on top of soft snow. The fur helps keep its feet warm.

An **adaptation** is an inherited trait that helps a living thing survive and reproduce in its ecosystem. Inherited means it passes from parents to their young. Scientists sort adaptations into two groups.

- A **physical adaptation** is a feature of the body: a body covering, a color, or a structure.
- A **behavioral adaptation** is an action or a pattern of actions.

| Adaptation | Type | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Hare’s large, furry back feet | Physical (structure and covering) | Moves on snow; keeps feet warm |
| Hare’s white winter coat | Physical (coloration) | Harder for predators to see on snow |
| Eastern box turtle’s shell | Physical (covering) | Shields its soft body from predators |
| Great blue heron’s long legs | Physical (structure) | Wades into water to catch fish |
| Monarchs flying south in fall | Behavioral (migration) | Reach a warmer place for winter |
| Groundhog’s long winter sleep | Behavioral (hibernation) | Needs much less food while food is scarce |

The hare’s white coat is **camouflage**, coloring that helps an animal blend in with its background. Blending in lowers the chance that a predator notices the hare. It does not make the hare invisible.

Bird beaks work a bit like tools. A cardinal’s short, thick beak cracks seeds. A hummingbird’s long, thin beak reaches nectar deep in a flower. A heron’s long, sharp beak grabs fish. The comparison breaks here: you can put a tool down, but a bird cannot swap its beak.

## Explain: behaviors, trade-offs and who changes

**Migration** is a regular journey between places used in different seasons. Many monarch butterflies pass through Virginia in fall on their way to Mexico. The monarchs that make this trip have never made it before. **Hibernation** is a deep, inactive state some animals enter for winter. A hibernating groundhog’s heart beats much more slowly, and its body cools. So hibernation changes the whole body, not only what the animal does. Many behaviors like these are instinctive: animals do them without being taught. How animals respond to seasons is the topic of [the next lesson](/lessons/daily-and-seasonal-rhythms/).

A **trade-off** means a trait helps in one way but costs in another. A white coat hides a hare on snow. On bare brown ground, the same coat stands out. A box turtle’s heavy shell protects it, but it is also heavy to carry. No living thing is perfectly adapted.

Hares in one forest are not all alike. In Montana, some hares turned white earlier than others. In some places with little snow, snowshoe hares stay brown all winter. These differences between individuals of a species are called **variation**.

A single hare cannot decide to change color. Its coat changes on a yearly schedule, day length seems to help set it, and snow can nudge the timing a little. Montana studies found no sign that white hares moved to snowier spots. Change in an inherited trait happens to a **population**, all the members of one species in one area. Hares whose inherited timing fits the snow may survive and have more young. Over many generations, that timing can become more common. This is natural selection, which you will study in [a later lesson](/lessons/natural-selection-in-action/). If the climate changes faster than that, a population can struggle.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Every adaptation has trade-offs, individuals vary, and only populations change over many generations.</Support>

## Elaborate: survival of matched and mismatched hares

Scientists followed 186 snowshoe hares with radio collars at two sites in Montana. Each week they found each hare by its radio signal. They scored how white its coat was and how much snow covered the ground. Then a math model estimated the chance of surviving one week for hares at each level of mismatch.

| Coat compared with the ground | Difference in whiteness (percent) | Estimated chance of surviving one week (percent) |
|---|---|---|
| Matched (white on snow, or brown on bare ground) | 0 | 96 |
| Partly mismatched | 60 | 92 |
| Fully mismatched (for example, all white on bare ground) | 100 | 89 |

Source: Zimova, Mills and Nowak, Ecology Letters (2016), Discussion and Figure 3. Hares were near Seeley Lake (2009 to 2012) and Gardiner (2010 to 2012), Montana. These are rounded estimates from a model, not counts of hares.

A second team tested color directly, with clay mice instead of live ones.

| Model color | Ground | Attacks recorded (number) |
|---|---|---|
| Dark | Light beach sand | 5 |
| Light | Light beach sand | 2 |
| Light | Dark inland soil | 16 |
| Dark | Dark inland soil | 5 |

Source: Vignieri, Larson and Hoekstra, Evolution (2010), Table 1. Models sat out in the field at Topsail Hill Preserve State Park (beach) and at an inland site, in Florida.

1. By how many percentage points does weekly survival drop from matched to fully mismatched hares?
2. Imagine 100 matched hares and 100 fully mismatched hares. About how many in each group would you expect to be alive after one week?
3. On each kind of ground, which model color was attacked more? What fraction of all 28 attacks hit models that did not match?

### Design problem

You cannot test live hares at school. Plan a safe classroom test of whether matching the background protects prey, using paper “prey” and classmates as “predators.”

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Are paper squares that match the background picked up less often than squares that do not?
- **Independent variable:** whether a square matches the background (white squares and brown squares on a white sheet, then on a brown sheet).
- **Dependent variable:** the number of each color picked up.
- **Constants:** 20 squares of each color, each 1 cm by 1 cm, a sheet about 1 m by 1 m, and 15 seconds per turn.
- **Control:** the same mix of squares on both backgrounds, so color matching is the only change.
- **Repeated trials:** five classmates each take three turns on each background. Find the mean for each color.
- **Safety:** use only paper, keep the floor clear so no one trips, and pick up every square afterward.

If matching squares are picked up less on both backgrounds, the result supports camouflage. Your test is a model: real predators also use smell and movement.

</details>
