**In fall, a snowshoe hare’s brown fur turns white over several weeks. On snow it is hard to see, but what if the snow melts early and the hare is still white?**

Snowshoe hares live in snowy forests in Canada and the northern United States, including Montana. Scientists there put radio collars on hares and checked on them each week to answer one question: does a coat that stands out cost a hare its life?

## What counts as an adaptation?

<WordBank terms={['adaptation', 'physical adaptation', 'behavioral adaptation', 'camouflage', 'migration', 'hibernation', 'trade-off', 'variation']} />

An **adaptation** is an inherited trait that helps survival and reproduction in a particular ecosystem. Inherited means passed from parents to their young. Scientists also name a third group, **physiological adaptations**, which are about how the body works inside.

- A **physical adaptation** is a feature of the body: a body covering, a color, or a structure.
- A **behavioral adaptation** is an action or a pattern of actions.

| Adaptation | Type | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Hare’s large, furry back feet | Physical (structure and covering) | Spread its weight on soft snow; keep feet warm |
| Hare’s white winter coat | Physical (coloration) | Harder for predators to see on snow |
| Eastern box turtle’s shell | Physical (covering) | Shields its soft body from predators |
| Great blue heron’s long legs | Physical (structure) | Wades into water to catch fish |
| Monarchs flying south in fall | Behavioral (migration) | Reach a warmer place for winter |
| Groundhog’s winter hibernation | Behavioral and physiological (hibernation) | Needs much less food while food is scarce |

The hare's white coat is **camouflage**, coloring that helps an animal blend into its background.

Bird beaks work a bit like tools. A cardinal’s short, thick beak cracks seeds. A hummingbird’s long, thin beak reaches nectar deep in a flower. A heron’s long, sharp beak grabs fish.

## Behaviors, trade-offs and variation

The film placed hibernation in both groups; here is where the line falls. **Hibernation** is a deep, inactive state some animals enter for winter. Going into a burrow and staying inactive is the behavior. Inside, the groundhog’s heart beats much more slowly and its body cools, which is a change in how the body works. It is not ordinary sleep: the groundhog warms up and wakes briefly now and then.

**Migration** is a regular journey between places used in different seasons. Many monarch butterflies pass through Virginia in fall on their way to Mexico, and the monarchs making the trip have never made it before. Many behaviors like these are instinctive, so animals do them without being taught. How animals respond to seasons is the topic of [the next lesson](/lessons/daily-and-seasonal-rhythms/).

<LessonFigure src="/figures/adaptations-for-survival/two-behavioral-adaptations.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two panels. On the left, a line of monarch butterflies flies across a fall field along a dashed arrow; on the right, a groundhog lies curled up, hibernating, in a burrow under a snowy hillside." caption="Migration moves monarchs to a warmer place, while hibernation lets groundhogs need much less food when food is scarce." />

A **trade-off** means a trait helps in one way but costs in another, like the white coat on bare ground or the turtle's heavy shell in the film. A trade-off is not a failure; no living thing is perfectly adapted.

**Variation** means differences among individuals of a species. In Montana, some hares turned white earlier than others. Variation is a difference between hares, not a change inside one hare: a hare cannot choose a new coat, because its molt follows a yearly schedule. Day length seems to help set that schedule. Snow can shift spring timing somewhat, but not enough to keep every Montana hare matched.

## Survival of matched and mismatched hares

Here are the rounded model estimates from 186 hares at two Montana sites. Snow cover was a weekly average for each site, and for weeks when a hare was missed, the model also estimated its coat color.

| Coat compared with the ground | Difference between coat whiteness and snow cover (percentage points) | Estimated chance of surviving one week (percent) |
|---|---|---|
| Matched (white on snow, or brown on bare ground) | 0 | 96 |
| Partly mismatched | 60 | 92 |
| Fully mismatched (for example, all white on bare ground) | 100 | 89 |

Source: Zimova, Mills and Nowak, Ecology Letters (2016), Discussion and Figure 3. Hares were near Seeley Lake (2009 to 2012) and Gardiner (2010 to 2012), Montana. These are rounded estimates from a model, not counts of hares, and the true values could be somewhat higher or lower. On average, each hare was badly mismatched for less than one week a year. The worry is that shorter snow seasons will make those weeks more common.

**Predict.** A drop from 96 percent to 89 percent is a drop of how many **percentage points**? For groups of 100, how many hares would the estimates leave alive in each group?

## Who changes?

A **population** is all the members of one species in one area. The second film traced how an inherited tendency to turn brown earlier may become more common over many generations. This is natural selection, which you will study in [a later lesson](/lessons/natural-selection-in-action/). The film left out one risk: if the climate changes faster than the population can change, the population can struggle.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Adaptations usually have trade-offs, individuals vary, and inherited traits become more or less common in a population over many generations.</Support>

### Design problem

You cannot test live hares at school. Plan a safe classroom test of whether matching the background protects prey, using paper “prey” and classmates as “predators.”

**Predict.** If you move the same white and brown paper prey from a white background to a brown one, which color should become easier to find? Name the independent variable and the dependent variable before opening the plan.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Are paper squares that match the background picked up less often than squares that do not?
- **Independent variable:** whether a square matches the background (white squares and brown squares on a white sheet, then on a brown sheet).
- **Dependent variable:** the number of each color picked up.
- **Constants:** 20 squares of each color, each 1 cm by 1 cm, a sheet about 1 m by 1 m, and 15 seconds per turn.
- **Control:** the same mix of squares on both backgrounds, so color matching is the only change.
- **Repeated trials:** five classmates each take three turns on each background. Find the mean for each color.
- **Safety:** use only paper, keep the floor clear so no one trips, and pick up every square afterward.

If matching squares are picked up less on both backgrounds, the result supports camouflage. Your test is a model: real predators also use smell and movement.

</details>
