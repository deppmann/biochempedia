      Griffith studied pneumococci, the bacteria behind much pneumonia, and the serological types

      living ones make it. What his work made possible was an assay: a change of type that later
      laboratories could reproduce in a test tube and use to purify the active material.

techniques:
  - name: "Transformation assay with enzyme controls"
    blurb: >-
      A fraction from donor bacteria is added to recipient cells, and the recipients are scored for
      a heritable donor trait such as a type III capsule. Treating the fraction first with
      proteases, ribonuclease or DNA-cutting enzymes, each checked on a known substrate, shows
      which kind of molecule the activity needs.
  - name: "Radioisotope tracing with physical separation"
    blurb: >-
      Two parts of a virus are labelled with different isotopes, sulfur-35 for protein and
      phosphorus-32 for DNA. After infection the attached virus is sheared off and the cells spun
      down, and counting each isotope in cells, fluid and progeny shows where each part went.
  - name: "X-ray fibre diffraction"
    blurb: >-
      A beam of X-rays passes through a stretched fibre of aligned molecules, and the pattern of
      spots records repeating distances. For DNA it gave the base spacing, the helical repeat and
      the position of the phosphates, though not the order of the bases.
  - name: "Stereochemical model building"
    blurb: >-
      Atoms are placed in a physical model at allowed bond lengths and angles, and the model is
      tested against chemical and diffraction data. Watson and Crick used it to find a two-chain
      structure in which the bases pair specifically inside the helix.

  - stem: "Which feature of the double helix most directly suggested to Watson and Crick a way to copy a gene?"
    choices:
      - "Each phosphate lies on the outside of the helix"
      - "Specific pairing means one strand fixes the sequence of its partner"
      - "The 1953 model had 10 base pairs per turn"
      - "The two chains coil in a right-handed direction"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Under canonical Watson-Crick pairing, adenine pairs with thymine and guanine with cytosine, so each strand determines the sequence of its partner. If the chains separate, each can serve as a template for a new partner. The outside phosphates, the number of base pairs per turn and the handedness of the coil describe the shape of the helix, but none of them fixes a sequence, so none suggests a copying rule.

  - stem: "A double-stranded DNA sample is 30% guanine. What percentage of its bases is adenine?"
    choices:
      - "30% adenine"
      - "40% adenine"
      - "20% adenine"
      - "70% adenine"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      In a duplex G pairs with C, so C is also 30% and G plus C is 60%. The remaining 40% is A plus
      T, and A equals T, so A is 20%. Thirty percent assumes adenine equals guanine, which pairing
      does not require. Forty percent is A and T together, not A alone. Seventy percent subtracts
      only guanine from the total and forgets cytosine and thymine.
  - stem: "A purified transforming fraction keeps full activity after trypsin, chymotrypsin and ribonuclease, but loses all activity after an enzyme preparation that breaks down known DNA. What do these results most directly show?"
    choices:

      - "Every gene in every organism is made of DNA alone"
      - "The agent is RNA that DNA shields from ribonuclease"
      - "Activity needs intact DNA in this bacterial fraction"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Only the DNA-destroying treatment removed activity, so in this pneumococcal system the
      activity needs intact DNA. A protein agent would be expected to fall to trypsin or
      chymotrypsin, and the DNA enzyme was checked on known DNA. RNA shielded by DNA is an extra
      assumption the data do not support, since ribonuclease had no effect. The claim about every
      gene in every organism goes far beyond one bacterial trait in one species.
  - stem: "One strand of a DNA segment reads 5'-ATGCCA-3'. What is the partner strand, written 5' to 3'?"
    choices:

      The partner is complementary and antiparallel. Pairing gives TACGGT read from its 3' end, so

      - "After one: all hybrid density. After two: equal amounts of hybrid and light DNA"
      - "After one: all hybrid density. After two: all hybrid density"
      - "After one: equal amounts of heavy and light DNA. After two: all light DNA"
      - "After one: all light DNA. After two: all light DNA"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      After one generation every double helix holds one heavy parental strand and one new light strand, so all of the DNA has hybrid density. The first film followed a heritable change to DNA. Everything in this lesson serves one idea:

> **The one idea. DNA carries inherited information; complementary pairing gives each strand a copying rule.**
>
> *Scope:* the identity claim is built here from pneumococcal transformation and DNA-phage experiments. The copying rule assumes canonical Watson-Crick pairing in duplex DNA; pairing supplies sequence information, not the machinery of replication.

A genetic material must store a great deal of information that differs among individuals and species. It must be copied faithfully before cell division, so an ordinary mitosis gives both daughters the same set. It must act by directing what the cell makes. These demands follow from [chromosomes and meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/).

Chromosomes contain protein and DNA. Protein looked the better candidate: it has about 20 amino-acid building blocks, while DNA has four bases and was thought to repeat simply. For nucleotide structure, see [nucleotide metabolism](/lessons/nucleotide-metabolism/).

## From a heritable change to an assay

The film's rough and smooth cells were pneumococcus (*Streptococcus pneumoniae*), whose serological types are set by the polysaccharide capsule. Smooth (S) strains have a capsule and can kill a mouse; rough (R) variants lack it and are generally harmless.

The film left out the mice. In January 1928, Frederick Griffith injected mice with a small dose of living R bacteria derived from type II together with heat-killed type III S bacteria. One control series showed that the heated culture was sterile. Some mice died, and from them Griffith recovered living, encapsulated type III bacteria that bred true.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/dna-the-genetic-material/griffith-mixed-injection.webp" width={1200} height={772} alt="AI-generated illustration: a syringe loaded with a few living plain rough bacteria of type II origin and heat-killed capsule-coated type III smooth bacteria is injected into a mouse; the mouse dies and living gold-haloed type III bacteria are recovered that breed true; an inset shows neither ingredient alone gives this." caption="Griffith's result established a heritable change to the dead donor's type, but it did not identify the responsible substance." />

Griffith suggested that a specific protein structure in the dead cells let the living ones make the capsule. He left an **assay**, a scored experimental readout: transformation to the donor's type, kept by descendants.

Later work at the Rockefeller Institute showed the change could be made in culture and then with extracts free of cells. Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty purified the active fraction from type III cells and tested it on type II-derived R cells. In their 1944 paper, the nitrogen-to-phosphorus ratio and ultraviolet absorption matched nucleic acid. The protein-digesting enzymes were crystalline trypsin and chymotrypsin, and the RNA-digesting one was ribonuclease (RNase). The inherited change was specific to the donor's type.

**Predict.** Why did each enzyme preparation need to be tested on a known substrate before the transformation result meant anything?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

An unchanged transformation assay is informative only if the enzyme was active. A failed protease could leave a protein agent untouched, just as a failed ribonuclease could leave RNA untouched. Showing that each preparation digested its known target makes “activity remained” evidence against that target being required.

</details>

Their conclusion, “principally, if not solely” highly polymerized DNA within the limits of their methods, made DNA the leading candidate. The hedge left one objection open: a protein below the detection limit might be the real agent, with DNA as its carrier. The design problem asks how to answer it.

## An independent tracer test

The film's phage was T2, a virus of *Escherichia coli*; see [bacterial and phage genetics](/lessons/bacterial-and-phage-genetics/). Its gold and blue paths stand for real isotope tracers. Cysteine and methionine put sulfur in protein, while DNA has none. DNA's backbone is rich in phosphorus, while protein has little. Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase therefore used sulfur-35 to label protein and phosphorus-32 to label mostly DNA.

In their summarized result, about 75% of the sulfur came off into the fluid after blending, but only about 15% of the phosphorus did. Progeny carried less than 1% of parental sulfur and 30% or more of parental phosphorus.

The authors assigned the coat a role in delivering DNA and said it probably had no function in intracellular phage growth. They left entry of sulfur-free material other than DNA undetermined, and a quarter of the sulfur stayed with the cells, so the result supports “most DNA entered, and most coat stayed out,” not “no protein entered.”

Heredity followed DNA; the next question is how that molecule could be copied.

## The double helix: constraints before a model

The second film built the pairing rule from matched bars and rails. These are the measurements behind them.

**Base composition asks what must match.** The matched bars come from Erwin Chargaff's laboratory at Columbia University, which around 1950 measured base composition, the percentage of all bases that are A, T, G or C, in DNA from many sources. Composition varied among species, arguing against a simple repeat. Within each sample, A was close to T and G close to C. Pairing requires those equalities only across double-stranded DNA as a whole.

**Fibre diffraction asks what geometry must fit.** At King's College London, Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling, and separately Maurice Wilkins with Alexander Stokes and Herbert Wilson, recorded X-ray patterns from DNA fibres. Franklin and Gosling showed that water content switches fibres between A and B forms. For B form, they measured a 3.4-angstrom spacing and a 34-angstrom repeat, judged the structure probably helical with phosphate outside, and considered two chains probable.

Their 1952 B-form pattern, later called Photograph 51, was shown to James Watson by Wilkins in early 1953. Wilkins, Stokes and Wilson found similar patterns from several sources, including biological ones. Diffraction could constrain repeated geometry, but not reveal base order.

**Model building asks whether one structure can satisfy both.** At Cambridge's Cavendish Laboratory, Watson and Francis Crick built two right-handed chains around one axis. The chains run in opposite directions, like the film's rails: they are **antiparallel**. Phosphates face outward. Bases face inward and pair by hydrogen bonds: adenine with thymine, guanine with cytosine. Their model, Franklin and Gosling's paper and the Wilkins paper appeared together in *Nature* in April 1953.

Two refinements followed. Modern structures show three hydrogen bonds in a G-C pair and two in an A-T pair, but stacking between flat bases contributes greatly to helix stability; see [water and weak bonds](/lessons/water-weak-bonds/). The 1953 model assumed 10 base pairs per turn. Later measurements of B-DNA in solution gave about 10.5.

**Predict.** Two B-form segments have the same number of base pairs, but one has more G-C pairs. Using the dimensions here, is one segment longer?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Length is base pairs times the rise per base pair. Hydrogen-bond counts do not enter, so equal base-pair counts give equal calculated lengths under this model.

### Work from the pairing rules

Assume perfect Watson-Crick pairing in a duplex and percentages over every base in the sample.

**Predict.** A double-stranded DNA sample is 22% A. What percentages are T, G and C?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

T equals A, so T is 22%. That leaves 56% for G plus C. Because G equals C, each is 28%.

</details>

> **A = T, G = C, and A + T + G + C = 100% in double-stranded DNA.**

**Predict.** A viral genome has A 25%, T 33%, G 24% and C 18%. What do the ratios say about strandedness?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The bases total 100%, but A does not equal T and G does not equal C. Under the perfect-pairing assumption, they are not paired across two full strands, so this is single-stranded DNA. Thymine with no uracil supports DNA, but sugar chemistry distinguishes DNA from RNA.

</details>

For helix dimensions, a **base pair**, abbreviated bp, is one paired position across the two strands. A 3,000 bp B-form segment with a rise of 0.34 nm per base pair and about 10.5 bp per turn has:

> **length = 3,000 × 0.34 nm = 1,020 nm, about 1.0 micrometre**
>
> **turns = 3,000 ÷ 10.5 = 285.7, about 286**

The 1953 model's 10 bp per turn would give 300 turns. Rise is per base pair, so do not double it for two strands.

### From complement to copying

The film's unzipped rail is the second half of the one idea: complementary pairing gives each strand a copying rule. To write a partner 5' to 3', pair the bases and then reverse the order, because the strands are antiparallel. In a second *Nature* paper later in 1953, Watson and Crick proposed that the chains separate and each serves as a template for a new partner.

The film's density bands are the 1958 test by Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl. They grew *E. coli* on heavy nitrogen, moved it to light nitrogen and separated DNA by density: all hybrid after one generation, equal hybrid and light after two. [DNA replication](/lessons/dna-replication/) takes the mechanism further.

## Design problem: make the claim no larger than the data

A purified extract from heat-killed S type III cells turns type II-derived R cells into type III. Remaining activity is: untreated 100%, trypsin plus chymotrypsin 100%, RNase 100%, DNase 0%, and heating to 65 C 90%. A critic proposes that a protein below 0.1% is the agent. What do the data show, and what would answer the critic?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Claim:** activity requires intact DNA and survives treatments that digest most protein and RNA. The small heat loss shows relative heat stability but does not identify the molecule.

**Limit:** a detection limit cannot prove that a trace protein is absent, and that protein might resist the proteases used.

**Next tests:** follow activity during purification. If activity per microgram of DNA stays constant while protein falls tenfold or more, a protein contaminant becomes harder to defend. Test dose response, though a co-purifying contaminant could follow DNA. Transfer another trait, such as drug resistance; later work by Rollin Hotchkiss and others did this. A protein account would then need a different trait-specific contaminant for each preparation. Finally, test an independent system, as Hershey and Chase did.

Together, the 1944 and phage results made DNA the strongest candidate in these systems without making either experiment universal by itself.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The 1944 preparations

The active material's nitrogen-to-phosphorus ratio ran from 1.58 to 1.75 across four preparations.

### Chargaff's second rule

Each strand of most double-stranded genomes also comes close to A = T and G = C. This is Chargaff's second rule; pairing does not require it.

### Franklin and the Nobel Prize

Franklin died in 1958. The 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Watson, Crick and Wilkins. The Nobel Prize has almost never been given after death; since 1974 its statutes have barred this, with rare exceptions such as death after announcement.
