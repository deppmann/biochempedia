summary: >-
  A small set of transmitters, above all glutamate and GABA, carries most fast synaptic
  signaling in the brain, and every transmitter goes through three steps: it is released, read
  by receptors, and removed. One idea carries the lesson: classify a drug by the main step it
  changes, and size its effect by how much of its target it fills. A drug that competes with
  the natural molecule makes it need 1 + L/K times its usual concentration to fill the same
  share of sites. With it you walk glutamate, GABA, glycine, acetylcholine, the amines
  and the peptides through the same three slots, then work out how far a reuptake blocker
  stretches a signal and how far an antagonist shifts a dose-response curve. Afterwards you
  can classify a drug's action, calculate how much of a target it occupies, and say what a
  drug's mechanism does and does not prove about a disease.

  - name: "Otto Loewi"
    contribution: >-
      Showed with isolated frog hearts that stimulating the vagus nerve released a substance that
      could slow a second heart, evidence that nerves act on their targets by releasing a chemical.
      He shared the 1936 Nobel Prize with Henry Dale.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Loewi, Pflügers Archiv 189:239–242 (1921); the 1936 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine record for Dale and Loewi at nobelprize.org."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In the early twentieth century physiologists argued about how a nerve acts on the organ it
      supplies. One camp held that the impulse passes as electricity. The other held that the nerve
      ending releases a chemical. Loewi tested it with isolated frog hearts kept beating in a salt
      solution. He stimulated the vagus nerve of one heart, which slows its beat, and then moved
      fluid that had bathed that heart onto a second heart. The second heart slowed as well,
      although its own vagus had not been touched. He reported the experiment in Pflügers Archiv
      in 1921 and called the active agent Vagusstoff, the vagus substance. Later work, much of it
      in Henry Dale's laboratory in London, identified acetylcholine as a transmitter released by
      nerves at autonomic ganglia and at the junction between nerve and skeletal muscle. Loewi's
      own later account of how the idea came to him is often retold and is best treated as his
      recollection. Dale and Loewi shared the 1936 Nobel Prize. Their work made chemical
      transmission, and so drugs that act on it, a subject for experiment.
  - name: "Vladimir Derkach"
    contribution: >-
      With Annmarie Surprenant and Alan North, recorded serotonin-gated single-channel currents in
      membrane patches from guinea-pig enteric neurons (1989), showing that the 5-HT3 receptor
      opens an ion channel directly.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Derkach, Surprenant & North, Nature 339:706–709 (1989)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the late 1980s most known serotonin receptors acted through G proteins, slowly and through
      chemical messengers inside the cell. One receptor type, called 5-HT3, produced fast
      responses, and the question was whether it was itself a channel. Derkach, Surprenant and North
      answered it with outside-out patches: small pieces of membrane pulled off a neuron so that the
      outer face meets the bath and the inner face meets an artificial pipette solution. They used
      neurons of the submucous plexus, a nerve network in the wall of the guinea-pig gut. Serotonin
      applied to such patches evoked unitary currents, the square steps of single channels opening,
      with apparent conductances of 15 and 9 pS. In the same experiments acetylcholine activated
      mainly a 40-pS channel. A response in an excised patch, without the cell's cytoplasm, pointed
      to a receptor that is its own channel. The result placed 5-HT3 among the ligand-gated
      channels, alongside the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, and showed that an amine is not
      always a slow signal.
  - name: "Jonathan Coleman"
    contribution: >-
      With Evan Green and Eric Gouaux at the Vollum Institute, reported X-ray structures of the
      human serotonin transporter bound to paroxetine or S-citalopram (2016), showing that the
      drugs sit in the central site where serotonin binds.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Coleman, Green & Gouaux, Nature 532:334–339 (2016); PDB entry 5I6X."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors were in clinical use for decades before anyone could
      see where they bind on their target, the serotonin transporter, SERT. Membrane transporters
      are hard to crystallize because they are flexible and unstable out of the membrane. Coleman,
      Green and Gouaux, working at the Vollum Institute of Oregon Health and Science University,
      engineered thermostabilized versions of human SERT, expressed them in HEK293 cells, and
      crystallized them with an antibody fragment. They reported structures bound to paroxetine or
      S-citalopram at about 3 Å resolution in 2016. Both drugs occupy the central binding site, so
      they block serotonin from binding there. The engineering had a cost the authors measured: the
      crystallized construct, ts3, had no detectable serotonin transport. They also reported
      binding to wild-type human SERT in HEK293 membranes, where paroxetine bound with a KD of 0.08
      nM. In the S-citalopram crystals a second drug molecule also sat in an allosteric site in the outer vestibule, where it slows the drug's escape from the central site. The structures turned the action of a widely used class of drugs into a picture of a drug
      in a pocket, which can be tested by mutating the residues that line it.
  - name: "Pedro Delgado"
    contribution: >-
      With Dennis Charney, Lawrence Price, George Aghajanian, George Heninger and colleagues, showed
      in a double-blind crossover study (1990) that depleting tryptophan made 14 of 21 patients
      whose depression had remitted on antidepressants relapse.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Delgado, Charney, Price, Aghajanian, Landis & Heninger, Archives of General Psychiatry 47:411–418 (1990)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Neurons make serotonin from the amino acid tryptophan, which they must take up from the
      blood. If you lower blood tryptophan for a day, brain serotonin synthesis is expected to fall.
      Delgado and colleagues used this to ask a pointed question: does a patient's
      improvement on an antidepressant depend on serotonin being available? They studied 21
      patients whose depression had remitted during antidepressant treatment. In a double-blind,
      placebo-controlled crossover design, each patient received a tryptophan-free amino-acid
      challenge on one occasion and a control challenge on another, without patient or rater
      knowing which. Fourteen of the 21 relapsed after the tryptophan-free challenge, and they returned to remission over 24 to 48 hours on normal food. The control
      challenge had no significant behavioural effect. The study did not test people who had never
      been depressed, or untreated patients, so it could not show what causes depression in the
      first place. What it gave the field was a reversible, blinded way to ask whether a treatment
      response depends on a particular transmitter.

techniques:
  - name: "Outside-out patch clamp"
    blurb: >-
      A pipette sealed onto a neuron is pulled away so that a small patch of membrane closes over
      its tip with the outer face toward the bath. You apply a transmitter to the bath and record
      single-channel currents. A response that survives without the cytoplasm points to a receptor
      that gates its own channel.
  - name: "Radioligand binding"
    blurb: >-
      Membranes carrying a target, such as SERT expressed in HEK293 cells, are incubated with a
      radioactive drug at a range of concentrations, and bound drug is separated and counted. A fit
      to a binding model gives the dissociation constant, KD. It measures affinity, not what the
      drug does once bound.
  - name: "X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Purified receptors or transporters are imaged with a drug bound, and the map shows which
      residues line the drug's pocket. Each structure is one state, so it must be paired with
      functional recordings before you say whether binding opens, blocks or modulates.
  - name: "Blinded crossover depletion challenge"
    blurb: >-
      Each participant receives a challenge that lowers a transmitter's precursor on one occasion
      and a matched control on another, with neither participant nor rater knowing which. Each
      person serves as their own control, and the result applies to the population that was
      enrolled.

    choices:

      - "5-HT3"
      - "5-HT2A"
      - "5-HT4"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The 5-HT3 receptor is a ligand-gated cation channel, shown directly by serotonin-gated
      single-channel currents in excised patches from guinea-pig enteric neurons. The other
      serotonin receptor families, including 5-HT1A, 5-HT2A and 5-HT4, are G-protein-coupled
      receptors. A student who assumes every amine receptor is metabotropic would miss that 5-HT3
      is the exception, and one who assumes every serotonin receptor is a channel would pick any of
      the GPCRs.
  - stem: "Which pairing of a transmitter with the main way its synaptic action ends is correct?"
    choices:
      - "Neuropeptide Y: reuptake by a monoamine carrier"
      - "Acetylcholine: hydrolysis by acetylcholinesterase"
      - "Serotonin: cleavage by extracellular peptidases"
      - "Glutamate: hydrolysis by an enzyme in the cleft"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses acetylcholine at many cholinergic synapses, notably the
      neuromuscular junction. Glutamate is removed by high-affinity transporters in glia and
      presynaptic terminals, not by an enzyme in the cleft. Serotonin is cleared mainly by uptake
      through SERT; peptidases act on peptides. Neuropeptides such as neuropeptide Y are broken
      down by peptidases, not taken back by a monoamine transporter.
  - stem: "Diazepam increases the current a GABA-A receptor passes in response to GABA. How is it best classified?"
    choices:
      - "A positive allosteric modulator at its own site"
      - "A full agonist that binds at the GABA site"
      - "A competitive antagonist at the GABA site"
      - "A blocker of GABA uptake by its transporters"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Diazepam binds a benzodiazepine site separate from the GABA-binding sites, shown in
      structures of the human α1β3γ2L receptor, and increases the response to GABA while opening
      few or no channels by itself: positive allosteric modulation. A full agonist at the GABA site would
      open the channel alone and compete with GABA. A competitive antagonist would reduce the GABA
      response, the opposite effect. A GABA uptake blocker acts on transporters, not on the
      receptor.
  - stem: "A toxin selectively destroys the neurons of the locus coeruleus. Which ascending projection is mainly lost?"
    choices:
      - "Serotonin from the raphe nuclei to the forebrain"
      - "Acetylcholine from the basal forebrain to cortex"
      - "Noradrenaline from the pons to the forebrain"
      - "Dopamine from the midbrain to the striatum"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The locus coeruleus, in the pons, supplies the major ascending noradrenergic projection. The
      major dopamine projections come from the substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area of the

  - stem: "Paroxetine binds wild-type human SERT with a KD of 0.08 nM. Assuming one-site equilibrium binding and no depletion of free drug, what fraction of transporters is occupied at 0.24 nM free paroxetine?"
    choices:
      - "67%"
      - "25%"
      - "90%"
      - "75%"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Fractional occupancy is f = L/(KD + L) = 0.24/(0.08 + 0.24) = 0.24/0.32 = 0.75. The value 25%
      is the unoccupied fraction, KD/(KD + L). The value 67% comes from dividing 0.16 by 0.24 or
      otherwise mixing up the terms. The value 90% needs 0.72 nM, three times more drug than given.
      Occupancy of a transporter is not the same as a change in synaptic serotonin or a clinical
      effect.
  - stem: "In the same model, free paroxetine is at 0.72 nM. Suppose SERT is the only route out for serotonin, works far below saturation, and its block follows the binding KD. About how many times longer does serotonin's clearance take?"
    choices:
      - "About 9 times"
      - "About 1.1 times"
      - "About 10 times"
      - "About 100 times"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Only free transporters clear serotonin, and the free share is 1 − f = KD/(KD + L), so
      clearance takes 1 + L/KD = 1 + 0.72/0.08 = 10 times as long. The value 9 is L/KD alone,
      which leaves out the transporters that were working before any drug. The value 1.1 is 1/f,
      the inverse of the occupied share rather than the free share. About 100 times needs 7.92 nM,
      which fills 99% of the sites. Any other route out of the space would make the real stretch
      smaller.

Strychnine, which blocks inhibitory glycine receptors, does not prevent the enlargement. In outside-out patches, glycine increases how often NMDA-activated channels open, without changing their unitary current. Which conclusion do these results best support?"
    choices:
      - "Glycine slows the removal of NMDA from the synapse"
      - "Glycine raises the unitary conductance of NMDA channels"
      - "Glycine acts at a site on or near the NMDA receptor"
      - "Glycine opens strychnine-sensitive chloride channels"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The effect persists with strychnine and appears in excised patches as more frequent NMDA
      channel openings, so glycine acts on the NMDA receptor complex itself, at a co-agonist site.
      Strychnine-sensitive chloride channels would be blocked by strychnine and would add a
      separate chloride current, not more frequent NMDA channel openings. NMDA is applied in the bath, and an
      excised patch has no synapse whose clearance could change. The unitary current did not
      change, so conductance did not rise; opening frequency did.
  - stem: "At a recombinant GABA-A receptor, GABA gives half its maximal current at 10 µM. With drug X present at 2 µM, the curve shifts right in parallel and half-maximal current needs 30 µM GABA. With X at 8 µM, it needs 90 µM GABA. The maximum current is unchanged at both concentrations. Which KB and mechanism best fit these results?"
    choices:
      - "KB = 3 µM; X lowers the maximal GABA response"
      - "KB = 2 µM; X competes with GABA at its site"
      - "KB = 1 µM; X binds a separate allosteric site"
      - "KB = 1 µM; X competes with GABA at its site"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      A parallel right shift with the same maximum is what simple competitive antagonism predicts, as
      bicuculline shows at GABA-A receptors, though one shift alone does not prove the mechanism.
      The dose ratios are 30/10 = 3 and 90/10 = 9. For a competitive antagonist dose ratio = 1 +
      [B]/KB, so 3 = 1 + 2/KB and 9 = 1 + 8/KB, and both give KB = 1 µM. Four times as much X
      raises the dose ratio minus 1 fourfold, from 2 to 8, as competition predicts. A negative
      modulator at a separate allosteric site with modest cooperativity would shift the curve less
      than 9-fold at 8 µM, because its effect levels off as its own sites fill. A modulator strong
      enough to mimic competition over this range cannot be ruled out by two concentrations, so
      competition is the best simple account here, not proof of a shared site. Taking KB as the antagonist
      concentration (2 µM) ignores the dose-ratio relation. Lowering the maximal GABA response does
      not fit, because the maximum did not fall, and 3 is the dose ratio, a pure number, not KB.
  - stem: "Mice lacking the dopamine transporter (DAT) from conception were compared with control mice. Which conclusion do these results best support?"
    choices:
      - "Lifelong DAT loss also changed dopamine storage and release"
      - "DAT plays no part in clearing dopamine from the space"
      - "Acute DAT block by a drug must give the same three changes"

      The 300-fold longer lifetime shows that DAT normally sets how long extracellular dopamine

**In 1921 Otto Loewi reported an experiment with two isolated frog hearts. He stimulated the vagus nerve of one, which slows its beat, and then moved fluid that had bathed that heart onto the second. The second heart slowed too, although its own vagus had not been touched.**

The nerve had acted by releasing a chemical, later identified as acetylcholine. If a nerve's message is a chemical, another chemical can imitate it, block it or make it last. That is why so many drugs act at synapses, and it raises three puzzles.

## A chemical message and three puzzles

1. **Opposite effects.** Four drugs that act on GABA, the brain's main fast inhibitory transmitter, push inhibition in opposite directions. How can one transmitter be pushed both ways?
2. **How much.** A drug that fills nine in ten of its targets looks nearly done. Filling ninety-nine in a hundred takes eleven times the concentration that filled nine in ten. Why would those last few sites matter?
3. **Mechanism and cause.** Drugs that block the serotonin transporter help many people with depression. Does that show that depression is caused by too little serotonin?

One idea answers all three. It needs one term: a drug's **target** is the protein it binds, such as a receptor, a transporter or an enzyme.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Classify a drug by the main step of a transmitter system it changes: release, reading or removal. How much it changes that step depends on how much of its target it fills. For a drug that competes with the natural molecule for its site, the size has a simple form, worked out below: the natural molecule needs **1 + L/K** times its usual concentration to fill the same share of sites, where L is the free drug concentration and K is the drug's dissociation constant. What that does to a disease is a separate question, which the mechanism alone cannot answer.

## The idea: three steps, and a drug changes mainly one

Follow a transmitter's signal across a typical synapse. It has three steps, in order.

1. **It is released.** A nerve terminal stores the transmitter in vesicles and releases it, mostly when a spike arrives; [synaptic transmission](/lessons/synaptic-transmission/) covers how. Small transmitters are usually made in the terminal, peptides are made in the cell body and shipped out, and some terminals take transmitter up instead. The neurons that release a given transmitter are its **source**.
2. **It is read.** Across the cleft, the transmitter binds a **receptor**, a protein that the transmitter activates and that then changes the target cell. Receptors come in two kinds.
   - An **ionotropic** receptor is itself an ion channel. Binding opens it, and current flows within milliseconds.
   - A **metabotropic** receptor is a G-protein-coupled receptor, or GPCR. It has no pore of its own. It starts a chain of messengers inside the cell, so its effects take tens of milliseconds to minutes.
3. **It is removed.** Each signal has to be cut short, so that the next change can be told apart. Something clears the transmitter: an **enzyme** that breaks it down, a **transporter**, a membrane carrier that pulls it back into a cell, or, for peptides, **peptidases**. This is the **off switch**.

Source, readers, off switch: together they make a **transmitter system**. Every transmitter in this lesson fills the same three slots. Only the contents differ. [The receptor field guide](/lessons/receptor-field-guide/) and [GPCRs and G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/) survey the receptor classes.

A lock and key helps you hold the steps. The transmitter is a key, the receptor is a lock it turns, and a cleaner sweeps used keys away. A drug can be:

- a copy of the key that also turns the lock: an **agonist**;
- a key that fits but will not turn, so the real key cannot get in: a **competitive antagonist**;
- oil in the lock, so the real key turns more easily: a **positive allosteric modulator** (allosteric means acting from another site);
- a way of stopping the cleaner, so keys pile up and keep turning locks: a **reuptake blocker** or an **enzyme inhibitor**.

The analogy breaks down in one place. Many real receptors need two transmitter molecules bound before they open, and a modulator's site can sit far from the transmitter's own site. The four roles still hold.

**Predict.** Here are four drugs that act on the GABA system:

- Baclofen activates GABA-B receptors.
- Bicuculline sits in the GABA site of GABA-A receptors without opening them.
- Diazepam binds a different site on GABA-A receptors and strengthens GABA's effect.
- Tiagabine blocks GAT1, a transporter that clears GABA.

Which of the four make inhibition stronger, and which make it weaker?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Picture the cell that receives GABA at a mature synapse, where GABA inhibits. There, three make inhibition stronger. Baclofen fakes the signal at one reader, diazepam strengthens it at another, and tiagabine keeps GABA around longer. Bicuculline weakens inhibition, and in animals it causes seizures. It is the same transmitter each time, with opposite effects. What decides the effect is which step a drug changes, and in which direction. Circuits can differ: baclofen also acts on GABA-B receptors on terminals, where it can cut GABA release.

</details>

## The reader decides the sign

A transmitter is not excitatory or inhibitory by nature. The receptor and the ion gradient decide.

Use the rule from [the action potential](/lessons/action-potential/): an open channel pulls the membrane potential, Vm, toward that ion's equilibrium potential, the voltage at which the ion has no net push in or out. GABA-A channels pass mainly chloride, with some bicarbonate, so they pull Vm toward a voltage near chloride's equilibrium potential, E_Cl. This lesson treats them as chloride channels.

Picture a number line with rest at −70 mV and threshold at −50 mV. Where E_Cl sits on it sets what GABA does.

- **E_Cl below Vm.** Opening the channels pulls Vm down, away from threshold.
- **E_Cl between rest and threshold**, say −60 mV. Opening the channels depolarizes the cell. With the chloride and resting conductances equal and no other input, Vm settles at the balance point, halfway: (−70 + −60)/2 = −65 mV. Yet any excitation that pushes Vm past −60 mV now meets an opposing chloride current, so GABA usually blunts excitation that arrives at the same time, an effect called **shunting**. Whether it helps or hinders a spike still depends on its size, timing and location.

In most mature neurons E_Cl sits near or below rest, which is why GABA usually inhibits.

**Predict.** GABA opens GABA-A channels in two neurons. In the first, E_Cl is −75 mV, below rest. The second is an immature neuron, and suppose its E_Cl is −45 mV, above its threshold. What does GABA do to each?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In the first neuron, the channels pull Vm down toward −75 mV, away from threshold: inhibition. In the second, they pull Vm up toward −45 mV, which lies past threshold, so GABA can excite. Same transmitter, same receptor; the chloride gradient decided the sign. That switch during development is in [CNS synapses](/lessons/cns-synapses/).

</details>

## Walking the systems

Now fill the three slots for each transmitter, and attach one drug to one step. How most of these transmitters are made belongs to [amino acids](/lessons/amino-acids/) in the biochemistry course.

### Glutamate

- **Source.** Glutamate is the brain's main excitatory transmitter. In the textbook account nearly all excitatory neurons in the central nervous system release it, so its source is everywhere rather than one nucleus.
- **Readers.** Three ionotropic receptors (AMPA, kainate and NMDA) and metabotropic glutamate receptors. NMDA receptors open more slowly than the other channels. The NMDA receptor has two kinds of binding site: glutamate sites and separate co-agonist sites. It opens only when both kinds are filled. At normal extracellular magnesium, magnesium ions strongly block its pore at negative membrane potentials; depolarization relieves much of that block and allows a larger current. Glycine or D-serine fills the co-agonist sites. The NMDA receptor's role as a coincidence detector is in [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/).
- **Off switch.** High-affinity transporters in glia and presynaptic terminals clear it from the cleft. There is no glutamate-destroying enzyme in the cleft.

### GABA

- **Source.** GABA is made by local **interneurons**, neurons whose axons stay within a region, and also by projection neurons: cerebellar Purkinje cells and the spiny projection neurons of the striatum are classic examples.
- **Readers.** GABA-A receptors are ligand-gated chloride channels. GABA-B receptors are GPCRs, built from two different subunits, GB1 and GB2.
- **Off switch.** Neurons and glia both take GABA back up through high-affinity transporters.
- **Drugs.** Of the four you met, three act at readers and one at removal. Baclofen is an agonist at GABA-B receptors. Bicuculline is a competitive antagonist at GABA-A receptors. Benzodiazepines such as diazepam are positive allosteric modulators: they bind at an interface of GABA-A subunits separate from the GABA sites. Tiagabine is a reuptake blocker at GAT1.

### Glycine

- **Source and readers.** Glycine carries much of the inhibition in the spinal cord, through strychnine-sensitive chloride channels.
- **Off switch.** Transporters remove it.
- **One drug.** Strychnine blocks these inhibitory glycine receptors.

### Acetylcholine and the amines

Most of these come from a **nucleus**, a cluster of neuron cell bodies inside the brain, whose axons reach large territories. That is why a drug acting on one can change arousal, mood or movement at once. Acetylcholine also comes from neurons that do not fit that pattern, such as striatal interneurons and motor neurons.

| Transmitter | Major source | Receptors | Off switch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acetylcholine | Basal forebrain; laterodorsal and pedunculopontine tegmental nuclei; striatal interneurons; motor neurons | Nicotinic channels; muscarinic GPCRs | Hydrolysis by acetylcholinesterase |
| Dopamine | Substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area (midbrain) | D1-like (D1, D5) and D2-like (D2, D3, D4) GPCRs | Uptake by the dopamine transporter, DAT |
| Noradrenaline | Locus coeruleus (pons) | α1, α2 and β GPCRs | Uptake by the noradrenaline transporter, NET |
| Serotonin | Dorsal and median raphe nuclei (brainstem) | GPCRs, plus the 5-HT3 channel | Uptake by the serotonin transporter, SERT |

The sources are the major ones, not the only ones, taken from review syntheses drawn largely from rodent anatomy. Note the exception among the receptors: the 5-HT3 receptor is a ligand-gated channel.

**Acetylcholine** is the one in the table ended by an enzyme: acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses it at many cholinergic synapses, most famously the neuromuscular junction. **One drug:** donepezil is an enzyme inhibitor that slows that breakdown. With the enzyme slowed, acetylcholine stays in the cleft longer and keeps reaching receptors, so each release acts for longer. In patients with probable Alzheimer disease, PET showed donepezil reducing cortical acetylcholinesterase activity: a measure of mechanism, not of clinical benefit.

**The amines** are ended by transporters that use the sodium gradient to carry them back into the terminal; SERT's uptake depends on sodium and chloride. In prefrontal cortex, dopamine is also taken up by NET. **One drug:** paroxetine is a reuptake blocker. It and S-citalopram sit in the central site of SERT, where serotonin binds; the structure on this page shows paroxetine there.

Dopamine deserves one more caution, since you will meet it again in [motivation and reward](/lessons/motivation-and-reward/) and [the basal ganglia](/lessons/basal-ganglia/). Popular accounts reduce it to a signal for pleasure. Recordings and manipulations in animals tie its signals instead to reward prediction, movement and motivation, among other roles, and those lessons give the evidence.

### Peptides and co-transmission

- **Source.** **Neuropeptides** are short chains of amino acids packaged in large dense-core vesicles. Neuropeptide Y, for example, is made by neurons spread throughout the brain.
- **Readers.** Most neuropeptides act at GPCRs.
- **Off switch.** Peptidases usually cut them into inactive fragments (some fragments stay active), so reuptake is not their main off switch as it is for the amines.

A neuron can release more than one transmitter. In adult mouse slices, some dopamine neurons of the substantia nigra also inhibit striatal projection neurons by releasing GABA, and Melani and Tritsch (2022) found that this GABA depends on uptake through the transporter Gat1 (see the anecdote "Where does a dopamine neuron get its GABA?"). So "a dopamine neuron" names a cell's main transmitter, not its only one, and a drug aimed at one transmitter of a neuron may leave its partner untouched.

## How much: occupancy and the factor 1 + L/K

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The quantitative side of binding is in [binding and dose-response](/lessons/binding-and-dose-response/). Here is the version you need, starting with three pictures.

1. **The occupancy curve.** Drug concentration runs along the bottom on a log scale, and the share of targets filled runs up the side. The curve rises steeply through the middle and flattens near the top.
2. **A signal that lingers.** Serotonin in the space around a synapse is plotted against time after one release. It falls as transporters clear it. Block more of the transporters and the fall stretches out.
3. **Two drugs at a receptor.** GABA concentration runs along the bottom on a log scale, and current up the side. The control curve rises from no current to a maximum. With bicuculline, the same curve slides to the right and still reaches the same top. With diazepam, it slides to the left.

**Predict.** Look at the first two pictures. If the transporter is the only route out, does going from 90% to 99% occupancy stretch the serotonin signal a little or a lot?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A lot. Each transporter still working clears serotonin, so blocking most of the few that remain cuts clearance far more than 9 points of occupancy suggest. Step 4 below puts numbers on it.

</details>

**Worked problem: how much transporter does a drug occupy, and what does that do?** Coleman, Green and Gouaux (2016) reported paroxetine binding to wild-type human SERT in HEK293 cell membranes and reported a dissociation constant, KD, of 0.08 nM. The **dissociation constant** is the free drug concentration at which half the binding sites are filled. Assume one class of site, equilibrium, and so little binding that free drug equals added drug.

> **f = L / (KD + L)**

Here f is the fraction of transporters occupied and L the free drug concentration.

*Step 1.* At L = 0.08 nM: f = 0.08 / (0.08 + 0.08) = 0.50. Half the transporters are occupied.

*Step 2.* What L gives 90%? Rearranging, L = f × KD / (1 − f) = 0.90 × 0.08 nM / 0.10 = 0.72 nM, nine times KD. Check: 0.72 / (0.08 + 0.72) = 0.72 / 0.80 = 0.90.

*Step 3.* For 99%: L = 0.99 × 0.08 nM / 0.01 = 7.92 nM. That is 11 times the concentration needed for 90%, all to fill the last 9% of sites.

*Step 4.* What does blocking do to how long serotonin lingers? Build a toy model. Suppose the transporter is the only route out and works far below saturation, so serotonin is too scarce to keep the free carriers busy. Suppose too that paroxetine binding stays at equilibrium, a filled transporter cannot transport, and the KD measured in membranes also describes the block. Then only the free transporters clear serotonin, and the free share is 1 − f = KD / (KD + L). Uptake runs slower, and serotonin lingers longer, by the factor

> **1 / (1 − f) = (KD + L) / KD = 1 + L / KD**

At 0.08 nM, half occupancy, it is 1 + 0.08/0.08 = 2: the signal lasts twice as long. At 0.72 nM it is 1 + 0.72/0.08 = 10. At 7.92 nM it is 1 + 7.92/0.08 = 100. So the costly last 9% of sites stretch the signal from ten times to a hundred times its normal length. In this model, the flat top of the occupancy curve is where the action is for a blocker.

This is the factor 1 + L/K from the one idea.

*Step 5.* The same factor appears at a receptor. Contrast two drugs, using made-up round numbers. Suppose GABA gives half its maximal current at a GABA-A receptor at 10 µM. That concentration is its **EC50**. A competitive antagonist with a dissociation constant KB = 1 µM, present at [B] = 3 µM, shifts the curve right by the **dose ratio**:

> **dose ratio = 1 + [B] / KB = 1 + 3/1 = 4**

Why that factor? The antagonist makes each GABA site 1 + [B]/KB times harder to fill, so GABA must be that many times more concentrated to fill the same share. It is the transporter factor again: on its own, this antagonist would fill 3 / (1 + 3) = 75% of the sites, and 1 / (1 − 0.75) = 4.

The apparent EC50 becomes 40 µM, and enough GABA still reaches the same maximum. A benzodiazepine does the opposite kind of thing: it shifts the GABA curve to the left and opens few or no channels by itself.

A key to the constants: KD is any drug's dissociation constant; KB names it for an antagonist at a receptor, and Ki for an inhibitor at a transporter or enzyme. Km is different: the serotonin concentration at which a transporter runs at half its maximum.

**Predict.** Keep adding bicuculline, then, in a separate experiment, keep adding diazepam. What happens to each curve?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The bicuculline curve keeps sliding right, by 1 + [B]/KB, for as long as you add more. The diazepam curve stops moving once diazepam's own sites are full: a positive allosteric modulator has a ceiling set by its own saturation.

</details>

**Where the factor holds.** It describes the immediate action of a drug at one target, at equilibrium, when the drug competes for the natural molecule's site: a competitive antagonist at a receptor, or a competitive inhibitor at a transporter or enzyme. At a receptor it gives the dose ratio. At the off switch it gives the slowing of uptake only if the carrier works far below saturation, and the stretch of the whole signal only if the blocked route is the only way out and the drug's occupancy stays fixed. The numbers come from a model and from SERT in cell membranes: they are not a measured change in synaptic serotonin and not a dose for a person. The factor does not predict:

- what an **agonist** does, because a cell with **spare receptors**, more than it needs for a full response, can respond strongly with few of them filled;
- the **ceiling** of an allosteric modulator;
- **chronic adaptation**, which the anecdote about the dopamine transporter knockout shows;
- drugs that hit several targets, or **releasers** such as amphetamine, which change both removal and release;
- clinical benefit or the cause of a disease.

## A mechanism is not a cause

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

SSRIs, the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors such as paroxetine, block SERT; that is established, down to a structure of the drug in its pocket. The step from there to "depression is caused by low serotonin" is a separate claim. One blinded experiment shows how to keep the two apart.

### Design problem

Neurons make serotonin from the amino acid tryptophan, which they take up from the blood. Delgado and colleagues (1990) gave 21 patients whose depression had remitted on antidepressants a tryptophan-free amino-acid challenge, which lowers the precursor of serotonin, and a control challenge, double-blind and in crossover: neither patient nor rater knew which was which. Fourteen of the 21 relapsed after tryptophan depletion and returned to remission over 24 to 48 hours on normal food; the control challenge had no significant effect. Does this show that low serotonin causes depression? If not, what would you need to test that claim?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

It does not. The patients were selected because they had responded to antidepressants, most of which act on serotonin or noradrenaline systems. The result suggests that remission in some treated patients depends on serotonin being available. A treatment can depend on a transmitter that was never low to begin with.

To test whether low serotonin causes depression, you would study people who are not on these drugs. Deplete tryptophan, with a balanced control drink that contains tryptophan, in people who have never been depressed and in untreated people with depression, double-blind and in crossover. If this acute drop in serotonin synthesis were enough by itself to lower mood in the people tested, over the hours observed, mood should fall relative to the control session. Analyse the two groups separately: further worsening in people already depressed is not guaranteed. A null result would not rule out a chronic or vulnerability-dependent role for serotonin. Depletion studies in such groups have been pooled. A 2007 meta-analysis found no significant mood drop in healthy volunteers without a family history of depression, a slight drop in healthy volunteers with one, and a moderate drop in drug-free people who had recovered from depression, so the answer depends on who is vulnerable. Check that blood tryptophan actually fell, and report how many participants changed, not only group means.

Then weigh the result against the wider evidence below, keeping apart whether a drug helps and what caused the illness.

</details>

### The wider evidence

A 2022 umbrella review by Moncrieff and colleagues found no consistent support for depression being caused by lowered serotonin concentration or activity. Other researchers dispute its methods and argue the serotonin system is still implicated. A separate question is whether the drugs help: a 2018 network meta-analysis by Cipriani and colleagues found all 21 antidepressants it included more effective than placebo for acute major depression in adults, with certainty of evidence from moderate to very low.

Both findings can be true at once. Aspirin relieves a headache, and headaches are not caused by a lack of aspirin. The comparison has a limit, though: Delgado's result suggests that serotonin may be part of how the drug works in some treated patients. It still does not show that serotonin was low before treatment.

## Going deeper

### Telling a competitor from a modulator

The dose ratio 1 + [B]/KB is the Schild relation. It holds at equilibrium for an antagonist that competes for the agonist's site, even when several agonist molecules must bind. So the dose ratio minus 1 grows in direct proportion to the antagonist's concentration. A negative allosteric modulator, which lowers the agonist's affinity from a separate site, can mimic one shift. In a model check (an affinity-only model with a cooperativity factor of 0.05), such a modulator gives a 2.73-fold shift where a competitor at the same [B]/KB = 2 gives 3-fold. At [B]/KB = 8 it gives about 6.4-fold against the competitor's 9-fold, because its effect levels off as its own sites fill, in this model at 20-fold. One concentration cannot tell the two apart. Several can help, and binding or structural experiments add evidence on where the drug sits.

### Where the factor 1 + L/K stops working

These are model calculations, made to show the scope conditions, not measurements.

- **Other routes out.** In a well-mixed model with fixed parallel first-order clearance, suppose diffusion and other drug-insensitive routes supply 5% of the baseline clearance rate constant. Then blocking the transporter can lengthen serotonin's decay time constant at most 1/0.05 = 20-fold, however much drug you add: at 50%, 90% and 99% block the stretch is about 1.9, 6.9 and 16.8 times, against 2, 10 and 100 with no other route. The bound need not hold if clearance is nonlinear or changes across space.
- **A busy carrier.** Take a competitive inhibitor at nine times its Ki and compare uptake at the same serotonin concentration. With serotonin far below the transporter's Km, uptake runs about 10 times slower. With serotonin at Km, it runs only about 5.5 times slower. During a falling serotonin transient the ratio changes as serotonin falls.
- **Spare receptors.** In the operational model with a transducer ratio of 10, filling 10% of the receptors gives half the largest response the system can give; with a ratio of 100, about 1% does.

### How the 5-HT3 receptor was shown to be a channel

In 1989 Derkach, Surprenant and North recorded single-channel currents opened by serotonin in outside-out patches from guinea-pig enteric neurons, in the submucous plexus of the gut wall. The channels had apparent conductances of 15 and 9 pS. A response in an excised patch, without the cell's cytoplasm, showed that the 5-HT3 receptor is a ligand-gated channel.

### Glycine's second job, and D-serine

In cultured mouse brain neurons, Johnson and Ascher found that glycine potentiated NMDA receptor responses by a mechanism separate from the inhibitory glycine receptor (see the anecdote "Glycine has a second job"). Whether glycine or D-serine fills the NMDA receptor's co-agonist site depends on the synapse and the stage of development.
