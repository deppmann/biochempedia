  - name: "Jonathan Raper"
    contribution: >-
      Built a simple assay in which growth cones collapse when exposed to brain membranes, and
      with Yuling Luo and David Raible purified and cloned the protein responsible from chick
      brain, collapsin, the first vertebrate semaphorin.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Raper & Kapfhammer, Neuron 4:21 (1990); Luo, Raible & Raper, Cell 75:217 (1993)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Raper's route to a repellent began with a question about behaviour: what makes a moving
      growth cone stop? At the University of Pennsylvania he and Josef Kapfhammer grew chick
      sensory neurons from dorsal root ganglia on laminin and added a suspension of embryonic
      brain membranes. Within minutes the flattened growth cones folded up, and the effect
      reversed when the membranes were removed. Membranes from liver or fibroblasts did not do
      this. The activity was destroyed by heat and by trypsin, so it was a protein, and in 1990
      they reported enriching it about a hundredfold. With an assay that simple, purification
      became possible. In 1993 Yuling Luo, David Raible and Raper reported a glycoprotein of
      about 100 kilodaltons from chick brain that collapsed sensory growth cones at roughly 10
      picomolar but left retinal growth cones alone. They named it collapsin. Half of its
      sequence resembled fasciclin IV, a guidance protein from the grasshopper, which tied a
      vertebrate repellent to an insect one and opened the way to a gene family.

    profileSourceAnchor: "Kolodkin et al., Neuron 9:831 (1992); Kolodkin, Matthes & Goodman, Cell 75:1389 (1993); Kolodkin et al., Cell 90:753 (1997)."

      Working in Corey Goodman's laboratory at Berkeley, Kolodkin and colleagues used a
      monoclonal antibody to find and clone fasciclin IV in the grasshopper. It sits on bands of
      epithelial cells in the developing limb bud, and one band lies where the growth cones of
      the Ti1 pioneer neurons make a sharp turn toward the nervous system. Embryos cultured in the
      antibody built that pathway abnormally. When the gene was first sequenced it matched nothing

      and others, he tested Semaphorin III as a selective repellent in the spinal cord. In 1997,
      with David Ginty and colleagues, his group showed that Semaphorin III binds neuropilin with
      high affinity. That gave the family its

techniques:
  - name: "Growth-cone collapse assay"
    blurb: >-
      Cultured neurons with spread growth cones are exposed to a membrane extract or a purified
      protein for a set time, then fixed, and the fraction of growth cones that have folded into
      thin tips is counted. Because it is fast and graded, it can follow an activity through each
      step of purification.
  - name: "Explant co-culture in collagen"
    blurb: >-
      A piece of embryonic tissue, such as a dorsal root ganglion, is set in a three-dimensional
      collagen gel a short distance from another tissue or from cells secreting a candidate cue.
      Axons that grow less on the near side than the far side are read as repelled; axons that grow more
      on the near side, as attracted. Turning assays help separate steering from simple effects on growth.
  - name: "Expression cloning and ligand binding"
    blurb: >-
      The candidate ligand is fused to an enzyme tag such as alkaline phosphatase and applied to
      cells transfected with pools of cDNA. Cells that turn color carry a protein that binds the ligand, and
      repeated subdivision of the positive pool isolates the gene.
  - name: "Growth-cone turning assay"
    blurb: >-
      A micropipette releases puffs of a cue to one side of a single growth cone of a cultured
      neuron, setting up a small gradient. The angle the axon turns, toward or away, is measured,
      and drugs or receptor mutants show what the turn depends on.

      - "It keeps NGF-dependent axons out of the ventral horn by repelling them"
      - "It attracts NT-3-dependent axons into the ventral horn"
      - "It kills the NGF-dependent neurons that reach the ventral cord"
      - "It makes every sensory axon stop at the cord surface"
    answer: 0
    rationale: "Sema3A is made in the ventral cord and repels axons grown in NGF, which come mostly from small TrkA neurons, while axons grown in NT-3 are largely unaffected, so the pain fibres are excluded from the ventral horn. The idea that Sema3A attracts NT-3 axons confuses a lack of response with attraction. Explant experiments show reduced outgrowth, not neuron death, and collapse is reversible. Making every sensory axon stop would also stop the muscle afferents, which do enter the ventral horn."

    rationale: >-
      Plexin carries the signal, so a plexin with no cytoplasmic part leaves binding through
      neuropilin intact and the response gone; truncated plexins act as dominant negatives that block Sema3A

      - "Every class spans the membrane and binds plexin alone"

    rationale: >-
      The class 3 semaphorins, including Sema3A, are secreted, while classes 4, 5 and 6 are

  - stem: "Cultured chick sensory growth cones that Sema3A repels are tested two ways. In one dish a micropipette releases Sema3A to one side of a growth cone. In another, a high dose of Sema3A is added evenly to the whole dish for several minutes. What is the typical outcome in each dish?"
    choices:
      - "Pipette: it collapses; bath: it turns away from the source"
      - "Pipette: it turns away; bath: it collapses"
      - "Pipette: it turns toward the source; bath: it ignores the cue"
      - "Both dishes: it turns away, more sharply in the bath"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A pipette gives one side of the growth cone more cue than the other, so a repelled growth cone
      typically turns away. A bath gives every side the same concentration, so there is nothing to
      steer by; in sensory growth cones a high enough dose typically makes the fan collapse.
      Collapse from a pipette with a turn in the bath reverses the two layouts. A bath has
      no source to turn from, so a sharper turn there is impossible. Turning toward the source is
      what Song and colleagues saw only after raising cGMP, and ignoring the bath would mean no
      response at all.
  - stem: "A Xenopus spinal neuron turns away from a gradient of Sema3A. The same experiment is repeated with a drug that raises cyclic GMP in the growth cone. What is the expected result?"
    choices:
      - "The growth cone turns away from it more sharply"
      - "The growth cone collapses and the axon retracts"
      - "The growth cone turns toward the Sema3A source"
      - "The growth cone ignores the gradient altogether"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Song, Poo and colleagues found that activating the cGMP pathway converted repulsion by Sema3A
      into attraction, so the growth cone turns toward the source. Sharper repulsion is the
      opposite of what raised cGMP did. Ignoring the gradient would mean the response
      was lost, but it was reversed, not lost. Collapse and retraction describe a strong
      repulsive response, which raised cGMP opposed.
  - stem: "In the developing cortex, Sema3A is high near the pial surface. A pyramidal neuron's axon grows away from the surface while its apical dendrite grows toward it. Which difference between the two compartments best explains this?"
    choices:
      - "The dendrite secretes its own supply of Sema3A"
      - "The axon has more soluble guanylate cyclase"
      - "The axon has neuropilin and the dendrite has none"
      - "The dendrite has more soluble guanylate cyclase"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Polleux, Morrow and Ghosh found soluble guanylate cyclase concentrated in the developing
      apical dendrite and needed for its attraction to Sema3A, so higher cGMP there flips the same
      cue into an attractant. An axon with more of the enzyme would not fit the result, since the
      axon is the compartment that is repelled. A dendrite without receptor would ignore the cue,
      not grow toward it. A dendrite making its own Sema3A would not explain a turn toward the
      surface source.

  - level: "highschool"

    text: >-
      A growing nerve fibre ends in a growth cone, a fan-shaped tip that reads signals around it.
      Some signals attract it and some repel it. Semaphorins are a large family of repellents.
      When a growth cone meets one, its fan can fold up within minutes, and the fibre turns away
      or stops. Some semaphorins float free and others are fixed on cell surfaces. The growth cone
      senses the floating kind with two partners: neuropilin grabs the semaphorin, and plexin
      passes the message into the cell. Because only some nerve fibres respond to these signals at a given stage, a
      tissue can keep one kind of fibre out while letting another in. Raising one messenger
      molecule inside the growth cone, cyclic GMP, can even flip the response, so the repellent
      attracts.
  - level: "college"

    text: >-
      Semaphorins share a large sema domain, a seven-bladed beta-propeller. Invertebrates have

**Two kinds of sensory axon enter the spinal cord side by side. Pain and temperature fibres stop in the dorsal horn. Muscle afferents run on into the ventral horn to reach the motor neurons. Both enter at the same place. What sorts them?**

A map needs walls as well as roads. An axon on its way to a target passes many places where it must not go: the wrong side of the spinal cord, the wrong layer, the wrong nerve. Cues whose message is "Don't go there" are repulsive cues, and the semaphorins are one of the largest and best-studied families of them.

## The puzzle in the spinal cord

Sensory neurons in a dorsal root ganglion are of several kinds. Two of them set the puzzle.

| | Pain and temperature neurons | Muscle stretch neurons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cell size | small | large |
| Neurotrophin they depend on | NGF | NT-3 |
| Its receptor on the neuron | TrkA | TrkC |
| Where the axon ends in the cord | dorsal horn | ventral horn, on motor neurons |

The ventral cord was known to release something that inhibits sensory axon growth. That raises three questions.

1. **Why does one tissue keep out one class of axon** and admit another?
2. **How does a growth cone hear a secreted cue** when the protein that binds it has almost no tail inside the cell?
3. **Why can the same cue repel** one part of a neuron and attract another?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** The cue marks the place; the growth cone decides what it means. In the spinal cord, Sema3A is made where pain fibres must not go.
>
> - **Does a growth cone answer at all?** It needs the receptor: neuropilin to catch the cue, plexin to signal. (At the stages tested, pain fibres answer strongly and muscle afferents weakly; the chick retinal growth cones in the first collapsin assay did not collapse.)
> - **Which way does it answer?** That depends on its internal state, such as its cyclic GMP level. (A cortical axon is repelled; the same neuron's dendrite is attracted.)
> - **If it is repelled, does it turn or collapse?** In typical assays, a gradient from one side turns it away, and a high dose all round can fold it up where the preparation supports collapse. Layout, dose, exposure time and the cell all shape which you see. (A pipette versus a bath.)

**What the rule covers.** Secreted class 3 semaphorins, mainly Sema3A, acting through neuropilin-plexin receptors on vertebrate growth cones: sensory axons from dorsal root ganglia in explants and in the spinal cord, Xenopus spinal neurons in turning assays, and cortical pyramidal neurons. Three limits matter.

- "Decides" does not mean the cue carries no information: which semaphorin is made, where and how much all matter, and membrane semaphorins bind plexins directly, with no neuropilin.
- The receiver's state is not fixed: muscle afferents appear to lose their sensitivity as they grow into the ventral cord.
- In the living cord Sema3A shares the job with other cues, so the rule explains selectivity, not the whole map.

## A growth cone that folds up

You met the [growth cone](/lessons/growth-cone/) as both sense organ and motor: a fan of actin-filled filopodia and lamellipodia at the axon's tip. It turns toward the side where actin builds and away from the side where actin falls apart. The [guidance cues lesson](/lessons/guidance-cues-and-pioneers/) sorted cues into four kinds: contact attraction, contact repulsion, long-range attraction and long-range repulsion. Semaphorins work in both repulsive modes. Some are secreted and repel from a distance; others sit in cell membranes and repel on contact. Repellents fence off wrong territories, so an axon growing between them is channelled along its path, though other cues tell it where to stop.

A repellent needs an assay. In 1990 Jonathan Raper and Josef Kapfhammer, at the University of Pennsylvania, grew chick dorsal root ganglion neurons on laminin and added a suspension of embryonic brain membranes. Within minutes the growth cones **collapsed**, folding from a spread fan into a thin tip. The effect reversed when the membranes were gone.

**Predict.** Is this something any membrane would do, and what kind of molecule is doing it? Name two controls that would tell you.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Membranes from other tissues are the first control: membranes from liver or fibroblasts did not cause collapse, so collapse was not a response to any membrane at all. Treatments that destroy proteins are the second: the activity was destroyed by heat and by trypsin, so it was a protein. Counting the fraction of collapsed growth cones gave a fast, graded readout that could follow the activity through each step of purification.

</details>

## Collapsin, the first vertebrate semaphorin

In 1993 Yuling Luo, David Raible and Raper purified the protein from chick brain, a glycoprotein of about 100 kilodaltons, and named it collapsin. Retinal growth cones did not respond to it: the cue was present, but those growth cones did not hear it.

Collapsin's first half resembled fasciclin IV, a guidance protein cloned the year before in the grasshopper by Alex Kolodkin, Corey Goodman and colleagues. So a membrane protein in an insect had a secreted relative in a bird. Later in 1993 Kolodkin, David Matthes and Goodman named the whole family the semaphorins, after the semaphore, a way of signalling with flags. Collapsin is now **Sema3A**, the semaphorin this lesson follows. A naming key: collapsin (chick) = Semaphorin III (the older vertebrate name) = Sema3A; fasciclin IV (grasshopper) = Semaphorin I. The vertebrate classes differ in how the protein is held:

| Vertebrate class | How it is held |
| --- | --- |
| Class 3, including Sema3A | secreted |
| Classes 4 to 6 | span the membrane |
| Class 7 | held by a lipid anchor |

Semaphorins also work far outside the nervous system, in immune cells, heart, blood vessels and bone.

## One tissue, two axons

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Back to the spinal cord, where Sema3A is made in the ventral cord. The test is an explant co-culture: an **explant**, a piece of living tissue, is set in a collagen gel beside a candidate source, and its axons grow out in a ring, the **halo**. Measure the axons on the side facing the source and on the far side. A near-to-far ratio near 1 means outgrowth is symmetric. A ratio well below 1 means the near side grew less, as a diffusible repellent or a local growth inhibitor would cause. Lengths compared with control cells, and turning assays, tell those apart.

**Predict.** You culture a dorsal root ganglion in a collagen gel next to a piece of ventral spinal cord, once in medium with NGF and once in medium with NT-3. The neurotrophin in the medium largely decides which neurons survive and extend axons. Predict the outgrowth in each case.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In NGF, the axons come mostly from small TrkA neurons, the pain and temperature fibres. Expect them to grow poorly on the side facing the ventral cord and well on the far side: an asymmetric halo, the mark of a diffusible repellent. In NT-3, the axons come mostly from large TrkC neurons, the muscle afferents. Expect a nearly symmetric halo.

</details>

That is the result. In 1995 Elizabeth Messersmith, Kolodkin and colleagues, with explants of embryonic rat tissue, showed that the ventral cord's activity repels axons grown in NGF but has little effect on axons grown in NT-3. Cells secreting Semaphorin III did the same, and the protein is made in the ventral cord. The first MCAT question below puts made-up numbers on this kind of result.

So one tissue treats two kinds of axon differently. At this stage the NGF-dependent class responds to Sema3A, through a neuropilin-plexin receptor, and the NT-3-dependent class mostly does not, so only the first is kept out. To test the idea, add an antibody against neuropilin-1 or against Sema3A: the NGF halo should become more symmetric.

The NT-3 result is a snapshot at one stage, and the answer can change with time. In chick, Raper's group found that antibodies against collapsin neutralized the ventral cord's repellent. Their data also suggest that muscle afferents lose their sensitivity as they grow into the ventral cord.

Be clear about what the explants do and do not show. A ventral cord explant tests everything the cord releases. Cells secreting Sema3A test Sema3A alone. Neither shows whether Sema3A is the only cue doing this job in an animal, and it probably is not: mice lacking Sema3A have clearly disorganized peripheral nerves, and some sensory axons enter spinal cord regions they normally avoid, so other cues probably share the work in the living cord.

## Neuropilin catches, plexin signals

Who receives Sema3A? Two groups found the answer independently, in back-to-back papers in the August 1997 issue of Cell: Zhigang He and Marc Tessier-Lavigne by expression cloning (putting pools of cDNA into cells and finding which pool lets them bind the tagged cue), and Alex Kolodkin, David Ginty and colleagues. The protein was **neuropilin**. He and Tessier-Lavigne also showed that antibodies to neuropilin block Semaphorin III repulsion of sensory axons.

Neuropilin presented a puzzle. It bound Sema3A tightly, but its tail inside the cell was only about forty amino acids long, with nothing that looked like an enzyme. A receptor has two jobs: catch the signal and pass it on. Neuropilin plainly does the first. Where was the wire for the second?

In 1999 two groups, Takahashi, Strittmatter and colleagues, and Tamagnone, Comoglio and colleagues, pointed to the **plexins**, a large family of membrane proteins with a substantial part inside the cell. The decisive tests added and removed the two parts. Read each table one row at a time and predict the last column before you look across. First, engineered cells that normally ignore Sema3A:

| Engineered cells carry | Does Sema3A bind? | Does the cell respond? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neither protein | no | no |
| Neuropilin-1 alone | yes | no |
| Plexin-A1 alone | little or none | no |
| Neuropilin-1 and Plexin-A1 together | yes, more tightly | yes: the cells shrink and round up |

Then neurons, which already have their own receptor parts and change one of them:

| Neurons carry | Does Sema3A bind? | Does the cell respond? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A neuropilin lacking its tail inside the cell (other work) | yes | yes: growth cones still collapse |
| Extra Plexin-A1 lacking its part inside the cell (sensory neurons) | expected, through the neurons' own neuropilin | no: the response is blocked |

**Predict.** The last row is the odd one. The neurons still make their own normal plexin. Why would adding a broken plexin stop them responding?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The broken plexin interferes with the cell's own working receptor complexes. One likely explanation is that it joins neuropilin and Sema3A in complexes that cannot pass the signal on; the blocking experiment alone does not settle the exact mechanism. A mutant that blocks the normal protein in this way is called a **dominant negative**. Taken with the row above it, the result splits the jobs: neuropilin's tail is not needed, and the plexin's inner part is. **Neuropilin catches; plexin signals.**

</details>

That is the first part of the lesson's rule: in these experiments, no neuropilin-plexin pair, no response to Sema3A. Having the pair is necessary; it does not by itself guarantee a strong response. Some plexins bind membrane semaphorins directly, while most class 3 semaphorins reach plexins through neuropilin.

What does the plexin's inner part do? It contains a domain that acts as a GAP, a protein that speeds GTP hydrolysis and so switches a small GTPase off (see [GTPase switches](/lessons/gtpase-switches/) and [Ras to MAPK](/lessons/ras-to-mapk/)). The target is a Ras-family GTPase, R-Ras or Rap depending on the report, and both help hold integrin adhesions to the substrate, so switching either off lowers adhesion. Plexin signalling also engages Rho-family GTPases and effectors such as MICAL and CRMP2 that act on actin and microtubules. The net result is local loss of actin and adhesion, the cytoskeletal response described in [integrins and Rho GTPases](/lessons/integrins-and-rho-gtpases/).

### Turn or collapse

**Predict.** Take a growth cone that Sema3A repels. In one dish, a micropipette releases Sema3A to one side of it. In another, Sema3A is added evenly to the whole dish, as in the collapse assay. What does each growth cone do?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

On one side, Sema3A typically loosens that side, and the growth cone turns away. All round, a high enough dose can loosen every side, and in sensory growth cones the fan folds up: collapse. These are typical outcomes in assays that support both, not a switch every growth cone must show; dose, exposure time and the preparation matter as well as layout.

</details>

**Worked problem: what a gradient gives the receptors.** Use simple binding, fraction occupied = c / (c + Kd), with illustrative Kd values: 0.2 nM for the neuropilin-plexin pair and 1.0 nM for neuropilin alone (the second MCAT question uses the same values). Let the concentration fall by 10% of its mean from the near edge of the growth cone to the far edge, an illustrative figure of the order estimated for pipette turning assays.

*Step 1: a gradient centred near Kd.* Near side 0.21 nM, far side 0.19 nM. Occupancy is 0.21 / 0.41 = 0.512 near and 0.19 / 0.39 = 0.487 far. The near side is about 2.5 percentage points more occupied: a signal on one side.

*Step 2: the same 10% gradient at 50 nM.* Near side 52.5 nM, far side 47.5 nM. Occupancy is 0.9962 near and 0.9958 far. The difference is 0.04 percentage points, about sixty times smaller. Almost every receptor is occupied, so even a gradient looks nearly uniform.

*Step 3: a bath.* With the same concentration on every side and identical receptors, there is no near-to-far difference in occupancy at any concentration, so no directional signal.

*Step 4: what the receptor sets.* For a small percentage gradient like this one, the near-to-far difference is greatest when the mean concentration is close to Kd. With neuropilin alone, the difference at 0.2 nM is only about 1.4 percentage points. Affinity sets the concentrations over which occupancy differs most, but neuropilin alone cannot signal, so only the pair can act on a difference.

The sketch describes receptor occupancy, not behaviour: it cannot predict a turn or a collapse, and it ignores amplification, adaptation and averaging over time. Collapsin was active at roughly 10 picomolar in the original assay, and these illustrative Kd values cannot say how many receptors were occupied there. How sensitive a growth cone is depends on binding and on how occupied receptors are coupled to a response.

## Same cue, opposite answers

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Is repulsion a fixed property of Sema3A? In 1998 Hongjun Song, Mu-ming Poo and colleagues put single Xenopus spinal neurons in culture and set up a small Sema3A gradient with a micropipette, a growth-cone turning assay. The growth cones turned away.

**Predict.** Now raise cyclic GMP inside the growth cone and repeat with the same gradient. Does the growth cone turn away more sharply, ignore the gradient, or do something else?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It turns toward Sema3A: with the cyclic GMP pathway activated, the same growth cones were attracted. The experimenters reapplied the same gradient conditions, repositioning the pipette relative to the growth cone's new direction for each test of the same neuron, so the change is in how the growth cone reads the cue.

</details>

The [senses and cGMP lesson](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/) introduced cGMP as a second messenger made by guanylate cyclases. Here its level sets the sign of the answer; a similar switch with cAMP governs netrin, covered in [netrin and DCC](/lessons/netrin-and-dcc/).

Does a living neuron use this? In the developing cortex, Sema3A is high near the pial surface, the brain's outer surface. Franck Polleux and Anirvan Ghosh, with colleagues, had shown that it repels cortical axons downward. In 2000 Polleux, T. Morrow and Ghosh showed it also attracts the apical dendrite of the same pyramidal neurons upward. Soluble guanylate cyclase, an enzyme that makes cGMP, sits mainly in the developing apical dendrite and is needed for that attraction: one cue, one neuron, two answers.

So from the spinal cord to the cortex, the cue marks the place and the growth cone decides what it means: the pain fibre and the muscle afferent differ in how strongly they answer, the axon and the dendrite in which way.

Where next: the same family keeps working after axons arrive. It prunes axon branches, covered in [axon pruning](/lessons/axon-pruning/), and limits dendritic spines, covered in [CNS synapses](/lessons/cns-synapses/).

### Design problem

In the explant experiment, axons grown in NT-3 barely respond to Sema3A. Design experiments to show whether they ignore it for lack of a receptor or for another reason, and not simply because they never meet it.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

First make sure they meet it. Set NT-3 explants beside cells secreting Sema3A, with control cells as the comparison, over a series of distances or doses. NGF-supported explants in the same set-up confirm that the source is active, though not the exact gradient at each NT-3 growth cone. Measure absolute outgrowth and collapse against matched controls as well as near-to-far asymmetry, and use turning assays for direction: a symmetric halo alone cannot tell weak signalling from growth inhibited equally on both sides (600 to 150 micrometres on both sides still gives a ratio of 1) or from too little spatial stimulation.

Next, ask what they carry. Compare how much neuropilin-1 and A-class plexin the NT-3-supported and NGF-supported growth cones have, and where it sits, at the same age. One possibility is that the NT-3 class lacks part of the pair or has less of it. The result could also show that both carry it, which would point instead to a difference in signalling state.

Then test the answer. If a part is missing or low, express it in the NT-3-supported neurons, with controls for the expression itself, and repeat the explant test. If the receptor is the reason, they should now respond: an asymmetric halo, with outgrowth and turning measured as above. As a check on the other side, a dominant-negative plexin in NGF-supported neurons should abolish their response by the same measures.

Two caveats. Sensitivity may change with age, so test at more than one. And the neurotrophin in the medium chooses which neurons grow, and may also change how they respond (see Going deeper), so compare the two classes in the same medium where you can.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Fasciclin IV and the semaphorin family, in detail

Fasciclin IV marks bands of epithelial cells in the grasshopper limb bud, and one band lies where the growth cones of the Ti1 pioneer neurons make a sharp turn. Embryos cultured in an antibody against fasciclin IV built that pathway abnormally. The 1993 paper naming the family described related genes in flies, in humans and in two viruses, all sharing one large region now called the sema domain.

### Two pairs of papers

Neuropilin was first described in the frog nervous system by the laboratory of Hajime Fujisawa. The plexin papers of 1999, by Takahashi, Strittmatter and colleagues and by Tamagnone, Comoglio and colleagues, also appeared together in one issue of Cell.

### Which GTPase does plexin switch off?

In the first case studied, Plexin-B1, which is the receptor for a membrane semaphorin rather than for Sema3A, the reported target is R-Ras. Later work with purified plexins points instead to its relative Rap.

### How the receptor is switched on

Structures of Sema3A with neuropilin-1 and Plexin-A2 show neuropilin bridging the semaphorin dimer and the plexin (see the structures on this page). Signalling proceeds through plexin dimerization or rearrangement, which relieves autoinhibition of a split GAP domain. There are exceptions to the neuropilin route: Sema3E binds Plexin-D1 without neuropilin.

### What the explant contrast mixes together

Beyond choosing which neurons grow, the neurotrophin a sensory neuron sees has been reported to change how sensitive its growth cones are to Sema3A. If so, the NGF and NT-3 explant contrast mixes which neurons grow with how they respond. An open question is how much of the selectivity seen in the dish is set by receptor presence, by the choice of plexin and neuropilin co-receptors, and by internal state such as cGMP, and how these change as afferents mature.
