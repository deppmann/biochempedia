techniques:
  - name: "Radioactive labelling with gamma-32P ATP"
    blurb: >-
      You incubate a kinase and its substrate with ATP whose end phosphate is radioactive, or
      grow cells in radioactive phosphate, then separate the proteins on a gel and expose it to
      film. A dark band shows which protein received phosphate. Only a label in the gamma
      position works in the test tube, because that is the phosphate the kinase transfers.
  - name: "Phosphatase-sensitive mobility shift"
    blurb: >-
      Many phosphorylated proteins run more slowly on an SDS gel. If treating the sample with a
      purified phosphatase, such as calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase, collapses the slow band
      into the fast one, the difference was phosphate. It is quick and needs no radioactivity,
      though some phosphates cause no shift at all.
  - name: "Phosphoamino acid analysis"
    blurb: >-
      You break a 32P-labelled protein down to its amino acids in acid and separate them by
      electrophoresis on a thin-layer plate, beside phosphoserine, phosphothreonine and
      phosphotyrosine standards. The
      position of the radioactive spot tells you which kind of residue carries the phosphate.
      This is the method that revealed phosphotyrosine in 1979.
  - name: "Site-directed mutagenesis: alanine and aspartate substitutions"
    blurb: >-
      You change the suspected residue to alanine, which cannot accept a phosphate, or to
      aspartate, whose negative charge imitates one. Loss of labelling in the alanine mutant maps
      the site, and the behaviour of the aspartate mutant suggests what the phosphate does. A
      carboxylate is only a rough copy of a phosphate, so the result needs a second line of
      evidence.

  - stem: "Which amino acid side chains carry regulatory phosphate groups in animal proteins, and what chemical feature do they share?"
    choices:
      - "Cysteine and methionine; each has a sulfur atom that bonds to phosphorus"
      - "Aspartate and glutamate; each has a negative charge like the phosphate itself"
      - "Lysine, arginine and histidine; each has a positive charge that attracts phosphate"
      - "Serine, threonine and tyrosine; each has a hydroxyl group that can form a phosphate ester"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      A protein kinase transfers phosphate to an oxygen, and in animal signaling the acceptors
      are the hydroxyl groups of serine, threonine and tyrosine. The positively charged residues
      can sit beside a phosphate and attract it, but they are not where these kinases attach it.
      Aspartate and glutamate are used in the laboratory to imitate a phosphate because they are
      already negative; they are the mimic and not the target. The sulfur-containing residues
      form disulfides and thioethers and are not acceptors for protein kinases.
  - stem: "A kinase adds a phosphate to a protein and a phosphatase removes it. Why can the cell regulate these two steps independently?"
    choices:
      - "The phosphate ester is too stable to break, so removal requires destroying the protein"
      - "The phosphatase returns the phosphate to ADP, so the add-and-remove cycle uses no net ATP"
      - "Adding uses ATP and removing uses water, so each is its own reaction with its own enzyme"
      - "The kinase is used up in each reaction, so a second enzyme is needed to regenerate it"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The kinase reaction transfers phosphate from ATP, and the phosphatase reaction hydrolyses the
      ester with water. They are different reactions, both favourable, so each has its own enzyme and
      its own controls. The phosphatase releases inorganic phosphate and does not remake ATP, which is
      why one round trip costs one ATP. The ester is stable when no enzyme is present, but a
      phosphatase removes it cleanly and the protein survives. Kinases are catalysts and are not
      consumed by the reactions they run.

  - stem: "Human cells have several hundred serine/threonine kinases but only a few dozen genes for serine/threonine phosphatase catalytic subunits. What best explains how removal of phosphate from serine and threonine can still be specific?"
    choices:
      - "It is not specific; these phosphatases remove every phosphate at the same rate"
      - "Most serine and threonine phosphates are never removed; the whole protein is degraded instead"
      - "Each catalytic subunit pairs with many regulatory subunits that aim it at particular substrates"
      - "Tyrosine phosphatases remove most of the phosphate on serine and threonine"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      A catalytic subunit such as PP1 combines with many regulatory subunits, and each
      combination is aimed at different substrates or locations, so a few catalytic genes give
      many distinct enzymes. Unselective removal at one uniform rate would make it impossible to
      shut off one pathway while another stays on. Tyrosine phosphatases have a deep pocket
      fitted to phosphotyrosine and do not handle the bulk of serine and threonine sites.
      Phosphorylation is reversed on the intact protein; degradation is a separate and slower
      form of control.
  - stem: "Kinase K is tested on four versions of a peptide that contains one target serine. The versions differ only in the residue four positions after the target: an unmodified serine, a phosphoserine, an aspartate, or an alanine. The relative rates of phosphorylation of the target are 2, 100, 35 and 2, respectively, in arbitrary units. Which conclusion do the data best support?"
    choices:
      - "Aspartate is as good as phosphoserine, so a serine-to-aspartate mutant can always stand in for the phosphorylated protein"
      - "A negative charge four positions away promotes phosphorylation, and aspartate supplies only part of the effect of a phosphate"
      - "Kinase K needs a phosphate at that position, because no other residue raises the rate above the 2 seen with alanine"
      - "Any residue with a hydroxyl group four positions away primes the reaction"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Aspartate raises the rate about 17-fold over alanine (35 against 2) but reaches only about
      a third of the rate with phosphoserine (35 against 100). Charge at that position matters,
      and one carboxylate is a partial copy of a phosphate's two charges. This is priming: like
      glycogen synthase kinase 3, kinase K works best on a substrate that already carries a
      phosphate nearby. The claim that nothing but a phosphate works is contradicted by the 35
      seen with aspartate. The hydroxyl idea fails because the unmodified serine, which has a
      hydroxyl, gives the same low rate as alanine, which has none. Treating aspartate as equal
      to phosphoserine ignores the threefold gap and is the usual way a mimic experiment gets
      over-read.
  - stem: "A transcription factor is phosphorylated on serine 50 when a certain kinase is active. A mutant with aspartate at position 50 fails to bind DNA whether or not the kinase is active. A mutant with alanine at position 50 binds DNA whether or not the kinase is active. What is the most reasonable conclusion?"
    choices:
      - "Serine 50 is needed for the protein to fold properly, and the mutants say nothing about phosphate"
      - "A negative charge at position 50 blocks DNA binding, so phosphorylation there turns binding off"
      - "Aspartate imitates the unphosphorylated serine, so the phosphate normally promotes DNA binding"
      - "The alanine mutant proves that the kinase phosphorylates a different residue in this protein"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Aspartate supplies a negative charge like a phosphate, and DNA binding is lost. Alanine cannot
      be phosphorylated, and DNA binding stays on even with the kinase active. Together these indicate
      that a phosphate on serine 50 turns DNA binding off. Aspartate stands in for the phosphorylated
      state and not for the bare serine, so reading it as the unphosphorylated form reverses the
      logic. An alanine mutant that no longer responds to the kinase supports serine 50 as the site
      that matters; it does not point to another residue. A folding defect would be expected to damage
      both mutants, yet the alanine mutant binds DNA normally.

    rationale: >-
      At steady state the phosphorylated fraction is the kinase rate constant divided by the sum

**Your genome holds about 518 genes for protein kinases, and nearly every one of those enzymes runs the same reaction: it takes the end phosphate off ATP and fastens it to a protein.** Why do cells rely so heavily on one chemical group, what does it do once it is on, which enzymes take it off, and how would you prove that a protein is controlled this way?

## The idea: a phosphate is a charge you can add and take away

Start with a question from the lecture: if we have a protein, what are some of the ways we could alter its function? You could make more of it, which is slow, or bind a small molecule to it. Or an enzyme could attach a chemical group to one of its side chains after the protein is made. That last route is covalent modification, and it always comes as a pair, modifications and modifiers: the mark, and the enzymes that write and erase it.

Of the many marks cells use, signaling pathways use phosphate most. A **protein kinase** is an enzyme that moves the end (gamma) phosphate of ATP onto a protein. A **protein phosphatase** is an enzyme that removes that phosphate with water.

> **protein-OH + ATP → protein-O-PO3(2−) + ADP   (kinase)**
>
> **protein-O-PO3(2−) + H2O → protein-OH + Pi   (phosphatase)**

In animals the phosphate almost always goes onto a side chain that ends in a hydroxyl group: serine, threonine or tyrosine. Nearly all of it sits on serine and threonine; tyrosine carries less than 1%, which kept it hidden until 1979.

Four properties make phosphate a good switch.

*Charge.* Near pH 7 a phosphate on a protein carries close to two negative charges, on a spot that had none. A folded protein is held in shape by charges attracting and repelling each other, so few changes this small disturb it this much.

*Reversibility.* The bond holds until an enzyme removes it, and removal leaves the protein exactly as it was.

*A separate writer and eraser.* The phosphatase does not run the kinase reaction backwards; it runs a different reaction, and each one goes downhill. A whiteboard works because the marker and the eraser are different tools that you can hand to different people. The cell likewise regulates adding and removing independently, at the price of one ATP per round trip.

*Supply.* ATP sits at millimolar concentration in the cell, so a kinase rarely waits for its donor.

## What a phosphate does: four results of modification

The phosphate has no activity of its own. What matters is how the protein and its neighbours respond to the new charge. The lecture lists four results.

**It changes shape, and with it activity.** Every copy of a protein flickers among several arrangements, some working and some not. A phosphate makes the arrangements that accommodate its charge cheaper to occupy, so more copies sit in them. In muscle glycogen phosphorylase the phosphate lands on serine 14, near the start of the chain and far from the catalytic site, and it shifts the enzyme toward its active form. The same trick can turn a protein off: glycogen synthase, which builds glycogen, is inhibited by phosphorylation.

**It creates a docking site.** A phosphorylated side chain is a surface that was not there a moment ago, and some proteins carry pockets built to recognise one (a later lesson covers them). A phosphate can therefore bring two proteins together without changing either one's catalytic rate.

**It changes location.** NFAT is a transcription factor, a protein that switches genes on. It waits in the cytoplasm carrying many phosphates, which hide the sequence that would send it into the nucleus. When the phosphatase calcineurin strips them off, NFAT enters the nucleus. Here the erasing enzyme carries the signal.

**It primes another modification.** Some kinases accept a substrate only if a phosphate is already nearby. Glycogen synthase kinase 3 is the standard example: it prefers a serine or threonine that lies four residues before one that another kinase has already phosphorylated. Priming lets a protein wait for two inputs before it responds.

## The modifiers: 518 kinases and the phosphatases that answer them

Of the 518 or so human kinases, about 90 are tyrosine kinases, and 58 of those are receptors, proteins that span the plasma membrane with the kinase on the inside. Most of the rest phosphorylate serine and threonine.

Why does a cell need phosphatases at all? Think of a sink with the tap running and the drain open. The water level depends on both, and you can change it at either end. The same holds for a pool of protein acted on by a kinase and a phosphatase at simple first-order rates:

> **fraction phosphorylated at steady state = k(kinase) / (k(kinase) + k(phosphatase))**

A signal can raise the phosphorylated fraction by activating the kinase or by inhibiting the phosphatase, and cells do both. The time the level takes to settle after a change is set by the sum of the two rates, so a pathway that must shut off in seconds keeps an active phosphatase running against its kinase and pays for the speed in ATP.

On the tyrosine side the lecture slide reads "107 PTPs to counteract 90 PTKs" (PTP, protein tyrosine phosphatase; PTK, protein tyrosine kinase). The eraser family is about as large as the writer family, and many erasers are built like membrane receptors. The 107 counts the whole PTP family, though: about 38 are classical enzymes that read only phosphotyrosine, and some relatives, such as PTEN, prefer substrates other than proteins.

On the serine/threonine side the counts are lopsided: hundreds of kinases against a few dozen genes for phosphatase catalytic subunits. Specificity comes from partners: PP1, a major catalytic subunit, is encoded by just three genes yet pairs with well over a hundred regulatory subunits, which decide where the enzyme goes and what it touches.

The counts suggest that tyrosine dephosphorylation is steered by the choice of enzyme, and serine/threonine dephosphorylation by the company the enzyme keeps. That is an inference from numbers, not a proof.

## How we know: one muscle enzyme, then all the rest

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

**1943.** Carl and Gerty Cori and their colleague Arda Green, in St. Louis, crystallized glycogen phosphorylase, the muscle enzyme that releases glucose units from glycogen, and found it in two forms. Phosphorylase *a* was active alone; phosphorylase *b* worked only when AMP was present. They also had an enzyme that turned *a* into *b* and assumed it clipped off a bound AMP. It was in fact a phosphatase.

**1954.** Burnett and Kennedy reported a liver enzyme that moves phosphate from ATP onto the milk protein casein, the first protein kinase described.

**1955.** Edmond Fischer and Edwin Krebs, in Seattle, reported what had started as an effort to make phosphorylase *a* by the Coris' recipe: they kept getting *b*, unless they filtered the extract through paper. The paper was leaching calcium, and extracts left standing ran out of ATP. Over the next few years the conversion of *b* to *a* proved to be the work of an enzyme that moves one phosphate from ATP onto one serine on each phosphorylase chain. The overview lesson on signal transduction tells this story in full.

**1979 to 1980.** Tony Hunter saw a radioactive spot run between the phosphoserine and phosphothreonine markers, and a phosphotyrosine standard he made ran to the same place. He and Bartholomew Sefton then showed that Src, the cancer-causing protein of Rous sarcoma virus, phosphorylates tyrosine. Within the year H. Ushiro and Stanley Cohen, using membranes from a tumour cell line and no living cells, found the same activity in the receptor for epidermal growth factor (EGF), a cell's own protein with no virus involved.

**1988.** Nicholas Tonks, working with Fischer, purified the first protein tyrosine phosphatase, PTP1B, from human placenta. Its sequence resembled part of CD45, a surface protein of white blood cells, so a tyrosine phosphatase could be built like a receptor too.

### Design problem

You have a protein that you suspect is phosphorylated. Show that it is, find where, and show what the phosphate does.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Show that it is.** Run the protein from cells on an SDS gel, which sorts proteins by size; a phosphorylated protein often runs a little slower. Treat half the sample with a purified phosphatase. If the slow band collapses into the fast one, and a phosphatase inhibitor prevents the collapse, the difference was phosphate. Confirm it by growing cells with radioactive phosphate (32P), pulling the protein out with an antibody, and checking that it is labelled. A site found only in a test tube must still be confirmed in cells.

**Find where.** Break the labelled protein down to amino acids and separate them to learn whether serine, threonine or tyrosine carries the label. Then change each candidate serine or threonine to alanine, or each tyrosine to phenylalanine, which leaves no hydroxyl, and see which change removes the label or the gel shift. Mass spectrometry does the same job faster: a phosphate adds 80 Da to a peptide, and fragmenting the peptide shows which residue holds it.

**Show what it does.** Compare the alanine mutant, which can never be phosphorylated, with an aspartate mutant, whose negative charge imitates a phosphate that never leaves. Ask which of the four results the mimic shows. Aspartate carries one charge where a phosphate carries two, and it is smaller, so a mimic that does nothing proves little. Check the answer by inhibiting or activating the kinase: the normal protein should respond and the alanine mutant should not.

To find which kinase does the writing, inhibitors narrow the list, and Kevan Shokat's analog-sensitive kinases, engineered to accept an ATP analog that normal kinases cannot use, label only their direct targets.

In lecture, Prof. Deppmann works this problem with a transcription factor from his 2003 paper: a phosphatase-sensitive gel shift, serine and threonine sites, and a serine-to-aspartate mimic.

## When the switch breaks: kinases in disease and in the clinic

One lecture slide carries the title "Nearly every kinase has been associated with disease." A common pattern in cancer is a gain-of-function mutation, one that makes a protein more active: a kinase, often a receptor tyrosine kinase, is stuck on and tells the cell to grow when no signal has arrived. Losing a phosphatase can do similar damage, because the brake is gone.

That makes kinases drug targets, and a second slide says why they are good ones: "Highly conserved: target for lots of drugs." Kinases bind ATP in a cleft between two lobes, a snug, mostly greasy pocket of the kind a small drug molecule can fill. Imatinib, approved in 2001 for chronic myeloid leukemia, sits in that pocket in the kinase that drives the disease: BCR-ABL, an overactive fusion protein made when a chromosome rearrangement joins two genes. Gefitinib (2003) and erlotinib (2004) do the same in the EGF receptor.

The conservation that makes the pocket a good target also makes selectivity hard. A molecule shaped to fit one ATP pocket tends to fit some of the other 517, and each extra kinase it blocks is a possible side effect. The drug also has to outcompete the cell's own millimolar ATP. Chemists find selectivity at the edges of the pocket, where kinases differ, or in shapes that only some kinases adopt: imatinib binds the Abl kinase of BCR-ABL in an inactive conformation, which helps explain why it spares most other kinases.
