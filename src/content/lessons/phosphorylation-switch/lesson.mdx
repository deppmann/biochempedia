techniques:
  - name: "Radioactive labelling with gamma-32P ATP"
    blurb: >-
      You incubate a kinase and its substrate with ATP whose end phosphate is radioactive, or
      grow cells in radioactive phosphate, then separate the proteins on a gel and expose it to
      film. A dark band shows which protein received phosphate. Only a label in the gamma
      position works in the test tube, because that is the phosphate the kinase transfers.
  - name: "Phosphatase-sensitive mobility shift"
    blurb: >-
      Many phosphorylated proteins run more slowly on an SDS gel. If treating the sample with a
      purified phosphatase, such as calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase, collapses the slow band
      into the fast one, the difference was phosphate. It is quick and needs no radioactivity,
      though some phosphates cause no shift at all.
  - name: "Phosphoamino acid analysis"
    blurb: >-
      You break a 32P-labelled protein down to its amino acids in acid and separate them by
      electrophoresis on a thin-layer plate, beside phosphoserine, phosphothreonine and
      phosphotyrosine standards. The
      position of the radioactive spot tells you which kind of residue carries the phosphate.
      This is the method that revealed phosphotyrosine in 1979.
  - name: "Site-directed mutagenesis: alanine and aspartate substitutions"
    blurb: >-
      You change the suspected residue to alanine, which cannot accept a phosphate, or to
      aspartate, whose negative charge imitates one. Loss of labelling in the alanine mutant points to
      the site, and the behaviour of the aspartate mutant suggests what the phosphate does. A
      carboxylate is only a rough copy of a phosphate, so the result needs a second line of
      evidence.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "Which amino acid side chains carry regulatory phosphate groups in animal proteins, and what chemical feature do they share?"
    choices:
      - "Cysteine and methionine; each has a sulfur atom that bonds to phosphorus"
      - "Aspartate and glutamate; each has a negative charge like the phosphate itself"
      - "Lysine, arginine and histidine; each has a positive charge that attracts phosphate"
      - "Serine, threonine and tyrosine; each has a hydroxyl group that can form a phosphate ester"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The serine/threonine and tyrosine kinases transfer phosphate to an oxygen, the hydroxyl of
      serine, threonine or tyrosine, and these are the best-studied regulatory sites in animals.
      Lysine and arginine can sit beside a phosphate and attract it, but these kinases do not
      attach it there. Histidine is the exception worth knowing: mammals have histidine kinases
      (NME1 and NME2), though phosphohistidine is unstable in acid and less well understood.
      Aspartate and glutamate are used in the laboratory to imitate a phosphate because they are
      already negative; for these kinases they are the mimic and not the target. Cysteine forms
      disulfides and methionine is a thioether; neither is an acceptor for these kinases.
  - stem: "A kinase adds a phosphate to a protein and a phosphatase removes it. Why can the cell regulate these two steps independently?"
    choices:
      - "The phosphate ester is too stable to break, so removal requires destroying the protein"
      - "The phosphatase returns the phosphate to ADP, so the add-and-remove cycle uses no net ATP"
      - "Adding uses ATP and removing uses water, so each is its own reaction with its own enzyme"
      - "The kinase is used up in each reaction, so a second enzyme is needed to regenerate it"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The kinase reaction transfers phosphate from ATP, and the phosphatase reaction hydrolyses the
      ester with water. They are different reactions, both favourable, so each has its own enzyme and
      its own controls. The phosphatase releases inorganic phosphate and does not remake ATP, which is
      why one round trip costs one ATP. The ester is stable when no enzyme is present, but a
      phosphatase removes it cleanly and the protein survives. Kinases are catalysts and are not
      consumed by the reactions they run.

  - stem: "Human cells have several hundred serine/threonine kinases but only a few dozen genes for serine/threonine phosphatase catalytic subunits. What best explains how removal of phosphate from serine and threonine can still be specific?"
    choices:
      - "It is not specific; these phosphatases remove every phosphate at the same rate"
      - "Most serine and threonine phosphates are never removed; the whole protein is degraded instead"
      - "Each catalytic subunit pairs with many regulatory subunits that aim it at particular substrates"
      - "Tyrosine phosphatases remove most of the phosphate on serine and threonine"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      A catalytic subunit such as PP1 combines with many regulatory subunits, and each
      combination is aimed at different substrates or locations, so a few catalytic genes give
      many distinct enzymes. Unselective removal at one uniform rate would make it much harder to
      shut off one pathway while another stays on. Tyrosine phosphatases have a deep pocket
      fitted to phosphotyrosine and do not handle the bulk of serine and threonine sites.
      Phosphorylation is reversed on the intact protein; degradation is a separate and slower
      form of control.
  - stem: "Kinase K is tested on four versions of a peptide that contains one target serine. The versions differ only in the residue four positions after the target: an unmodified serine, a phosphoserine, an aspartate, or an alanine. The relative rates of phosphorylation of the target are 2, 100, 35 and 2, respectively, in arbitrary units. Which conclusion do the data best support?"
    choices:
      - "Aspartate is as good as phosphoserine, so a serine-to-aspartate mutant can always stand in for the phosphorylated protein"
      - "A negative charge four positions away promotes phosphorylation, and aspartate supplies only part of the effect of a phosphate"
      - "Kinase K needs a phosphate at that position, because no other residue raises the rate above the 2 seen with alanine"
      - "Any residue with a hydroxyl group four positions away primes the reaction"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Aspartate raises the rate about 17-fold over alanine (35 against 2) but reaches only about
      a third of the rate with phosphoserine (35 against 100). Charge at that position matters,
      and one carboxylate is a partial copy of a phosphate's two charges. This is priming: like
      glycogen synthase kinase 3, kinase K works best on a substrate that already carries a
      phosphate nearby. The claim that nothing but a phosphate works is contradicted by the 35
      seen with aspartate. The hydroxyl idea fails because the unmodified serine, which has a
      hydroxyl, gives the same low rate as alanine, which has none. Treating aspartate as equal
      to phosphoserine ignores the threefold gap and is the usual way a mimic experiment gets
      over-read.
  - stem: "A transcription factor is phosphorylated on serine 50 when a certain kinase is active. A mutant with aspartate at position 50 fails to bind DNA whether or not the kinase is active. A mutant with alanine at position 50 binds DNA whether or not the kinase is active. What is the most reasonable conclusion?"
    choices:
      - "Serine 50 is needed for the protein to fold properly, and the mutants say nothing about phosphate"
      - "A negative charge at position 50 blocks DNA binding, so phosphorylation there turns binding off"
      - "Aspartate imitates the unphosphorylated serine, so the phosphate normally promotes DNA binding"
      - "The alanine mutant proves that the kinase phosphorylates a different residue in this protein"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Aspartate supplies a negative charge like a phosphate, and DNA binding is lost. Alanine cannot
      be phosphorylated, and DNA binding stays on even with the kinase active. Together these indicate
      that a phosphate on serine 50 turns DNA binding off. Aspartate stands in for the phosphorylated
      state and not for the bare serine, so reading it as the unphosphorylated form reverses the
      logic. An alanine mutant that no longer responds to the kinase supports serine 50 as the site
      that matters; it does not point to another residue. A general folding defect from losing serine 50 would be expected to damage
      both mutants, yet the alanine mutant binds DNA normally. A defect peculiar to aspartate is still possible, which is why a mimic result needs a second line of evidence.

  - stem: "In cultured cells, a kinase phosphorylates protein S with a first-order rate constant of 0.9 per minute, and a phosphatase removes the phosphate with a rate constant of 0.1 per minute. At steady state, 90% of S is phosphorylated. A researcher adds a drug that lowers the kinase rate constant to 0.09 per minute and leaves the phosphatase unchanged. About what fraction of S is phosphorylated at the new steady state?"
    choices:
      - "About 9%, because the phosphorylated fraction falls in proportion to kinase activity"
      - "0%, because the kinase has been inhibited"
      - "About 90%, because phosphates already on the protein stay there"
      - "About 47%, because the kinase and phosphatase rate constants are now nearly equal"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      At steady state the phosphorylated fraction is the kinase rate constant divided by the sum
      of the kinase and phosphatase rate constants, and 0.09 / 0.19 is about 0.47. The 9% answer
      assumes the fraction scales directly with kinase activity, but the fraction depends on the
      ratio of the two rates, and the kinase is still about as fast as the phosphatase. The 90%
      answer forgets that the phosphatase keeps removing phosphate, so the old level cannot
      persist. The 0% answer treats a tenfold reduction as a complete block, yet a kinase running
      at one tenth of its rate still holds almost half the protein phosphorylated against this
      phosphatase.
  - stem: "A protein from growth-factor-treated cells runs as two bands on an SDS gel, at apparent sizes of 30 and 32 kDa; from untreated cells only the 30 kDa band appears. Treating the stimulated sample with alkaline phosphatase leaves only the 30 kDa band, and adding a phosphatase inhibitor along with the enzyme preserves both bands. By mass spectrometry, one peptide from the upper band is 160 Da heavier than the same peptide from the lower band. Which interpretation fits all of the data?"
    choices:
      - "The upper band carries two phosphates on that peptide; the 2 kDa shift is slower migration, not mass"
      - "The upper band carries about 25 phosphates, because 2 kDa divided by 80 Da is 25"
      - "The upper band carries one phosphate, because one phosphate adds 160 Da"
      - "The upper band is a different, larger protein that a protease in the phosphatase preparation degrades"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Each phosphate adds 80 Da, so a peptide that is 160 Da heavier carries two. The
      phosphatase removes the upper band and an inhibitor of the phosphatase prevents this, so
      the shift depends on phosphate. Two phosphates add only 0.16 kDa. The band moves by an
      apparent 2 kDa because phosphorylation changes how the protein migrates in the gel, so the
      apparent size cannot be converted into a phosphate count, which rules out 25 phosphates. A
      contaminating protease would not be stopped by a phosphatase inhibitor, and the same
      peptide is found in both bands, so a different protein being degraded does not fit. A
      single phosphate adds 80 Da, not 160.
  - stem: "A new compound inhibits purified kinase A. With 10 µM ATP in the assay, a concentration equal to the kinase's Km for ATP, 20 nM compound gives half-maximal inhibition. With 1 mM ATP, about 1 µM compound is needed. At 1 µM the compound also inhibits two unrelated kinases. Which explanation best accounts for both findings?"
    choices:
      - "The compound competes with ATP for the ATP pocket, which is similar across kinases"
      - "The compound binds the protein-substrate site, which is identical in all kinases"
      - "The compound is a noncompetitive inhibitor that binds far from the active site"
      - "ATP at high concentration phosphorylates the compound and inactivates it"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Needing about fifty times more compound when ATP is raised a hundredfold is the signature
      of competition with ATP: for a competitive inhibitor the half-inhibiting dose rises as
      1 + [ATP]/Km, here from 2 to 101. Because the ATP pocket is conserved, a compound that fits
      it in one kinase often fits others, which explains the inhibition of unrelated kinases.
      Protein-substrate sites differ from kinase to kinase, which is how kinases tell their
      targets apart, and binding there would not make potency depend on ATP. A noncompetitive
      inhibitor would be about equally potent at both ATP concentrations. ATP does not hand its
      phosphate to a molecule unless an enzyme catalyses the transfer, and nothing in the data
      suggests the compound is a kinase substrate.

  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Proteins do most of the work in a cell, and the cell has to turn them on and off quickly.
      One common way is to attach a phosphate group, a small cluster of atoms with a negative
      charge. Enzymes called kinases attach the phosphate, taking it from ATP, and enzymes
      called phosphatases remove it. The added charge changes the protein's shape or lets other
      proteins hold onto it, and that changes what the protein does. Because adding and removing
      are done by different enzymes, the cell can control each direction separately. Humans have
      more than 500 kinases, and faulty ones are a common cause of cancer.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      Protein phosphorylation is a reversible covalent modification. A protein kinase transfers
      the gamma phosphate of ATP to the hydroxyl of a serine, threonine or tyrosine side chain; a
      protein phosphatase hydrolyses the phosphate ester and releases inorganic phosphate. The
      phosphate adds about two negative charges, which can shift the protein's conformational
      equilibrium (as at Ser14 of glycogen phosphorylase), create a binding site for another
      protein, change where the protein sits in the cell, or prime a second modification. The
      phosphorylated fraction of a protein, once it settles, is a steady state set by the opposing kinase and
      phosphatase rates. Humans encode about 518 kinases, about 90 of them tyrosine kinases.
      The tyrosine phosphatase gene family is about as large as the tyrosine kinase family, though only about 38 of its members are classical phosphotyrosine-specific enzymes, while a
      small set of serine/threonine phosphatase catalytic subunits gains specificity from many
      regulatory subunits. To show that a site matters you detect the phosphate, map the residue, and then
      test alanine and aspartate mutants.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      A phosphorylation site behaves as a futile cycle. For first-order kinase and phosphatase
      rate constants, the steady-state occupancy is k(kinase) / (k(kinase) + k(phosphatase)) and
      the relaxation rate is their sum, so a fast response is bought with ATP turnover. When
      both enzymes are saturated by substrate the same cycle becomes switch-like (the zero-order
      ultrasensitivity of Goldbeter and Koshland, 1981), which is one reason site occupancy
      cannot be read as kinase activity alone. Eukaryotic protein kinases share a two-lobed fold
      with ATP in the cleft and are often themselves switched by phosphorylation of an
      activation loop. Classical tyrosine phosphatases use a cysteine nucleophile and a
      phosphocysteine intermediate, whereas the major serine/threonine phosphatases are
      metalloenzymes that activate water. The count of 107 PTP genes includes dual-specificity
      enzymes and members such as PTEN whose preferred substrate is a lipid, so the comparison
      with 90 tyrosine kinases overstates the number of dedicated phosphotyrosine erasers.

      An open question is specificity on the phosphatase side. For most of the tens of
      thousands of phosphorylation sites catalogued by mass spectrometry, neither the
      responsible phosphatase holoenzyme nor any function for the site is known, and what
      fraction of sites is regulatory, as opposed to incidental, remains unsettled.

The first film shows the switch; this page adds the chemistry, numbers and evidence.

> **The one idea.** A stable, removable phosphate changes protein behavior through its charge and shape.
>
> *Scope:* regulatory phosphorylation of proteins, especially the well-studied serine, threonine and tyrosine sites in animal signaling. The charge is the main handle, but partners also read the phosphate's shape and the residues around it, which is why aspartate is only a rough mimic. It may favor activity or inhibition; it is not an automatic on signal.

## Write the mark, erase the mark

A cell can change what a protein does by making more of it, which is slow, by binding a small molecule to it, or by attaching a chemical group after the protein is made. That last route is a **covalent modification**, and signaling pathways use phosphate most. The first film's two reactions:

> **protein-OH + ATP → protein-O-PO3(2−) + ADP   (kinase)**
>
> **protein-O-PO3(2−) + H2O → protein-OH + Pi   (phosphatase)**

A **protein kinase** moves the end, or gamma, phosphate of ATP onto the protein; a **protein phosphatase** removes it with water. ATP sits at millimolar concentration in the cell. Near pH 7 the attached phosphate carries close to two negative charges where there were none.

Most protein-bound phosphate sits on serine and threonine. Tyrosine carries well under 1% of it in a cell, about 2% of mapped sites, which kept phosphotyrosine hidden until 1979. Mammals also phosphorylate histidine, but the acid in standard methods strips that phosphate, so how common phosphohistidine is remains unclear.

## Four consequences of one added phosphate

The phosphate has no activity of its own; the protein and its neighbours respond to it. The first film's four outcomes, case by case:

**Activity through shape.** In muscle glycogen phosphorylase the phosphate lands on serine 14, near the chain's start and far from the catalytic site, yet it favors the active form. The same mark inhibits glycogen synthase.

**A docking site.** A partner whose pocket recognises the phosphorylated side chain can dock; a later lesson covers those pockets.

## How much protein carries the phosphate?

**Predict.** A kinase works at 0.6 per minute against a phosphatase at 0.2 per minute. A drug cuts only the kinase rate tenfold, to 0.06 per minute. Does the phosphorylated fraction fall tenfold?

The **phosphorylated fraction**, *f*, is the share of a protein pool carrying phosphate at the measured site. Take the second film's simplest model: one site, a fixed total pool and effective first-order rates, with no saturated enzymes and no multisite network. Fraction *(1 − f)* is available to the kinase:

> **rate in = k(kinase) × (1 − f)**
>
> **rate out = k(phosphatase) × f**

At steady state the rates match, so:

> **f = k(kinase) / (k(kinase) + k(phosphatase))**

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Before the drug, f = 0.6 / (0.6 + 0.2) = 0.75. After it, f = 0.06 / (0.06 + 0.2), about 0.23. A tenfold cut in kinase rate gives only about a threefold fall, because the phosphatase rate stays in the denominator.

</details>

So the phosphorylation level depends on both the kinase and the phosphatase, not on kinase activity alone. Because the summed rates set how fast the level settles, a pathway that must shut off in seconds needs a fast phosphatase; running it constantly costs ATP, so some pathways switch it on only when the signal ends.

## How the enzymes find the right targets

The human genome holds about 518 protein-kinase genes. Most encode serine/threonine kinases; about 90 encode tyrosine kinases. The tyrosine-phosphatase family is about as large by count, but only about 38 members are classical enzymes that read only phosphotyrosine, and many erasers are built like membrane receptors.

Serine/threonine counts are lopsided: hundreds of kinases but only a few dozen genes for phosphatase catalytic subunits. Specificity comes from partners. PP1, a major catalytic subunit, is encoded by three genes yet pairs with well over a hundred regulatory subunits, which choose its location and substrates. The counts suggest that tyrosine dephosphorylation is steered more by enzyme choice, and serine/threonine dephosphorylation by company. That is an inference from counts, not proof.

## Build the evidence: detect, map, test

Three claims need different evidence: the protein carries phosphate, a particular residue carries it, and changing that site changes function.

**Predict.** A stimulated sample shows an extra, slower band on an SDS gel. What control would tie that band to phosphate, and what would it still leave open?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Treat half the sample with purified phosphatase. If the slow band collapses into the fast one and a phosphatase inhibitor prevents that, the shift depends on phosphate. The residue and the function remain open.

</details>

**Detect it.** A phosphorylated protein often runs more slowly on an SDS gel, which sorts proteins by apparent size; the phosphatase test above is the quick first check. Confirm that the protein carries phosphate by growing cells with radioactive phosphate (32P), pulling the protein out with an antibody and checking for label. A site found only in a test tube must still be confirmed in cells.

**Map it.** Break the labelled protein into amino acids and separate them to learn which kind of residue carries the label. Change candidate serine or threonine residues to alanine, or tyrosine to phenylalanine, removing the hydroxyl, and ask which change removes the label or shift. Mass spectrometry is faster: each phosphate adds 80 Da to a peptide, and peptide fragments usually locate the residue.

**Predict.** A peptide from the upper gel band is 240 Da heavier than the matching peptide below, and the band runs an apparent 3 kDa higher. How many phosphates does the peptide carry?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Three, because 240 / 80 = 3. Three phosphates add only 0.24 kDa; the 3 kDa is apparent migration, not mass, so it cannot be converted into a phosphate count.

</details>

**Test function.** Compare an alanine mutant, which cannot be phosphorylated, with an aspartate mutant, whose negative charge roughly imitates a persistent phosphate. Ask which of the four consequences the mimic reproduces: activity, docking, location or priming.

**Predict.** A kinase activates enzyme E. The serine-to-alanine mutant no longer responds to the kinase, but the serine-to-aspartate mutant is no more active than the unstimulated normal enzyme. What can you conclude?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The alanine result points to this serine as the site that matters. The silent mimic proves little: aspartate carries one charge where phosphate carries two, it is smaller, and a partner may not recognise it. Seek another line of evidence.

</details>

The normal protein should respond to kinase inhibition or activation; the alanine mutant should not. Inhibitors can narrow the writer list. Kevan Shokat's analog-sensitive kinases are engineered to accept an ATP analog that normal kinases cannot use, so they label only direct targets.

Work this approach on a real case: the transcription factor in Deppmann and colleagues' 2003 paper, which combined a phosphatase-sensitive gel shift, serine and threonine sites and a serine-to-aspartate mimic.

## When the switch breaks: kinases in disease and the clinic

A lecture slide claims nearly every kinase has been linked to disease, a slight stretch: many have, but much of the kinome is poorly studied. A common cancer pattern is a gain-of-function mutation that leaves a kinase, often a receptor tyrosine kinase, active without a signal and telling the cell to grow. Losing a phosphatase can cause similar damage because the brake is gone.

A second slide calls kinases "highly conserved: target for lots of drugs." The ATP cleft between the two lobes is snug and mostly greasy, so small drugs can fill it. Imatinib, approved in 2001 for chronic myeloid leukemia, binds BCR-ABL, the overactive fusion kinase made when a chromosome rearrangement joins two genes. Gefitinib and erlotinib, approved in the United States in 2003 and 2004, target the EGF receptor similarly.

Conservation makes selectivity hard: a molecule fitting one ATP pocket may fit some of the other 517, causing side effects, and it must compete with millimolar cellular ATP. Chemists find selectivity at the edges of the pocket, where kinases differ, or in inactive shapes that only some kinases adopt. Imatinib binds Abl in an inactive conformation, helping explain why it spares most other kinases.

## Going deeper

### How reversible protein phosphorylation emerged

**1943.** Carl and Gerty Cori and Arda Green in St. Louis crystallized glycogen phosphorylase, the muscle enzyme that releases glucose units from glycogen. Phosphorylase *a* was active alone; *b* required AMP. An enzyme turned *a* into *b*. They thought it removed bound AMP; it was a phosphatase.

**1954.** Burnett and Kennedy described a liver enzyme moving phosphate from ATP onto casein, the first protein kinase described.

**1955.** Edmond Fischer and Edwin Krebs in Seattle set out to make phosphorylase *a* by the Coris' recipe. They kept getting *b* unless they filtered the extract through paper. The paper leached calcium, and standing extracts ran out of ATP. Over the next few years, conversion of *b* to *a* proved to be an enzyme moving one phosphate from ATP onto one serine on each chain. The overview lesson on signal transduction tells the full story.

**1979 to 1980.** Tony Hunter saw a radioactive spot between phosphoserine and phosphothreonine markers; his phosphotyrosine standard matched it. He and Bartholomew Sefton showed that Src, the cancer-causing protein of Rous sarcoma virus, phosphorylates tyrosine. Within the year, H. Ushiro and Stanley Cohen used tumour-cell membranes without living cells to show that EGF switches on the same activity. It was later traced to the cell's own EGF receptor.

**1988.** Nicholas Tonks, working with Fischer, purified PTP1B, the first protein tyrosine phosphatase, from human placenta. Its sequence resembled part of CD45, a white-blood-cell surface protein, showing that a phosphatase could be receptor-like.

### Pseudokinases and family counts

About one in ten human kinases, called pseudokinases, has lost catalytic activity and instead works by binding other proteins.

Of the roughly 90 human tyrosine kinases, 58 are receptors spanning the plasma membrane with the kinase inside. The tally “107 PTPs to counteract 90 PTKs” (protein tyrosine phosphatases and kinases) counts the whole phosphatase family: only about 38 are classical phosphotyrosine-specific enzymes, and relatives such as PTEN prefer substrates other than proteins.
