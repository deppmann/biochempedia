techniques:
  - name: "Ussing chamber with short-circuit current and isotope fluxes"
    blurb: >-
      A stripped sheet of epithelium is mounted between two bathing solutions and the
      transepithelial voltage is clamped at zero, so the current supplied measures net active
      ion transport with no electrical driving force left in the tissue. Radioisotopes added to
      one bath at a time give the flux in each direction separately, and tissue conductance plus
      a paracellular probe such as mannitol report whether the barrier is intact.
  - name: "Expression cloning in Xenopus oocytes"
    blurb: >-
      Messenger RNA is injected into frog eggs, which then make the encoded transporter, and
      uptake of a labelled substrate is measured. Fractionating the library until the uptake
      follows a single clone identifies the protein by its function alone, and the same assay
      tests whether a mutant version still transports.
  - name: "Targeted gene deletion in mice"
    blurb: >-
      One transporter or signal is removed from the whole animal and the flux it was supposed to
      carry is measured, here as fecal bile acid output, pool size and composition. It shows
      whether other routes can take over, and it says nothing by itself about the size of the
      same effect in humans.
  - name: "Carbon-13 NMR of liver glycogen with a glucose tracer"
    blurb: >-
      Natural-abundance carbon-13 signals from liver glycogen are followed in a person over a
      fast while an isotopically labelled glucose tracer measures whole-body glucose production.
      Subtracting measured glycogen breakdown from total production leaves gluconeogenesis,
      without a liver biopsy.

**About 2 litres of drink enter an adult gut each day, the body adds roughly 7 to 9 litres of its own secretions, and about 200 grams of stool comes out the far end.** Stool water is a small difference between two large flows, which makes it a sensitive quantity. This lesson is about the transporters that set it, the route fat takes out of the gut, and the organ that receives everything else first.

[Digestive systems](/lessons/digestive-systems/) walks a meal down the tube, [digestion](/lessons/digestion/) has the enzyme chemistry, and [gut motility and secretion](/lessons/gut-motility-secretion/) covers the secretions themselves. Start from the products of hydrolysis in the lumen.

## Eleven litres in, two hundred grams out

Take an adult human at steady state, with no change in the fluid held in the gut. Reviews give intake as about 2 L a day and secretions (saliva, gastric juice, bile, pancreatic juice, intestinal fluid) as about 9 L a day, within a range of 7 to 9 L. About 99 percent of the total is reabsorbed and about 200 g of stool is passed a day. The colon can absorb up to 4 to 5 L a day, reserve capacity rather than routine load.

> stool water = load entering the lumen x (1 - fractional reabsorption)

**Worked problem: the water balance.** Round illustrative numbers: intake 2.0 L a day plus secretions 9.0 L a day gives a load of 11.0 L a day.

1. *At 99 percent reabsorption.* Stool water = 11.0 x 0.01 = 0.11 L a day, consistent with a stool of about 200 g a day.
2. *At 96 percent.* Stool water = 11.0 x 0.04 = 0.44 L a day, four times as much for a change of three percentage points. Check: 10.56 + 0.44 = 11.0 L a day.
3. *Read it.* The reabsorbed fraction sits close to one, so what you observe is the residue, which magnifies any shift in transport.

Circular folds, villi and microvilli give the area that makes 99 percent possible, and secretions lost rather than reabsorbed take their bicarbonate with them, which is how the route of a loss sets the consequence in [acid-base balance](/lessons/acid-base-balance/).

## Crypts secrete, villi absorb, and the stool is the difference

The two directions belong to different cells along the crypt-villus axis of mammalian small intestine, where, in the classic model, crypt cells carry the secretory machinery and villus cells the absorptive machinery ([epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/) and [membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/) have the routes, pumps and carriers).

In a crypt cell, the basolateral sodium pump and NKCC1, which brings sodium, potassium and chloride in together, load chloride above its electrochemical gradient. Apical CFTR, a chloride channel regulated mainly by cyclic AMP- and cyclic GMP-dependent protein kinases, is the exit. Open CFTR and chloride leaves, sodium follows paracellularly to balance charge, and water follows the osmotic force of both. On the villus, the apical sodium/hydrogen exchanger NHE3 appears to mediate electroneutral sodium absorption, and SGLT1 carries sodium with sugar. Net fluid movement is the sum of the two populations.

{/* DIAGRAM: one crypt and one villus in cross-section; basolateral NKCC1 and pump with apical CFTR on the crypt cell, apical NHE3 and SGLT1 on the villus cell, arrows for chloride out and sodium in */}

**The classic experiment.** Into the 1960s the dominant explanation of cholera diarrhea was a nineteenth-century model from Virchow: fluid leaking from the circulation through a damaged lining. In cholera, clearance of small molecules such as mannitol, a paracellular probe, into the stool was found to be very low, which already made filtration untenable.

Michael Field and colleagues took the question to an isolated sheet: isolated rabbit ileal mucosa in an Ussing-type chamber, voltage clamped at zero so the current supplied measures net active ion transport, with radioisotopes giving each direction separately. On that picture a sheet with no circulation behind it should move no fluid toward the lumen; the report was the opposite. Toxin added on the luminal side raised short-circuit current slowly over hours, and at 3 to 4 hours the net chloride flux was secretory and net sodium absorption had been reduced to zero, not reversed. Adding glucose to the lumen then restored a large net sodium absorption, so that route was intact. The authors read this as a cyclic AMP-dependent active secretory process of the epithelial cells, which points to secretion by the epithelium rather than filtration. Scope: one species, one segment, an isolated sheet with no blood flow, nerves or motility, at a voltage it never sees in an animal.

## Why glucose in the lumen changes the sodium flux

A messenger that opens CFTR and suppresses NHE3 takes out one absorptive route. It does not take out the other. SGLT1 runs on the inward sodium electrochemical gradient maintained by the basolateral pump, which is why poisoning that pump stalls it ([digestion](/lessons/digestion/) works that through in one cell). Add a transported sugar on the luminal side and sodium absorption through SGLT1 proceeds whether or not the cyclic-nucleotide-sensitive route works.

Human SGLT1 couples two sodium ions to each sugar, a 2:1 stoichiometry supported by transport studies and by a cryo-electron microscopy structure resolving two sodium sites. Treat the ratio as an upper bound on what cotransport can carry, not a measurement of how much is absorbed. If a litre of luminal fluid held 75 mmol of a transported sugar and 75 mmol of sodium, the sugar could carry at most 150 mmol of sodium, so sodium is the limiting partner here and at most half the sugar travels coupled.

## Fat takes the other door

Sugars and amino acids leave the enterocyte basolaterally into capillaries draining to the portal vein. Long-chain fat does not. Bile acids combine with the products of lipolysis into mixed micelles about 4 to 5 nm across, small enough to reach the spaces between microvilli. Westergaard and Dietschy, in rabbit jejunum in vitro, corrected for the unstirred layer of water held against the brush border and found the micelle's main role is to beat that resistance, with absorption proceeding from individual lipid molecules in equilibrium with the micelles.

Uptake is partly protein-assisted: in mice, NPC1L1 lies in the ezetimibe-sensitive sterol pathway, and CD36-null mice with lymph fistulae secrete less lipoprotein into lymph. Inside the cell, products are re-esterified and microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTP) assembles lipid with newly made apoB-48 into chylomicrons; MTP activity was absent in intestinal biopsies from four people with abetalipoproteinemia. Chylomicrons pass into the lamina propria, enter the villus lacteal and travel the mesenteric lymphatics to the thoracic duct ([capillary exchange and lymph](/lessons/capillary-exchange-and-lymph/)). Only short- and medium-chain fatty acids enter portal blood directly, so most dietary lipid bypasses the liver's first pass.

## A pool used five times a day

Bile acids are made from cholesterol in the liver, held in the gallbladder between meals, delivered to the duodenum, used, and recovered, mostly actively and mostly in one place: ASBT in the terminal ileum. Returning bile acids are removed efficiently in their first passage through the sinusoids, NTCP carrying most conjugated bile acid uptake, with BSEP the major canalicular export pump. CYP7A1 is the rate-limiting enzyme of the classic synthesis pathway, and gut bacteria make the secondary bile acids deoxycholic and lithocholic acid. Human conjugation is mostly to glycine, about 3:1 over taurine; mice are about 95 percent taurine, so pool composition does not transfer.

**Worked problem: the bile acid budget.** Human review figures: pool 3.5 g, about 5 cycles a day, about 95 percent reabsorbed per cycle, fecal loss 200 to 400 mg a day. Assume steady state and that the whole pool recirculates each cycle.

1. *Delivery to the ileum.* 3.5 g x 5 a day = 17.5 g a day.
2. *Loss predicted at 95 percent.* 17.5 x 0.05 = 0.875 g a day, two to four times the measured 200 to 400 mg a day, so the textbook numbers do not close.
3. *Solve for the implied efficiency.* At a mid-range loss of 0.30 g a day, fractional escape = 0.30 / 17.5 = 0.017, so reabsorption is about 98.3 percent per cycle. The 95 percent figure is rounded; use one self-consistent set at a time.
4. *Synthesis.* It replaces 0.30 g a day, 8.6 percent of the pool; the rest of the delivery is recycled, not made.
5. *Sensitivity.* At 90 percent reabsorption, loss = 17.5 x 0.10 = 1.75 g a day, six times as much, with synthesis rising to match.

The brake on synthesis runs through the gut. Bile acids taken up by ileal ASBT activate FXR, a nuclear receptor ([nuclear receptors](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/)), which induces FGF19; FGF19 reaches the hepatocyte and acts through FGFR4 with beta-klotho to lower CYP7A1. Mice lacking the ortholog FGF15 have more hepatic CYP7A1 and more fecal bile acid.

**The modern experiment.** Dawson and colleagues deleted Slc10a2, the gene for ASBT, in mice. If other routes could take over, the pool would stay roughly intact. Instead fecal bile acid excretion rose 10- to 20-fold, and the pool fell by about 80 percent and was enriched in cholic acid despite increased synthesis, with no steatorrhea on a low-fat diet. The authors concluded that nothing compensates for ASBT. It does not show the same consequences in people, or behaviour at a high fat load.

**Reasoning from the lesion.** Interrupt ileal uptake and the loop reads forwards. Delivery to the colon rises, and bile acids there have been shown to induce fluid secretion and raise mucosal permeability, so colonic net fluid absorption falls. Ileal FXR activation and FGF19 fall, the brake on CYP7A1 is released, and synthesis rises while the pool shrinks, leaving fewer bile acids available to form micelles at a meal. Fat absorption need not change: those mice had no steatorrhea at a small fat load.

## The liver as the first organ downstream

Textbooks give about 75 percent of hepatic blood flow by the portal vein and about 25 percent by the hepatic artery, in a fasting adult at rest (measured human values vary, and portal flow rises after a meal), so most of what the gut absorbs, long-chain fat excepted, meets hepatocytes first. For a compound completely absorbed with no gut-wall metabolism, systemic availability F = 1 - E_H, where E_H is the fraction removed in one hepatic passage; a measured F of 0.10 to 0.20 gives hepatic extraction of 0.80 to 0.90 if these conditions hold; if they are unverified, these values are only upper bounds.

> F = 1 - E_H, for a completely absorbed compound with no metabolism in the gut wall

Between meals the liver is a glucose source, by glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis ([integration of metabolism](/lessons/integration-of-metabolism/), [gluconeogenesis](/lessons/gluconeogenesis/) and [insulin, glucagon and glucose](/lessons/insulin-glucagon-glucose/) hold the pathways and their control). They do not run in sequence. Rothman and colleagues followed liver glycogen in healthy humans by carbon-13 NMR through a 68-hour fast with a labelled tracer for whole-body glucose production: gluconeogenesis already supplied 64 percent of production in the first 22 hours, then 82 and 96 percent in the following 14 and 18 hours.

The liver makes about 10.5 g of albumin a day in healthy people, a turnover of about 25 days, and the evidence that it readily doubles this on demand is thin: in one controlled study of a volunteer under daily plasmapheresis, synthesis rose about 25 percent.

Bilirubin is handled in three steps. Heme breakdown is in [blood cells and hemostasis](/lessons/blood-cells-and-hemostasis/); begin with unconjugated bilirubin arriving bound to albumin. UGT1A1 conjugates it with glucuronic acid, transporters including MRP2 export the conjugate into bile, and gut bacteria convert it to urobilinogen and urobilin. If heme breakdown outruns conjugating capacity, unconjugated bilirubin rises and does not appear in urine, because albumin-bound bilirubin is not filtered in free form. If conjugating capacity is low, as with the UGT1A1 promoter variant of Gilbert's syndrome, unconjugated bilirubin rises with export intact. If canalicular export fails, through loss of MRP2 or obstruction of bile flow, conjugated bilirubin rises and appears in urine because it is water soluble. When bile flow is blocked, less conjugate reaches the gut, so urobilinogen and stool pigment fall. One caution concerns assays rather than physiology: delta-bilirubin is protein-bound and poorly excreted, so folding it into "direct" bilirubin overstates the conjugated fraction.

### Design problem

You have isolated rabbit ileal mucosa in an Ussing-type chamber, sodium and chloride radioisotopes, glucose, mannitol as a paracellular probe, and a cell-free bacterial filtrate that produces secretion. Design measurements that separate three mechanisms for fluid appearing in the luminal bath: active electrogenic anion secretion, loss of barrier integrity, and reversal of the villus absorptive pumps. Then design the one further test that asks whether an absorptive route survives the filtrate.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start electrically. Clamp the voltage at zero and record short-circuit current, which reports net active ion transport, with conductance alongside it. A rise in current with little change in conductance points to electrogenic transport; a large rise in conductance to a permeability change.

Then resolve the net fluxes. Measure unidirectional sodium and chloride fluxes, one isotope per bath, so that net secretion separates into which flux moved. A rise in serosa-to-mucosa chloride rules in active anion secretion; a fall in mucosa-to-serosa sodium without reversal is a reduced absorptive flux, not a reversed pump, which would show as net sodium movement toward the lumen.

Test the barrier with mannitol clearance, which should not rise if the junctions held, and with resistance over time. Test the messenger with bumetanide serosally, for whether the current depends on NKCC1 chloride loading, and with a membrane-permeant cyclic AMP analogue, for whether the messenger alone reproduces the response.

The further test: add glucose to the mucosal bath and ask whether sodium-coupled absorptive flux still rises, with a non-transported sugar at the same concentration as the control separating an osmotic effect from cotransport. Limits: one species, one segment, an isolated sheet with no blood flow, nerves or motility, at a clamp no living animal imposes.
