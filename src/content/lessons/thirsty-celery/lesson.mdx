## The big idea

Stand a celery stalk in blue water and wait. After a few hours, thin blue lines creep up the stalk and the leaves start to turn blue at the edges. The colour shows a path your child can't normally see: the water tubes inside a plant.

A bottle of blue food colouring sits beside them." caption="Two glasses with the same blue water. One celery stalk keeps its leaves and one has them cut off, so you can see whether leaves change how fast the colour climbs." />

Roots take in water, and the stem carries it up to the leaves. Inside are bundles of tiny tubes, too thin to see one by one. Water moves up them, and food colouring rides along. The celery stalk we buy is really the long stalk of a leaf, but it carries water the same way a stem does.

Leaves matter here. Water evaporates from tiny pores in the leaves, and as it leaves, it pulls more water up the tubes behind it. That is why a stalk with leaves usually takes up colour faster than one without. Virginia's grade 1 standard asks what stems and leaves do; grade 4 adds how roots, stems and leaves work together.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay nearby while the coloured water is out. An adult does all the cutting, and children watch from across the board.
- Food colouring stains skin, clothes, counters and carpets. Work on a tray, wear old clothes, and wipe drips right away so no one slips.
- Use sturdy cups or jars that won't tip, plastic if you have them, and keep them out of reach of toddlers and pets, who may knock them over or drink from them. If one breaks, an adult cleans it up.
- Don't eat the celery or drink the coloured water. They sit out for hours at room temperature and get handled. Throw both away at the end, and wash hands with soap and water after handling them.
- If anyone in the group is allergic to celery, or gets an itchy mouth from raw celery, leave the celery out for everyone and use white carnations or a napa cabbage leaf instead. If you try other flowers, use store-bought white carnations, not plants from outdoors.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Mix the colour.** Fill each glass with about 5 cm of water. Stir in 15 to 20 drops of food colouring per glass. A dark colour is much easier to see.
2. **Fresh cut.** You trim about 2 cm off the bottom of each stalk just before it goes in the water. A fresh cut takes up water well; a dried-out end is slow. From one stalk, also cut off all the leaves.
3. **Predict.** Before the stalks go in, ask your child where the colour will go and how long it will take. Which stalk will change first, the one with leaves or the one without? Write or draw their guesses.
4. **Stand them up.** Put one leafy stalk and the leafless stalk in two glasses of the same colour. Keep the third leafy stalk for the two-colour test in "Ages 8 to 10", or add it as a spare. Put the glasses in a bright spot out of direct sun.
5. **Check after about 1 hour.** Little may show yet. Look at the bottom of the stalk and the leaf edges, using the magnifying glass.
6. **Check after about 4 hours.** Most often, coloured lines show along the ribs of the stalk and the leaf edges begin to colour. Compare the two stalks. Times vary with the room's warmth, the light, how strong the dye is and how fresh the celery is.
7. **Check the next day.** Overnight usually gives the clearest result, with coloured veins in the leaves. You cut across the stalk about 3 cm above the water. Look at the cut end: a curved row of coloured dots. Each dot is a bundle of water tubes.
8. **Peel a string.** Snap a short piece of stalk and pull out one of the stringy strands. Some strands hold bundles of water tubes and may show colour; the others are tough fibres that hold the stalk firm and stay pale.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "Where do you think the blue will show up first?"
- "I wonder if the stalk with no leaves will change as fast. Why or why not?"
- "What do you think we will see when we cut across the stalk?"
- "Where does a real celery plant get its water?"
- "What do you think would happen if we used two colours on one stalk?"
- "I wonder if a flower would do the same thing."

## Why it works

The water tubes are part of a tissue called **xylem**. Many of its cells are long and hollow, and they die when mature, leaving open pipes joined end to end.

The pull comes mostly from the leaves. Water evaporates from inside the leaf and escapes through tiny pores called **stomata**. Water molecules cling to each other, so as some leave at the top, the whole thin column is drawn up behind them. This loss of water from leaves is called **transpiration**. Water also climbs a short way up very narrow tubes by itself, but that cannot explain how it reaches the top of a tall tree.

A stalk with no leaves has far fewer pores, so it usually colours more slowly. Celery varies, so treat this as a test, not a sure thing. The dots in a curved row are the stalk's vascular bundles, which also carry sugar from the leaves in a second set of tubes. The tough "strings" of celery are mostly support fibres that sit just outside the bundles.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use one leafy stalk in one colour and skip the leafless comparison. Check twice, after about 4 hours and the next morning. Your child draws the stalk each time and colours in where the blue has reached. Let them use the magnifying glass on the cut end and count the dots.

### Ages 8 to 10
Keep the leaf comparison and add a split stalk. You cut up the middle of the bottom 8 to 10 cm of a leafy stalk. Stand one half in blue and the other in red, in two glasses side by side. Predict what the leaves will look like. Measure in centimetres how far the colour has climbed at each check and record it in a table.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If nothing shows after several hours, the dye may be too weak or the cut end may have dried. Add more colouring and have the adult trim a fresh 2 cm off the bottom. Old, limp celery is slow, so use a crisp bunch. Yellow and green dyes are hard to see; blue and red work best. A cool room slows everything, so give it until the next day.
