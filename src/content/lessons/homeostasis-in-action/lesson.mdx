## Explain: the negative feedback loop

<WordBank terms={['homeostasis', 'negative feedback', 'sensor', 'control center', 'effector', 'positive feedback', 'selectively permeable', 'passive transport', 'active transport', 'stomata', 'guard cells', 'hydrotropism']} />

**Homeostasis** is keeping internal conditions within a stable range while the outside changes. The word "range" matters. Your body temperature is usually near 37 °C, but it shifts a little through the day.

Most homeostasis works by **negative feedback**, a loop in which the response opposes the change that started it. "Negative" does not mean bad. It means the response pushes the other way.

Every negative feedback loop has the same parts:

- **A change.** Something pushes a variable away from its usual range.
- **A sensor**, a cell or organ that detects the change.
- **A control center**, which compares the sensed value with the usual range and sends a signal. In your body this is often the brain, but for some loops it is a gland, such as the pancreas for blood sugar.
- **An effector**, a muscle, gland or cell that carries out the response.
- **The response**, which pushes the variable back.

Here is the loop for body temperature on a hot day:

> body warms → temperature sensors in skin and brain → control center in the brain → sweat glands release sweat → sweat evaporates and the skin cools

On a cold day the same control center sends different signals. Muscles shiver, which releases heat, and blood vessels in the skin narrow, so less heat is lost.

A thermostat in a house is a fair model. It senses the room, compares it with a setting, and turns the heat on or off. The model breaks down in a few places: your body has many sensors and many effectors working at once, most of its responses are graded rather than simply on or off, and no one sets the dial.

A few loops run the other way. In **positive feedback**, the response makes the change bigger until some event ends it. Contractions during childbirth are the usual example.

## Explain: three examples, and the membrane

**Heart rate during exercise.** When you start to climb, nerve signals from your brain speed up your heart. Sensors in your working muscles and in your blood vessels add information, and the brain combines it with its own commands. A faster heart moves more blood past the muscles, bringing oxygen and carrying carbon dioxide away. During moderate exercise, faster breathing keeps the carbon dioxide level in your blood fairly steady, so carbon dioxide sensors are not the main reason your heart speeds up. Two sets of nerves reach the heart: one speeds it up and one slows it down. Exercise turns up the first and turns down the second. When you stop, the slowing nerve becomes active again, mostly in the first minutes, so heart rate falls fast at first and then more gradually. Exercise control is a little more than one feedback loop: the brain also raises heart rate as exercise begins, partly through commands sent before any large change in the blood, not only in response to such a change.

**Stomata in a leaf.** A leaf’s surface has tiny pores called **stomata** (one is a stoma). Each pore sits between two **guard cells**, which change shape to open or narrow it. Carbon dioxide enters through these pores and water vapor leaves through them. Experiments show that drier air can make guard cells close the pores, which slows water loss. The cost is that less carbon dioxide gets in. Temperature matters too, but not simply: in one 2020 study of Arabidopsis leaf tissue, warming opened the pores. In a whole plant on a hot afternoon, what the guard cells do depends on several signals together, including dry air and the water supply.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/homeostasis-in-action/stomata-open-and-narrowed.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a leaf turned to show its underside, with two zoomed views of one pore between two curved guard cells: open, with arrows for water vapor leaving and carbon dioxide entering, and narrowed, with thinner arrows." caption="Each stoma sits between two guard cells. When dry air makes them narrow the pore, the leaf loses water vapor more slowly, and less carbon dioxide gets in." />

**Roots and water.** Roots grow toward wetter soil, a response called **hydrotropism**. It is slow compared with a heartbeat, and it changes the direction of growth rather than a number inside the plant. It still helps keep the plant’s water supply steady. Scientists usually describe this as a response to the environment, not a complete feedback loop with a control center.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/homeostasis-in-action/root-bends-toward-water.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a cut-away of soil that is dry on the left and wet on the right, with a seedling whose root grows down and curves toward the wet side; faint dashed outlines show the root's earlier positions." caption="Hydrotropism: a root grows toward wetter soil. It changes the direction of growth, and biologists usually call it a response rather than a full feedback loop." />

**The cell membrane.** Every cell has its own homeostasis, and the membrane does much of the work. It is **selectively permeable**: it lets some substances cross and holds others back. In **passive transport**, substances move from where they are more concentrated to where they are less, and the cell spends no energy. In **active transport**, the cell spends energy to move substances against that direction, from less to more. Together these keep the inside of a cell different from the outside. [Crossing the Membrane](/lessons/crossing-the-membrane/) covers them in detail.

| Example | Change | Response | What stays steady |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exercise | Muscles work harder | Heart beats faster | Oxygen supply keeps up with what the muscles use (much of this is set ahead by the brain, not only by feedback) |
| Hot room | Body warms | Sweating | Body temperature |
| Dry air | Leaf loses water faster | Guard cells narrow stomata | Water in the leaf |
| Dry soil | Water is uneven around a root | Root bends toward water | Water supply to the plant |

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** hearts, guard cells, roots and cell membranes all respond to change in ways that keep conditions inside the organism within a range.</Support>

### Design problem

Plan a safe class investigation: how does heart rate change after the same short activity, and how long does it take to come back toward rest? Name your variables, what you keep the same, how many trials you run, and how you keep it safe and fair.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Safety and fairness first.** Anyone with a health condition, an injury, or a reason to sit out records data instead. Anyone can stop at any time. Stop and tell the teacher if you feel chest pain, dizziness, unusual trouble breathing, or unwell. This is a measurement, not a fitness test, so nobody's results are ranked or shared by name.
- **Independent variable:** time after the activity (0, 1, 2, 3 and 5 minutes).
- **Dependent variable:** heart rate in beats per minute.
- **Constants:** the same comfortable activity for the same time (for example, marching in place for one minute), the same recovery position (sitting), the same way of counting, the same room.
- **Control:** a resting heart rate taken after sitting quietly, before each trial.
- **Trials:** practise counting your pulse first. Run three trials on separate occasions, each with a fresh resting reading. At each time, mean = sum of the three values ÷ 3. Keep every value; mark a count you know was a mistake instead of deleting a result you did not expect.
- **Graph and meaning:** plot time on the x-axis and mean heart rate on the y-axis, with a line for your resting value. A steep early drop that flattens out fits the published data. A count over a full minute is an average for that minute, so record when each count started. Shorter counts, such as 15 seconds multiplied by 4, let you take readings closer together but are only estimates of the rate.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

When you run up the stairs, nerve signals speed your heart, and when you stop, the signals change and it slows over several minutes, back toward its resting range. On a dry afternoon, guard cells narrow a leaf’s stomata, so it loses water more slowly. Both are responses that hold conditions inside the organism within a range while the outside changes.
