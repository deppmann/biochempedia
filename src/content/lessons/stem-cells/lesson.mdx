techniques:
  - name: "Inducible Cre lineage tracing"
    blurb: >-
      A gene expressed in the cells of interest drives CreERT2, a Cre recombinase that enters the
      nucleus mainly after tamoxifen; a little leaks in without it, so untreated mice are a needed control. A pulse of tamoxifen lets Cre remove a stop sequence from a reporter
      gene, and the mark is inherited by every descendant. Looking weeks later shows what the
      labelled cells made.
  - name: "Multicolour clonal analysis (Confetti)"
    blurb: >-
      A reporter that, after Cre acts, expresses one of four fluorescent proteins at random gives
      neighbouring clones different colours most of the time. Counting how many clones survive and how large they
      grow over time tests models of how stem cells compete.
  - name: "Single-cell organoid culture and replating"
    blurb: >-
      One sorted cell is embedded in basement-membrane matrix with defined growth factors and
      grows into a crypt-villus organoid. Breaking the organoid up and replating it again and
      again tests sustained propagation under those stated conditions.
  - name: "Spleen colony transplantation assay"
    blurb: >-
      Marrow cells are injected into irradiated mice, and the colonies that appear in the spleen
      are counted and analysed. Colony number per cell injected, colony clonality and the lineages
      in each colony measure blood-forming stem and progenitor cells.

      symmetric. Strictly asymmetric division would give every clone exactly one stem cell

## What the lineage trace proves—and what it does not

The film supplies the moving evidence; the first task here is to judge it. A stem-cell population produces differentiated descendants and **self-renews**, meaning that a stem-cell pool persists over long periods. One division proves little because **transit-amplifying cells**, short-lived progenitors that divide a few times and then differentiate, also copy themselves briefly. Repeated growth and replating from one cultured cell or transplantation through successive hosts can test persistence.

The small-intestinal **epithelium**, its cellular lining, folds into finger-like villi above pocket-like crypts (see [epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/)). Absorptive, mucus-secreting goblet and hormone-secreting enteroendocrine cells move up from crypts and are shed at villus tips. Paneth cells instead move down, live for weeks and secrete antibacterial proteins; slender crypt-base columnar cells sit between them.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/stem-cells/crypt-villus-cell-map.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a cutaway of a small-intestine villus above a crypt, with absorptive, goblet and enteroendocrine cells on the villus, cells shed at the tip, and an inset of the crypt base showing slender gold columnar cells wedged between granule-filled Paneth cells." caption="Cells are born in the crypt, move up the villus as they differentiate, and are shed at the tip. At the crypt base sit long-lived Paneth cells, which move down rather than up, with slender crypt-base columnar cells squeezed between them." />

In 2007 Nick Barker, Hans Clevers and colleagues found that *Lgr5*, a gene activated by Wnt signalling, is expressed in these cycling columnar cells in mouse small intestine and colon. Expression is a marker, not a fate test. In *Lgr5*-CreERT2 mice, one tamoxifen pulse permanently marks cells expressing *Lgr5* at that moment, and every daughter inherits the mark (the construct is explained in Going deeper; more in [tools for development](/lessons/tools-for-development/)). Label began at the crypt base and, over 60 days, filled ribbons containing every epithelial lineage.

That sustained, multilineage output supports the conclusion that the labelled population contains stem cells; it does not mean every initially marked cell survives. It shows **multipotency**, production of several cell types within one tissue, not **pluripotency**, the ability to make all lineages of the embryo proper. Stem cells need not be dormant, and their definition does not require one stem and one differentiated daughter from every division.

## Put neutral drift to work

The film's changing colours suggest a population rule; a prediction makes that rule quantitative. If every division produced one stem cell and one transit-amplifying cell, pool size would be fixed by asymmetric division. In 2010 Hugo Snippert, Clevers and colleagues tested that idea, and a fixed hierarchy, with Confetti: a reporter that switches on one of four fluorescent proteins so neighbouring clones usually differ in colour. Mouse small-intestinal Lgr5-high cells divided about once a day, most divisions were symmetric, and over 1 to 6 months surviving clones grew while others vanished. The dynamics are consistent with daughters stochastically retaining a stem-cell position or entering the transit-amplifying pool.

**Predict.** A low tamoxifen dose labels 100 crypts, each starting with one marked stem cell. Suppose each crypt has an effective pool of five equivalent competitors—an illustrative number smaller than the number of Lgr5-high cells counted per crypt. After every labelled clone has vanished or filled its crypt, how many fully labelled crypts do you expect?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only one equivalent competitor eventually fills a crypt, so each of the five is equally likely to win. One starting clone therefore has probability 1/5, giving 100 × 1/5 = 20 fully labelled crypts; the other 80 labelled clones are lost.

</details>

The point of the arithmetic is population balance on average. The start has 100 × 1 = 100 labelled stem cells, and the expected end has 20 × 5 = 100. At an intermediate time with 50 surviving clones, their mean size is 100/50 = 2 cells: survivors grow because others disappear. This is not a promise about any one crypt.

That pattern is **neutral drift**, chance competition among equivalent lineages for limited stem-cell positions. The film's handful of positions was the simple case; the fuller picture includes unequal short-term odds and a distinction between cells counted and cells effectively competing, developed in Going deeper.

## The niche: what limits a stem-cell position

The film treated positions as limited; this section supplies the mechanism. A stem-cell **niche** is the local environment whose contact and signals bias whether a daughter retains a stem-cell position or differentiates. Wnt signalling is strongest at the crypt base and promotes self-renewal; R-spondin, EGF and Notch also support the base, whereas BMP is strongest higher among differentiated cells and suppresses self-renewal. As daughters move up, they become more likely to leave the stem state. Paneth cells in the epithelium and **stromal cells**, connective-tissue cells beneath it, both contribute.

The experimental distinction is simple: adding support tests **sufficiency**, whereas removing it in the animal tests **requirement**. Loss followed by rescue is especially strong; no failure after removal can mean that another source compensated.

In 2009 Toshiro Sato and colleagues grew single sorted mouse Lgr5 cells into **organoids**, small crypt-villus structures made in culture. The cells sat in Matrigel, a basement-membrane matrix, with EGF, R-spondin 1 and Noggin, a BMP inhibitor, and could be replated repeatedly. No stromal cells were present, although matrix and added factors still supplied a niche (see [organoids](/lessons/organoids/)).

**Predict.** In 2011 Sato and colleagues found that Paneth cells express Wnt3, EGF, TGF-alpha and the Notch ligand Dll4. Paneth co-culture greatly improved organoid formation, and a Wnt pulse could substitute for Paneth cells. What does that show, and what does it not show?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Paneth cells can supply support under those culture conditions: a sufficiency result. It does not show that the living gut requires Paneth cells, because another source could substitute there. Requirement needs removal in the animal (see [Wnt signalling](/lessons/wnt-signaling/) and [Notch signalling](/lessons/notch-signaling/)).

</details>

### Design problem

Are Paneth cells indispensable in the living mouse gut? Design an in vivo test, state what “Paneth cells only” and “other sources can stand in” predict, and list controls.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Express the human diphtheria-toxin receptor from a Paneth-cell lysozyme gene, then give toxin to ablate Paneth cells while sparing other cells. Score Lgr5-positive cells, EdU incorporation (DNA synthesis in S phase; see [cell-cycle control](/lessons/cell-cycle-control/)) and output from a separate Lgr5 tracing pulse over days to weeks.

Separately delete *Porcn*, which encodes the enzyme Porcupine that lipidates Wnt for secretion, either in the epithelium or in stromal FOXL1-positive **telocytes**, flat cells with long extensions beneath the epithelium. If Paneth cells are the only niche, their ablation should remove Lgr5 cells and stall renewal, while telocyte *Porcn* deletion should do nothing. If sources can substitute, stem cells may persist after ablation; telocyte deletion then tests whether that source is required.

Controls include toxin in mice without the receptor, receptor mice without toxin, staining to confirm Paneth-cell removal and tamoxifen-only controls for Cre lines. The published results favour distributed support: telocyte *Porcn* deletion reduced crypt Wnt signalling and proliferation within 24 hours in 2018, whereas Lgr5 cells survived Paneth-specific ablation in 2019.

</details>

## After injury, other cells can refill the stem pool

The film tested normal renewal; injury asks whether cells outside that usual pool can regain its two jobs. This is a distinct condition, not an exception to the film's homeostatic result.

**Predict.** Secretory precursors are permanently labelled, and immediate staining finds no labelled Lgr5-positive cells. Lgr5-positive-cell killing is then confirmed. Weeks later, labelled Lgr5-positive cells and labelled epithelial ribbons appear. What is the narrowest conclusion?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Labelled precursors regained a stem-cell state after injury: they became Lgr5-positive cells whose descendants rebuilt the epithelium. The result does not make them pluripotent or show that they are normal stem cells before injury.

</details>

In 2020 Murata and colleagues found that after lethal injury to Lgr5 cells in mouse gut, absorptive and secretory progenitors **dedifferentiated**, meaning that they regained a tissue stem-cell state. They switched on *Ascl2* days before *Lgr5*, and restoration required ASCL2. Reviews describe related behaviour after other injuries, including irradiation, and from more than one precursor type. Cell fate is therefore less fixed than a one-way ladder suggests. Regaining gut stem activity is not becoming pluripotent; neural tissues pose related questions (see [adult neurogenesis](/lessons/adult-neurogenesis/) and [injury and regeneration](/lessons/injury-and-regeneration/)).

## Resetting beyond one tissue: Gurdon to Yamanaka

Injury shows that committed gut progenitors can regain a tissue stem-cell state. Nuclear transfer and reprogramming use different tools to ask a broader question: can a specialized nucleus be reset beyond its tissue?

**Potency** is the range of cell types a cell can produce. A **unipotent** cell makes one type; a multipotent cell makes several types within one tissue; a pluripotent cell makes the embryo proper's lineages; and a **totipotent** cell can make an entire conceptus, including placenta and other extra-embryonic tissues. The strongest test begins with one cell and measures its descendants.

In 1962 John Gurdon transplanted nuclei from intestinal cells of feeding *Xenopus* tadpoles into eggs whose own nuclei had been destroyed by ultraviolet light. Most failed, but a small fraction became normal feeding tadpoles.

**Predict.** Does the large number of failed nuclear-transfer eggs show that specialized nuclei had discarded developmental genes?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Each normal tadpole directed by a transplanted nucleus shows that at least that specialized nucleus retained the needed instructions and that egg cytoplasm could read them. Failures alone cannot identify what was missing, and a genetic nuclear marker ruled out a surviving egg nucleus.

</details>

In 2006 Kazutoshi Takahashi and Shinya Yamanaka used four transcription factors—Oct3/4, Sox2, Klf4 and c-Myc—to turn mouse fibroblasts into induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells under embryonic-stem-cell culture conditions (see [eukaryotic regulation](/lessons/eukaryotic-regulation/)). The cells formed tumours containing tissues from all three **germ layers**, the foundational layers of the embryo, and contributed to mouse embryos. In 2007 Yamanaka's laboratory reprogrammed adult human skin fibroblasts with the same factors, and James Thomson's laboratory reported human iPS cells made with a different set.

**Predict.** Mouse iPS cells contribute to tissues from all three embryonic germ layers. Which potency claim does that support: multipotent, pluripotent or totipotent?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Pluripotent. The result spans the embryo proper's lineages. It does not show that one cell can form an entire conceptus with extra-embryonic tissues, the test for totipotency.

</details>

At least some specialized nuclei therefore retain the instructions needed for development, and defined proteins can reset a specialized cell to pluripotency. Neither result shows that a specialized cell itself became totipotent, and passing a developmental test is separate from clinical safety.

## Going deeper

The film and main text use functional results first; this separate toolbox holds the historical assay, molecular mechanics and refinements needed to inspect those results closely.

### The functional definition came before Lgr5

In 1961 James Till and Ernest McCulloch injected marrow cells into irradiated mice and found that spleen nodule counts rose with the number of cells injected. In 1963, with Andrew Becker, chromosome markers showed that each colony was a clone. One cell, its descendants and a count of lineages set the pattern; later work from the group found new colony-forming cells inside colonies, evidence of self-renewal.

### How Cre lineage tracing works

Cre is a recombinase that cuts out DNA between two loxP sites pointing the same way. In CreERT2, fusion to a mutant estrogen-receptor domain keeps Cre mostly outside the nucleus until tamoxifen is given. Cre then removes a stop sequence from a Rosa26 reporter. Some Cre can leak into the nucleus without tamoxifen, so untreated mice are a necessary control.

### The fuller neutral-drift picture

Live imaging by Ritsma and colleagues in 2014 found that cells at the crypt bottom survive better than cells at its edge, but movement between positions makes the months-long outcome look neutral. The effective competitor number can therefore differ from the roughly 14 Lgr5-high cells counted per mouse small-intestinal crypt. With N = 14, 100 starting clones give 100/14, about 7, **fully labelled** crypts in expectation; counting these outcomes can estimate the effective number.

### What may replace Paneth-cell support

Sato and colleagues' 2011 study reported that Paneth-cell removal coincided with Lgr5-cell loss. In the later cell-specific ablation experiment, enteroendocrine and tuft cells moved into the gaps and were proposed to supply Notch ligand. A review notes that too few of these cells touched every stem cell, so the substitute support remains unsettled.

### Early and later iPS lines

The first mouse iPS clones, selected with an *Fbx15* reporter, did not make adult **chimaeras**, animals containing cells from both the host embryo and introduced iPS cells. Nanog-selected clones in 2007 did, and one passed through the germline. Thomson's human iPS cells used OCT4, SOX2, NANOG and LIN28. Reprogramming does not show that every iPS line matches embryonic stem cells in every test.
