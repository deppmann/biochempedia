techniques:
  - name: "Inducible Cre lineage tracing"
    blurb: >-
      A gene expressed in the cells of interest drives CreERT2, a Cre recombinase that enters the
      nucleus mainly after tamoxifen; a little leaks in without it, so untreated mice are a needed control. A pulse of tamoxifen lets Cre remove a stop sequence from a reporter
      gene, and the mark is inherited by every descendant. Looking weeks later shows what the
      labelled cells made.
  - name: "Multicolour clonal analysis (Confetti)"
    blurb: >-
      A reporter that, after Cre acts, expresses one of four fluorescent proteins at random gives
      neighbouring clones different colours most of the time. Counting how many clones survive and how large they
      grow over time tests models of how stem cells compete.
  - name: "Single-cell organoid culture and replating"
    blurb: >-
      One sorted cell is embedded in basement-membrane matrix with defined growth factors and
      grows into a crypt-villus organoid. Breaking the organoid up and replating it again and
      again tests sustained propagation under those stated conditions.
  - name: "Spleen colony transplantation assay"
    blurb: >-
      Marrow cells are injected into irradiated mice, and the colonies that appear in the spleen
      are counted and analysed. Colony number per cell injected, colony clonality and the lineages
      in each colony measure blood-forming stem and progenitor cells.

**The epithelium lining a mouse's small intestine is replaced every few days, yet the animal keeps it for life. Stem cells at the bottom of each crypt make that possible: they keep making new specialized cells, and they keep up their own numbers while they do it.**

Those two jobs, making differentiated descendants and maintaining the stem pool, are what make a cell a stem cell. The word is used loosely, so this lesson pins it down the way cell biologists do: by tests. You will see the lineage-tracing experiment that identified which gut cells do both jobs. Then you will see what their neighbours, the niche, contribute, what happens when the stem cells are killed, and how far a specialized cell can be reset.

## Two jobs, two tests: what makes a stem cell

A stem cell has two properties, and each has its own test.

**Self-renewal** means that dividing cells maintain a stem population over long periods. One division that makes a copy proves little, because fast-dividing transit-amplifying cells (short-lived progenitors that divide a few times and then differentiate) also make copies. The test is persistence: transplant the cells and then transplant their descendants into a second host, or grow them from one cell and replate them again and again.

**Potency** is the range of cell types a cell can produce. The scale runs from **totipotent** (one cell can make an entire conceptus, including placenta and other extra-embryonic tissues), through **pluripotent** (all the lineages of the embryo proper), **multipotent** (the several cell types of one tissue) and **unipotent** (one cell type). The test is clonal: start from one cell and count the kinds of descendant.

The pattern was set in 1961 by James Till and Ernest McCulloch. They injected marrow cells into irradiated mice and counted nodules in the spleen; the count rose in proportion to the cells injected, and in 1963, with Andrew Becker, chromosome markers showed that each colony was a clone. One cell, its descendants, and a count of lineages; later work from the same group added a check for new colony-forming cells inside colonies, a sign of self-renewal. Later definitions follow that template. Notice what is missing from it. Stem cells need not be dormant, and nothing in the definition says each division must be asymmetric. A marker is not a definition either. A marker is useful only after the functional tests have been passed.

## Proving it in the gut crypt

The small intestine is a sheet of epithelium folded into finger-like villi and pocket-like crypts (see [epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/)). Cells are born in the crypts, move up the villus as they differentiate into absorptive cells, mucus-secreting goblet cells and hormone-secreting enteroendocrine cells, and are shed at the tip. At the very bottom of each crypt sit Paneth cells, which secrete antibacterial proteins. Unlike the other cells, they move down rather than up after they are born, and they live for weeks. Squeezed between them are slender crypt-base columnar cells.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/stem-cells/crypt-villus-cell-map.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a cutaway of a small-intestine villus above a crypt, with absorptive, goblet and enteroendocrine cells on the villus, cells shed at the tip, and an inset of the crypt base showing slender gold columnar cells wedged between granule-filled Paneth cells." caption="Cells are born in the crypt, move up the villus as they differentiate, and are shed at the tip. At the crypt base sit long-lived Paneth cells, which move down rather than up, with slender crypt-base columnar cells squeezed between them." />

In 2007 Nick Barker, Hans Clevers and colleagues found that a Wnt target gene, *Lgr5*, is expressed in these cycling columnar cells in mouse small intestine and colon. Expression alone shows nothing about fate, so they traced the cells. In *Lgr5*-CreERT2 mice, one pulse of the drug tamoxifen puts a permanent genetic mark in cells expressing *Lgr5* at that moment, and every daughter inherits the mark (how the construct works is in Going deeper; more in [tools for development](/lessons/tools-for-development/)).

After one pulse, label began at the crypt base and, over 60 days, filled ribbons of epithelium containing every epithelial lineage. That is long-term multilineage output from cells in their own tissue: these cells keep making every kind of epithelial cell while the labelled pool persists, which the authors took as evidence that these cells are the stem cells. It shows multipotency within the gut, not pluripotency.

### How the pool stays the same size

If every division gave one stem cell and one transit-amplifying cell, the pool would hold steady by rule. In 2010 Hugo Snippert, Clevers and colleagues tested that cartoon with the Confetti reporter, which after Cre acts turns on one of four fluorescent proteins at random, so that neighbouring clones usually differ in colour. Mouse small-intestinal Lgr5-high cells divide about once a day, so these stem cells are busy, not dormant. Most divisions were symmetric. Each daughter then stayed a stem cell or left the niche by chance, as cells competed for space at the crypt base. Over 1 to 6 months, surviving clones grew while others vanished, and crypts drifted toward a single colour. The data fit **neutral drift**: chance, not a fixed hierarchy, decides which lineage wins, and balance is kept by the population, not by each division. Position near the niche biases the odds in the short term (worked numbers, live imaging and an interactive model are in Going deeper).

## The niche: local signals that balance staying and leaving

Stem cells do not keep their two jobs in balance on their own. The **niche**, the local surroundings of the stem pool, sends the signals that decide whether a daughter stays a stem cell or differentiates. In the crypt these include Wnt, R-spondin, EGF, Notch ligands and inhibitors of BMP signalling. Wnt signalling is strongest at the crypt base and promotes self-renewal. BMP signalling is strongest up among the differentiated cells and suppresses it. These opposing gradients mean that position biases fate: as daughters move up and away from the base, they become more likely to leave the stem state. The signals also come from more than one kind of cell, including Paneth cells in the epithelium and stromal cells beneath it.

The test for a niche is loss and rescue: remove a candidate signal or cell and ask whether the stem cells fail; if they do, restore the signal and see whether they recover. If nothing fails, another source may be compensating.

In 2009 Toshiro Sato and colleagues grew single sorted mouse Lgr5 cells into organoids, small crypt-villus structures, embedded in Matrigel (a basement-membrane matrix) with EGF, R-spondin 1 and Noggin (a BMP inhibitor). Organoids could be broken up and replated repeatedly. They contained no stromal cells, which made them a clean test of propagation, though matrix and growth factors still stood in for a niche (see [organoids](/lessons/organoids/)).

In 2011 Sato and colleagues reported that Paneth cells express Wnt3, EGF, TGF-alpha and the Notch ligand Dll4. Sorted stem cells formed organoids far better with Paneth cells beside them, and a pulse of Wnt could substitute for the Paneth cells. That is a clean sufficiency result in culture: Paneth cells can supply niche signals. Whether the living gut requires them is a separate question (for the pathways, see [Wnt signaling](/lessons/wnt-signaling/) and [Notch signaling](/lessons/notch-signaling/)).

## After injury, other cells can refill the stem pool

Lineage tracing showed which cells renew the gut day to day. It did not show that only those cells can rebuild it. When the Lgr5 stem cells are killed, the crypt can still recover. In 2020 Murata and colleagues found that after lethal injury to Lgr5 cells in mouse gut, absorptive and secretory progenitors dedifferentiated: they went back to a stem state and rebuilt the stem pool. Reviews of the field describe the same behaviour after other injuries, such as irradiation, and from more than one kind of precursor. In a tissue, then, fates are less fixed than a one-way ladder suggests.

That is an injury result. It does not mean mature cells act as stem cells during normal renewal, and regaining stem activity in the gut is not the same as becoming pluripotent. Neural stem cells raise similar questions (see [adult neurogenesis](/lessons/adult-neurogenesis/) and [injury and regeneration](/lessons/injury-and-regeneration/)).

## Resetting a cell all the way: Gurdon to Yamanaka

Injury shows that a gut cell can go back one step. A deeper question is whether a specialized cell still holds everything needed to make a whole embryo.

Does a specialized cell lose the genes it no longer uses? In 1962 John Gurdon transplanted nuclei from intestinal cells of feeding *Xenopus* tadpoles into eggs whose own nuclei had been destroyed by ultraviolet light. Most failed, but a small fraction became normal feeding tadpoles. At least some specialized nuclei still held the instructions; the egg could read them out again.

In 2006 Kazutoshi Takahashi and Shinya Yamanaka asked whether defined proteins could do what egg cytoplasm does. Four transcription factors, Oct3/4, Sox2, Klf4 and c-Myc, turned mouse fibroblasts into induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells under embryonic-stem-cell culture conditions (for how such factors reshape chromatin, see [eukaryotic regulation](/lessons/eukaryotic-regulation/)). The cells formed tumours with tissues of all three germ layers and contributed to mouse embryos. Those first lines did not make adult chimaeras; lines selected more stringently in 2007 did (details in Going deeper). Also in 2007, Yamanaka's laboratory reprogrammed adult human skin fibroblasts with the same four factors, and James Thomson's laboratory reported human iPS cells with a different set of factors.

Read the result for what it is. Specialization does not always throw away developmental potential: at least some specialized nuclei can be reset, and a specialized cell's state can be reset to pluripotency with a short list of defined proteins. Pluripotent is not totipotent, and passing developmental tests is a separate question from clinical safety.

### Design problem

Back to the niche section: the culture data make Paneth cells look like the niche. Are they indispensable in the living mouse gut? Design an in vivo test, state what "Paneth cells only" and "other sources can stand in" each predict, and list your controls.

<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Ablate Paneth cells genetically. Express the human diphtheria-toxin receptor from a Paneth-cell gene such as a lysozyme gene, so toxin kills Paneth cells and spares other cells. Separately, block Wnt secretion at its source. Delete Porcn, which encodes Porcupine, the enzyme that adds the lipid Wnt needs to be secreted, either in the epithelium or in stromal FOXL1-positive telocytes, flat cells with long extensions lying just beneath the epithelium.

Predictions: if Paneth cells are the only niche, ablation should lose Lgr5 cells and stall renewal, and telocyte Porcn deletion should do nothing. If other sources can stand in, stem cells persist after ablation, and telocyte Porcn deletion tests whether one of those other sources is itself required.

Controls: toxin given to mice without the receptor; receptor mice without toxin; staining to confirm how completely Paneth cells were removed; tamoxifen alone for the Cre lines.

The published mouse results favour a niche with more than one source. Michal Shoshkes-Carmel and colleagues (2018) found that telocyte Porcn deletion cut crypt Wnt signalling and proliferation within 24 hours. Johan van Es and colleagues (2019) found that Lgr5 cells survived Paneth ablation (who stands in for the Paneth cells is discussed in Going deeper).

So the niche is a set of signals with more than one source. Paneth cells supply some of them and help stem cells in culture, stromal telocytes are a needed Wnt source in vivo, and removing one source does not always remove the signal.

## Going deeper

These sections hold the methods and numbers behind the main line.

### How Cre lineage tracing works

The method relies on Cre, a recombinase that recombines two loxP sites and, when they point the same way, cuts out the DNA between them. Fused to a mutant estrogen-receptor domain (CreERT2), Cre stays mostly out of the nucleus until the animal receives tamoxifen, and it then removes a stop sequence from a Rosa26 reporter gene, leaving a mark every daughter inherits. A little Cre leaks into the nucleus without tamoxifen, so untreated mice are a needed control (more in [tools for development](/lessons/tools-for-development/)).

### Neutral drift in numbers

Later live imaging by Ritsma and colleagues (2014) refined the neutral-drift picture: cells at the very bottom of the crypt survive better than those at its edge, but cells move between these positions, so over months the outcome still looks neutral.

Here is the logic in numbers. The model below is illustrative, with round numbers chosen to show the arithmetic.

Suppose a low dose of tamoxifen labels 100 of 1,000 crypts, each with a single labelled stem cell at day 2. Assume each crypt holds N = 5 equivalent, competing stem cells. This is an effective number for the model; it is smaller than the roughly 14 Lgr5-high cells per crypt reported for the mouse small intestine (a figure from the clonal-tracing literature), possibly because not every marked cell competes equally.

> **probability a single clone takes over its crypt = 1 / N**

1. **Fixation.** With N = 5, each labelled clone takes over its crypt with probability 1/5 = 0.2. Expect 100 x 0.2 = 20 fully labelled crypts. The other 80 clones are lost.
2. **Conservation check.** Labelled stem cells at the start: 100 clones x 1 cell = 100. At the end: 20 crypts x 5 cells = 100. Under neutral drift the total is conserved on average.
3. **An intermediate time.** Suppose 50 labelled clones survive. Their mean size must be about 100 / 50 = 2 stem cells each. Surviving clones grow precisely because others vanish, the pattern Snippert and colleagues reported.

Change N to 14 and the expected count drops to 100 / 14, about 7 crypts. Counting monoclonal crypts therefore estimates how many cells really compete.

### Paneth cells in the living gut

Sato and colleagues' 2011 paper also reported that genetic removal of Paneth cells in vivo coincided with loss of Lgr5 cells. Later Paneth-specific ablation did not reproduce that result: when Johan van Es and colleagues (2019) killed Paneth cells alone, Lgr5 cells survived. Enteroendocrine and tuft cells moved into the gaps, and the authors proposed that they supplied Notch ligand instead. A later review notes that these cells were too few to touch every stem cell, so how the stem pool copes without Paneth cells is still open.

### The injury switch

In the Murata study, the progenitors that rebuilt the stem pool switched on *Ascl2* days before *Lgr5*, and restoration of the stem pool needed ASCL2.

### Early and later iPS lines

The first mouse iPS clones were selected with an *Fbx15* reporter and did not make adult chimaeras. Nanog-selected clones in 2007 did, and one passed through the germline. Thomson's human iPS cells used OCT4, SOX2, NANOG and LIN28. Reprogramming also does not show that every iPS line matches embryonic stem cells in every test.
