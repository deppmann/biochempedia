## Explain: how natural selection works

<WordBank terms={['natural selection', 'variation', 'inherited trait', 'fitness', 'adaptation', 'mutation', 'extinction', 'gradualism', 'punctuated equilibrium', 'species', 'speciation', 'geographic isolation']} />

**Natural selection** is the process in which individuals with some inherited traits survive and reproduce more than others, so those traits become more common in a population. It works only when three things are true at once.

- **Variation:** individuals differ in a trait. **Variation** means the differences among individuals in a population.
- **Inheritance:** the differences are passed to offspring. An **inherited trait** is a trait carried in genes from parent to young.
- **Unequal success:** the differences change how many offspring an individual leaves.

Where does variation come from? A **mutation** is a change in DNA, and it is the source of new versions of genes. Sexual reproduction then shuffles those versions into new combinations, as you saw in [that lesson](/lessons/meiosis-and-variation/). Gene versions can also arrive from other populations, and even from another species when two species hybridise, as Darwin's finches on Daphne Major sometimes do. Selection does not make variation. It sorts the variation that is there, including new mutations as they arise.

**Fitness** is how many offspring an individual leaves that survive to reproduce. An **adaptation** is an inherited trait that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its present environment. On Daphne Major in 1977, a deep beak was an adaptation. The study that measured the birds found that large birds, especially males with large beaks, survived best, because they could crack the large, hard seeds left after the drought.

No bird's beak grew because of the drought. The birds that died were, on average, different from the birds that lived. Studies of parents and young showed that beak size is strongly inherited, so the next generation's beaks were deeper on average too. Over many generations, selection like this can make a population better suited to its environment, but it does not produce a population of perfect fits. On Daphne Major, survivors and nonsurvivors overlapped a lot, and in later years the Grants saw selection on these finches change direction more than once as rainfall and seeds changed. Chance and genes arriving from other groups can change a population too.

## Explain: fast change, slow change and new species

Selection can be fast. Bacteria divide often, so an antibiotic can sort a population within days. In one laboratory study, *E. coli* grew resistant to a very high dose of an antibiotic in about 10 days, as new mutations arose and spread one after another. You can read more in [that lesson](/lessons/germs-vaccines-antibiotics/). Large animals can change in decades: poaching for ivory in Mozambique favoured female elephants with no tusks. Other changes build up over thousands or millions of years.

Biologists describe two patterns in the history of life.

| Pattern | What the change looks like | Picture |
|---|---|---|
| Gradualism | Small changes add up steadily over long periods | A ramp |
| Punctuated equilibrium | Long periods of little change, broken by bursts of change, mostly as new species form, that are fast in geologic time | A staircase |

**Gradualism** is the idea that change builds up in small steps over long periods. **Punctuated equilibrium** is the idea that species stay much the same for most of their history, and that most change happens in short bursts when new species branch off. A burst that is short in geologic time can still last thousands of years. This pattern comes from the fossil record. It is not the same thing as the fast changes in bacteria and elephants above. The two can occur together in one lineage at different times.

A species that cannot adapt to a change may suffer **extinction**, the death of every member of a species. Adapting does not guarantee rescue, though. A population can become better suited and still shrink to nothing if the environment worsens faster than it changes.

A **species** is a group of populations whose members interbreed in nature and produce fertile offspring, and that rarely breed with other groups. This is the biological species concept, the definition most biology courses use. It works only for organisms that reproduce sexually, and its edges are fuzzy: Darwin's finch species on Daphne Major still hybridise now and then. **Speciation** is the process in which one species splits into two or more groups that no longer interbreed and produce fertile offspring, or only rarely do. Speciation often starts with **geographic isolation**, the separation of groups of one species by a barrier such as a river, a mountain range or a canyon. On each side, different mutations arise and different traits are favoured, so the groups change in different ways.

Separation alone is not speciation. The Kaibab squirrel on the north side of the Grand Canyon looks different from the Abert's squirrels on the south side, yet biologists usually treat it as a subspecies of Abert's squirrel, so as one species. They show a split that may or may not be finished. Speciation can also happen without a barrier. It can also begin fast, and not only by splitting. On Daphne Major, an immigrant male of another finch species bred with a resident medium ground finch. Their hybrid descendants bred only among themselves and stopped breeding with the island's other finches within three generations. This special case, where two species give rise to a third, is called hybrid speciation.

### Design problem

A different island has a population of seed-eating finches that has never been studied. Plan a study that could show whether the next drought selects on beak depth.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Before any drought, band as many birds as you can (a few hundred if possible) and measure each beak depth three times with the same tool, then take the mean. Record body mass too, since big birds tend to have big beaks. Keep the method, measurer and season constant. Handle each bird briefly, release it unharmed, and work under the permits wildlife studies require.

After a drought, record which banded birds are still alive. Beak depth is the independent variable and survival the dependent variable. Compare the mean beak depth of survivors and nonsurvivors. A wet year is your comparison: if beak depth predicts survival only in the drought year, selection by the drought is the likely cause. Then measure the survivors' young; a higher mean in them, with young resembling their own parents, is evidence the change was inherited. Equal means in survivors and nonsurvivors would suggest that selection did not favour either deeper or shallower beaks.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

Because beak depth is inherited, their young had deeper beaks too, so the population changed between generations.
