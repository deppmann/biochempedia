## How natural selection changes a population

The first film named the three conditions. **Natural selection** is the process in which individuals with some inherited traits survive and reproduce more than others.

- **Variation:** the differences among individuals in a population, such as in beak depth.
- **Inheritance:** the differences pass to offspring. An **inherited trait** is carried in genes from parent to young.
- **Unequal reproductive success:** the differences change how many reproducing offspring an individual leaves.

**Fitness** is how many offspring an individual leaves that survive to reproduce. The third condition means fitness differs with the trait. Fitness does not mean strength: a strong animal that leaves no young has a fitness of zero.

A **mutation** is a change in DNA and the source of new versions of genes; sexual reproduction shuffles them (see [meiosis and variation](/lessons/meiosis-and-variation/)).

An **adaptation** is an inherited trait that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its present environment. On Daphne Major in 1977, a deep beak was an adaptation. Large birds, especially males with large beaks, survived best because they could crack the large, hard seeds left after the drought.

## From population change to a species split

For this lesson, a **species** is a group of populations whose members interbreed in nature and produce fertile offspring, while rarely breeding with other groups. This is the **biological species concept**, the definition most biology courses use. It works only for organisms that reproduce sexually, and its edges are fuzzy. Darwin's finch species on Daphne Major still hybridise now and then, so gene versions can even arrive from another species.

**Speciation** is the formation of new species. It often happens when one species splits into groups that become reproductively isolated: they no longer interbreed and produce fertile offspring in nature, or do so only rarely. A split often begins with **geographic isolation**, when a river, mountain range or canyon separates populations and reduces **gene flow**. Gene flow moves gene versions between populations when individuals, or sex cells such as pollen, arrive and add their genes.

## What the simple split leaves out

Real cases are less tidy than the second film's river. The Kaibab squirrel north of the Grand Canyon looks different from Abert's squirrels to the south. Yet biologists usually treat it as a subspecies of Abert's squirrel, so as one species. The split may or may not be finished.

Speciation can also begin fast, and not only by splitting. On Daphne Major, an immigrant male from another finch species bred with a resident medium ground finch. Their hybrid descendants bred only among themselves and stopped breeding with the island's other finches within three generations. This special case, in which two species give rise to a third, is **hybrid speciation**.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A barrier can start a split; interbreeding tests whether it is complete. If barriers prevent interbreeding, or allow only rare hybrids, the groups count as separate species.</Support>

## Fast change, slow change and extinction

Natural selection can shift a population within days or over millions of years, depending partly on how fast generations turn over. Bacteria divide often, so an antibiotic can sort a population within days. In one laboratory study, *E. coli* grew resistant to a very high antibiotic dose in about 10 days as mutations arose and spread one after another. Read more in [germs, vaccines and antibiotics](/lessons/germs-vaccines-antibiotics/). Large animals can change in decades: ivory poaching in Mozambique favoured female elephants with no tusks.

How fast one population changes is a different question from the two long-term patterns biologists see in the fossil record.

| Pattern | What the change looks like | Picture |
|---|---|---|
| **Gradualism** | Small changes add up steadily over long periods | A ramp |
| **Punctuated equilibrium** | Species stay much the same for most of their history; most change comes in bursts when new species branch, bursts that are fast in geologic time | A staircase |

A geologically short burst can still last thousands of years, so it is not the same as fast change in bacteria or elephants. Both patterns can occur in one lineage at different times.

A species that cannot adapt to change may suffer **extinction**, the death of every member. Adaptation does not guarantee rescue. A population can become better suited and still shrink to nothing if its environment worsens faster than it changes.

<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Before any drought, band as many birds as you can, a few hundred if possible. Measure each beak depth three times with the same tool, then take the mean. Record body mass too, since big birds tend to have big beaks. Keep the method, measurer and season constant. Handle each bird briefly, release it unharmed, and work under the permits wildlife studies require.

After a drought, record which banded birds are alive. Beak depth is the independent variable and survival the dependent variable. Compare the mean beak depth of survivors and nonsurvivors. Use a wet year as the comparison. Then measure the survivors' young. A higher mean in them, with young resembling their parents, is evidence that the change was inherited. Equal means in survivors and nonsurvivors would suggest that selection did not favour deeper or shallower beaks.

## Check your thinking

Use the evidence chain: the birds varied before the drought; beak depth was inherited; and birds with deeper beaks were more likely to survive and breed.
