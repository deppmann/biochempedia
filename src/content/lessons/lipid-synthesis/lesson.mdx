techniques:
  - name: "Isotopic precursor tracing"
    blurb: "Feed an animal acetate labeled with deuterium or carbon-14 and follow the atoms into the product. This is how Bloch and Rittenberg proved animals build cholesterol from two-carbon acetate, and how Bloch and Langdon later caught squalene as an on-pathway intermediate. Every one of cholesterol's 27 carbons traces back to acetyl-CoA, and the label is what proved it."
  - name: "Microbial natural-product screening"
    blurb: "Grow thousands of fungal strains, extract their broths, and assay each for the ability to shut down a target enzyme. Endo screened more than 6,000 strains to pull compactin from Penicillium citrinum; Alberts built a sensitive HMG-CoA reductase assay at Merck and pulled lovastatin from Aspergillus terreus. The principle: organisms make potent enzyme inhibitors as chemical weapons, and you can mine them."
  - name: "Radioligand binding and receptor-mediated endocytosis assays"
    blurb: "Tag LDL with radioactive iodine and measure how much a cell binds and internalizes. Brown and Goldstein showed normal fibroblasts gobble LDL while familial-hypercholesterolemia cells barely take it up — pointing to a defect in the receptor, not in cholesterol chemistry. Electron micrographs of one patient's cells then showed receptors that bind LDL but never cluster into clathrin-coated pits, so binding alone is not enough; location matters."
  - name: "P450 monooxygenase chemistry"
    blurb: "Cytochrome P450 enzymes install single oxygen atoms onto inert carbon scaffolds: R-H plus O2 plus NADPH yields R-OH plus water. Working with hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases, tissue-specific P450s turn the same cholesterol-derived backbone into cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, and estradiol: CYP11A1 cuts the side chain to make pregnenolone, CYP17A1 cuts it again on the way to androgens, and aromatase converts androgens to estrogens. A P450 also strips a methyl group off lanosterol on the way to cholesterol. Aromatase (CYP19A1) and CYP7A1, the 7alpha-hydroxylase that commits cholesterol to bile salts, are both P450s. Because each isoform runs particular steps, and each gland expresses its own set, a drug such as an aromatase inhibitor can block one branch without shutting down the rest."

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm 5"
    text: "Your body has a tiny building block called acetyl-CoA. It is like one kind of LEGO brick. With that same brick your cells can build three different things: fat to store energy, the walls around your cells, and a stiff, ring-shaped molecule called cholesterol that gets turned into messengers your body uses to grow and feel things. The amazing part is that the body has little on-off switches that decide which thing to build. A famous medicine called a statin flips one of those switches so your body makes less cholesterol, which helps keep the pipes that carry your blood clean."
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: "Most lipid carbon comes from one molecule, acetyl-CoA, which forks three ways: storage fat (triacylglycerol), membrane lipids (phospholipids), and cholesterol. Cholesterol is a 27-carbon molecule built entirely from two-carbon acetyl-CoA units in three stages: condense to mevalonate, assemble 5-carbon units up to 30-carbon squalene, then cyclize squalene into the four-ring sterol. The key enzyme is HMG-CoA reductase, which makes mevalonate. It is the rate-limiting step, and statins block it to lower cholesterol. Cholesterol travels through blood in lipoproteins like LDL ('bad') and HDL ('good'), and your cells grab LDL using a receptor that reads the protein ApoB-100. If that receptor is broken (familial hypercholesterolemia), cholesterol piles up in the blood. Cholesterol is also the starting material for steroid hormones, bile salts, and vitamin D."
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: "Lipid biosynthesis radiates from acetyl-CoA into three fates governed by three switches. The glycerolipid hub is phosphatidate (glycerol-3-P doubly acylated by GPAT and AGPAT); PAP/Lipin 1 dephosphorylates it toward DAG and triacylglycerol (storage), while reaction with CTP gives CDP-DAG for phospholipids (membrane). Lipin 1 is controlled largely by localization: less phosphorylated and ER-bound = more active (epinephrine, oleate in cultured fat cells), more phosphorylated and cytosolic = less active (insulin raises phosphorylation). Cholesterol synthesis runs condense (acetyl-CoA to HMG-CoA to mevalonate, via the committed HMG-CoA reductase + 2 NADPH), assemble (mevalonate + 3 ATP, lose CO2, to IPP; head-to-tail elongation to FPP; two FPP tail-to-tail to squalene), cyclize (epoxidation, then oxidosqualene cyclase to lanosterol; ~19 trim steps to cholesterol). HMG-CoA reductase is regulated four ways: SREBP transcription, mRNA/translation, AMPK phosphorylation at Ser872, and sterol-induced degradation. SREBP is released only when SCAP, free of Insig at low sterol, escorts it to the Golgi for S1P/S2P cleavage. Statins competitively inhibit the reductase and secondarily upregulate LDL receptors via SREBP. LDL is cleared by ApoB-100/LDL-receptor endocytosis; FH is a receptor defect; PCSK9 degrades the receptor and is itself a drug target."
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: "The chapter's load-bearing idea is regulatory architecture at a single committed node. HMG-CoA reductase is held under multi-timescale, multi-modal control to give >200-fold range. Four levers act on it: SREBP-2-driven transcription (a bHLH-zip factor that is sterol-sensed at SCAP, Insig-gated, and cleaved in the Golgi before it binds the SRE); translational attenuation by nonsterol mevalonate products; AMPK phosphorylation at Ser872 when energy is low; and Insig-dependent ER-associated degradation triggered by sterols and oxysterols. This layered design is why statin inhibition produces a paradoxical SREBP activation that upregulates LDLR and amplifies serum LDL clearance beyond the synthetic block itself. Mechanistically, note the conserved use of activated carriers (CDP-DAG and CDP-choline mirroring UDP-sugar logic) and pyrophosphate hydrolysis as the thermodynamic driver across prenyl couplings. The cyclization by oxidosqualene cyclase is a single catalytic event, a substrate-folding-controlled polycyclization that sets multiple stereocenters in one turnover, the canonical example of enzyme-templated cation-olefin cascade chemistry. The systems lesson, recurring from glycolysis, is that flux control tends to concentrate at irreversible reactions held far from equilibrium (in this pathway shared with squalene monooxygenase), and that sub-cellular localization (Lipin 1 membrane recruitment; SCAP trafficking) is itself a regulatory variable on par with mass action and covalent modification."

**Cells get cholesterol two ways: they build it from two-carbon acetyl-CoA, and they take it up as LDL. Sterol feedback through SREBP coordinates both, and statins work by blocking HMG-CoA reductase.** Blocking it lowers cell cholesterol, which turns on SREBP, which makes more LDL receptors, which clear more LDL from the blood. This chapter follows that chain from acetate to a 27-carbon sterol and back out to the bloodstream.

Cholesterol is one of three destinies for acetyl-CoA. The same two-carbon unit that burns for energy in the mitochondrion can instead be built up into storage fat, into the phospholipids of a membrane, or into cholesterol and the hormones it scaffolds. Three forks, three switches. The sterol switch (SREBP) governs cholesterol and is the main line of this chapter. The glycerolipid switch (PAP, the lipin enzymes) tilts carbon between storage and membrane. The energy switch (AMPK) brakes fatty-acid and cholesterol synthesis when fuel runs low. Learn where each switch sits and you can predict what a fed liver, a fasting liver, or a statin-treated liver will do with its acetyl-CoA.

## The phosphatidate hub: where storage and membrane lipids part ways

Start with the fats and membranes, because they share a backbone. Glycerol 3-phosphate gets acylated twice. GPAT adds the first fatty acid at the sn-1 position, making lysophosphatidate; AGPAT adds the second at sn-2, making **phosphatidate**. And note the name carefully. Phosphatidate is diacylglycerol-3-phosphate; it is not the same as diacylglycerol. The phosphate is the whole point.

Phosphatidate is the fork. PAP activity, carried by the lipin enzymes (Lipin 1 is the best studied of three), removes that phosphate to give diacylglycerol, which is then acylated a third time into triacylglycerol, the storage form. If instead phosphatidate reacts with CTP, it becomes CDP-diacylglycerol, the activated donor for phosphatidylinositol, phosphatidylglycerol, and cardiolipin (and, in bacteria and yeast, phosphatidylserine). So PAP does not send carbon only to storage. Diacylglycerol feeds membranes too: the two most abundant membrane lipids, phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine, are made by handing diacylglycerol an activated head group from CDP-choline or CDP-ethanolamine.

PAP is also controlled by where it sits: Lipin 1 that binds the ER membrane reaches its substrate and makes diacylglycerol, and Lipin 1 held in the cytosol does not. The effects depend on the lipin paralog, its phosphorylation and the cell type.

This is the same activated-intermediate logic you have already seen with UDP-sugars. Making the CDP-activated head group releases pyrophosphate, and its hydrolysis is what pulls the reaction forward.

Sphingolipids run on a different backbone entirely. Palmitoyl-CoA condenses with serine, and after a few steps you reach **ceramide**, the sphingolipid hub. Add phosphocholine and you get sphingomyelin; add sugars and you get cerebrosides and then gangliosides. The clinical anchor is Tay-Sachs disease: loss of hexosaminidase A blocks breakdown of the ganglioside GM2, which builds up in the lysosomes of neurons, and the result is fatal neurodegeneration.

## The cholesterol spine: condense, assemble, cyclize

Now the third fork, and the spine of the chapter. Cholesterol is 27 carbons, every one of them delivered by two-carbon acetyl-CoA, across roughly thirty enzymatic steps. Konrad Bloch and his colleagues proved this with labeled acetate, and the proof took a detour through a shark tank in Bermuda (the story is under Stories & asides). Hold three words in mind: condense, assemble, cyclize.

**Condense** (cytosolic). Two acetyl-CoA make acetoacetyl-CoA; a third gives HMG-CoA; then HMG-CoA reductase uses two NADPH to make **mevalonate**. That reductase step commits carbon to the mevalonate pathway and is the main rate-limiting, irreversible step for cholesterol, and it is the single most important enzyme in this chapter. The first step committed to sterols alone comes later, at squalene synthase.

**Assemble.** Mevalonate is phosphorylated by three ATP and decarboxylated to **isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP)**, the universal five-carbon isoprenoid unit. IPP and its isomer DMAPP add head-to-tail: C5 plus C5 gives geranyl-PP (C10), plus another C5 gives farnesyl-PP (C15). Then two farnesyl-PP join tail-to-tail to make **squalene** (C30). Each coupling spits out pyrophosphate.

**Cyclize.** Squalene monooxygenase adds an epoxide; then oxidosqualene cyclase runs one cascade that builds all four fused rings and sets the stereochemistry in a single turnover. A linear chain becomes a sterol in one of the most elegant enzyme steps in all of biochemistry. The product, lanosterol, is then trimmed over roughly nineteen steps to cholesterol. Mevalonate is not only the road to cholesterol. The same intermediate feeds CoQ10, dolichol, the side chain of one form of vitamin K (MK-4, built on a ring taken from dietary vitamin K), and the prenyl anchors (farnesyl-PP, geranylgeranyl-PP) that tether signaling proteins like Ras to membranes. That branching is exactly why statins, which block the step that makes mevalonate, have effects beyond cholesterol.

## One enzyme, four controls, and the statin

The cell regulates HMG-CoA reductase at four levels, on timescales from minutes to hours that overlap. Overlapping controls act on both the activity and the amount of the enzyme. The four levels:

- **Phosphorylation (minutes).** **AMPK** phosphorylates the enzyme and switches it off when ATP is low.
- **mRNA and translation (minutes to hours).** Nonsterol products made from mevalonate slow translation of the reductase mRNA.
- **Transcription (hours).** **SREBP** drives the reductase gene when cholesterol is low.
- **Degradation (tens of minutes to hours).** Accumulating sterols, chiefly lanosterol-type intermediates and oxysterols, with help from nonsterol isoprenoids, trigger the enzyme's own proteolytic destruction in the ER.

SREBP itself is a small mechanism worth getting right. When cholesterol is low, SCAP escorts SREBP from the ER to the Golgi inside a COPII vesicle. Two proteases, S1P then S2P, clip SREBP, releasing a transcription factor that travels to the nucleus, binds the sterol regulatory element, and turns ON the reductase and the LDL receptor. That cholesterol arm is SREBP-2. A sister form, SREBP-1c, drives fatty acid synthase and answers mainly to insulin. When cholesterol is high, it binds SCAP's sterol-sensing domain, which recruits Insig and locks the whole complex in the ER. Get the wiring right: Insig binds SCAP, not SREBP.

Statins exploit all of this. They are nanomolar competitive inhibitors shaped like the reaction intermediate, so hepatic cholesterol synthesis drops. The lower cell cholesterol then turns SREBP ON, the liver makes more LDL receptors, and serum LDL falls, on average by 30 to 50 percent. That is the double hit, and it is why a drug that blocks synthesis ends up working largely by boosting uptake.

## Transport, uptake, and the five identities

Cholesterol does not dissolve in blood, so it rides in lipoproteins: a shell of phospholipid, free cholesterol, and apolipoprotein around a core of triacylglycerol and cholesteryl ester. ApoB-100 is the address label the LDL receptor reads. The liver secretes VLDL; lipoprotein lipase strips its triglyceride to make IDL; hepatic lipase finishes the job to make LDL. LDL is made in the blood, not secreted as such.

Cells take LDL up by receptor-mediated endocytosis. ApoB-100 binds the receptor, and the receptors cluster into a clathrin-coated pit that pinches off as a vesicle. In the acidic endosome the LDL lets go of its receptor. The receptor recycles to the cell surface, while the LDL continues to the lysosome, which breaks it down and releases free cholesterol. HDL runs the traffic in reverse, pulling cholesterol back to the liver.

Familial hypercholesterolemia, an inherited disorder usually caused by an LDL-receptor mutation and found in roughly one person in 250, is the disease that cracked the whole system open. Brown and Goldstein proved it is a disorder of uptake, not synthesis. PCSK9, a secreted protein, binds the LDL receptor and sends it to the lysosome to be destroyed, so fewer receptors return to the surface. That makes PCSK9 a newer drug target: inhibitors spare the receptors, and the antibodies lower LDL by about 55 percent, alone or on top of a statin.

Finally, the payoff. Cholesterol is biology's Swiss army knife, one scaffold with five identities: bile salts (committed by CYP7A1), glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids and sex steroids (tailored by tissue-specific enzymes such as the P450 aromatase and 5alpha-reductase), and vitamin D (a secosteroid hormone made in skin from 7-dehydrocholesterol, the last precursor before cholesterol, then activated in liver and kidney). Many drugs in this chapter, from statins to finasteride (a 5alpha-reductase inhibitor) to aromatase inhibitors, work by blocking one known enzyme in the pathway. ## Going deeper

### Lipin 1 localization in cultured fat cells

Less phosphorylated Lipin 1 binds the ER membrane, where its substrate lives, and more diacylglycerol is made. More heavily phosphorylated Lipin 1 stays mostly in the cytosol, away from its substrate, and less is made. In cultured fat cells, epinephrine and the fatty acid oleate dephosphorylate Lipin 1 and move it onto the membrane, while insulin, surprisingly, phosphorylates it and shifts more of it into the cytosol. Losing Lipin 1 causes lipodystrophy in mice and recurrent muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis) in children. Where an enzyme sits can matter as much as how much of it there is.

### Position of the fatty acids on phosphatidate

Note the position chemistry, because it shows up in downstream lipids: sn-1 tends to carry a saturated chain, sn-2 an unsaturated one.

### The numbers behind HMG-CoA reductase control

In cultured cells, sterol and nonsterol feedback together can change the amount of enzyme more than 200-fold. AMPK phosphorylates the enzyme at Ser872. Sterol-triggered degradation can cut the enzyme's half-life more than tenfold.
