techniques:
  - name: "Isotopic precursor tracing"
    blurb: "Feed an animal acetate labeled with deuterium or carbon-14 and follow the atoms into the product. This is how Bloch and Rittenberg proved animals build cholesterol from two-carbon acetate, and how Bloch and Langdon later caught squalene as an on-pathway intermediate. Every one of cholesterol's 27 carbons traces back to acetyl-CoA, and the label is what proved it."
  - name: "Microbial natural-product screening"
    blurb: "Grow thousands of fungal strains, extract their broths, and assay each for the ability to shut down a target enzyme. Endo screened more than 6,000 strains to pull compactin from Penicillium citrinum; Alberts built a sensitive HMG-CoA reductase assay at Merck and pulled lovastatin from Aspergillus terreus. The principle: organisms make potent enzyme inhibitors as chemical weapons, and you can mine them."
  - name: "Radioligand binding and receptor-mediated endocytosis assays"
    blurb: "Tag LDL with radioactive iodine and measure how much a cell binds and internalizes. Brown and Goldstein showed normal fibroblasts gobble LDL while familial-hypercholesterolemia cells barely take it up — pointing to a defect in the receptor, not in cholesterol chemistry. Electron micrographs of one patient's cells then showed receptors that bind LDL but never cluster into clathrin-coated pits, so binding alone is not enough; location matters."
  - name: "P450 monooxygenase chemistry"
    blurb: "Cytochrome P450 enzymes install single oxygen atoms onto inert carbon scaffolds: R-H plus O2 plus NADPH yields R-OH plus water. Working with hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases, tissue-specific P450s turn the same cholesterol-derived backbone into cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, and estradiol: CYP11A1 cuts the side chain to make pregnenolone, CYP17A1 cuts it again on the way to androgens, and aromatase converts androgens to estrogens. A P450 also strips a methyl group off lanosterol on the way to cholesterol. Aromatase (CYP19A1) and CYP7A1, the 7alpha-hydroxylase that commits cholesterol to bile salts, are both P450s. Because each isoform runs particular steps, and each gland expresses its own set, a drug such as an aromatase inhibitor can block one branch without shutting down the rest."

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm 5"
    text: "Your body has a tiny building block called acetyl-CoA. It is like one kind of LEGO brick. With that same brick your cells can build three different things: fat to store energy, the walls around your cells, and a stiff, ring-shaped molecule called cholesterol that gets turned into messengers your body uses to grow and feel things. The amazing part is that the body has little on-off switches that decide which thing to build. A famous medicine called a statin flips one of those switches so your body makes less cholesterol, which helps keep the pipes that carry your blood clean."
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: "Most lipid carbon comes from one molecule, acetyl-CoA, which forks three ways: storage fat (triacylglycerol), membrane lipids (phospholipids), and cholesterol. Cholesterol is a 27-carbon molecule built entirely from two-carbon acetyl-CoA units in three stages: condense to mevalonate, assemble 5-carbon units up to 30-carbon squalene, then cyclize squalene into the four-ring sterol. The key enzyme is HMG-CoA reductase, which makes mevalonate. It is the rate-limiting step, and statins block it to lower cholesterol. Cholesterol travels through blood in lipoproteins like LDL ('bad') and HDL ('good'), and your cells grab LDL using a receptor that reads the protein ApoB-100. If that receptor is broken (familial hypercholesterolemia), cholesterol piles up in the blood. Cholesterol is also the starting material for steroid hormones, bile salts, and vitamin D."
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: "A liver cell gets cholesterol two ways, by making it from acetyl-CoA and by importing LDL through the LDL receptor, and one ER sterol gauge (SCAP, Insig, SREBP-2) sets both. Cholesterol synthesis runs condense (acetyl-CoA to HMG-CoA to mevalonate, via the committed HMG-CoA reductase + 2 NADPH), assemble (mevalonate + 3 ATP, lose CO2, to IPP; head-to-tail elongation to FPP; two FPP joined at their pyrophosphate ends to squalene), cyclize (epoxidation, then oxidosqualene cyclase to lanosterol; ~19 trim steps to cholesterol). HMG-CoA reductase is regulated four ways: SREBP transcription, mRNA/translation, AMPK phosphorylation at Ser872, and sterol-induced degradation. SREBP is released only when SCAP, free of Insig at low sterol, escorts it to the Golgi for S1P/S2P cleavage. Statins competitively inhibit the reductase and secondarily upregulate LDL receptors via SREBP. LDL is cleared by ApoB-100/LDL-receptor endocytosis; FH is a receptor defect; PCSK9 degrades the receptor and is itself a drug target. Beyond cholesterol, acetyl-CoA also feeds storage fat and membrane lipids. The glycerolipid hub is phosphatidate (glycerol-3-P doubly acylated by GPAT and AGPAT); PAP/Lipin 1 dephosphorylates it toward DAG and triacylglycerol (storage), while reaction with CTP gives CDP-DAG for phospholipids (membrane). Lipin 1 is controlled largely by localization: less phosphorylated and ER-bound = more active (epinephrine, oleate in cultured fat cells), more phosphorylated and cytosolic = less active (insulin raises phosphorylation)."
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: "The chapter's load-bearing idea is one ER sterol gauge running two supply lines, synthesis and LDL uptake, with HMG-CoA reductase as the main throttle. The reductase is held under multi-timescale, multi-modal control to give >200-fold range. Four levers act on it: SREBP-2-driven transcription (a bHLH-zip factor that is sterol-sensed at SCAP, Insig-gated, and cleaved in the Golgi before it binds the SRE); translational attenuation by nonsterol mevalonate products; AMPK phosphorylation at Ser872 when energy is low; and Insig-dependent ER-associated degradation triggered by sterols and oxysterols. This layered design is why statin inhibition produces a paradoxical SREBP activation that upregulates LDLR and amplifies serum LDL clearance beyond the synthetic block itself. Mechanistically, note the conserved use of activated carriers (CDP-DAG and CDP-choline mirroring UDP-sugar logic) and pyrophosphate hydrolysis as the thermodynamic driver across prenyl couplings. The cyclization by oxidosqualene cyclase is a single catalytic event, a substrate-folding-controlled polycyclization that sets multiple stereocenters in one turnover, the canonical example of enzyme-templated cation-olefin cascade chemistry. The systems lesson, recurring from glycolysis, is that flux control tends to concentrate at irreversible reactions held far from equilibrium (in this pathway shared with squalene monooxygenase), and that sub-cellular localization (Lipin 1 membrane recruitment; SCAP trafficking) is itself a regulatory variable on par with mass action and covalent modification."

**Children with familial hypercholesterolemia have blood flooded with LDL, the particle that carries cholesterol. Yet when Michael Brown and Joseph Goldstein grew skin cells from these children, the cells kept making cholesterol in conditions where normal cells shut synthesis down.**

Brown and Goldstein tagged LDL with radioactive iodine and measured how much each kind of cell took in. Normal cells gobbled it up. Cells from children with familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) barely touched it. Why would a cell bathed in cholesterol keep building it? Because the cell does not read the blood. It reads the cholesterol that has reached its own interior.

(Acetyl-CoA, the two-carbon unit that can burn for energy in the mitochondrion, also becomes storage fat and membrane phospholipids. The film above opens with all three fates; this lesson follows cholesterol, and the other two are in Going deeper. Building the fatty acids themselves has its own lesson, [fatty acid synthesis](/lessons/fatty-acid-synthesis/).)

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A liver cell gets cholesterol two ways: it **makes** it from acetyl-CoA, or it **imports** it as LDL through the LDL receptor. Both supply lines run off one gauge, the cholesterol level in the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). When the gauge reads low, the cell turns up **both**: the synthesis genes, led by HMG-CoA reductase (the main throttle), and the LDL-receptor gene. When it reads high, it turns both down. So lowering ER cholesterol in liver cells, whether with a statin, a drug that blocks cholesterol absorption or a bile-acid resin, tends to raise LDL receptors and pull LDL out of the blood. The parts of the gauge (SCAP, SREBP-2 and Insig) come one at a time below.

The rule covers the sterol arm in liver cells, which carry most of the body's LDL receptors. It has four limits, listed after the drugs.

## The spine: condense, assemble, cyclize

All 27 of cholesterol's carbons come from acetyl-CoA, across roughly thirty enzymatic steps, as Konrad Bloch and his colleagues proved with labeled acetate (Stories & asides has the shark-tank detour).

**Condense** (in the cytosol, ending on the ER membrane). Two acetyl-CoA make acetoacetyl-CoA, and a third gives HMG-CoA, a six-carbon molecule. Then HMG-CoA reductase uses two NADPH to make **mevalonate**. The reductase sits in the ER membrane, its working end facing the cytosol. It runs the main rate-limiting, irreversible step, and it is the most important enzyme in this lesson.

**Assemble.** Three ATP phosphorylate mevalonate, and it loses one CO₂ to become **isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP)**, the universal five-carbon unit. Molecules built from these C5 units are called isoprenoids. Each C5 unit has a pyrophosphate "head" and a branched "tail". IPP and its isomer DMAPP join head to tail, releasing pyrophosphate each time: C5 plus C5 gives geranyl-PP (C10), and one more C5 gives farnesyl-PP (C15). Squalene synthase then joins two farnesyl-PP at their heads, the pyrophosphate ends, releasing both pyrophosphates and using one NADPH. The product is **squalene** (C30), and because the heads were joined, it has a tail at each end. (Textbooks name this one reaction both ways, "head to head" and "tail to tail"; it is the same coupling.) Hydrolysis of the released pyrophosphate pulls each coupling forward.

**Cyclize.** Squalene monooxygenase uses O₂ and NADPH to put an epoxide on one end of squalene, making 2,3-oxidosqualene. Oxidosqualene cyclase then builds all four fused rings and sets the stereochemistry in a single turnover, one of the most elegant enzyme steps in biochemistry. The product is **lanosterol**. Roughly nineteen more steps, many using O₂ and NADPH, trim it to cholesterol. (The film splits the same road slightly differently: its first stage runs all the way to the isoprene unit.)

## Where the throttle sits

Mevalonate is not only the road to cholesterol. Its C5 units also feed CoQ10, dolichol, the side chain of one form of vitamin K, and the prenyl anchors (farnesyl-PP, geranylgeranyl-PP) that tether signaling proteins such as Ras to membranes. So the pathway is a trunk that branches: everything up to farnesyl-PP is shared, and carbon turns into the sterol branch only at squalene synthase.

The cell puts its main brake at the reductase, so it stops carbon before any ATP is spent on the phosphorylation steps. A step is called committed when carbon that passes it has left the general pool for one pathway: the reductase commits carbon to the mevalonate trunk (which still branches), and squalene synthase commits it to sterols alone. The reductase is the main throttle, not the only one. Squalene monooxygenase is a second regulated point, and the gauge raises nearly every enzyme of the pathway.

## How LDL gets in

The second supply line starts in the blood, so follow the particle the liver's receptors pull back in. Cholesterol does not dissolve in blood, so it rides in lipoproteins: a shell of phospholipid, free cholesterol and apolipoprotein around a core of triacylglycerol and cholesteryl ester. ApoB-100 is the address label the LDL receptor reads. The liver secretes VLDL (very-low-density lipoprotein); lipoprotein lipase strips its triglyceride to make IDL (intermediate-density lipoprotein); hepatic lipase finishes the job to make LDL. LDL is made in the blood, not secreted as such.

Cells take LDL up by receptor-mediated endocytosis. ApoB-100 binds the receptor, and the receptors cluster into a clathrin-coated pit that pinches off as a vesicle. In the acidic endosome the LDL lets go of its receptor. The receptor recycles to the cell surface, while the LDL goes on to the lysosome, which breaks it down and releases free cholesterol. HDL runs the traffic in reverse, pulling cholesterol back to the liver.

If the receptor is missing or broken, LDL stays in the blood. That is familial hypercholesterolemia, an inherited disorder usually caused by an LDL-receptor mutation, found in roughly one person in 250, and the disease that cracked the whole system open. Brown and Goldstein proved FH is a disorder of uptake, not synthesis. Even a receptor that binds LDL fails if it never gathers into a pit (Stories & asides, "Binding is not enough").

## One gauge, two supply lines

Making cholesterol is expensive and importing it is cheap. The cell sets both with one reading: how much cholesterol sits in its ER membrane, the membrane that holds the reductase. The gauge has three parts. **SREBP-2** (sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2) is a transcription factor made in an inactive form stuck in the ER membrane; it is the passenger. **SCAP** is its escort, and SCAP's sterol-sensing domain is what actually reads cholesterol. **Insig** is an ER membrane protein that grabs SCAP once SCAP has bound cholesterol.

| | ER cholesterol low | ER cholesterol high |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SCAP | has no cholesterol bound, so Insig leaves it alone | binds cholesterol, changes shape, and is grabbed by Insig |
| The SCAP-SREBP-2 pair | is packed into COPII vesicles bound for the Golgi | is held in the ER |
| SREBP-2 | is cut twice in the Golgi, first by the protease S1P and then by S2P; the freed piece enters the nucleus and binds the sterol regulatory element | never reaches the proteases and stays inactive |
| Genes switched on | the reductase and the other synthesis enzymes, **and the LDL receptor** | none of them, so their expression falls |

The bottom row is the point: one switch turns on **both** supply lines.

If this wiring feels slippery, that is normal; it is where most exam points are lost. Cholesterol binds SCAP, and Insig binds SCAP. Neither acts on SREBP directly; SREBP is the passenger that either reaches its stop or does not. (Oxysterols such as 25-hydroxycholesterol bind Insig instead of SCAP; either way SCAP is held in the ER.) The film says the SREBP pathway "senses" cholesterol; strictly, it is SCAP within that pathway that does the sensing.

The gauge works over hours, because it changes how much protein is made. The reductase also has faster controls, each reading something specific.

| Control on the reductase | What it reads | What it does | How fast |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Transcription (SREBP-2) | ER cholesterol | changes how much reductase is made | hours |
| Degradation | sterols, chiefly lanosterol-type intermediates and oxysterols, helped by nonsterol isoprenoids | when they are high, Insig sends the reductase for destruction in the ER | tens of minutes to hours |
| Translation | nonsterol products of mevalonate | slows reading of the reductase mRNA | minutes to hours |
| Phosphorylation (AMPK) | the cell's energy charge, not sterols | switches the enzyme off when ATP is low | minutes |

Three of the four read the pathway's own products; only AMPK reads fuel, a famine brake on an expensive molecule. The film calls the four layers "independent", but they overlap: sterols act through Insig at two of them. Of the four, only the transcription control, the gauge itself, also sets the LDL receptor.

## Same gauge, more drugs

SREBP-2 also turns on the gene for **PCSK9**, a secreted protein that binds the LDL receptor and sends it to the lysosome to be destroyed, so fewer receptors return to the surface. A statin therefore raises PCSK9 along with the receptors, and gives back part of its gain.

Two more drugs act from the gut side. Ezetimibe blocks cholesterol absorption, so less cholesterol reaches the liver from the gut. Bile-acid resins trap bile acids in the gut, so the liver converts more of its own cholesterol into new bile acids. **Where the rule stops.**

- **The energy brake is separate.** AMPK (last row of the table) also switches off fatty-acid synthesis, and it acts mainly on the enzyme itself, not through the sterol gauge.
- **The gauge brakes itself** through PCSK9, as above.
- **It needs working receptors.** Without them, as in the FH child, the gauge calls for receptors that cannot be added, so a statin loses most of its LDL-lowering step.
- **Fat synthesis follows a different lead.** A sister factor, SREBP-1c, also escorted by SCAP, drives fatty acid synthase and answers mainly to insulin.

## What cholesterol becomes

Most of a cell's cholesterol stays in its membranes, and excess is stored as cholesteryl ester (the liver also exports it in lipoproteins). In particular tissues, cholesterol, or for vitamin D a late precursor, is also the scaffold for three kinds of product, each tailored by known enzymes:

- **Bile salts.** CYP7A1, the 7alpha-hydroxylase, commits cholesterol to bile salts.
- **Steroid hormones** (glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids and sex steroids), tailored by tissue-specific enzymes such as the P450 aromatase, which makes estrogens, and 5alpha-reductase.
- **Vitamin D**, a secosteroid hormone made in skin from 7-dehydrocholesterol, a late precursor of cholesterol, then activated in liver and kidney.

Many drugs in this lesson, from statins to finasteride (a 5alpha-reductase inhibitor) to aromatase inhibitors, work by blocking one known enzyme in the pathway. In the chapter on integration of metabolism, these same switches answer to the fed and fasted state.

## Going deeper

### The other two fates of acetyl-CoA: the phosphatidate hub

Storage fat and membrane phospholipids share a backbone. GPAT adds a fatty acid to glycerol 3-phosphate at the sn-1 position, making lysophosphatidate; AGPAT adds the second at sn-2, making **phosphatidate**. The positions show up in downstream lipids: sn-1 tends to carry a saturated chain, sn-2 an unsaturated one. Mind the name. Phosphatidate is diacylglycerol-3-phosphate, not diacylglycerol; the phosphate is the whole point.

Phosphatidate is the fork, and it splits two ways:

- **Remove the phosphate.** PAP activity, carried by the lipin enzymes (Lipin 1 is the best studied of three), gives diacylglycerol. Diacylglycerol is acylated a third time into triacylglycerol, the storage form. But it also feeds membranes: the two most abundant membrane lipids, phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine, are made by handing diacylglycerol an activated head group from CDP-choline or CDP-ethanolamine.
- **Add CTP.** Phosphatidate becomes CDP-diacylglycerol, the activated donor for phosphatidylinositol, phosphatidylglycerol and cardiolipin (and, in bacteria and yeast, phosphatidylserine).

The CDP steps use the activated-intermediate logic you have seen with UDP-sugars: making the activated head group releases pyrophosphate, whose hydrolysis pulls the reaction forward.

### Lipin 1 localization in cultured fat cells

PAP is also controlled by where it sits. Less phosphorylated Lipin 1 binds the ER membrane, where its substrate lives, and makes diacylglycerol. More heavily phosphorylated Lipin 1 stays mostly in the cytosol, away from its substrate, and makes less. The effects depend on the lipin paralog, its phosphorylation and the cell type. In cultured fat cells, epinephrine and the fatty acid oleate dephosphorylate Lipin 1 and move it onto the membrane, while insulin, surprisingly, phosphorylates it and shifts more of it into the cytosol. Losing Lipin 1 causes lipodystrophy in mice and recurrent muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis) in children. Where an enzyme sits can matter as much as how much of it there is.

### Sphingolipids and Tay-Sachs disease

Sphingolipids run on a different backbone entirely. Palmitoyl-CoA condenses with serine, and after a few steps you reach **ceramide**, the sphingolipid hub. Add phosphocholine and you get sphingomyelin; add sugars and you get cerebrosides and then gangliosides. The clinical anchor is Tay-Sachs disease: loss of hexosaminidase A blocks breakdown of the ganglioside GM2, which builds up in the lysosomes of neurons, and the result is fatal neurodegeneration.

### More branches of the mevalonate trunk

The shared trunk above squalene synthase is why statins reach beyond cholesterol, and three details fill it in. The vitamin K made from mevalonate is MK-4, whose side chain is built on a ring taken from dietary vitamin K. Nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates, the main osteoporosis drugs of this class, inhibit farnesyl-PP synthase in osteoclasts, below mevalonate on the shared trunk. And while farnesyl-PP anchors Ras, geranylgeranyl-PP anchors Rho-family GTPases.

### The numbers behind HMG-CoA reductase control

The four controls in the reductase table come with sizes. In cultured cells, sterol and nonsterol feedback together can change the amount of enzyme more than 200-fold. AMPK phosphorylates the enzyme at Ser872. Sterol-triggered degradation can cut the enzyme's half-life more than tenfold.
