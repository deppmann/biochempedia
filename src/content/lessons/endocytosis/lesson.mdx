  - name: "Michael S. Brown"
    contribution: >-
      With Joseph Goldstein, showed in 1974 that cultured skin fibroblasts from people with
      homozygous familial hypercholesterolaemia lack the high-affinity LDL binding of normal
      cells, which pointed to a cell-surface receptor as the site of the disease.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Brown & Goldstein, Nobel Lecture, 1985; Brown & Goldstein, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 71:788 (1974)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Brown and Goldstein asked a question that could be answered in a dish. Familial
      hypercholesterolaemia raises blood LDL, and the most severe cases inherit two defective
      copies of a gene. If the defect lay in how cells handle LDL, it might show up in cells taken
      from a patient and grown in culture, away from the liver and the blood. They grew skin
      fibroblasts from normal people and from patients and measured how much radiolabelled LDL the
      cells bound. In 1974 they reported that fibroblasts from five unrelated homozygous patients
      lacked the high-affinity binding that normal fibroblasts showed, binding that in normal
      cells was saturated at about 20 micrograms of LDL per millilitre. A disease of the blood had
      become a missing site on a cell surface. That result set up the next questions, about where
      the bound LDL goes and how the receptor works, and it gave the field a way to sort patients'
      cells by which step of the receptor's cycle had failed.
  - name: "Joseph L. Goldstein"
    contribution: >-
      With Michael Brown and colleagues at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical School,
      worked out the LDL receptor cycle and, with the receptor gene in hand, traced patient
      mutations to the receptor domains that bind, enter and release.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Brown & Goldstein, Nobel Lecture, 1985; Davis et al., Cell 45:15 (1986)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Goldstein's partnership with Brown turned a list of patients into a map of a protein. After
      the binding defect of 1974, cells from other patients showed that a receptor could fail in
      more than one way. Some cells bound LDL normally and still did not take it in. Once the
      receptor gene was cloned, the Dallas group could ask which change in the protein caused
      which failure. In 1986 C. G. Davis, with Goldstein, Brown, David Russell, Richard Anderson

      for tyrosine in the receptor's short tail inside the cell, and that a receptor rebuilt with
      that substitution bound LDL but entered cells slowly. A 1987 study from the same group
      showed that removing a different domain stopped the receptor releasing its ligand at acid pH
      and slowed its return. Brown and Goldstein shared the 1985 Nobel Prize in Physiology or
      Medicine. Their mutant receptors became standard tools for asking what each part of a
      cycling receptor does.
  - name: "Richard G. W. Anderson"
    contribution: >-
      Used electron microscopy with Brown and Goldstein to show that LDL bound to fibroblasts in
      the cold is concentrated in coated pits, which carry it inside on warming, and that an

    profileSourceAnchor: "Anderson, Brown & Goldstein, Cell 10:351 (1977); Anderson, Goldstein & Brown, Nature 270:695 (1977)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Anderson brought the electron microscope to the LDL problem. The difficulty was to see a
      single kind of particle on a cell surface. The answer was to attach ferritin, an iron-storage
      protein dense enough to show up as a dark dot, to LDL. Cultured human fibroblasts were given
      ferritin-LDL at 4 °C, where binding happens but uptake does not, then washed and warmed. In
      the cold, 60 to 70 percent of the bound label lay over coated segments of membrane that made
      up no more than 2 percent of the surface. On warming, the label moved inside through coated
      vesicles. The 1977 paper in Cell tied receptor-mediated uptake to coated pits. A second
      paper, in Nature later that year, showed that in fibroblasts from an internalization-defective

  - name: "Marino Zerial"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory at the Max Planck Institute of Molecular Cell Biology and Genetics in Dresden
      imaged single endosomes in live human A431 cells and found Rab5 replaced by Rab7 on
      endosomes carrying LDL, evidence that an early endosome matures into a late one.

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Zerial's laboratory studies Rab proteins, the small GTPases that mark the identity of a
      membrane. By the early 2000s two pictures of the route from early to late endosome competed.
      In one, early and late endosomes are fixed compartments and cargo travels between them in
      carriers. In the other, an early endosome itself changes into a late one. Fixed cells could
      not tell the two apart, because both predict that cargo is first in one kind of compartment
      and later in the other. In 2005 Jochen Rink, Eric Ghigo, Yannis Kalaidzidis and Zerial used
      fast live-cell imaging and image analysis to follow single endosomes in cultured human A431
      cells expressing fluorescent Rab5 and Rab7 while the cells took up LDL. On the endosomes
      carrying LDL, Rab5 was lost as Rab7 was gained, a switch they called Rab conversion. The
      work gave the maturation model direct evidence in a living cell, while showing recycling
      tubules leaving the same endosomes along the way.

techniques:
  - name: "Cold binding, warm uptake"
    blurb: >-
      Cells are given labelled ligand at 4 °C, where it binds while membrane traffic is largely
      arrested, then washed and warmed to 37 °C for a set time. An acid or heparin wash strips what is still on
      the surface, so binding and internalization are measured separately in the same cells.
  - name: "Ferritin-LDL electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      LDL is coupled to electron-dense ferritin so each particle shows as a dot in thin sections.
      Counting dots over coated and uncoated membrane shows where bound ligand gathers. It reports
      where the ligand is, not the receptor directly.
  - name: "Site-directed mutagenesis and expression"
    blurb: >-
      A patient's mutation is rebuilt in a cloned receptor and expressed in cells that lack their
      own receptor, such as receptor-deficient hamster cells. If the rebuilt receptor shows the
      patient's defect, that one change is enough to cause it.
  - name: "Live-cell Rab imaging"
    blurb: >-
      Rab proteins tagged with fluorescent proteins are followed on single endosomes over time
      while the cell takes up a labelled cargo. A compartment that changes its Rab coat while
      keeping its cargo is maturing; cargo hopping between stable compartments is not.

  - stem: "LDL and its receptor enter a fibroblast together in one coated vesicle. Why does the LDL reach the lysosome while the receptor returns to the surface?"
    choices:
      - "The receptor is ubiquitinated and sorted into recycling tubules"
      - "Acid pH in the early endosome makes LDL fall off the receptor"
      - "The LDL is too large to fit inside an early endosome"
      - "Hsc70 removes the LDL from the receptor during uncoating"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      At the mildly acid pH of the early endosome, roughly pH 6, LDL dissociates from its receptor;
      the free LDL stays in the lumen and travels on to the lysosome, while the empty receptor
      enters recycling tubules. Ubiquitin sends membrane proteins into multivesicular-body vesicles
      for destruction, not into recycling tubules. LDL fits inside endosomes; it is carried in
      their lumen. Hsc70 acts on the clathrin coat on the outside of the vesicle and never touches
      LDL, which is inside.
  - stem: "In live A431 cells, an endosome carrying fluorescent LDL loses Rab5 and gains Rab7 while the LDL stays in it. What does this observation support?"
    choices:
      - "Rab7 drives the acid release of LDL from its receptor"
      - "The early endosome itself changes identity into a late endosome"
      - "LDL leaves the endosome in a carrier and moves to a late endosome"
      - "Rab5 and Rab7 are two names for the same small GTPase"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The cargo stays put while the compartment's Rab marker changes, which is what maturation
      predicts and what Rink and colleagues called Rab conversion. If LDL left in a carrier for a
      separate late endosome, the LDL would move and the original endosome would keep Rab5. Rab5
      and Rab7 are different GTPases that mark different compartments. The observation shows a
      change of marker, not that Rab7 causes LDL release, which depends on acid pH and occurs
      earlier, in the early endosome.
  - stem: "Yeast cells lack ESCRT-I function. A membrane protein that is normally ubiquitinated at the endosome and destroyed is followed. What is the most likely result?"
    choices:
      - "It enters internal vesicles faster and is degraded sooner"
      - "It is released to the outside as soluble free protein"
      - "It loses its ubiquitin tag and is never internalized"
      - "It stays in the limiting membrane and is degraded less"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      ESCRT-I, working after ESCRT-0, recognizes ubiquitinated cargo and helps sort it into the vesicles that bud into the lumen
      of the multivesicular body; without it the protein stays in the limiting membrane, out of
      reach of full digestion: after fusion, enzymes in the lumen digest the inner vesicles, but of a protein held in the limiting membrane only the part facing the lumen is exposed to them, so less of it is degraded.
      Faster entry into internal vesicles is the opposite of losing the sorter. ESCRT-I reads the
      ubiquitin tag rather than adding it, and internalization from the surface happens before
      the ESCRT step. A membrane protein is not released as soluble protein by losing a sorting
      complex.

**Chapter 1 of the film ended by asking which step of the loop breaks in a patient's cells. In one patient with familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH), the LDL receptors grip LDL normally, yet most of the LDL stays on the cell surface.**

LDL, low-density lipoprotein, carries much of the cholesterol in human blood. In the 1970s Michael Brown and Joseph Goldstein grew skin fibroblasts (connective-tissue cells that grow well in a dish) from people with FH, an inherited disease of very high blood LDL. The homozygous cultures they first tested (two defective gene copies) lacked normal high-affinity LDL binding, the tight binding that fills normal cells' receptors at low LDL levels. Cells from a patient known as J.D. bound LDL but took little of it in.

The rest of the page answers that question, and each part comes back to the loop: first an assay that measures binding and entry separately, then chapter 1's steps (entry, the acid fork, return), each with a receptor that fails there, J.D.'s at entry. Last, with chapter 2, come receptors meant to be destroyed, which never finish the loop, and, for contrast, the other doors into a cell.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Chapter 1 follows four assayable jobs: bind, enter, release and return. For one matched batch, loss of binding or entry prevents later handling of that batch; a release defect can reduce return and promote degradation, so each transition must be measured, not assumed all-or-none. So measure the steps in order in matched cells and find the earliest one that falls; then test that step directly, because a low reading tells you where, not why.

## The record: binding, then uptake, read in order

First, the tool the rest of the page reads: one assay that measures the loop's first two steps separately. Cells are given labelled LDL at 4 °C, where it binds while membrane traffic is largely arrested: that is **bound**. They are washed free of unbound LDL and warmed to 37 °C for a set time, so the loop runs. An acid or heparin wash then strips LDL still on the surface; what resists the strip is **internal**.

> binding relative to normal = bound (strain) ÷ bound (normal)
>
> fraction internalized = internal ÷ bound at 4 °C

### Worked problem: reading binding and entry

The numbers are made up for practice. Cultured fibroblasts bind LDL labelled with radioactive iodine (125I-LDL) at 4 °C, are washed, warmed to 37 °C for 10 minutes and stripped. Assume no labelled LDL is gained or lost while the cells are warm, the strip removes all surface LDL and none of the internal LDL, and nothing is degraded.

| Cells | Bound at 4 °C | Surface after warming | Internal after warming |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | 40 | 10 | 30 |
| Strain X | 1.0 | | |
| Strain Y | 38 | 35 | 3 |

(ng LDL per mg cell protein; because strain X binds very little LDL and no warm-uptake values are shown, its entry fraction is not estimated here)

**Step 1.** Strain X binds 1.0 ÷ 40 = 2.5 percent of normal. Binding is the earliest step that fails: a binding defect. Cold binding measures ligand (the molecule a receptor binds, here LDL), not receptor, so the receptor may be absent from the surface or unable to bind; an antibody to its outer part tells the two apart. Brown and Goldstein's 1974 fibroblasts from five unrelated homozygous FH subjects showed this pattern.

**Step 2.** Normal cells internalize 30 ÷ 40 = 75 percent in 10 minutes. Strain Y binds nearly normally (38) but internalizes 3 ÷ 38 = 7.9 percent. Binding passes and entry is the earliest step that fails: an entry defect, like J.D.'s receptor. Check: 35 + 3 = 38, so no LDL went missing.

## Entry: adaptors choose, clathrin cages, dynamin cuts

Chapter 1 built the coated vesicle, whose lumen is topologically outside the cytosol ([membrane topology](/lessons/membrane-organization-topology/)); here is what it leaves out, for mammalian cells.

- **The cage.** Clathrin is a coat, a protein lattice that assembles on one face of a membrane and bends it. Chapter 1's three-legged piece is the triskelion, three heavy and three light chains joined at a hub; the legs interlock into hexagons and pentagons, like a geodesic dome.
- **The adaptors.** AP2 and other clathrin-associated sorting proteins bind PI(4,5)P2, a phosphorylated lipid of the inner leaflet ([lipids](/lessons/lipids/)), and read short sorting motifs in cargo tails. AP2 anchors the coat and binds YXXΦ motifs (tyrosine, any two residues, then a bulky hydrophobic residue), such as the transferrin receptor's. The LDL receptor's tail carries NPXY instead, which two other adaptors, Dab2 and ARH, can recognize; they also bind clathrin and AP2. Chapter 1's adaptors stand for the whole set.
- **The cut and the uncoating.** Dynamin is a GTPase, an enzyme that hydrolyses GTP; using that hydrolysis, its collar drives the neck to split. The chaperone Hsc70, recruited by its helper auxilin, then pulls the lattice apart, so the triskelia can be reused and the vesicle can fuse with an endosome.

### J.D.'s receptors, seen and counted

In 1977 Richard Anderson, with Brown and Goldstein, bound LDL coupled to ferritin (an iron-storage protein that shows as a dot in the electron microscope) to cultured fibroblasts at 4 °C. In normal cells 60 to 70 percent of the label lay over coated segments covering no more than 2 percent of the surface, and on warming it entered through coated vesicles. Take 60 percent, and set total label and area to 1.

> density in pits ÷ whole-surface density = 0.60 ÷ 0.02 = 30
>
> uncoated density = 0.40 ÷ 0.98 = 0.408
>
> density in pits ÷ uncoated density = 30 ÷ 0.408 = 73.5

Check: 0.02 × 30 + 0.98 × 0.408 = 0.60 + 0.40 = 1. Both are right; they differ only in the reference (whole surface or uncoated membrane), so say which you mean. These are calculated ratios, not binding constants.

**Predict.** Ferritin-LDL is bound to J.D.'s cells in the cold. If the receptors are spread at random over the surface, what fraction of the label lies over coated pits, and what is the density ratio over pits?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

About 2 percent of the label, so the density over pits matches the whole-surface average: a ratio near 1, not 30. The reported result fits: in J.D.'s cells the receptors that bound LDL failed to become incorporated into coated pits (Anderson, Goldstein and Brown, 1977).

</details>

The cause was inside the cell. In 1986 Davis and colleagues traced J.D.'s receptor to a single tyrosine-to-cysteine substitution in its short tail in the cytosol. Rebuilt in receptor-deficient hamster cells, it bound LDL normally but entered slowly.

## The fork: acid parts receptor from cargo

Chapter 1's fork is the early endosome's mildly acid lumen, roughly pH 6 (reported values run from about 5.6 to 6.5) and kept so by proton pumps ([organelles as environments](/lessons/organelles-as-environments/)). For LDL, acid is the sorting signal: at this pH LDL comes off its receptor. A crystal structure of the receptor's outer part at acid pH suggests one way (3D viewer): the arc of ligand-binding repeats folds back over a propeller-shaped domain, which binds two of them and so takes the place of LDL. The receptor grips itself and lets the cargo go.

Chapter 1 used the simplest case, one cargo that lets go; here is the fuller picture. Letting go does not by itself decide where a thing goes; the sorting machinery, reading each cargo and receptor, does. A cargo that holds on shows this.

**Predict.** Transferrin carries iron in the blood and enters cells on the transferrin receptor by the same route. In the acid endosome the iron comes off, but transferrin itself stays bound to its receptor. Where does transferrin go?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Back to the surface, still on its receptor. Outside, at neutral pH, transferrin without iron binds its receptor only weakly, so it lets go and can pick up iron again. The iron, which let go in acid, stays in the cell.

</details>

## Return: the loop closes, or runs down

Chapter 1 closed the loop; this section asks what happens when release fails, and how that fools the assay. Returning receptors reach the plasma membrane directly or by way of a recycling endosome. In the lysosome, enzymes digest LDL's protein and hydrolyse its cholesteryl esters, releasing cholesterol, which damps the cell's own synthesis through the enzyme HMG-CoA reductase ([lipid synthesis](/lessons/lipid-synthesis/)).

**Predict.** A receptor engineered without its EGF-precursor domain (an outer stretch resembling the precursor of epidermal growth factor) still enters cells, but cannot let go of its bound ligand at acid pH. Follow it through several rounds of uptake. What happens to it, and to the amount of receptor on the surface?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It returns inefficiently. Still carrying its ligand, much of it is rapidly degraded instead (Davis and colleagues, 1987), so surface receptor falls round by round. A release defect cuts return and raises degradation: the rule at work.

</details>

Chapter 1's picture was the simple case: one batch of receptors through one round. Over hours, though, the cell also makes and destroys receptor, so a late block can show up early. Suppose, for illustration, that with protein synthesis blocked half the receptors return each round: surface receptor falls to 0.5, 0.25 and 0.125 of the start over three rounds (a normal cell returning 99 percent keeps 0.99, 0.98, 0.97). Hours in, a cold-binding assay on the defective line reads like a binding defect, so measure binding before uptake starts.

One defect can also hide another. A patient receptor lacking exons 7 to 14 failed to recycle efficiently, but it also could not bind LDL, which hid the return defect until it was tested with beta-VLDL, another lipoprotein that it still bound. That is why the rule asks for the earliest step that fails, not the only broken one.

### Does the LDL move, or the endosome change?

Chapter 1 said the endosome matures. How do we know? LDL appears first in early endosomes and later in late ones, and fixed cells cannot tell whether it moved between permanent compartments or its compartment changed. Compartments carry name tags, **Rabs**: small GTPases that, bound to GTP, sit on one membrane and mark its identity by recruiting the tethers and enzymes that belong there. In 2005 Marino Zerial's laboratory imaged live human A431 cells with fluorescent Rab5 (early) and Rab7 (late) taking up LDL. On single endosomes carrying LDL, Rab5 was replaced by Rab7, which the authors read as Rab conversion: the compartment matures. Rab4-positive recycling tubules separated from Rab5-positive endosomes by fission, so maturation and carriers coexist.

### Design problem

A patient's receptor appears to reach the cell surface, but the cells lose surface receptor after repeated rounds of uptake. Design experiments to show a recycling defect.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Choose a ligand the mutant still binds (beta-VLDL if, like the exons 7 to 14 receptor, it cannot bind LDL). Measure surface receptor with an antibody to its outer part, surface biotinylation (tagging surface proteins with biotin) or Pronase sensitivity (an outside protease clips only surface receptor), and total receptor by immunoblot.

With protein synthesis blocked by cycloheximide, so no new receptor refills the surface, measure surface receptor after each round of uptake: a recycling defect predicts normal entry with surface receptor falling round by round, while normal cells hold it nearly steady. Faster destruction could also explain the loss, so label the receptor at the surface, let it enter, strip the surface, and measure how fast it returns and how much is degraded. Also measure acid release of bound ligand, since failure to release can keep the receptor from returning.

Controls: normal cells, mutant and normal cells matched for starting surface receptor, and a line whose receptor cannot bind the ligand used (it should show no ligand-driven loss).

</details>

## When the receptor itself is the cargo

Chapter 2 showed this sorting sequence. Some receptors, such as many activated growth-factor receptors, are meant to be destroyed: tagged with the small protein [ubiquitin](/lessons/ubiquitin-and-proteolysis/), the receptor itself rides to the lysosome. From the early endosome on, patches of the endosome's outer (limiting) membrane bud into the lumen; an endosome full of these inner vesicles is a multivesicular body. ESCRT complexes (endosomal sorting complexes required for transport), ESCRT-I among them, recognize the ubiquitin tag and sort the tagged protein into the inner vesicles. When the multivesicular body fuses with a lysosome, the inner vesicles are digested. This is how those receptors are switched off ([turning signals off](/lessons/turning-signals-off/), [receptor tyrosine kinases](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/), [signalling endosomes](/lessons/endocytosis-and-signaling-endosomes/)).

When the body fuses with a lysosome, those inner vesicles are digested." />

A recycling receptor can also be pulled out of its loop. PCSK9, chapter 2's last example, is a secreted protein that binds the EGF-A part of the LDL receptor's EGF-precursor domain (the 3D viewer shows the pair). In the cultured human cells tested, it sends the receptor to the lysosome instead of back to the surface.

**Predict.** An antibody that blocks PCSK9 is given. What should happen to the LDL receptor on liver cells, and to blood LDL?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

More receptors finish the loop, so surface receptor rises, the liver takes up more LDL, and blood LDL falls. People who carry loss-of-function PCSK9 variants have lower LDL, and PCSK9-blocking antibodies are approved LDL-lowering drugs.

</details>

## Other doors into a cell

Both chapters followed one door, the clathrin-coated pit, the best-understood route for receptors. Cells have others, each with its own machinery.

| Route | Typical cargo or defining feature | Machinery |
|---|---|---|
| Phagocytosis | Large particles, such as bacteria or dead cells, into phagosomes | Actin; in animals mostly specialized cells, such as macrophages and neutrophils |
| Macropinocytosis | Gulps of fluid, in large vacuoles | Sheet-like projections fold back onto the surface; stimulated by growth factors in many mammalian cells |
| Clathrin-mediated | Chosen receptors, such as the LDL and transferrin receptors | Adaptors, clathrin and dynamin (this lesson) |
| Caveolae | Small flask-shaped pits; cargo disputed (Going deeper) | Caveolin |
| FEME and CLIC/GEEC | Distinct clathrin-independent routes | FEME (fast endophilin-mediated endocytosis) needs dynamin; CLIC/GEEC does not |

## Going deeper

### How the coat bends

This is still argued. Live imaging in human SK-MEL-2 cells found curvature appearing early in some selected events and only after much of the clathrin had arrived in others. A 2023 analysis of fixed coats in three cell lines inferred that coats, on average, gain about half their final area before bending, with a fitted preferred radius of about 72 nm in SK-MEL-2 cells. The [vesicle traffic lesson](/lessons/vesicle-traffic/) treats coats in general.

### Not every clathrin cargo leans on AP2

In HeLaM cells depleted of AP2, a chimeric protein carrying the LDL receptor's tail was still taken up, consistent with other adaptors reading its NPXY motif. That result is about one construct in one cell line; it does not make AP2 dispensable in general.

### FH beyond the three defects

FH is usually inherited as a dominant trait, and people with two defective copies are rarer and more severely affected (see [pedigrees](/lessons/pedigrees/)). Many FH mutations make little or no receptor, or a receptor held in the endoplasmic reticulum; a cold-binding assay reads these as binding defects. Some FH comes from changes in other genes, such as APOB, or gain-of-function changes in PCSK9. J.D.'s substitution is at residue 807, in the numbering of the 1986 paper.

### Caveolae and the clathrin-independent routes

Caveolae are often drawn as a major entry route, but a 2020 review cautions that few if any cargoes are shown to rely on caveolae to enter. Their clearest job is mechanical: they flatten when the membrane is stretched, which buffers the tension. FEME is triggered when ligands activate selected surface receptors.

### The ESCRT hand-off

ESCRT-0 binds the ubiquitinated cargo first and passes it to ESCRT-I and ESCRT-II, which also bind ubiquitin, and ESCRT-III cuts the inner vesicles free. These complexes were first worked out in yeast and also work in mammalian cells.
