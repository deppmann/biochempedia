      drug that blocks division. If the cells now survive or repair, the gene is needed for the

  - name: "Laser microsurgery with video microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      A focused laser destroys one chosen structure, such as a single kinetochore, in a living
      cell while the cell is filmed. Timing the next event shows whether the destroyed structure
      was the source of a signal. Rieder's group used it in PtK1 cells.
  - name: "Antibody microinjection"
    blurb: >-
      An antibody against one protein is injected into a living cell at a chosen stage to block
      that protein acutely, avoiding the slow protein loss of a knockout. Injecting anti-Mad2 into
      mitotic PtK1 cells and human keratinocytes caused premature anaphase.
  - name: "Live imaging of a fluorescent reporter"
    blurb: >-
      A protein such as securin is tagged with a fluorescent protein and filmed in single cells,
      so its destruction can be timed against visible events like metaphase and anaphase. Drugs
      added during filming test what the destruction depends on.

**Irradiate a budding-yeast cell that lacks a gene called *RAD9* and it does not pause in G2; it keeps dividing for several generations and dies. Hold the same cell back with a drug for a few hours, and it repairs the damage.**

So the defect in *rad9* cells was not in repair. *RAD9* was needed for waiting. That result, from Ted Weinert and Leland Hartwell in 1988, opened a question you can ask of every step in the cycle: what makes one event hold off until another is done, and once the cell moves on, what stops it going back?

## The idea: a checkpoint makes one event wait for another

In 1989 Hartwell and Weinert gave these controls a name. A **checkpoint** is a control mechanism that enforces dependency between cell-cycle events.

Think of a train whose doors report to the driver. The train can run perfectly well with the doors open, so a separate circuit must hold it at the platform until they close. Map it onto the cell: the doors are the job (copying DNA, repairing it, attaching chromosomes to the spindle), the motor is the enzyme that drives the next step, and the circuit is the checkpoint. Remove the circuit and the cell can still divide, but it divides on time instead of on completion.

The rad9 experiment is the test for such a circuit. After irradiation, wild-type haploid yeast arrested or delayed in G2, the gap between DNA synthesis and mitosis; rad9 cells did not. When a microtubule poison held irradiated rad9 cells in G2 for several hours, repair could occur efficiently.

**Predict.** The opening gave the result away, so run the logic the other way. What result would have told Weinert and Hartwell that *RAD9* is a repair gene after all?

Every checkpoint in this lesson is built the same way.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A checkpoint is a brake wired to a sensor. While the sensor sees an unfinished job (a broken end, a stretch of single-stranded DNA, an unattached kinetochore), it restrains the regulators of the next transition: particular cyclin-CDKs, or APC/C-Cdc20-dependent destruction of securin and cyclin B.
>
> - **Wait.** In a cell still able to resume cycling, resolving the problem and silencing the signal permit recovery, which can also need new protein. Some damage responses end in lasting withdrawal from the cycle.
> - **Move.** The step the cell then takes is hard to undo. Depending on the step, it destroys proteins (securin and cyclin B at anaphase, Sic1 at yeast G1/S), which must be made again, or flips a positive-feedback switch (Cdk1 at entry into mitosis), which resists reversal.

The rule is about timing on the core engines of the cycle, not about repair, though the same kinases also act on replication forks. The brake is not perfect or global: the spindle checkpoint still lets cyclin A2 be destroyed, and a cell held in mitosis can eventually slip out, because cyclin B is still slowly destroyed.

## The map: three checkpoints, two engines

Every name in the table is explained in the sections below.

| Checkpoint (where the cell waits) | What it senses | Signals through | Transition output restrained | Released when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DNA damage (at G1/S and at G2/M) | broken ends; single-stranded DNA | ATM and Chk2; ATR and Chk1; p53 and p21 | cyclin E-Cdk2 in G1; cyclin B-Cdk1 in G2 | damage signals are resolved, signalling is silenced, and the cell can still recover |
| DNA replication (at G2/M) | single-stranded DNA at stalled forks | ATR and Chk1 | cyclin B-Cdk1 | stalled forks restart and replication is finished |
| Spindle assembly (metaphase to anaphase) | an unattached kinetochore | the mitotic checkpoint complex (MCC) | APC/C-Cdc20 targeting of securin and cyclin B | the last kinetochore attaches |

The two engines, from [the previous lesson](/lessons/cell-cycle-control/):

- **Cyclin-CDK.** A CDK works only with its cyclin, and inhibitory phosphates can still hold it off. For Cdk1, the kinases Wee1 and Myt1 add those phosphates and Cdc25 phosphatases remove them. The cell enters mitosis only when the Cdc25 side wins.
- **APC/C.** This ubiquitin ligase marks proteins for destruction. To pick its mitotic targets it needs the activator Cdc20.

The table also predicts what happens when a brake is lost. A yeast *rad9*, *mad* or *bub* mutant often grows normally when nothing goes wrong. It fails when the event it should wait for is slow or broken, and then it moves on anyway. The cost shows up as broken or missing chromosomes in the next generation. That holds for the classic yeast mutants, not for every checkpoint protein: in budding yeast the kinases Mec1 and Rad53 are essential even without damage, and in animals some checkpoint proteins, such as Mad2, are needed in every cycle (Going deeper).

## Damage and replication: kinases that keep a CDK off

Both DNA checkpoints run the same circuit in four steps. Something finds the damaged DNA, a large kinase is switched on there, it switches on a smaller messenger kinase, and the messenger's targets keep a cyclin-CDK off. (How a phosphate works as a switch is covered in [that lesson](/lessons/phosphorylation-switch/).)

**Step 1: find the trouble.** The cell recognizes two kinds of abnormal DNA.

- **A double-strand break** leaves two loose ends. The MRN complex (Mre11, Rad50 and Nbs1) binds the ends and recruits the kinase **ATM**.
- **Single-stranded DNA** is coated by RPA, the single-strand binding protein. RPA helps bring in the kinase **ATR** with its partner ATRIP. Single strands appear at a stalled replication fork, when the polymerase stops but the helicase keeps unwinding. They also appear at a break once its ends have been trimmed back.

**Step 2: pass the signal on.** ATM and ATR are the upstream kinases. Each switches on a smaller messenger kinase: ATM mainly **Chk2**, ATR mainly **Chk1**. ATM also phosphorylates p53 directly. Reaching the DNA is not enough to switch ATR on: the protein TopBP1 strongly stimulates its kinase activity, and ETAA1 can do so too.

| What the cell finds | Found by | Upstream kinase | Messenger kinase |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Broken ends | MRN | ATM | Chk2 |
| RPA-coated single strands | RPA and ATRIP | ATR | Chk1 |

A break in S or G2 uses both rows. ATM promotes trimming of the broken ends, which the Mre11 nuclease starts. Trimming exposes single strands, RPA coats them, and ATR joins in.

**Step 3: hold the CDK off.** Which CDK gets held depends on where the cell is in the cycle. Start with G2.

**Predict.** Chk1 and Chk2 inhibit the Cdc25 phosphatases. What happens to Cdk1, and to the cell?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Cdk1 keeps its inhibitory phosphates and stays off, so the cell waits in G2. In this branch nothing was destroyed and nothing new had to be made: the brake is a shift in a phosphate balance, so it can lift once the signal stops. The full damage response also destroys Cdc25A and makes p21 (below).

</details>

- **In G2, Cdk1 stays off,** as predicted. Several Cdc25 phosphatases share this job: mouse cells lacking Cdc25C still entered mitosis on time and responded normally to damage, probably because Cdc25A and Cdc25B covered for it. In human cells, p53 and p21 were needed to keep the G2 arrest going after radiation.
- **In G1, fast: Cdk2 stays off.** The checkpoint kinases trigger rapid ubiquitin-dependent destruction of Cdc25A, the phosphatase that activates Cdk2. Cdk2 keeps its inhibitory phosphate, and S phase does not start. This step needs no new gene expression and, in the classic experiment, no p53 (Going deeper).
- **In G1, slow: p53 and p21 keep Cdk2 off.** p53 is a transcription factor ([that lesson](/lessons/stress-signals/)). Activated after damage, it induces the gene for p21, a CDK inhibitor; in biochemical assays p21 blocks cyclin E-Cdk2 from phosphorylating Rb, so S phase stays off. In isogenic human colon cancer lines, Waldman, Kinzler and Vogelstein found that losing p21 abolished the G1 arrest after DNA damage altogether, a result in those cells, not every cell type.

**Step 4: let go.** As the breaks are resolved, or replication is completed, checkpoint signalling can be silenced. In a cell still able to recover, phosphatases and the remaking of destroyed proteins such as Cdc25A then restore CDK activity. That is the *wait* half of the rule.

Now place Rad9 from the opening. Later work showed that it is an adaptor at step 2: it helps the kinase Mec1 (yeast's ATR) switch on Rad53, yeast's messenger kinase. It also limits how far broken DNA ends are trimmed. So Rad9 does more than make the cell wait, but the 1988 test isolated its role in waiting.

## The spindle checkpoint: one kinetochore says wait

For the chromosomes to separate correctly, every pair of sister chromatids should be attached to opposite spindle poles before anaphase. The attachment point is the **kinetochore**, a protein structure on each chromatid's centromere that grips spindle microtubules. How it grips is left to [the mitosis lesson](/lessons/mitosis/).

The genes came from yeast. In 1991 Rong Li and Andrew Murray, and in the same issue of *Cell* Hoyt, Totis and Roberts, isolated budding-yeast mutants that kept going when a drug destroyed their microtubules. These were named *mad* (mitotic arrest deficient) and *bub* (budding uninhibited by benzimidazole).

Where does the signal come from? Conly Rieder's group used PtK1 cells, flat rat-kangaroo cells whose chromosomes can be seen one by one. They waited for a cell with a single chromosome still attached to only one pole, then destroyed its unattached kinetochore with a laser. The chromosome was left stranded near the pole.

**Predict.** Every other chromosome is lined up at the spindle's equator, and this one never will be. Does anaphase wait?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Anaphase followed about 20 minutes later, although that chromosome never moved to the equator. The cell was not checking alignment. It was listening to a wait signal from an unattached kinetochore, and removing the kinetochore removed the signal.

</details>

The signal is a protein complex. Unattached kinetochores promote assembly of the **mitotic checkpoint complex (MCC)**: Mad2, Cdc20, BubR1 and Bub3. The MCC holds APC/C-Cdc20 back. Think of it as a decoy key. BubR1 carries sequences that look like a substrate's destruction signal, and they sit in the slots on Cdc20 that would grip a real substrate, so securin and cyclin B are kept out, and BubR1 also blocks the E2 enzyme UbcH10. Once proper attachments silence the checkpoint, kinetochore-driven MCC production subsides, and continued disassembly of the MCC lets APC/C-Cdc20 target securin and cyclin B efficiently.

**Predict.** Inject an antibody that blocks Mad2 into a cell in prometaphase, while some kinetochores are still unattached. Does anaphase come earlier or later?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Earlier. Without Mad2 no MCC forms, APC/C-Cdc20 is free, and the wait is gone. Injecting an anti-Mad2 antibody into PtK1 cells or primary human keratinocytes in prophase or prometaphase brought on premature anaphase. Errors that escape this checkpoint produce the aneuploidy discussed in [that lesson](/lessons/chromosome-changes/).

</details>

The block also leaks. Brito and Rieder found that PtK and human RPE1 cells held in nocodazole can slip out of mitosis while the checkpoint is still active, because cyclin B is slowly destroyed by a proteasome-dependent route.

## One-way doors: destruction and feedback

Now the cell moves on, and the move is hard to undo.

### Anaphase: APC/C-Cdc20 destroys securin and cyclin B

Freed APC/C-Cdc20 ubiquitinates securin and cyclin B. APC/C is the ubiquitin ligase; the proteasome does the destroying (in HeLa cells, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 blocked securin breakdown). Ubiquitin chemistry is in [that lesson](/lessons/ubiquitin-and-proteolysis/).

- **Securin** binds and inactivates **separase**, a protease. In vertebrate cells cyclin B-Cdk1 also inhibits separase, and Hagting and colleagues found that both securin and cyclin B1 have to be degraded before sister chromatids can separate. Freed separase cuts Scc1, the cohesin subunit that holds sister chromatids together, and anaphase begins.
- **Cyclin B.** Losing it is a major way Cdk1 is turned off at mitotic exit.

### Why it does not run backwards

Compare the two kinds of brake. A phosphate can be removed and put back within seconds to minutes, depending on the substrate, because the protein is still there. A destroyed protein can come back only by being made again. But is destruction what actually stops a cell from going back?

**Predict.** Potapova and colleagues forced cultured Xenopus and human cells out of mitosis with a Cdk1 inhibitor, while keeping cyclin B intact. Then they washed the inhibitor out. Did the cells stay out of mitosis?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Many cells returned to mitosis: the cyclin B was still there, so Cdk1 switched back on once the drug was gone. Cells whose cyclin B had been destroyed before washout did not return, because Cdk1 had no partner until new cyclin was made. Destruction is what made exit stick. A later study found that after mitotic exit, inhibitory phosphorylation of Cdk1 can also hold it inactive in G1 even when cyclin B is preserved, a second lock.

</details>

On the left, cyclin B stays intact; after washout it pairs with Cdk1 again and the cell returns to mitosis. ### Feedback: the Cdk1 switch

Entry into mitosis is switched on by positive feedback. Active Cdk1 activates its activator Cdc25 and inhibits its inhibitor Wee1, so once Cdk1 activity starts to rise, the rise feeds itself.

A switch like this is **bistable**: over a middle range of cyclin B, it can sit stably off or on, depending on where it came from. If cyclin B is the only input varied and other regulators are held fixed, the switch turns on above an upper threshold and off below a lower one. That gap gives it memory: wobbles that stay short of the thresholds do not flip it. Changing Cdk1 inhibition or other regulators can still turn it off without lowering cyclin B. The table is a cartoon of that fixed-regulator case, with feedback strong enough to give a switch.

| Total cyclin B | Cdk1 activity, without the feedback | Cdk1 activity, with the feedback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low | low | off |
| Middle | in between, following cyclin B up and down | off or on, depending on history |
| High | high | on |

This suggests why the damage brake normally acts in G2, before the switch flips: a cut in Cdc25 activity that keeps an off switch off may not turn an on switch off, because the on state is held up by its own feedback.

That is the *move* half of the rule.

### G1/S: SCF destroys Sic1

The same logic runs at the budding-yeast G1/S transition through a second ligase, SCF. Sic1 is a CDK inhibitor that holds the S-phase cyclin-CDK off. Once phosphorylated, Sic1 is gripped by Cdc4 (the SCF subunit that recognizes it, assembled with Skp1) and ubiquitinated. When the SCF subunit Hrt1 was inactivated, Sic1 stayed stable and cells arrested at G1/S. In human cells, commitment to a new cycle also rests on a feedback switch. How destruction and feedback turn a gradual input into an all-or-none switch is taught in [that lesson](/lessons/signaling-circuits/).

### Design problem

Knocking down protein Z with RNAi changes how long mitosis takes. Design experiments to decide whether Z is a spindle-checkpoint component, is needed for kinetochores to attach to microtubules, or both.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Film single cells and time nuclear envelope breakdown to anaphase in four conditions: control, Z-depleted, Mad2-depleted, and Z plus Mad2 depleted. Repeat each in nocodazole.

A checkpoint component should behave like Mad2. Mitosis may get shorter, as it did after anti-Mad2 injection, and Z-depleted cells cannot stay arrested in nocodazole.

A pure attachment protein does the opposite. Unattached kinetochores keep signalling, so mitosis gets longer. That delay should collapse if Mad2 is depleted as well, because the delay needs the checkpoint. Nocodazole arrest should be intact.

Score lagging chromosomes in anaphase in each condition. Rescue with an RNAi-resistant Z to show the effect is Z's. One caveat: a strong checkpoint can still look weak if cells slip out of mitosis by slow cyclin B loss, so measure how long cells stay arrested, not only whether they eventually leave.

## Going deeper

Optional: the experiments behind the main text.

### When checkpoint proteins are needed in every cycle

Checkpoints are not only emergency brakes. Mouse embryo cells lacking Mad2 grow normally at first, but once the epiblast starts dividing rapidly they missegregate chromosomes and die, even without spindle damage. Reducing Mad2 or BubR1 in human cancer cells killed them within six divisions.

In a 2018 study of cultured human cells, Saldivar and colleagues reported that the kinase ATR, activated by a protein called ETAA1 during ordinary replication, holds back a CDK1-driven switch on the transcription factor FOXM1 until S phase ends; when ATR was inhibited, cells entered mitosis early with under-replicated DNA.

In budding yeast, *MEC1* and *RAD53* are essential. Their lethality is suppressed by deleting *SML1*, an inhibitor of ribonucleotide reductase, so their essential job is mainly supplying DNA building blocks (dNTPs) rather than making the cell wait.

### Who showed what in the DNA checkpoints

- **ATM.** In a purified system, Lee and Paull showed that MRN and DNA together activated ATM to phosphorylate p53 and the kinase Chk2. In mammalian cultured cells, Chk2 activation after damage depended on ATM.
- **ATR.** Zou and Elledge found that in human cells RPA was needed both for ATR to reach damage sites and for ATR to activate Chk1, and that RPA stimulates ATRIP to bind single strands in vitro.
- **Cdc25A.** In human cells, ultraviolet light or radiation triggered its destruction. After ultraviolet light this needed Chk1 but not p53; after ionizing radiation the ATM-Chk2 route also drives it (Falck and colleagues, 2001).
- **At the fork.** In replisomes rebuilt from purified budding-yeast proteins, the yeast checkpoint kinase Rad53 restrained excess unwinding by the CMG helicase after DNA synthesis was blocked, and only kinase-active Rad53 did so. That is a yeast reconstitution; how closely human ATR and Chk1 match it is still being worked out.

### Cdc25C, serine 216 and 14-3-3

The classic example of a Cdc25 target is Cdc25C: human Chk1 phosphorylates it on serine 216, and Chk2 can do so too. A Cdc25C mutant that cannot be phosphorylated there no longer binds 14-3-3, a small adaptor protein; 14-3-3 binding is thought to keep Cdc25C away from Cdk1.

### Spindle checkpoint: timing and structure

The numbers and structure behind the spindle section: in Rieder's laser experiment, anaphase followed 20 plus or minus 2.5 minutes after the kinetochore was destroyed. The decoy-key picture comes from the cryo-EM structure of human APC/C bound to the MCC, in which BubR1 sequences that mimic a substrate's degron (the short sequence that marks it for recognition) sit in Cdc20's degron-binding sites.

### Commitment switches in data

For the human G1/S commitment switch named in the main text, Cappell and colleagues found that EMI1 is first a substrate of the ligase APC/C-Cdh1 and then becomes its inhibitor, and that this double-negative loop is bistable, a one-way switch.

For mitotic entry, frog egg extracts show the gap between the on and off thresholds directly: Sha and colleagues (2003) found that entering mitosis takes a higher cyclin B concentration than staying in it.

### Securin's backup signal

The D-box is not securin's only handle. The D-box mutant from the worked problem escapes destruction at metaphase, but Hagting and colleagues found it was degraded later, in anaphase, through a second APC/C recognition sequence, the KEN box.
