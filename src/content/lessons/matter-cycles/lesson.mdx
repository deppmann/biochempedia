## Explain: carbon moves, and it sets the temperature

<WordBank terms={['photosynthesis', 'respiration', 'combustion', 'decomposition', 'ocean absorption', 'transpiration', 'nitrogen fixation', 'nitrification', 'assimilation', 'ammonification', 'denitrification', 'greenhouse effect']} />

Carbon is in every sugar, fat, protein and piece of DNA in your body. Five processes move it around.

- **Photosynthesis** is the process in which plants, algae and some bacteria use light energy to build sugar from carbon dioxide and water. It pulls carbon out of the air or water.
- **Respiration** (cellular respiration) is the process in which cells break down sugar with oxygen and release carbon dioxide. Plants, animals and microbes all do it.
- **Combustion** is burning. Burning wood, coal, oil or natural gas joins carbon with oxygen and releases carbon dioxide quickly.
- **Decomposition** is the breakdown of dead organisms and wastes by fungi and bacteria. Their respiration returns much of the carbon to the air, and some stays in the soil for years.
- **Ocean absorption** is carbon dioxide dissolving into seawater at the surface. Carbon dioxide also comes back out, but today the ocean takes in more than it gives off.

> carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen
> 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2

Respiration runs the same equation backward and releases the stored energy for the cell to use. You met both in [Capturing Sunlight](/lessons/photosynthesis-energy-capture/) and [Respiration and ATP](/lessons/respiration-and-atp/).

Coal, oil and natural gas formed from ancient organisms, and their carbon was locked underground for millions of years. Burning them adds that old carbon to today’s air.

Carbon dioxide in the air also matters for temperature. Sunlight warms the ground, and the ground gives off heat as infrared light. Carbon dioxide absorbs some of that infrared light and sends heat back toward the surface. This is the **greenhouse effect**, the warming of Earth’s surface by gases that absorb outgoing heat. Without it Earth would be far colder. More carbon dioxide means more heat held in.

## Explain: water carries everything else

You know the water cycle from grade 7. **Evaporation** turns liquid water into vapor, using energy from the Sun. **Transpiration** is the release of water vapor from plant leaves. **Condensation** turns vapor back into cloud droplets, and **precipitation** returns water to the ground as rain or snow. Some water soaks into the ground and later seeps back into streams.

The water cycle matters for the other cycles because moving water carries things with it.

| What moving water carries | Example in Virginia |
|---|---|
| Nutrients such as nitrogen | Fertilizer washed from a field into a creek |
| Sediment (soil particles) | Muddy water in a river after a storm |
| Pathogens (germs) | Bacteria from animal waste reaching a swimming spot |

So a rainstorm in Pennsylvania can move nitrogen into the Susquehanna, which flows into the Bay.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Water moves in a cycle, and as it moves it carries nutrients, sediment and germs between land, rivers and the sea.</Support>

## Explain: nitrogen needs microbes

Your body uses nitrogen to build proteins and DNA. Plants also need it for chlorophyll, the green pigment of photosynthesis. Air is about 78 percent nitrogen gas, but the two atoms in each molecule are held by a very strong bond. Most organisms cannot break it. Microbes do most of the work in five steps.

1. **Nitrogen fixation** is the conversion of nitrogen gas into ammonia. Only certain microbes, mostly bacteria, can do it, some free in the soil and some inside nodules on the roots of beans, peas and other legumes. Factories also fix nitrogen to make fertilizer.
2. **Nitrification** is the change of ammonia into nitrite and then nitrate by soil microbes. Most are bacteria, but some are archaea.
3. **Assimilation** is a plant or alga taking up nitrate or ammonium from the soil or water and building it into proteins and DNA. Animals get nitrogen by eating plants or other animals.
4. **Ammonification** is decomposers turning the nitrogen in wastes and dead bodies back into ammonium.
5. **Denitrification** is bacteria turning nitrate back into nitrogen gas, usually in wet soil or mud with little oxygen. The gas returns to the air.

A crab gets nitrogen by eating clams, worms, small animals and dead material, much of whose nitrogen traces back to algae and plants that assimilated it from the water.

Nitrogen also ties to carbon. To build amino acids, a plant needs carbon compounds made by photosynthesis. No cycle runs alone.

### Design problem

Does fertilizer runoff make algae grow faster? Plan a test you could run in a classroom with jars of pond water that already contain algae.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Independent variable:** the amount of liquid plant fertilizer added: none, a small dose and a large dose.
- **Dependent variable:** how green the water becomes after 10 days, judged against a color chart or measured with a light sensor.
- **Constants:** the same pond water, jar size, water volume, light, temperature and number of days.
- **Control:** the jars with no fertilizer.
- **Repeated trials:** at least three jars at each dose, so one odd jar does not decide the result.
- **What results would mean:** if the jars get greener as the dose rises, the data support the idea that added nutrients feed algae.
- **Safety and ethics:** wear gloves, wash your hands, never drink the water, and pour it down a drain rather than into a stream, so you do not add nutrients to real water.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

A blue crab’s atoms keep moving. Its carbon passed through water and food, and some of it left again as carbon dioxide from its own respiration, and its nitrogen passed through bacteria, plants and its food, maybe after washing down a river from a farm. When the crab dies, decomposers return those atoms to the water and air, where they start another trip.

**Science practices you used:** reading a data table and finding a trend, comparing modeled and measured data, using a model of a cycle and naming its limits, and planning a fair test with a control and repeated trials.
