techniques:
  - name: "Marking and measuring a whole population over years"
    blurb: >-
      Nearly every bird is caught, banded with a unique combination and measured, then looked for in later seasons. Comparing the birds that survive or breed with the population they came from
      measures selection directly, and following their offspring measures what was passed on.
  - name: "Microsatellite parentage assignment"
    blurb: >-
      Offspring and candidate parents are typed at several highly variable DNA loci. A chick whose
      alleles cannot have come from its social father is an extra-pair young, so the pedigree used
      for heritability can be corrected.
  - name: "Parent-offspring regression"
    blurb: >-
      Offspring trait values are plotted against their parents' values. The slope against the
      mid-parent mean estimates narrow-sense heritability, if parents and offspring do not share
      environments that also shape the trait.
  - name: "Field transplant experiment"
    blurb: >-
      Organisms are moved from one environment to another that differs in a chosen factor, such as
      the type of predator, and the moved population is compared with its source years later. It
      tests a cause that natural comparisons can only suggest.

The first film split the 1977 drought on Daphne Major into three claims: selection happened, the trait is inherited, and the offspring changed. Here is the real evidence for each, then what the film left out.

## Claim 1: selection happened

**Natural selection** is a consistent difference in survival or reproduction among individuals that differ in a trait. Its currency is **fitness**: reproductive success, the number of surviving offspring an individual leaves in a given environment.

Boag and Grant (1981) compared the 1977 survivors with the same banded population before the drought. Larger birds, especially males with larger beaks, survived best. That shows selection on the measured traits, or on traits correlated with them: beak size and body size go together, so survival alone cannot say which was the target.

That comparison measures only the survival part of selection. The **selection differential** that predicts the chicks, S, is the mean of the parents, each counted once for every offspring it contributed, minus the mean of the population before selection. The survivor difference equals S only if the survivors contribute about equally, as the worked examples below assume.

## Claim 2: the trait is inherited

This needs a comparison among relatives, which survival data lack. Plot each chick's adult beak depth against the average of its two parents, the **mid-parent** value. If deep-beaked parents have deep-beaked chicks, the points rise from left to right. The **slope**, how many millimetres the chick's beak rises for each millimetre the parents' average rises, estimates the film's dial, **narrow-sense heritability** (h²), but only if parents and chicks do not share an environment that also shapes the trait. On Daphne Major these regressions gave high heritabilities for beak and body size.

Every estimate depends on knowing the parents, usually taken to be the birds seen feeding a chick. Keller, Grant, Grant and Petren (2001) tested that shortcut with DNA, typing birds at eight microsatellite loci, places in the genome where short repeated stretches of DNA vary between individuals. Twenty percent of the offspring in their sample were **extra-pair young**, whose social father was not the genetic father. In this study, wrong fathers made the traits look less inherited than they are (figure). The error can run the other way too: chicks still resembled their mothers more than their fathers, which the authors read as **maternal effects**, influences of the mother other than her genes, such as egg size or the food she brings. A maternal effect that tracks the mother's own trait can make a trait look more inherited than it is.

## Claim 3: the offspring changed

The Grants (1995) measured the offspring of selected birds and showed that selection had changed the next generation.

> **The one idea.** A nonrandom difference in survival shows selection, not yet evolution. The **breeder's equation**, R = h² × S, predicts how far the next generation's mean moves: a fraction h² of the selection differential S. So to show that selection changed a trait's average, you need three claims, each with its own evidence: selection happened (S), the trait is inherited (h²), and the offspring changed (R).
>
> *Scope:* one trait, one population, one episode of directional selection; an additive model, in which the effects of genes simply add up; offspring raised in the same environment as their parents; and no unmeasured trait, correlated with this one, that also affects fitness. Other kinds of selection, and evolution shown directly from changes in gene frequencies, are tested in other ways. The environment sets the sign of S, so the rule predicts one generation, not a decade.

Heritability itself is covered in [complex traits and GWAS](/lessons/complex-traits-and-gwas/).

**Predict.** These numbers are illustrative, not the Grants' measurements. Before a drought, adult mean beak depth in a finch population is 9.40 mm. The birds that survive and breed average 9.80 mm, and h² is 0.60. What will their offspring average?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

9.64 mm, inside the scope above.

1. *Selection differential.* S = 9.80 − 9.40 = 0.40 mm.
2. *Predicted response.* R = 0.60 × 0.40 = 0.24 mm. Heritability has no units, so R stays in millimetres.
3. *Predicted offspring mean.* 9.40 + 0.24 = 9.64 mm.

A check for any problem: R must lie between 0 and S. It is 0 only if h² is 0; otherwise it has the same sign as S. If your offspring mean lies beyond the survivors' mean, you have used a heritability above 1.

</details>

Only an actual offspring sample, raised in an unchanged environment, tests the prediction.

## The direction of selection can reverse

**Predict.** Drought favoured large beaks in 1977. Will every later drought do the same?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

For a while the same selection recurred (Price and colleagues, 1980 and 1982). Then the wet El Niño of 1982 and 1983 left a seed supply dominated by small soft seeds, and in the 1985 drought small birds with pointed beaks survived disproportionately well. Selection reversed: S changed sign. Drought did not favour large beaks as such; as far as the record shows, the seeds that remained did.

</details>

Over the whole record from 1972 to 2001, the Grants (2002) found selection on these populations that ranged from one-directional to oscillating, and from episodic to gradual. They concluded that evolution in these finches could be predicted in the short term from selection and inheritance, but that unpredictable fluctuations in the environment limited longer-term forecasts: the rains and rare events, such as hybridization and the arrival of a competitor, set the sign and size of S and could not be foreseen. R = h² × S carries a population one generation forward from an S that the environment sets afresh each time.

A second limit lies inside the equation, in the scope's last condition. Beak depth, beak length and body size go together in these birds, so selection on body size could show up as an apparent response in beak depth; Morrissey and colleagues (2010) showed that the single-trait prediction is then unreliable (see Going deeper). For the algebra of drift and selection on single genes, see [population genetics](/lessons/population-genetics/).

The finch record cannot say for certain what did the selecting: the remaining seeds are the leading suspect, and 1977 cannot be rerun. Where a proposed cause can be moved, an experiment can test it.

## Three kinds of evidence, and what each can carry

The second film set **observation of natural variation**, **experimental contrast** and **historical inference** side by side. What the guppy (*Poecilia reticulata*) transplant did not show is how the predators acted. Later work by Reznick's group found that guppies at sites with predators of adults die at higher rates at every size, not mainly as adults, and that the change in population density this causes helps explain the evolution.

Historical inference works only with traces that rival explanations predict differently. The philosopher Carol Cleland has argued that this is a different method from experiment, not a weaker one. On Daphne Major, a male finch arrived and bred with a resident medium ground finch. Was he an unusual local bird or an immigrant? His genome answered it: Lamichhaney and colleagues placed him as a *Geospiza conirostris* from Española, more than 100 km away. The breeding was watched. His origin was inferred.

## What a dataset cannot show

- **Survival comparison** (1977): selection on the measured traits that year. It cannot show inheritance, nor which of the correlated traits was the target.
- **DNA-checked parent-offspring regression**: how strongly the traits pass on in that population. It cannot identify what did the selecting, and it can still mistake a shared environment for inheritance, because parents rear their own chicks.
- **The 30-year record**: that selection varied in direction and pattern on one island, in two species, over those years; not what happens on other islands or in other lineages. "Darwin's finches show" is a broader claim than the data make.
- **Genomes**: Enbody and colleagues (2023) analysed 3,955 genomes from Daphne Major finches and found six loci of large effect that together explained 45 percent of beak-size variation in the medium ground finch. That leaves most of the variation unassigned, and an association with a locus is not a mechanism.
- **The hybrid lineage** (Lamichhaney and colleagues, 2018): reproductive isolation, the lineage breeding only among itself, established within three generations in this case, but not that the lineage will persist or is a new species, nor any general timescale for speciation.

Uncertainty sits in different places in each: sample size in a survival count, pedigree errors and shared environments in a heritability, the next year's weather in a forecast.

### Design problem

The offspring of drought survivors have deeper beaks than the population before the drought. A colleague says that proves beak depth is inherited. Another says well-fed parents simply raise well-fed chicks. Design a study that tells these explanations apart, and state its limits.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Separate genes from rearing.** Swap eggs between nests soon after laying, deep-beaked pairs' eggs to shallow-beaked foster parents and the reverse, leaving other clutches with their own parents. Measure every chick as an adult, when beak growth is complete.

**Get the pedigree right.** Type chicks and all candidate parents at microsatellite loci, and assign each chick its genetic father by DNA, not by who fed it: Keller and colleagues found 20 percent extra-pair young in their Daphne Major sample.

**State the prediction first.** If genes alone explain the resemblance, the slope on genetic parents is positive and the slope on foster parents near zero. If rearing alone explains it, the reverse. If both contribute, both slopes are positive, and comparing them shows roughly how much each matters. Report each slope with its uncertainty.

**Limits.** This is one island, one species and one cohort, and the estimate belongs to that environment. Foster nests still differ in territory and food, and swapping happens after the egg is laid, so effects of the mother through the egg remain. A result in one year may not hold in a year with different seeds.

</details>

## Going deeper

### When the sexes are selected differently

In 1977 survival especially favoured males with larger beaks. For a trait inherited equally through both parents, with no sex-specific inheritance, the S that predicts the offspring is the average of the mothers' and fathers' differentials, each calculated within its sex, weighting every parent by its offspring. Using only the larger differential, when both point the same way, overpredicts the response.

### Beyond the single-trait equation

Morrissey and colleagues (2010) pointed out that even the multivariate breeder's equation, which tracks several correlated traits at once, is valid only if every correlated trait that affects fitness has been measured. They advocated the Robertson-Price identity as a more robust alternative: it predicts the response from the genetic covariance between the trait and fitness itself.
