techniques:
  - name: "Environmental DNA sampling with qPCR"
    blurb: >-
      Water is collected, filtered or precipitated, and DNA is extracted; a species-specific qPCR
      assay is then run several times per extraction. A run either amplifies or does not, and the
      pattern of runs, extractions and samples is the raw evidence of whether the species' DNA was
      there.
  - name: "Multiscale occupancy modelling"
    blurb: >-
      A statistical model that treats occupancy as a hidden state and estimates, from the pattern
      of replicates, the probability of detection at each nested stage (DNA in the sample,
      captured in an extraction, amplified in a run). It turns a set of negatives into a
      probability that the site is still occupied.
  - name: "Independent validation of occupancy"
    blurb: >-
      Cameras, field signs or direct surveys establish which sites are occupied by another route,
      so detection probabilities can be estimated at sites whose true state is known, as in the
      wild pig trial in central Texas.
  - name: "Negative controls and sequence confirmation"
    blurb: >-
      Field blanks, extraction blanks and PCR no-template controls are carried through the
      workflow to flag contamination (blanks can reveal contamination that reaches them or runs
      broadly through a batch, but clean blanks cannot exclude localized or rare one-off
      contamination), and amplified products are sequenced to confirm that the
      signal comes from the target species and not a relative or an off-target product.

  - stem: "Using gamma = 0.49 per extraction and delta = 0.38 per qPCR run, what is the probability that DNA present in a water sample is detected by one extraction analysed in five qPCR wells?"
    choices:
      - "0.19, the product of gamma and delta"
      - "0.38, the chance that any one run amplifies"
      - "0.91, the chance that at least one of five runs amplifies"
      - "0.45, gamma times the five-run probability"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      At least one of five runs amplifies captured DNA with probability 1 - 0.62^5 = 0.908, and the DNA must first
      be captured, so 0.49 x 0.908 = 0.445. Multiplying gamma by a single delta gives the chance
      for one well only. The 0.91 figure leaves out the capture step and so overstates detection.
      The single-run value of 0.38 ignores both the capture step and the extra four runs.
  - stem: "A team has a fixed budget. At an occupied pond, DNA is in a water sample with probability 0.60, and lab detection given DNA in the sample is already 0.95; pooling more subsamples into one sample would leave the 0.60 unchanged. Which change most reduces the chance of missing the pond?"
    choices:
      - "Report the result as a naive fraction of positive qPCR wells"
      - "Run each extraction in twelve qPCR wells instead of five wells"
      - "Take a second, independent water sample on a separate visit"
      - "Pool forty subsamples into the single water sample"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      With one sample, the miss probability is 1 - 0.60 x 0.95 = 0.43, and almost all of it
      comes from DNA being absent from the sample. A second independent sample brings it to about
      0.43^2 = 0.185. More wells can only push lab detection from 0.95 toward 1, which lowers the
      miss to no less than 0.40. Pooling more subsamples still yields one sample, and the stem fixes its
      chance of holding DNA at 0.60, so the miss stays at 0.43. A naive fraction of positive wells is a summary,

  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Environmental DNA, or eDNA, is DNA that organisms shed into water or soil. A survey takes
      water samples, extracts DNA and uses PCR to test for one species. Each step can miss: the
      sample may hold no DNA, the extraction may not capture it, and a PCR run may not amplify
      it. So a set of negatives lowers the chance that the species is present but does not make
      it zero. Combining the chances at each step, then multiplying the chance of missing across samples, tells you how many samples you need
      before you can be fairly confident a site is empty.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      eDNA detection is a nested observation process: at an occupied site, DNA is available in a
      water sample with probability theta, captured by an extraction with probability gamma, and
      amplified by a qPCR run with probability delta. Assuming conditional independence and no
      false positives, per-sample detection is q = theta x (1 - (1 - gamma x A)^k), where A is the
      chance that at least one run on an extraction amplifies, and k is the number of extractions per sample. The
      probability of missing an occupied site with n samples is (1 - q)^n, and Bayes' rule
      combines that with a prior to give the probability the site is still occupied. Because
      laboratory replicates share one sample, they cannot push detection above theta; only
      independent field samples can.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      Hierarchical occupancy models estimate theta, gamma and delta jointly from replicate
      structure, and they rest on assumptions a real survey can break: closure during sampling,
      independence among samples given occupancy, parameters constant across sites and visits or explained by covariates,
      and either no false positives or a false-positive rate that is identifiable. Davis and
      colleagues estimated gamma = 0.49 (SE 0.07) and delta = 0.38 (SE 0.04) for wild pig DNA in
      central Texas, at sites whose occupancy was confirmed independently, and found availability
      varied with month, pH and water-body type. Buxton and colleagues fitted two models to 2,958
      English newt ponds and obtained overall occupancy of 0.26 and 0.47, with no ground truth to
      check against (they judged the model ignoring false positives to overestimate), and noted that false-positive parameters can need calibration or
      informative priors to be identified.

      An open question is how to transfer detection parameters between species, waters and
      seasons. A stop rule built on borrowed point estimates ignores their uncertainty and their
      covariates; propagating parameter uncertainty through the posterior, and testing whether
      decisions change, is the honest next step for any management use.

Background: [spatial populations](/lessons/spatial-populations/), [conservation](/lessons/conservation/) and [global change](/lessons/global-change/).

> **The one idea.** A run of negatives never proves a species is absent. Each independent negative water sample multiplies the occupied ponds still hiding among the negatives by the chance that an occupied pond is missed, while the empty ponds stay put. So the share still occupied falls with every sample but never reaches zero. (This needs a prior between 0 and 1 and a sample that can both detect and miss.) Wells and extractions from one water sample all test that sample's water, so they cannot lift its detection above the chance the water holds DNA. Only new water is a new try at the scoop.

## What eDNA detects and what it misses

**Environmental DNA (eDNA)** is DNA collected from an environmental sample, such as water, soil or air, rather than from the organism itself; for animals, much of it is DNA they shed. A targeted survey filters water, extracts DNA and runs a quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay for one species ([recombinant DNA and PCR](/lessons/recombinant-dna-and-pcr/), [sequencing and genomes](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/)). Each qPCR run, in one well of a plate, either amplifies the target or does not.

A site is **occupied** if the species uses it during the survey. A **false negative** is a miss: the organism or its DNA is present and nothing amplifies. A **false positive** is a report of the target when it is absent; contamination and a poorly specific assay are two routes.

The first film's three nets get symbols, in its colours: **θ** (theta, blue), the chance that a water sample from an occupied pond holds the species' DNA in a form the assay can detect; **γ** (gamma, green), the chance that an extraction captures DNA that is in the sample; and **δ** (delta, gold), the chance that one qPCR run amplifies captured DNA. One reason θ is below 1 is decay: it can leave too few intact copies of the target sequence in the scoop, even when shorter fragments remain. In upland Welsh stream mesocosms (outdoor experimental stream channels), DNA from three species fell by more than 90% within three hours and was nondetectable within two days, faster in acidic water (Seymour and colleagues, 2018): one experimental system, not a universal eDNA lifetime.

## Detection probability and an all-negative site

Made-up great crested newt data: four ponds, two water samples each, two extractions per sample, five qPCR runs per extraction. Each string is one extraction's five runs; 1 means amplification.

| Pond | Sample 1 (extraction 1 / 2) | Sample 2 (extraction 1 / 2) | Positive wells (of 20) |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | 10000 / 00000 | 00000 / 00000 | 1 |
| S2 | 00000 / 00000 | 00000 / 00000 | 0 |
| S3 | 00000 / 00000 | 00000 / 00000 | 0 |
| S4 | 11100 / 00000 | 11000 / 00000 | 5 |

Two of four ponds amplified at least once, so the **naive occupancy estimate** is 2/4 = 0.50. It ignores misses: S2 or S3 may be occupied. S1 counts as occupied only under the no-false-positive assumption below; its lone positive well is taken up after the decision.

**Worked problem: is S2 still occupied?** S2 gave 20 negative wells: 2 samples × 2 extractions × 5 wells.

| Measured elsewhere (borrowed, illustrative) | Our inputs |
|---|---|
| **γ = 0.49**, estimated without inhibitor removal | **θ = 0.60** (assumed) |
| **δ = 0.38** | **prior = 0.20**: our belief, before sampling, that S2 is occupied |
| Wild pigs, central Texas, one autumn (Amy Davis and colleagues, 2018); each has a standard error | **Stop rule** (a decision choice): stop when P(occupied) is 5% or below |

Assume no false positives (an empty pond always tests negative), S2 occupied or empty for the whole survey, and independence at every level: at an occupied pond, one sample missing does not change the chance the next one misses, and likewise for extractions from one sample and wells from one extraction. θ and the prior are assumed; a real analysis would estimate or inform them from relevant data, but four ponds are far too few to do so reliably. Work from the inside out with the product rule from [probability and crosses](/lessons/probability-and-crosses/): replicates at one level all miss with the product of their miss chances, and detection needs every step to succeed.

1. *Wells.* One run misses with 1 − 0.38 = 0.62; all five miss with 0.62^5 = 0.092. So five wells catch captured DNA with **A = 0.908**.
2. *Extractions.* One extraction finds the DNA only if it captures it *and* the wells catch it: 0.49 × 0.908 = 0.445, so it misses with 1 − 0.445 = 0.555. Both miss with 0.555² = 0.308, so the pair finds DNA in the water with **B = 0.692**.

A sample detects only if its water holds DNA *and* the lab finds it, so **q**, the first film's chance that one water sample detects an occupied pond, is θ × B.

**Predict.** The water holds DNA only 60% of the time. Will q be closer to 0.69 or to 0.40?

In the English newt surveys analysed by Andrew Buxton and colleagues (2021), twenty subsamples from around each pond were **pooled** into one water sample: wider coverage, but one field sample, leaving no field replicates to estimate θ. In the Texas pig trial, the authors suspected that pooling each site's ten samples diluted the DNA, and pooled data underestimated occupancy at sites known to be occupied. Buxton's simulations found that replicating both field and lab work reduced bias and narrowed credible intervals (Bayesian ranges of plausible values). Only new water is a new try at the scoop.

## What a positive can and cannot mean

So far we assumed no false positives. Clean blanks make contamination less likely but cannot exclude a rare, one-off event. S1's single positive well out of twenty is the sparse pattern Buxton and colleagues judged more likely than others to be a false positive, and their two published models, one of which ignored false positives, gave overall occupancy of 0.26 and 0.47 for the same 2,958 English ponds surveyed in 2019 (one pooled sample each, twelve qPCR replicates). With no ground truth, the authors judged that the model ignoring false positives overestimated; false-positive parameters can need calibration or informative priors to be identified.

Place matters too: in flowing water, a true DNA detection can falsely signal occupancy at that spot. In the river Glatt below Lake Greifensee, Switzerland, Kristy Deiner and Florian Altermatt (2014) detected the lake-dwelling water flea *Daphnia longispina* at every sampled river location, as far as 12.3 km downstream, but did not detect the mussel *Unio tumidus* beyond 9.1 km.

### Design problem

A river survey detects an endangered mussel's eDNA at one site 5 km below a lake where the mussel lives. Does the mussel occur at the site? Design the follow-up.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

A positive can mean a local population, DNA carried down from the lake, or contamination. In the Glatt, mussel DNA was detected downstream though not beyond 9.1 km, so transport over 5 km is plausible in at least one river.

1. *Check for contamination.* Carry field blanks, extraction blanks and PCR negatives through every batch, and sequence positives to confirm the species.
2. *Test transport.* Sample from the lake outlet down past the site, plus each tributary between. A local population would tend to add signal at and below the site; transport alone would tend to fade with distance from the lake. DNA settling on the streambed and stirred back up, and patchy flow, blur both patterns, so use replicate samples and flow data.
3. *Replicate in the field.* Independent water samples on separate dates, not more wells; record flow and pH.
4. *Model and confirm.* DNA carried from the lake is a false positive for this site, so the no-false-positive model above fits only once transport is excluded; otherwise use a model that allows transport or other false positives, with stated assumptions. Either way, search the site directly with a method suited to mussels.
5. *Agree access first.* Settle with whoever governs the river and the data how results will be shared.

</details>

## Reproducible analysis and the decision

Keep the raw file untouched; work on copies. A **data dictionary** defines every column: site, date, sample, extraction, PCR replicate, control, treatment, covariates. Record a non-detection differently from a missing, inhibited or inconclusive run; an inhibited well, where substances carried over from the sample blocked the PCR, is not a negative.

Sandve and colleagues (2013) set out rules that apply here: replace manual editing with scripts, archive the exact program versions, record every parameter and input, and record random seeds whenever an analysis uses randomness. FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable) adds provenance and a clear licence. Then rerun from the raw file and check you get 7.9% and 4.8% again, with θ, the prior and the threshold beside them.

## Whose data? CARE and FAIR

Surveys happen on someone's land and water. FAIR makes data usable by machines and people, and it permits authentication and authorization; it does not mean open to all. The **CARE principles** for Indigenous data governance, drafted in Gaborone, Botswana, on 8 November 2018 and published in 2020, add the question of who decides: **Collective Benefit** (data should benefit Indigenous Peoples), **Authority to Control** (Indigenous Peoples determine governance protocols for their data), **Responsibility** (respectful relationships with the people the data come from) and **Ethics** (their rights and wellbeing are central across the data's life). A 2021 paper describes CARE as a complement to FAIR that covers scientific data as well as traditional knowledge.

For eDNA, a rare species' location in a community's water is itself data whose release that community may have the authority to control. This workshop uses no real community dataset and claims no permission. Merging eDNA results with a community's observations would first mean identifying the rights holders, the benefit and the permitted purpose, and getting their protocols; a downloadable file is not permission.

## Going deeper

### The model in one line

With w wells per extraction and k extractions per sample, A = 1 − (1 − δ)^w and q = θ × [1 − (1 − γA)^k]; n independent samples miss an occupied site with m = (1 − q)^n. In odds, each negative sample multiplies the odds of occupancy by 1 − q: the hidden-pond count in other words. These equations assume no false positives, closure (occupancy fixed during sampling), conditional independence at every replicate level (wells, extractions and samples, as in the worked problem), and parameters constant across sites and visits (or explained by covariates). A model that allows false positives needs extra false-positive parameters, which must be identifiable, and a different posterior.

### When lab work could cross the line

Perfect lab work makes q = θ = 0.60: one negative sample leaves 0.20 × 0.40 / (0.80 + 0.08) = 9.1%, two leave 3.8%. With unlimited wells an extraction still captures only with γ = 0.49, so q = 0.60 × [1 − 0.51^k] for k extractions: two samples with four extractions each give 4.6% (three extractions, 5.4%).

### The pseudoreplication arithmetic

Treating each of S2's 20 wells as an independent try detecting with 0.60 × 0.49 × 0.38 = 0.112 gives a miss probability of 0.888^20 = 0.094 and the second film's 2.3%.

### Carrying the uncertainty through

Borrowed point estimates hide their uncertainty and covariates (Davis and colleagues found DNA availability varied with month, pH and water-body type). The γ table is a one-at-a-time sensitivity check. For management use, compute the posterior across the joint uncertainty of γ, δ, θ and the other fitted parameters, with relevant covariates, and test whether the decision changes.
