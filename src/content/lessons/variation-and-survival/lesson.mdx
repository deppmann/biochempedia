## Explain: where variation comes from

<WordBank terms={['variation', 'allele', 'gene pool', 'mutation', 'gene flow', 'sexual reproduction', 'natural selection', 'selective pressure', 'reproductive strategy']} />

Look around a classroom. People differ in height, hair, voice and much more. Some of those differences are inherited, and some come from the environment, such as food or exercise. Only inherited differences pass to the next generation.

**Variation** is the differences among individuals in a population. An **allele** is one version of a gene. The **gene pool** is all the alleles of all the individuals in a population. Three processes add to or reshape it.

- **Mutation** is a change in an organism’s DNA. It is the original source of new alleles. Many mutations have no effect, some harm, and a few help, depending on the environment. In most animals, only mutations in the cells that make eggs or sperm pass to offspring.
- **Gene flow** is the movement of alleles from one population to another, when individuals move and breed or when pollen blows to another field. It can bring in an allele the population did not have.
- **Sexual reproduction** joins an egg and a sperm from two parents. You saw in [Meiosis and Variation](/lessons/meiosis-and-variation/) that meiosis shuffles chromosome pairs and swaps pieces between them. This makes new combinations of alleles that already exist, so each offspring is a new mix.

Over many generations, new mutations and gene flow change what is in the gene pool. Natural selection and chance (genetic drift) also change how common each allele is; you meet selection next.

## Explain: more young than can survive

Populations tend to make more young than the environment can support. A female blue crab in the Chesapeake Bay may carry an egg mass of about 750,000 to 8 million eggs, according to the Virginia Institute of Marine Science. Only a small fraction of those eggs become adult crabs. Most die young.

**Natural selection** is the process by which individuals with certain inherited variations survive and reproduce more than others, so those variations become more common in later generations. Strength alone does not decide it. What counts is how many offspring an individual leaves.

A **selective pressure** is anything in the environment that makes some variants more likely to survive or reproduce than others: predators, disease, drought or human hunting. In Gorongosa, poachers wanted ivory, so tusked elephants were killed more often. Tuskless females already existed, and they survived more often. In this park tusklessness is seen only in females; no tuskless male has been recorded there. To test whether chance alone could explain the rise, the researchers simulated the park's population crash with tusked and tuskless females surviving equally often. The real rise was far larger than chance would produce, and tuskless females were estimated to have survived about five times as well. They concluded that poaching most likely caused strong selection for tusklessness.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/variation-and-survival/tusked-and-tuskless-females.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two female African elephants side by side on a savanna, one with a pair of tusks and one with no tusks at all." caption="Tuskless females were already part of the Gorongosa population before the war. Poachers killed tusked elephants more often, so tuskless females survived more often." />

Pressure can be strong or weak. A strong pressure, like heavy poaching, can change a trait’s frequency within a few decades. A weak pressure changes it slowly over many generations. Biologists argue about how often evolution happens in quick bursts and how often it creeps along; you will meet those two ideas in [Natural Selection and New Species](/lessons/natural-selection-and-speciation/).

A **reproductive strategy** is a species’ pattern of how many young it makes and how much it invests in each. The table compares two endpoints.

| | Blue crab | African savanna elephant |
|---|---|---|
| Young per breeding | Hundreds of thousands to millions of eggs | Usually one calf |
| Care | Mother carries the eggs until they hatch | Mother nurses the calf, and births are years apart |
| Survival of each young | Very low | Higher |

Many species fall between these endpoints, so treat them as the ends of a range rather than two boxes.

### Design problem

Plan an ethical study to test whether hunting, not chance, explains the rise in tuskless females in a park. You cannot assign hunting to elephants. What groups would you compare, what would you count, and what could mislead you?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Did tuskless females become more common in parks with heavy poaching than in parks with little poaching?
- **Comparison groups:** several parks with heavy poaching and several with little poaching, with photos or counts from before and after the same years.
- **What you count:** the number of adult females with and without tusks in each park and period. Report the number of animals, not only the percentage.
- **Constants:** the same definition of "tuskless", the same age range of females, and observers trained the same way.
- **Sample size and repeats:** as many recognized females as possible, and more than one park in each group, because one small sample can change by chance.
- **What could mislead you:** old photos may show some animals more than others, and tuskless elephants may be easier or harder to spot. Recording each known individual once helps.
- **Ethics:** observe and photograph only; never harm animals to test an idea.

A larger rise in heavy-poaching parks than in low-poaching parks would support selection by hunting.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The rise did not come from Gorongosa elephants losing their tusks. Tuskless females were already part of the population, and poaching killed tusked elephants more often, so tuskless survivors left more of the next generation. The population changed because some individuals survived and reproduced while others did not.
