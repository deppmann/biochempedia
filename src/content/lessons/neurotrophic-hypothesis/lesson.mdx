scientists:
  - name: "Rita Levi-Montalcini"
    contribution: >-
      With Viktor Hamburger, showed in chick spinal ganglia that many neurons form and then
      degenerate when their peripheral field is reduced; with Barbara Booker, showed in 1960 that
      purified NGF enlarges sympathetic ganglia in young mammals and that an antiserum to it
      destroys them in newborns.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Hamburger & Levi-Montalcini, J. Exp. Zool. 111:457 (1949); Levi-Montalcini & Booker, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 46:373 and 46:384 (1960)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      A single count of a small ganglion cannot tell you why it is small. Too few neurons may
      have been made, or many may have been made and lost. In a 1949 paper with Viktor Hamburger,
      Levi-Montalcini followed chick spinal ganglia stage by stage after removing or adding limb
      tissue, recording cell counts, mitoses and degenerating cells. Many neurons formed and sent
      out fibres before the deficit appeared, and dying cells became conspicuous later. The
      periphery was implicated in keeping neurons alive, not only in calling them into
      existence. Years later, once Stanley Cohen had purified the nerve growth factor from mouse
      salivary glands and raised an antiserum against it, Levi-Montalcini and Barbara Booker
      tested both reagents in mammals in two papers in the same 1960 issue of PNAS. The purified
      protein enlarged sympathetic ganglia. The antiserum, given to newborn mice, destroyed them. The pair gave the field an add-it and a
      take-it-away experiment on one protein in a living animal, the form that later tests of
      trophic factors followed.
  - name: "Stanley Cohen"
    contribution: >-
      Purified the nerve growth-promoting protein from the mouse submaxillary salivary gland and
      raised an antiserum that blocked its activity, the reagent behind immunosympathectomy.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Cohen, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 46:302 (1960); Cohen, J. Biol. Chem. 237:1555 (1962)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Cohen was the biochemist in the search for the factor. Snake venom had turned out to be a
      far richer source of the activity than the mouse tumour where it was first seen, and the
      venom gland is a modified salivary gland. Mouse submaxillary salivary glands proved
      rich in the activity too, and in a 1960 PNAS paper Cohen described a protein purified from
      them, the assay used to follow it, and an antiserum that blocked its activity in culture.
      That antiserum turned a factor that could only be added into one that could also be taken
      away, and it went straight into the newborn-animal tests that Rita Levi-Montalcini
      and Barbara Booker ran. The salivary-gland extracts did something else in newborn mice: the
      eyelids opened and the incisors erupted early. Cohen isolated the protein responsible, a
      different one, and in 1962 called it a factor that accelerates incisor eruption and eyelid
      opening. That protein is now known as epidermal growth factor. The hunt for one
      developmental signal had handed him a second.
  - name: "Viktor Hamburger"
    contribution: >-
      Made the size of the periphery an experimental variable in chick embryos, and across four
      decades showed that removing it shrinks neural centres and enlarging it reduces the normal
      loss of motor neurons.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Hamburger, J. Exp. Zool. 68:449 (1934); Hollyday & Hamburger, J. Comp. Neurol. 170:311 (1976)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In 1934 Hamburger removed one wing bud from chick embryos about three days old and left the
      other side as the comparison. Muscle and the motor and sensory populations serving that
      limb came out smaller on the operated side. He first read this as the periphery recruiting
      or controlling the growth of cells in the neural centres. The 1949 study with Rita
      Levi-Montalcini added stages, and with them a different reading: neurons formed and were
      then lost. In 1976, with Margaret Hollyday, he ran the converse experiment. An extra leg
      grafted beside the normal one did not raise the number of lumbar motor neurons at day 6,
      before the normal loss, but in embryos whose grafts moved vigorously more motor neurons
      remained later. They proposed a word for it, hypothanasia: not extra birth but reduced
      death. Removing target and adding target, read across time, are the first two predictions
      of the neurotrophic hypothesis, and Hamburger's chick operations made both testable.
  - name: "Mariano Barbacid"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory, with Richard Smeyne, Ruediger Klein and colleagues, made mice lacking a
      functional TrkA receptor and showed that they suffer severe sensory and sympathetic
      neuropathies.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Smeyne et al., Nature 368:246 (1994); Fagan et al., J. Neurosci. 16:6208 (1996)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Barbacid's laboratory first met trk as a cancer gene, a fusion of a tropomyosin sequence
      with a tyrosine kinase found in a human tumour. Once trk was identified as a receptor for
      NGF, a question that the antiserum experiments had left open became answerable. An
      antiserum might kill neurons by binding something other than NGF; a deleted gene has no
      such side route. In 1994, in Nature, Richard Smeyne, Ruediger Klein, Barbacid and
      colleagues reported mice carrying a disrupted gene for the TrkA receptor. The mice had
      severe sensory and sympathetic neuropathies. In 1996, Anne Fagan, Story Landis, Smeyne,
      Barbacid and colleagues followed the superior cervical ganglion of these mice across
      embryonic and postnatal ages. Loss of TrkA did not affect neurogenesis or neuronal markers, but a deficit had appeared by embryonic day 15.5 and kept growing. That result made the genetic result read
      as a failure of survival rather than of production, which is what the neurotrophic
      hypothesis predicts.

techniques:
  - name: "Antibody neutralization (immunosympathectomy)"
    blurb: >-
      An antiserum against a purified factor is injected into an animal to remove the factor's
      activity. If a population dies, it needed something the antiserum removed. The caveat is
      specificity: an antiserum might also bind something else.
  - name: "Gene knockout in the mouse"
    blurb: >-
      A knockout is a mouse in which a gene has been disrupted so that it makes no working
      protein. Deleting the gene for NGF or for its receptor TrkA tests necessity without relying
      on an antibody, though the gene is missing from every cell for the whole of life.
  - name: "Neuron counts across the death window"
    blurb: >-
      Neurons are counted in serial sections at a series of ages before, during and after the
      normal period of cell death, together with dividing cells and pyknotic (condensed, dying)
      nuclei. The time course shows whether a population was never made or was made and lost.
  - name: "Birthdating with a proliferation marker"
    blurb: >-
      A labelled nucleotide such as tritiated thymidine or BrdU is taken up only by cells copying
      their DNA, so it marks the cells that divide while it is present. A short pulse counted soon
      after measures how many cells are copying DNA, not how many neurons are produced; following
      the labelled cells until they become neurons (pulse-chase birthdating) shows whether a factor
      changed how many neurons were born.

      synapses. Oxygen is not the resource. The factor is a protein made by the target's cells, and blood

  - stem: "Which experiment can tell whether a factor that keeps sympathetic neurons alive also chooses where their axons go?"
    choices:

      - "Remove the factor and count how many neurons die"
      - "Supply the factor evenly and ask if the pattern forms"
      - "Delete the death gene Bax and count surviving neurons"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Only a change in where or when the signal is delivered separates permissive from instructive. If
      the innervation pattern still forms with the factor everywhere, something else reads
      position; if the pattern follows the delivery, the factor instructs. Removing the factor
      tests necessity, and adding it tests sufficiency for survival; neither says whether it
      chooses the target. Deleting Bax keeps neurons alive, which is a useful background for the
      test but by itself does not ask the question.
  - stem: "A saturating dose of NGF is given to chick embryos throughout the period when lumbar motor neurons that supply the leg normally die. What do you predict for those motor neurons?"

      - "They switch to a sympathetic neuron identity"
      - "Extra motor neurons are born and the cord grows"
      - "Their death proceeds much as it normally does"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Limb motor neurons are not NGF-dependent, so NGF should leave their normal death roughly
      unchanged; in chick embryos several other factors each reduce part of that death. Sparing
      most of them is the prediction for an NGF-dependent population, such as sympathetic
      neurons. No survival factor acting on neurons already made adds extra neurons; that would be proliferation. A survival

    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Normally 18,000 minus 11,000, or 7,000, neurons die. With NGF, 16,500 minus 11,000, or
      5,500, extra neurons survive, and 5,500 divided by 7,000 is about 79 percent. About 31
      percent divides the 5,500 saved by all 18,000 neurons, not by those that would have died.

  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Embryos make more nerve cells than they keep. The neurotrophic hypothesis says the tissues
      that nerves grow into release a small amount of a survival protein, and nerve cells compete
      for it. Nerve growth factor (NGF) was the test case. Adding NGF to young animals made some
      nerve clusters bigger, blocking it with antibodies destroyed them, and mice missing the NGF
      gene or its receptor lost largely the same nerve cells. During the period when nerve cells
      normally die, NGF works by keeping cells alive, so extra NGF can save cells that would have
      died but cannot add cells that were never made.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      The neurotrophic factor hypothesis holds that neurons compete for a limiting amount of
      target-derived neurotrophic factor, and those that obtain too little undergo apoptosis.
      For NGF the tests run in four steps: purified NGF enlarges sympathetic and NGF-dependent
      sensory ganglia (sufficiency); antiserum to NGF destroys sympathetic ganglia in newborn
      rodents (necessity, with a specificity caveat); NGF-null and TrkA-null mice lose
      sympathetic neurons and TrkA-expressing, mostly small nociceptive and thermoceptive sensory
      neurons (genetic necessity); and the TrkA-null ganglion forms with a normal count before
      its neurons die (survival, not production). For survival, NGF is permissive: it lets
      neurons that were made persist, so it raises neuron number only up to the number
      generated. It also has instructive effects on axon growth, target innervation and gene
      expression.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      Necessity and sufficiency for survival leave two distinct questions. One is the ceiling:
      in an ideal allocation model, a cohort of N neurons already made, with none added, shares a
      supply of F units per day, and each survivor needs at least q per day, so survivors S ≤
      min(N, F/q), rounded down. This is a model bound, not a dose-to-neuron law. When supply is
      short, survivors reach that bound only if the factor ends up concentrated on a subset;
      equal sharing with commitment to death before freed factor is redistributed can leave far
      fewer. Extra NGF acting on survival cannot exceed the cohort; because requirements vary the
      response is graded, and NGF-independent death can hold the plateau below the generated
      number. Counts across the death window with proliferation and death markers test this, and
      the ceiling holds only for the population measured; limb motor neurons respond to several
      factors and not to NGF. The other is whether NGF is instructive for anything beyond
      survival. With death blocked by Bax deletion, sympathetic neurons lacking NGF still fail
      to innervate many targets, and NGF raises its own receptor's expression, so it is needed for more
      than survival. Spatial tests show it also instructs: when sympathetic neurons make NGF
      themselves, adding a neuronal source to the target's, axons still reach their tissues but
      innervate them sparsely, and NGF made in pancreatic islets raises innervation of the islets
      but not of the surrounding exocrine tissue (Hoyle et al. 1993; Edwards et al. 1989). In the targets examined, these results fit a model in
      which NGF gradients chiefly set the density and pattern of innervation within a target rather
      than long-range approach to it; the experiments altered NGF sources and did not show that
      every guidance-relevant gradient was absent. The open question is how a permissive survival rule and
      these instructive effects combine to set which neurons win.

**Inject a newborn mouse with an antiserum against one protein, and its sympathetic ganglia waste away. Give it extra of the same protein, and they swell.**

The protein is nerve growth factor, NGF, and the neurotrophic hypothesis explains its power: neurons are made in excess, the targets they reach supply a limiting amount of a survival factor, and the neurons compete for it. This lesson follows the tests of that idea one at a time and ends on a limit: what extra NGF can and cannot do to the number of neurons.

## Too small, or lost?

The idea began with chick embryos and a puzzle about size. Two words first. A **target** is the tissue an axon grows to and connects with, such as a limb muscle or a gland. A **ganglion** is a cluster of neuron cell bodies outside the brain and spinal cord; the spinal ganglia beside the cord hold sensory neurons, and the sympathetic ganglia hold neurons of the sympathetic nervous system, the fight-or-flight division that speeds the heart and readies the body for action.

In 1934 Viktor Hamburger removed one wing bud from chick embryos about three days old and left the other side as the comparison. The neurons serving that limb came out reduced on the operated side. He first read this as the limb controlling how many cells grew in the spinal cord and its ganglia.

**Predict.** A spinal ganglion on the operated side ends up smaller. Name two different histories that could have produced it, and say what you would measure to tell them apart.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Fewer neurons might have been made, or the normal number made and then lost. A single final count cannot separate the two, because both give a small ganglion. You need counts at a series of stages, with dividing and dying cells marked.

In 1949 Hamburger and Rita Levi-Montalcini did that, following chick spinal ganglia stage by stage after removing or adding limb tissue. Many neurons formed and grew fibres before the deficit appeared, and degenerating cells became conspicuous later. The neurons had been made, and then were lost.

</details>

Three conclusions follow from this early work.

1. Neurons are made in excess and then lost.
2. Whether they are kept depends on the target.
3. So the target must supply something neurons need, and if it supplies too little for all of them, they must in effect compete for it.

Neither the "something" nor the competition had been shown. Those were inferences, and the hypothesis would have to earn them. How many neurons die, and when, is the subject of [the lesson on naturally occurring cell death](/lessons/naturally-occurring-death/).

<LessonFigure src="/figures/neurotrophic-hypothesis/made-then-matched.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: three frames of one ganglion of eight neurons whose axons all reach a target. In the first all eight reach it; in the second the target supplies a few dots of survival factor, taken up by four of the axons; in the third those four survive and the other four are dashed outlines, dying." caption="The neurotrophic hypothesis in three steps: neurons are made in excess, the target supplies a limited amount of a survival factor, and the neurons that get too little die. The middle step was at first an inference that later experiments had to earn." />

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Make too many, then let the target keep what it can feed. Acting as a survival factor, NGF decides how many of the neurons that were made stay alive. It can never raise that number above the number made.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which neurons.** NGF-dependent populations during their death windows: sympathetic neurons, and the sensory neurons that carry TrkA, the receptor NGF binds, mostly small neurons that sense pain and temperature. Limb motor neurons and some other populations fall outside it, and older animals are much less sensitive.
- **Which effect.** Only NGF's survival effect. NGF also affects division, growth and branching.
- **A ceiling, not a count.** The rule says how many can survive at most, not how many do.

## The hypothesis and what it predicts

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Stated formally: neurons compete for a limiting amount of neurotrophic factor, which their targets supply and which axons reaching those targets take up, and neurons that get too little die by apoptosis, a built-in program of cell death. The hypothesis makes five predictions you can check one at a time; the rest of the lesson tests the first four.

- **Remove the target, and more neurons die.** Hamburger's wing-bud removal, read across time in 1949, fits.
- **Add target, and fewer die.** Tested next.
- **Add factor, and fewer die.** Tested in [Add it, take it away](#add-it-take-it-away).
- **Remove factor, and more die.** Tested by antibody and then by genetics.
- **Most of the death should happen around the time axons reach the target and begin to compete.**

In 1976 Margaret Hollyday and Hamburger ran the add-target test. They grafted an extra leg onto chick embryos beside the normal one. Lumbar motor neuron numbers were not raised at day 6, before the normal loss, but more remained later in embryos whose grafts moved vigorously. They proposed a word for it, hypothanasia: not extra birth but reduced death.

The caution: these were motor neurons, which do not depend on NGF. The result supports a limiting target resource but says nothing about which molecule; Hollyday and Hamburger left open whether it was synaptic space or a trophic agent.

## Add it, take it away

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

You met the discovery in [the lesson on finding a signal](/lessons/finding-a-signal/): a mouse tumour grafted into a chick embryo drew in sensory and sympathetic fibres, an activity it released could do the same in a culture dish, and Stanley Cohen purified that activity as a protein. The question here is what the protein does in a normal animal.

Two words do most of the work from here on. A factor is **sufficient** if it is enough, by itself, to produce an effect. It is **necessary** if the effect does not happen without it. Each experiment below tests one of these and no more.

**Sufficiency.** In 1960 Levi-Montalcini and Barbara Booker gave the purified salivary-gland protein to young mammals and reported greatly enlarged sympathetic ganglia. Purified factor injected into chick embryos likewise makes sensory and sympathetic ganglia overgrow. A larger ganglion does not yet say whether neurons were saved or born.

**Necessity, by antibody.** Cohen raised an antiserum, serum containing antibodies against one molecule, that blocked NGF's activity in culture. In companion papers that year, Levi-Montalcini and Booker injected it into newborn mice, with rats and other mammals for comparison. The sympathetic ganglia were destroyed. The procedure became known as immunosympathectomy.

The same antiserum has much less effect on the sympathetic ganglia of older animals, so dependence on NGF is tied to a developmental window, and the window differs between populations. NGF-dependent sensory neurons are hit hardest by earlier exposure: in 1980 Johnson and colleagues destroyed rat dorsal root ganglion neurons with maternal antibody to NGF that reached the fetuses in utero.

An antiserum carries a doubt. It might kill neurons by binding something on them other than NGF. How would you remove NGF without an antibody?

## Delete it, and watch the time course

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

A **knockout** mouse has a gene disrupted so that it makes no working protein, and it settles the antibody doubt. In 1994 Crowley and colleagues reported mice lacking NGF, and Richard Smeyne, Ruediger Klein, Mariano Barbacid and colleagues reported mice lacking a functional TrkA. TrkA is a receptor tyrosine kinase ([the lesson on receptor tyrosine kinases](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)), and its family has its own lesson, [the lesson on Trk and p75 receptors](/lessons/trk-and-p75/). Both mutants lose sympathetic neurons and many sensory neurons.

A ganglion counted only at birth in a TrkA-null mouse looks depleted, which could mean too few precursors, failed differentiation or excess death. The question from the chick is back: were its neurons never made, or made and lost?

**Predict.** In 1996 Anne Fagan, Story Landis, Smeyne, Barbacid and colleagues counted the superior cervical ganglion, the sympathetic ganglion at the top of the neck, in TrkA-null mice at a series of embryonic and postnatal ages. Sketch neuron number against age for normal and TrkA-null mice. What shape do you expect if TrkA is needed for survival but not for production?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Loss of TrkA did not affect neurogenesis (the birth of neurons from dividing precursors), neuronal markers or initial axon growth, but the receptor was absolutely necessary for the survival of sympathetic neurons after embryonic day 15.5. The counts were alike at embryonic day 13.5, soon after the ganglion forms. By embryonic day 15.5 the mutant ganglion already had about 15 percent fewer cells, and the gap kept growing. The neurons were produced; what failed was survival.

</details>

That time course is the signature evidence for a failure of survival. Fagan's team also counted dividing cells and pyknotic (condensed, dying) nuclei: the shortfall came from more dying cells, not fewer dividing ones.

How does NGF keep a neuron alive? Neurons that get too little NGF die by a death that needs the pro-apoptotic protein Bax. Mice lacking Bax keep sympathetic neurons that would otherwise die, and in culture, sympathetic neurons lacking Bax survive when NGF is withdrawn. So NGF works by holding back a Bax-dependent death program. That story is told in [the lesson on death machinery in neurons](/lessons/death-machinery-in-neurons/).

## What extra NGF can do

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

Every test so far says one thing: NGF keeps neurons alive. Now turn that around and ask what follows from it.

**Predict.** Give a chick embryo extra NGF every day through the period when its sympathetic and sensory neurons normally die. Which happens: no change; more of the neurons survive than normal; new sympathetic ganglia appear in the wrong places; or sympathetic fibres invade every tissue? And is there a limit to how large the ganglia can get?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

More of the neurons survive. Given during the death period, NGF enlarges the ganglia mainly because more of the neurons that were made escape the normal death. The survivors also grow larger and sprout more fibres, so the "invade" option is not entirely wrong, but survival support is not expected to change neuron type. Extra survival support should preserve neurons in the existing ganglia; by itself it does not specify new ganglion locations. Ectopic ganglia would need evidence of migration, aggregation or survival at ectopic sites, not necessarily extra births. In 1981 Hamburger, Brunso-Bechtold and Yip counted chick dorsal root ganglia across the death period and found that NGF reduced the normal death.

There is a limit: a survival factor can save a neuron that would have died, not one that was never made.

</details>

**How would you know the extra neurons were saved, not born?** A larger ganglion at one late age cannot tell you. Three checks can.

1. **Count across the window.** Count neurons, and pyknotic nuclei, in the same ganglia, treated and untreated, at several stages: soon after the ganglion forms, during the death period, and after it. If NGF acts on survival, the counts match early and diverge only during the death period, and the treated ganglia contain fewer pyknotic nuclei.
2. **Mark division and follow the progeny.** A short pulse of a proliferation marker such as BrdU or tritiated thymidine, counted soon after, shows how many precursors are copying DNA, not how many neurons are produced. Combine such proliferation and cell-cycle measurements with pulse-chase birthdating or lineage tracing across the whole interval to test whether cumulative neuron production changed.
3. **Check identity and lineage.** Confirm the expected type with several markers, such as tyrosine hydroxylase, an enzyme sympathetic neurons need to make noradrenaline, and follow the original cohort by lineage tracing or pulse-chase birthdating. Markers alone cannot exclude a fate change, because converted cells would carry the same markers in the same place.

The checks matter in the chick. Very early, before sympathetic axons reach their targets, extra NGF can also increase division in the primary sympathetic ganglia, the first sympathetic clusters to form, and it also reduces the low level of death there. The extra division is NGF acting as something other than a survival factor, and these checks catch it.

### The ceiling

Put the logic in one line, as an ideal model:

> **neurons surviving ≤ the smaller of (neurons made) and (supply per day ÷ the least each survivor needs per day)**

The model takes one group of N neurons already made, with none added, and a shared supply of F units per day, the target's plus any you add, from which every neuron needs at least q units per day. S survivors take at least S × q, which cannot exceed F, so S ≤ the smaller of N and F/q, rounded down. It is a bound for this model, not a measured law linking an NGF dose to neuron number. Work it for a ganglion that has made 1,000 neurons, each needing one unit a day.

| Supply per day | Neurons made | Survivors, at most | What limits them |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 500 units | 1,000 | 500 | the target |
| 1,000 units | 1,000 | 1,000 | both |
| 3,000 units | 1,000 | 1,000 | the number made |

In the middle row the two limits are equal, so either one alone would stop survivors at 1,000.

Extra factor raises the second term. Acting as a survival factor, nothing raises the first. Once every neuron that was made is supported, more factor adds no survivors. When the three checks together support a survival effect, the larger ganglia are made of neurons that would have died, and their number stays at or below the number made. Real ganglia can fall short of the ceiling. Neurons differ in how much they need, so survival rises gradually with dose. Some die for reasons NGF cannot fix, so even plenty of NGF can leave the count below the number made. And if winners take more than they need, fewer survive.

### A puzzle hidden in the ceiling

The first row states only a bound. To reach it, all 500 units would have to go to 500 neurons, enough for each, with none wasted. Suppose instead the target spread them evenly over all 1,000.

**Predict.** Every neuron gets half of what it needs. How many survive?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It depends on timing, and the answer could be none. If survival needs a full unit, every neuron is equally short and at risk at once. Then:

1. Some neurons commit to dying.
2. Each death frees factor for the rest, because the continuing supply can be redirected to the neurons still taking it up, but a neuron that has already committed cannot use it.
3. If neurons commit slowly and freed factor is shared out quickly, close to 500 can survive. If many commit before it is shared out again, far fewer are left, perhaps none.

Equal sharing alone does not say how many survive.

</details>

So the "at most" is doing real work: when supply is short, survivors reach it only if the factor ends up with some neurons rather than spread over all, through slow, random loss with quick re-sharing or through competition that picks winners early.

In mouse sympathetic neurons, evidence supports a model in which competition does this actively. NGF raises the level of its own receptor, TrkA, in the neurons that receive it (shown in mouse and rat sympathetic neurons), so a neuron that gets a little more can take up still more. Neurons with strong NGF signalling also release two related factors, BDNF and NT-4. These act through a second receptor, p75, on neighbours whose NGF signalling is weak, and push them toward death (Deppmann and colleagues, 2008). In this model, competition is more than a shortage: winners are amplified and losers are punished.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/neurotrophic-hypothesis/winners-and-losers.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two sympathetic neurons below a target strip. The left neuron takes up NGF at its axon tip, carries many TrkA receptors, and releases BDNF and NT-4 toward its neighbour. The right neuron gets little NGF, has few TrkA receptors, and binds the released factors through p75, with a dashed arrow to 'Pushed toward death'." caption="In the model the evidence supports for mouse sympathetic neurons, a neuron that gets more NGF makes more TrkA and takes up still more, and it releases BDNF and NT-4 that act through p75 on neighbours with weak NGF signalling, pushing them toward death." />

The ceiling holds only for the population you measured, with its own factor. One found for sympathetic neurons and NGF does not transfer to limb motor neurons, which do not depend on NGF: in chick embryos several other factors each reduce part of their normal death, and extra NGF should leave it roughly unchanged.

## Permissive for survival, instructive for wiring

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

Survival is not the whole story. NGF passes the tests of necessity and sufficiency for survival, but neither test says whether NGF chooses anything. A **permissive** signal allows an outcome that something else chooses. An **instructive** signal chooses the outcome itself. Fuel lets a car go anywhere; the driver picks where. Fuel is permissive, the driver instructive. The analogy breaks in one place, as you will see: within a target, NGF also acts like the driver.

Removing a signal and restoring its normal pattern cannot separate the two; it shows only that the signal is needed. What separates them is changing where or when the signal is delivered while keeping it present. Supply it evenly everywhere, or at an arbitrary new place or time, and ask whether the pattern still forms. If it does, something else is reading position, and the signal was permissive. If the pattern follows the delivery, the signal is instructive.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/neurotrophic-hypothesis/permissive-vs-instructive.webp" width={1200} height={774} alt="AI-generated illustration: three strips of eight tiles. A normal strip has four sage then four clay tiles. In the permissive case the signal is spread evenly and the pattern is unchanged. In the instructive case the signal is delivered at a new place and the clay region forms near it; the picture shows a different pattern, not cells moving." caption="To tell the two apart, keep the signal present but change where it is delivered. If the pattern is unchanged, something else is reading position and the signal was permissive; if the pattern follows the delivery, the signal is instructive." />

**Predict.** In 1989 Edwards and colleagues directed NGF to the pancreatic islets of otherwise normal mice. Islets are small clusters of hormone-making cells scattered through the pancreas. If NGF only permits survival, where should sympathetic fibres go? If it instructs where they branch, where should they go?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Sympathetic innervation of the islets rose sharply, with no matching rise in the tissue around them. The pattern followed the delivery, so here NGF was instructive.

</details>

Hoyle and colleagues in 1993 ran the converse test. They made mice whose sympathetic neurons made NGF themselves, so the target no longer held the only supply. Axons still reached their tissues, so reaching them did not need the target to be the only source. But they branched sparsely inside them, and making NGF in the pancreatic islets of these mice restored islet innervation. In the targets examined, the results fit a model in which NGF is permissive for reaching a target and instructive for how densely, and where, axons innervate within it. The experiments altered NGF sources; they did not show that every guidance-relevant gradient was absent.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/neurotrophic-hypothesis/islet-tests.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: three patches of pancreas, each with two islets and sympathetic fibres entering from above. Normal: fibres branch moderately throughout. NGF made in islets: fibres branch densely in and around the islets. Neurons make their own NGF: fibres reach the tissue but branch sparsely." caption="Where NGF is made changes where sympathetic axons branch: NGF made in the islets drew dense innervation there, and when the neurons made NGF themselves, axons still reached their tissues but branched sparsely inside them. In the targets examined, NGF looks permissive for reaching a target and instructive for branching within it." />

For survival, NGF behaves as permissive: it keeps alive neurons that were already specified as sympathetic or sensory. For other effects, such as axon growth, branching and the rise in TrkA described above, it is not merely permissive. Blocking death gives a cleaner test. In mice lacking both NGF and Bax, sympathetic neurons survive without NGF, so any remaining defect is not about survival. They fail to innervate many of their targets.

## Putting it together

Four of the five predictions from the start of the lesson now have tests, each of its own kind. The fifth, the timing of death, needs counts of when axons arrive and when neurons die in the same population; see [Going deeper](#when-the-superior-cervical-ganglion-starts-to-depend).

| Experiment | Animal and age | Observed result | What it supports | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Remove wing bud | chick embryo | fewer neurons later; they had formed first (1949) | implicates the target in keeping neurons alive | which molecule |
| Graft extra leg | chick embryo | no more motor neurons at day 6; more later in embryos whose grafts moved vigorously | extra target can reduce death | which limiting resource; motor neurons are not NGF-dependent |
| Inject NGF | young mammals, chick embryos | larger ganglia | NGF is enough to enlarge the ganglia | whether neurons were saved, born or just grew larger |

| NGF antiserum | newborn mice and rats | sympathetic ganglia destroyed | necessity | that the antibody hit only NGF |
| NGF antiserum, later | older animals | much smaller effect | a window of dependence | — |

For NGF-dependent neurons the guiding idea holds up: make too many, then let the target keep what it can feed. Survival and place are linked, though. NGF does not create a sympathetic or sensory lineage, but because dependence on it differs among types, its availability can influence which types survive. Where it is supplied helps decide which connections persist, and within a target where axons branch.

The ceiling also left a question open: when supply is short, which neurons win, and how? That is the subject of [the lesson on competition for targets](/lessons/competition-for-targets/).

### Design problem

Factor X keeps cultured enteric neurons, the neurons of the gut wall, alive. Design the series of experiments in living animals that would show X is a target-derived neurotrophic factor for them, and say what each experiment can and cannot prove.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Walk the evidence table, with X in place of NGF, but first check the premise.

0. **Is there a death period?** Define which enteric neurons you mean, and measure when they are born, migrate, mature and die. Do not assume an NGF-like death window. If normal death is rare, adding X may not raise survival because endogenous support already suffices; that does not rule out a survival requirement, which removal tests (steps 2 and 3). X may also act on precursors; test that separately.
1. **Sufficiency.** If there is a death period, give extra X then. Count neurons and dying cells across ages, with pulse-chase birthdating and lineage markers. More neurons, fewer dying cells, unchanged production and stable identity support a survival effect. This cannot show that the animal needs X.
2. **Necessity by antibody.** Neutralize X during defined developmental intervals, even without a normal death window, and measure induced death, production, migration and differentiation. More death means the neurons need something the antibody removes, but the antibody might bind something else.
3. **Necessity by genetics.** Delete the gene for X, or for its receptor, conditionally to time the loss. Loss of the same neurons removes the antibody doubt.
4. **Made versus lost.** Count the mutant ganglia at a series of ages. Normal production followed by later loss, with more dying cells and no neurons leaving, means survival failed, not production.
5. **Target-derived.** Show that the target tissue makes and releases X. Delete X only in that tissue, leaving the tissue intact, and check that the neurons die and that local X rescues them. Removing or enlarging the tissue supports the idea but changes many things at once.
6. **Mechanism and the ceiling.** Find which death program these neurons use, block it, and ask whether they then survive without X. Follow one birthdated group: extra X should never leave more of it alive than were born.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Where NGF is made

On the list of NGF-producing targets are the eye, salivary gland, pineal gland, blood vessels, spleen, heart and fat.

### Which neurons depend on NGF

Large neurons that sense muscle stretch depend on a different neurotrophin. Basal forebrain cholinergic neurons, which respond to NGF in culture, still develop in NGF-null mice. Not every neuron that can respond to a factor needs it to exist.

### How a distant target reaches the cell body

The factor acts at the axon ending, and a signal returns to the cell body to support survival.

### When the superior cervical ganglion starts to depend

In the mouse superior cervical ganglion some neurons may come to depend on neurotrophins a little before target innervation is complete.

### A fly example of permissive versus instructive

In 1985 Kathryn Anderson and colleagues used this logic in fly eggs. Embryos from Toll mutant mothers are dorsalized: they lack ventral, belly-side structures. When wild-type cytoplasm was injected into them, the ventralmost part of the rescued pattern formed at the injection site, wherever the needle went in. The pattern followed the delivery, which is the instructive result.
