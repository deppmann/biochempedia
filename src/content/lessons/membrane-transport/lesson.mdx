techniques:
  - name: "Patch clamp"
    blurb: >-
      A glass pipette sealed onto a cell isolates a small patch of membrane, and an amplifier
      records the current through it at a voltage you set. With one channel in the patch you see
      its openings as square steps; excised patches let you change the solution on the
      cytoplasmic face (inside-out) or the outer face (outside-out).
  - name: "Radiotracer flux"
    blurb: >-
      You add a radioactively labelled substrate, stop uptake at timed points by filtering and
      washing, and count the label retained by cells or vesicles. Subtracting a matched control
      and dividing by specific activity gives an amount; a time series gives a rate. Tracer entry
      alone does not show net accumulation, since a carrier can exchange labelled for unlabelled
      substrate.
  - name: "Reconstitution into vesicles and planar bilayers"
    blurb: >-
      A purified membrane protein is placed in artificial lipid vesicles, which can be assayed
      directly or fused with a flat bilayer for electrical recording. If the activity appears with
      the purified protein and nothing else, that protein is enough to carry it.
  - name: "Expression in Xenopus oocytes"
    blurb: >-
      RNA injected into large frog oocytes (immature eggs) is translated into protein, and many membrane proteins made this way reach the surface.
      Comparing injected oocytes with matched control oocytes, by swelling in dilute solution or by
      electrical recording, reveals what the new protein adds.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "In one cycle of its usual forward reaction, what does the Na+/K+-ATPase of an animal cell move?"
    choices:
      - "Three Na+ out and two K+ in, using one ATP"
      - "Two Na+ out and three K+ in, using one ATP"
      - "Three Na+ in and two K+ out, using one ATP"
      - "One Na+ out and one K+ in, using one ATP"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Each cycle hydrolyses one ATP, exports three sodium ions and imports two potassium ions, so
      one net positive charge leaves and the pump is electrogenic. Two sodium out and three
      potassium in reverses the stoichiometry. Sodium in and potassium out reverses the direction;
      that would run the pump down both gradients. One for one would be electroneutral, which the
      pump is not.
  - stem: "Which property identifies a membrane protein as a channel rather than a carrier?"
    choices:
      - "It binds its solute with a definite affinity"
      - "It opens a pore continuous between both sides"
      - "It needs ATP or an ion gradient to move its solute"
      - "It moves only one kind of solute, such as glucose"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A channel, when open, forms an aqueous pathway reaching both sides at once, so ions stream
      through. A carrier exposes its binding site to one side at a time (alternating access).
      Moving one kind of solute describes a uniporter as well as many channels, so it does not
      separate them. Needing ATP or a gradient describes pumps and coupled carriers, not a
      distinguishing feature of channels. Channels also have binding sites in their filters, and
      carriers bind solute, so binding alone does not decide.
  - stem: "Intestinal cells take up glucose from the gut through SGLT1, which can carry glucose into the cell against its concentration gradient. What directly supplies the energy?"
    choices:
      - "The outward concentration gradient for K+"
      - "Phosphorylation of glucose by hexokinase"
      - "Hydrolysis of ATP by SGLT1 during each cycle"
      - "The inward electrochemical gradient for Na+"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      SGLT1 is a symporter: sodium moving down its electrochemical gradient into the cell carries
      glucose with it, which is secondary active transport. The Na+/K+-ATPase keeps that sodium
      gradient up, but SGLT1 itself does not split ATP. The potassium gradient is not coupled to
      SGLT1. Hexokinase acts on glucose after it has entered the cell; it is not what moves glucose across the membrane against its gradient.
  - stem: "A K+-selective membrane is held exactly at the K+ equilibrium potential. What is happening to K+ ions?"
    choices:
      - "K+ ions cross both ways at equal rates"
      - "K+ moves in steadily, pulled by the negative inside"
      - "K+ moves out steadily, down its concentration gradient"
      - "No K+ ions cross because the channels close"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      At the equilibrium potential the electrical and concentration forces on potassium balance, so
      the unidirectional fluxes are equal and net flux is zero. Ions keep moving through open
      channels. Saying no ions cross confuses zero net flux with no movement, and channels do not
      close because of equilibrium. Steady efflux ignores the electrical force, and steady influx
      ignores the concentration force; at equilibrium neither wins.
  - stem: "A patch contains one channel. Its open level is −2 pA. In 1 s of recording it is open for 200 ms in total; after a drug, the open level is still −2 pA but it is open 500 ms per second. What changed?"
    choices:
      - "Channel number rose from one to two or three"
      - "Conductance rose 2.5-fold at a fixed voltage"
      - "Open probability rose from 0.2 to 0.5"
      - "Unitary current rose from −0.4 to −1.0 pA"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Open probability is open time divided by total time: 0.2 before and 0.5 after, so mean
      current goes from −0.4 to −1.0 pA while the step height stays at −2 pA. The unitary current
      is the step height, which did not change; −0.4 and −1.0 pA are mean currents. Conductance
      sets the step height at a given voltage, so it did not change either. Two channels open
      together would show a −4 pA level; a longer open time at one level is not extra channels.

**Hold a cell at −70 millivolts with 140 mM potassium inside and 5 mM outside, open potassium channels, and potassium leaves. Hold it at −100 millivolts and potassium comes in, against a 28-fold concentration difference.**

"Hold" means a voltage clamp: an amplifier fixes the membrane voltage at a value you choose, whatever current flows. The concentrations did not change between the two cases; only the voltage did. Why the voltage can win is the first thing this lesson teaches you to calculate. The second is how to tell which kind of protein carries a solute across, from a recording of one channel and a count of labelled molecules.

## The wall and the one rule

A lipid bilayer has an oily core about as thick as two lipid tails. Gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide and small nonpolar molecules dissolve in that core and cross it by simple diffusion, needing no protein. Water crosses too, though slowly. Ions and larger polar molecules such as glucose cross almost not at all, because leaving water for oil costs them too much energy. You met the chemistry behind this in [membrane structure and function](/lessons/membrane-structure-function/).

So ions and sugars cross the bilayer in useful amounts only through proteins, and the proteins are specific: a potassium channel passes potassium and almost no sodium, and the glucose carrier GLUT1 passes glucose and not fructose. A cell decides what crosses by choosing which proteins to build, where to put them and when to open them. The sidedness of those proteins in the membrane, covered in [membrane organization and topology](/lessons/membrane-organization-topology/), fixes which face of each protein looks into the cell. For a pump that orientation sets which way it pushes; for a channel or a passive carrier, the gradients decide.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Any solute, charged or not, flows on its own only downhill in its own electrochemical energy. That energy has two parts, a concentration push and a voltage push. Channels and passive carriers only open a path: they decide *which* solute crosses and *how fast*, never *which way*. To move something uphill, a protein has to couple it to something else running further downhill: ATP splitting (a pump), another downhill process such as light or electron flow in some pumps, or another ion's gradient (a symporter or antiporter, such as the sodium-glucose carrier SGLT1). The coupled total must still be downhill.

The rule sets the direction of *net* flow at a given moment, treating concentrations as if they were activities (effective concentrations). It does not set the rate, which is the protein's business, or where a cell permeable to several ions will rest (that is [the resting membrane](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/)). Water follows it too: aquaporins open the path, and water's own energy difference, set mainly by the solutes on each side (and by pressure, in walled cells), decides the direction.

## Two pushes, one number

The film at the top of the page works this section's potassium example.

Picture moving one mole of a solute from outside to inside as a walk over a hill made of two slopes. The **concentration push**: from high concentration to low is downhill. The **voltage push**: membrane potential, Vm, is the inside minus the outside, so a negative Vm makes the inside downhill for cations and uphill for anions. The sum is one number:

For each ion there is one voltage at which the two pushes cancel exactly. That is its **equilibrium potential**, E_X. Set ΔG_in to zero and solve for Vm, and you have the Nernst equation:

**Worked problem.** Take a membrane permeable only to K+, at 37 °C (310.15 K), with [K+]in = 140 mM and [K+]out = 5 mM. These are teaching values close to those of a typical mammalian cell.

*Step 1: find E_K.* RT/F = 26.73 mV (from the Nernst card). The ratio 5/140 = 0.0357, and ln(0.0357) = −3.332 (a calculator’s ln key; see [e and ln](/math-toolkit/#e-and-ln)). Here z = +1, so RT/zF = RT/F. So

> E_K = 26.73 mV × (−3.332) = −89.1 mV

**Predict.** Clamp the membrane at −70 mV and open the channels. Does net K+ flow in or out? Now clamp at −100 mV.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

*At −70 mV,* out. Vm − E_K = −70 − (−89.1) = +19.1 mV, so ΔG_in = (+1)(96 485)(+0.0191) = +1.84 kJ/mol. Moving K+ in is uphill. In two-push terms, the concentration push on entering K+ is +8.59 kJ/mol and the voltage push only −6.75 kJ/mol: the inside is not negative enough to hold potassium in.

*At −100 mV,* in. Vm − E_K = −10.9 mV, so ΔG_in = −1.06 kJ/mol. The voltage push has grown to −9.65 kJ/mol and now outweighs the same +8.59. This is the reversal promised at the top.

</details>

*One channel.* Suppose a single K+ channel has a conductance γ (how easily current passes) of 20 picosiemens (pS) and behaves ohmically: its current is proportional to the driving force, Vm − E_K, and is zero at E_K. By convention, **positive current is outward movement of positive charge**. At Vm = −40 mV its current is

> i = γ × (Vm − E_K) = 20 pS × 49.1 mV = 0.98 pA, outward

A current of 0.98 pA is 0.98 × 10^−12 coulombs per second; dividing by the charge on one ion (1.602 × 10^−19 C) gives about 6.1 × 10^6 ions per second through one pore.

**Predict.** Now take chloride (z = −1) with the same gap, Vm − E_Cl = +10 mV. Which way does net chloride flow?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Inward. ΔG_in = (−1) × 96 485 × 0.010 = −965 J/mol, negative. Because z is negative for an anion, the same Vm − E_X gives ΔG_in the opposite sign.

The physical reading, for the usual case of chloride more concentrated outside and a negative inside: the negative inside pushes chloride out. When Vm is less negative than E_Cl, that outward electrical push is weaker than chloride's inward concentration push, so chloride comes in.

</details>

Do not conclude that a cell rests at E_K. A real membrane is permeable to several ions, and the resting potential usually lies between their equilibrium potentials, pulled toward each according to its share of the membrane's conductance (see [the resting membrane](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/)). Many cells rest less negative than E_K for exactly this reason.

## The machinery, sorted by the rule

Two kinds of protein carry solutes across: **channels** and **carriers**. ("Carrier" and "transporter" mean the same thing here. A pump is a carrier with its own power supply.) The rule cuts across those two kinds with one question: does the protein only open a path, or does it pay for uphill transport?

### Downhill only: channels and uniporters

**Channels** form an aqueous pore that, when open, connects both sides at once. Ions flow through, often in single file, down their electrochemical gradient. A channel can switch between open and closed (gating) in response to voltage, a ligand or stretch, but while open it has no cycle to complete, so it is fast: millions of ions per second through one pore. Aquaporins are water channels. Ligand-gated channels that act as receptors belong to [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/) in the signaling course.

**Carriers** work differently. A carrier binds its solute on one side, changes shape, and releases it on the other; in the ideal cycle its binding site is never open to both sides at once, though some carriers also have a small channel-like leak. This is **alternating access**. Because each crossing needs a shape change, a carrier works far more slowly than a channel, and because it has a limited number of binding sites, its rate saturates as solute rises, like an enzyme with [Michaelis-Menten kinetics](/lessons/enzyme-kinetics/).

<LessonFigure src="/figures/membrane-transport/channel-vs-carrier-access.webp" width={1200} height={541} alt="AI-generated illustration: three panels of a plasma membrane. In the first, an open channel has a pore running straight through it with ions in single file. In the second, a carrier's binding site is open only to the outside with one solute bound. In the third, the carrier has changed shape and its site is open only to the inside." caption="An open channel is one pathway joining both sides at once, so ions stream through. A carrier's binding site is open to one side at a time: it binds its solute on one side, changes shape and releases it on the other, which is why it is slower and can saturate." />

A **uniporter** is a carrier that moves one solute, as GLUT1 moves glucose into red blood cells. Glucose has no charge, so only the concentration push counts: GLUT1 can speed glucose across, but with glucose alone, only until inside and outside are equal; it can never concentrate it.

### Uphill by coupling: pumps, symporters and antiporters

**Pumps** couple transport to a direct energy source, usually ATP hydrolysis, and so move ions uphill. This is **primary active transport**. Pumps work by alternating access too. The Na+/K+-ATPase in the plasma membrane of animal cells exports three Na+ and imports two K+ for each ATP it splits. At −70 mV both moves are uphill, but together they cost less than one ATP supplies (Going deeper, "The pump's bill"). It moves one net charge out per cycle, so it makes the inside more negative directly. In most cells that direct effect is small, a few millivolts, but in some it is large. Mostly it builds the sodium and potassium gradients that other proteins spend.

Carriers that move two or more solutes can do the same with no ATP. A **symporter** moves them the same way. An **antiporter** (or exchanger) moves them opposite ways, as the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger brings sodium in and sends calcium out. Spending one solute's gradient to move another is **secondary active transport**.

SGLT1 in intestinal cells is a symporter that brings glucose in with sodium. Sodium's steep downhill run inward pays for glucose to go uphill. You will see SGLT1 again in [digestion](/lessons/digestion/), and the same logic with protons instead of sodium in [the proton-motive force](/lessons/proton-motive-force/).

**Predict.** Block the pump with a drug. What happens to glucose uptake through SGLT1, and does it happen at once?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Uphill glucose uptake fails, but not at once. SGLT1 needs only that the coupled total, sodium's payment plus glucose's cost, is still downhill. With the pump blocked, the sodium gradient runs down as sodium keeps leaking in, the payment shrinks, and SGLT1 can no longer pull glucose uphill.

</details>

## Telling a channel from a carrier from the data

Downhill flow cannot tell a channel from a carrier, since both carry it; net uphill flow points to a coupled carrier. Beyond that, weigh rate, saturation and exchange.

### Rate

The worked problem's one open K+ channel carried about 6.1 million ions per second. Carriers typically cycle from tens to a few thousand times a second, and even the fastest known reach only tens of thousands, far below a million, so millions per second through one protein points to a pore.

### Watching one channel

In 1976 Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann recorded a small patch of frog muscle membrane switching between levels in square steps of a few picoamperes, each the current through one open channel. The 1981 gigaseal methods made such records routine.

The record looks like this: the trace sits at zero while the channel is closed, jumps to a fixed level when it opens, stays there for a random time, and jumps back. In a patch with one simple channel, every opening has the same height (some channels also show lower sub-levels); the openings come at irregular intervals.

From a one-channel record you read two numbers. The **unitary current**, i, is the height of the step, set by the channel's conductance and the driving force. The **open probability**, P_o, is the fraction of time the channel is open. A drug that changes P_o without changing i alters gating; one that changes i alters conduction or driving force. Across N identical channels, the mean current combines all three:

<EquationCard

  eq="I = N × i × P_o"
  say="The total current equals the number of channels, times the current through one open channel, times the fraction of time each is open."

    ["P_o", "P sub o", "the open probability: the fraction of time a channel is open, from 0 to 1", "no unit"],
  ]}
  toolkit={["subscripts"]}

>

    <p>Fifty channels that are always open (P_o = 1) give the same 50 pA: 50 × 1 × 1 = 50. That is why a whole-cell current alone cannot separate N, i and P_o.</p>

### Counting molecules: tracer flux

One carrier moves too few molecules per second to show up as a current step, so carriers are often studied by counting. In a **tracer-flux** experiment you add radiolabelled substrate outside cells or vesicles, stop uptake at timed points by filtering and washing, and count the label inside. The early, straight part of the time course gives the initial rate. The numbers in the two experiments below are invented for teaching.

**Does the rate level off?** Two unknown proteins, A and B, are each put into matched vesicles. You measure the initial rate of uptake at six substrate concentrations and subtract the rate into protein-free vesicles.

| Substrate outside (mM) | A: rate (pmol/min) | B: rate (pmol/min) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | 20 | 2.5 |
| 1 | 33 | 5 |
| 2 | 50 | 10 |
| 5 | 71 | 25 |
| 10 | 83 | 50 |
| 20 | 91 | 100 |

Look at the last two rows first: doubling substrate from 10 to 20 mM doubles B's rate but raises A's by about a tenth.

**Predict.** Which protein behaves like a carrier?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A. Its rate climbs steeply at first, then levels off toward a ceiling near 100 pmol/min (its Vmax), and it reaches half that ceiling at 2 mM (its Km). This is the Michaelis-Menten curve, v = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S]): once the few binding sites are nearly always occupied, more substrate cannot speed them up. B's rate stays proportional to concentration across the whole range, which is what a pore or simple diffusion does. Channels can saturate too, but usually only at tens to hundreds of millimolar, while carriers often level off at micromolar to low millimolar substrate.

</details>

**Is label going in the same as substrate building up?** Now a third protein, C. Preload one batch of C vesicles with 10 mM unlabelled substrate and leave another batch unloaded. Add 1 mM labelled substrate outside both. After a minute, measure the label inside and, by chemical assay, the *total* substrate inside.

| Vesicles | Label inside | Total substrate inside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unloaded | little | little |
| Preloaded with 10 mM | a lot | unchanged |

**Predict.** Label pours into the preloaded vesicles. Are they taking up substrate?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The total inside did not rise. C is swapping: labelled molecules come in as unlabelled ones go out, one for one overall, so the total stays put. At first, label counts only inward traffic; the outgoing molecules were unlabelled, so the counter missed them. The unloaded vesicles take up little label because C moves quickly only when it has something to carry back on the return trip. That is the mark of an **exchanger** (though preloading can also speed up some uniporters; Going deeper, "Trans-stimulation and exchange").

</details>

Did the label enter *against* a gradient, 1 mM outside and 10 mM inside? At first, no. Labelled molecules have a gradient of their own, 1 mM outside and none inside, and they ran down it. Given time, an exchanger can even pile label up inside well above 1 mM, the classic counterflow test for a carrier, because each labelled molecule that enters is paid for by an unlabelled one leaving down its own gradient.

Two rules for tracer data: **a rate that levels off at low substrate concentrations points to a carrier.** **Label entering is not net uptake until you measure the total.** A carrier's Km is not a simple binding constant either (Going deeper, "Km is not a binding constant").

### Family does not decide mechanism

CFTR, the chloride channel mutated in cystic fibrosis, belongs to the ABC family, most of whose members are ATP-driven transporters, yet it is a channel. It still binds and splits ATP, but to open and close its gate, not to push chloride uphill: chloride flows through it downhill. The reverse surprise came in the ClC family of chloride channels: its bacterial member ClC-ec1, from *E. coli*, proved in reconstituted vesicles to be a carrier that exchanges protons for chloride. Only functional tests like those above decide.

### Design problem

You inject RNA for an unknown membrane protein, Q, into Xenopus oocytes, and the oocytes become more permeable to chloride. Oocytes have chloride channels of their own. Is Q itself a chloride pore, or a protein that switches on an endogenous channel? And how would you tell whether Q is a channel or a carrier?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with controls: compare Q-injected oocytes with oocytes injected with the same volume of water, from the same frog and on the same day, so that any change can be traced to expressing Q. That alone does not make Q the pore: high expression of many foreign membrane proteins switches on the oocyte's own chloride current.

Then take Q out of the oocyte. Purify it, put it into lipid vesicles and fuse them with a planar bilayer. If chloride current appears with purified Q and no other protein, Q is enough to form the pathway. This is how Christine Bear, John Riordan and colleagues showed in 1992 that purified CFTR is itself a regulated chloride channel.

Next, look for single-channel events: square current steps of fixed height whose reversal potential (the voltage at which the current changes direction) tracks E_Cl as you change chloride on one side. Then mutate residues you suspect line the pore. If a mutation changes the unitary conductance or which anions pass, Q most likely forms the pore. A mutation that changes only P_o is consistent with Q being a regulator, or a channel whose gating you hit, so it decides less.

To separate a channel from a carrier, weigh rate, saturation, discrete unitary events, and exchange (does chloride entry need another ion leaving?). Remember ClC-ec1: a family name will not answer this for you.

</details>

## Going deeper

### 58 or 61.5 mV per tenfold

Using base-10 logs gives the same E_K as the worked problem: 61.5 mV × log10(5/140) = 61.5 × (−1.447) = −89.0 mV, the small difference being rounding. Many books quote 58 mV per tenfold change in concentration. That figure holds near room temperature. At 37 °C it is 61.5 mV.

### Resting with several ions

With two ions, in the simple conductance picture (passive currents only, no pump current), the resting potential sits nearer the equilibrium potential of the ion with the larger conductance. With three or more, that can fail: if a second ion's conductance nearly matches potassium's and a third ion pulls the same way, the voltage can end up nearer the second ion's equilibrium potential than E_K, as [the resting membrane](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/) works through.

### The pump's bill

At −70 mV, with the lesson's teaching values, pushing Na+ out costs about +13.2 kJ/mol (the two-push example, reversed) and bringing K+ in +1.84 kJ/mol. Three Na+ out and two K+ in cost about 3 × 13.2 + 2 × 1.84 ≈ +43 kJ/mol. Splitting ATP in a cell releases roughly 50 to 60 kJ/mol, so the coupled total is downhill, with a margin, as the rule requires.

### Km is not a binding constant

A carrier's Km depends on the conditions on the far side (trans conditions), on the membrane voltage and on any coupled ion. Different protocols, such as zero-trans uptake (nothing inside to start) and equilibrium exchange (the same substrate on both sides), give different values for the same protein.

### Trans-stimulation and exchange

The preloaded-vesicle test above read fast label entry as exchange. A uniporter whose empty return step is slow also speeds up when preloaded, so faster label entry into preloaded vesicles alone does not prove an obligate exchanger. Exchange and net flux can even be pulled apart within one protein: in 2023 a study of the E. coli carrier DgoT found a mutant, E133Q (glutamate 133 replaced by glutamine), that kept robust substrate exchange although Glu133 is needed for net flux.
