summary: >-
  Can an enzyme make an impossible reaction happen? No — it can only make a
  possible one fast. This chapter separates what a reaction *will* do
  (thermodynamics: ΔG, Keq) from how *fast* it does it (kinetics: the activation
  barrier), and shows that an enzyme touches only the second — by gripping the
  fleeting transition state more tightly than the substrate.

techniques:
  - name: "Measuring rate enhancement"
    blurb: >-
      Run the reaction with the enzyme and without it, take each rate, and divide.
      A catalyzed rate of 540 s⁻¹ over an uncatalyzed 2.9×10⁻⁶ s⁻¹ gives 1.9×10⁸ —
      the number that says how much work the protein is doing.
  - name: "Reading ΔG°′ from the equilibrium constant"
    blurb: >-
      Let a reaction reach equilibrium, measure [products]/[reactants], and that
      ratio is Keq. Plug it into ΔG°′ = −RT ln Keq (R = 8.314 J/mol·K, T = 298 K)
      to convert a concentration you can measure into a free energy you cannot.
  - name: "Transition-state analog binding"
    blurb: >-
      Build a stable molecule shaped like the transition state, then measure how
      tightly the enzyme holds it versus the real substrate. Proline racemase
      binds its analog 160× tighter — a classic example of evidence that the
      active site is complementary to the transition state, not the substrate.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "A newly characterized enzyme catalyzes the intramolecular conversion of glucose-6-phosphate to fructose-6-phosphate, rearranging atoms within a single molecule without adding or removing any group. To which EC class does this enzyme belong?"
    choices:
      - "Transferases"
      - "Hydrolases"
      - "Isomerases"
      - "Ligases"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Isomerases (EC class 5) catalyze intramolecular rearrangements, converting a
      molecule into one of its isomers without changing its molecular formula —
      phosphoglucose isomerase is the classic example. Transferases move a group
      between molecules; hydrolases use water to cleave bonds; ligases join two
      molecules using ATP. None of those describes a simple rearrangement.

  - stem: "A purified enzyme shows no activity in a tube of buffer and substrate, but full activity returns when Zn²⁺ is added. Using this chapter's cofactor terms, which statement is correct?"
    choices:
      - "The enzyme alone is the holoenzyme, and adding Zn²⁺ converts it to the apoenzyme"
      - "The enzyme alone is the apoenzyme, and the Zn²⁺-bound active form is the holoenzyme"
      - "Zn²⁺ is an organic coenzyme derived from a vitamin"
      - "Zn²⁺ is a prosthetic group because it dissociates freely from the protein"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The inactive protein lacking its required cofactor is the apoenzyme (apo =
      away/absent); the active protein-plus-cofactor complex is the holoenzyme
      (holo = whole). Zn²⁺ is an inorganic metal-ion cofactor, not an organic
      vitamin-derived coenzyme, which rules out the vitamin choice. A prosthetic
      group is a tightly bound cofactor, so calling Zn²⁺ a prosthetic group because
      it "dissociates freely" contradicts the definition.

  - stem: "A reaction has ΔG°′ = +13.8 kJ/mol (the chapter's value for forming glucose-6-phosphate from glucose and phosphate). Adding the appropriate enzyme to a mixture at equilibrium will:"
    choices:
      - "Make the reaction spontaneous by lowering ΔG°′ below zero"
      - "Increase the equilibrium constant so more product accumulates"
      - "Have no effect on ΔG°′ or Keq, because enzymes change only the rate"
      - "Reverse the reaction so products become reactants"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Enzymes lower the activation energy (ΔG‡) and speed the approach to
      equilibrium, but they cannot change ΔG, ΔG°′, or Keq. A positive-ΔG°′
      reaction stays thermodynamically unfavorable under standard conditions
      regardless of enzyme — the catalyst just lets the system reach its
      (unchanged) equilibrium faster. Making the reaction spontaneous, raising Keq,
      and reversing the reaction all wrongly move the position of equilibrium.

  - stem: "Proline racemase binds the analog pyrrole-2-carboxylate (Kd = 0.022 mM) about 160-fold more tightly than its substrate L-proline (Kd = 3.6 mM). What does this most directly support?"
    choices:
      - "The lock-and-key model, because the enzyme binds substrate with maximum affinity"
      - "Transition-state complementarity, because the active site is most complementary to the transition state, not the substrate"
      - "That the enzyme increases the equilibrium constant of the reaction"
      - "That the analog is the true physiological substrate of the enzyme"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The active site achieves maximal complementarity to the transition state, not
      the ground-state substrate — and tighter binding of a transition-state analog
      than of the substrate is the experimental hallmark of that idea. Lock-and-key
      predicts the opposite, tightest binding to the substrate. Binding affects
      rate, not Keq, so the equilibrium-constant choice fails. And a
      transition-state analog is a stable mimic, not the molecule the enzyme turns
      over, so it cannot be the physiological substrate.

  - stem: "A student claims that because all known catalysts in cells are proteins, RNA cannot perform catalysis. Which counterexample from this chapter best refutes the claim?"
    choices:
      - "DNA ligase, which seals nicks in the DNA backbone"
      - "Carbonic anhydrase, which interconverts CO₂ and bicarbonate"
      - "The ribosome, whose peptidyl transferase activity is carried out by RNA"
      - "Trypsin, which cleaves peptide bonds after Lys and Arg residues"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Ribozymes are catalytic RNA molecules, and the ribosome's peptide-bond-forming
      center is itself RNA-catalyzed — the ribosome is a ribozyme, which directly
      refutes the "all enzymes are proteins" trap. DNA ligase, carbonic anhydrase,
      and trypsin are all protein enzymes, so none serves as a counterexample.

mcatQuestions:
  - stem: "An enzyme catalyzes its reaction at 540 s⁻¹, whereas the uncatalyzed rate is 2.9 × 10⁻⁶ s⁻¹. Approximately what rate enhancement does the enzyme provide, and what does this tell you about the reaction's equilibrium constant?"
    choices:
      - "~1.9 × 10⁸; the enhancement also increases Keq by the same factor"
      - "~1.9 × 10⁸; Keq is unchanged because catalysis affects only rate"
      - "~5.4 × 10⁻⁹; Keq is decreased proportionally"
      - "~2.9 × 10²; Keq is unchanged because catalysis affects only rate"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Rate enhancement = catalyzed/uncatalyzed = 540 / (2.9×10⁻⁶) ≈ 1.9×10⁸. A
      catalyst speeds the forward and reverse reactions by the same factor (counted
      from free substrate and free product), so it accelerates
      the approach to equilibrium without altering the equilibrium constant — Keq is
      unchanged. The choice that raises Keq by the same factor wrongly couples Keq
      to rate; the 5.4 × 10⁻⁹ and 2.9 × 10² choices miscompute the ratio.

  - stem: "Using ΔG°′ = −RT ln Keq with R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) and T = 298 K, a reaction with ΔG°′ = −17 kJ/mol has an equilibrium constant closest to which value, and which direction does it favor?"
    choices:
      - "Keq ≈ 10⁻³; favors reactants"
      - "Keq ≈ 1; equal reactants and products"
      - "Keq ≈ 10³; favors products"
      - "Keq ≈ 10¹⁷; favors products"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Keq = e^(−ΔG°′/RT) = e^(17000 / (8.314 × 298)) = e^(6.86) ≈ 950, on the order of
      10³. A negative ΔG°′ gives Keq > 1, so products are favored. Keq ≈ 10⁻³ would
      need a positive ΔG°′; Keq ≈ 1 needs ΔG°′ near zero; Keq ≈ 10¹⁷ needs a far more
      negative ΔG°′.

  - stem: An enzyme speeds a reaction a millionfold. A student measures the equilibrium constant of the reaction with and without the enzyme. What should the student find, and why?
    choices:
    - Keq is a millionfold larger with the enzyme, because the products are stabilized
    - Keq is unchanged, because the enzyme lowers the activation barrier but not ΔG°′
    - Keq is a millionfold smaller with the enzyme, because the reverse reaction is also sped up
    - Keq cannot be measured with an enzyme present, because it is consumed
    answer: 1
    rationale: An enzyme lowers the activation barrier by stabilizing the transition state. It does not change the free energy of the substrate or product, so ΔG, ΔG°′ and Keq are the same. It speeds the forward and reverse reactions by the same factor (counted from free substrate and free product) and so reaches the same equilibrium faster. The other choices wrongly let the enzyme change the destination, or invent an effect on measurement.

  - stem: "At 25 °C, an enzyme speeds a reaction a millionfold (10⁶). By about how much has it lowered the activation barrier?"
    choices:
      - "About 6 kJ/mol"
      - "About 15 kJ/mol"
      - "About 34 kJ/mol"
      - "About 57 kJ/mol"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      At 298 K every factor of ten in rate is RT ln 10 = 2.48 × 2.30 = 5.7 kJ/mol
      of barrier. A millionfold is six factors of ten, so 6 × 5.7 ≈ 34 kJ/mol.
      About 6 kJ/mol is a single factor of ten; about 15 kJ/mol uses RT (2.48)
      per factor of ten and forgets the ln 10; about 57 kJ/mol counts ten factors
      of ten instead of six.

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm 5"
    text: |
      Pretend your body needs to push a heavy ball over a hill. Some balls roll
      down the other side all by themselves once they're over the top — those
      reactions like to happen. But the hill is in the way, so it's slow. An enzyme
      is a helper that opens a lower path over the hill. The ball still ends up
      in the same low spot it was always going to roll to — the enzyme can't move
      that spot — but now it gets there really, really fast. An enzyme can't make a
      ball roll uphill on its own. It can only make a downhill trip quicker.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: |
      Two different questions hide inside "does this reaction work?" The first is
      whether it will happen at all. That depends on Gibbs free energy: if the
      products have lower free energy than the reactants (negative ΔG), the reaction
      is spontaneous and called exergonic. If they have higher free energy (positive
      ΔG), it needs an energy push and is endergonic. The second question is how
      fast it happens. Even a spontaneous reaction can be painfully slow, because
      the molecules first have to pass through a high-energy, awkward in-between form
      called the transition state. The energy needed to reach it is the activation
      energy.

      An enzyme is a catalyst, usually a protein, that lowers that activation energy. It does it by
      binding the transition state more tightly than it binds the starting molecule,
      which makes the awkward in-between easier to reach. The key idea: the enzyme
      speeds things up, but it never changes whether the reaction was going to favor
      products in the first place. It changes the speed, not the destination.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: |
      Thermodynamics and kinetics answer different questions, and an enzyme touches
      only one of them.

      Thermodynamics asks whether a reaction is favorable. The sign of ΔG decides:
      ΔG < 0 is exergonic (spontaneous), ΔG > 0 is endergonic (needs energy input),
      ΔG = 0 is equilibrium. The standard free-energy change connects directly to
      the equilibrium constant:

      ΔG°′ = −RT ln Keq

      where R = 8.314 J/mol·K and T = 298 K. A ΔG°′ of −17 kJ/mol corresponds to
      Keq ≈ 950 — products strongly favored. The relationship is logarithmic, so
      small free-energy changes shift equilibrium dramatically. Crucially, an enzyme
      changes none of these: not ΔG, not ΔG°′, not Keq.

      Kinetics asks how fast. Each step of a reaction proceeds through a transition state (X‡),
      the highest-energy point of that step; the gap between substrate
      and that peak is the activation energy ΔG‡, which governs rate. Enzymes lower
      ΔG‡. They do it by binding the transition state more tightly than the
      substrate — the active site is complementary to X‡, not to the ground-state
      substrate. The binding energy released pays down the barrier, and a
      thermodynamic cycle gives kcat/kuncat = Kd(S)/Kd(X‡), assuming the same kind of
      transition state and frequency factor: the speed-up from the bound substrate
      equals how many times more tightly the enzyme holds X‡ than the substrate. Fischer's rigid lock-and-key (1894) explained specificity;
      Koshland's induced fit (1958) added that substrate binding changes the enzyme's
      conformation. Conformational selection, in which the enzyme already samples the
      active shape, is the other limit, and many enzymes use both. The proof is
      quantitative — proline racemase binds a transition-state analog 160× tighter
      than its substrate.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: |
      The conceptual core is that ΔG‡ and ΔG are independent quantities, and
      catalysis acts strictly on the former. ΔG (and ΔG°′, Keq via ΔG°′ = −RT ln
      Keq) are state functions — path-independent thermodynamic quantities fixed by
      the free energies of substrate and product. ΔG‡ = G(X‡) − G(S) is a kinetic
      quantity describing the barrier height, and by transition-state theory the
      rate scales with exp(−ΔG‡/RT). A catalyst stabilizes X‡ relative to S,
      lowering ΔG‡, and by microscopic reversibility it lowers the barrier equally
      in both directions, measured from free substrate and free product — which is precisely why Keq is untouched: equilibrium is
      reached faster, not shifted.

      The physical basis is differential binding. Pauling's insight, formalized by
      Jencks, is that the active site is complementary to the transition state
      rather than the ground-state substrate; the intrinsic binding energy not
      consumed in substrate recognition is channeled into transition-state
      stabilization. Induced fit (Koshland, 1958) describes the conformational
      coupling — substrate binding triggers a protein rearrangement that completes
      the catalytically competent geometry. Conformational selection from a pre-existing ensemble is
      the other limiting case, and many enzymes use a mix of both pathways. In this accounting, ΔG‡ falls by the difference between
      transition-state and substrate binding energies; general acid-base, covalent and
      metal-ion catalysis act alongside it. Formally any rate enhancement can be written as a transition-state affinity, but covalent catalysis changes the transition state being compared. Transition-state analogs are the experimental and
      pharmacological corollary: a stable mimic of X‡ geometry captures part of that
      binding energy and can bind orders of magnitude tighter than substrate — proline
      racemase's pyrrole-2-carboxylate at 160×, the basis of rational transition-state analog design.

  - title: "Lock-and-key is incomplete — and its inventor called it a hypothesis"
    source: "CH06 (Chapter 7) deck, slide 52; The Molecule Hunters — Fischer profile."
    text: >-
      The model in every textbook is the one this chapter takes apart. If
      lock-and-key were right, an enzyme would grip its substrate with maximum
      affinity. It doesn't — it grips the transition state hardest, which is the
      whole reason it speeds the reaction. Emil Fischer floated "lock and key" in
      1894 and, in an 1898 review, warned that it was only a hypothesis. Two
      corrections followed: Haldane and Pauling argued that the site fits the
      transition state best, and Koshland's 1958 induced-fit model added that the
      site changes shape. Locks don't change shape. Enzymes do.
  - title: "A fake transition state that binds 160× tighter"
    source: "CH06 (Chapter 7) deck, slide 55."
    text: >-

      hard it holds on. Proline racemase binds its real substrate, L-proline, with a
      Kd of 3.6 mM. It binds pyrrole-2-carboxylate — a flat mimic of the planar
      transition state — with a Kd of 0.022 mM. That's 160-fold tighter binding to a
      molecule the enzyme can't even turn over.

**An enzyme cannot make an impossible reaction happen. It can only make a possible one fast, by lowering the energy barrier the reaction has to cross.**

OMP decarboxylase, the enzyme that finishes making UMP (a building block of RNA), speeds its reaction by a factor of about 10¹⁷. Without it, that single step has a half-life of 78 million years, longer than the time since the dinosaurs vanished. With it, the reaction finishes in milliseconds. Enzymes are not gentle nudges. They are the difference between a reaction that effectively never happens and one fast enough to keep you alive.

So why can't a protein that strong push a reaction uphill? Because "does this reaction work?" hides two questions: where does the reaction end up, and how fast does it get there? Picture two valleys, the free substrate and the free product, with a ridge between them.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** An enzyme, like any catalyst, lowers only the ridge, never the two valleys. The valleys decide where the reaction goes: the drop between them at the actual concentrations (ΔG) sets which way it runs, and the drop at standard conditions (ΔG°′) sets where it stops (Keq). The ridge's height (ΔG‡) sets how fast: at 25 °C, every 5.7 kJ/mol taken off makes the reaction ten times faster. An enzyme takes height off by gripping the transition state, the structure at the top, more tightly than the substrate; the valleys, and so the equilibrium, stay put.

## Where a reaction ends up: ΔG, ΔG°′ and Q

Which valley is lower is thermodynamics; how hard the ridge is to cross is kinetics.

The currency of thermodynamics is **Gibbs free energy**, G, the energy in a system available to do work. The sign of the change, ΔG, gives the direction. A negative ΔG means the reaction releases free energy and can go forward on its own: it is **exergonic**, or spontaneous. A positive ΔG means it needs an input of energy: it is **endergonic**. Spontaneous means downhill, not quick, as uncatalyzed OMP decarboxylation shows. And exoTHERMIC is not exoGONIC: exothermic is about heat (ΔH), and a reaction can release free energy without releasing much heat.

ΔG also depends on how much product and reactant are present right now; ΔG°′ is the standard reference value, and ΔG the value for the mixture actually in the tube or the cell.

So ATP hydrolysis is the textbook exergonic reaction and direct G6P formation the textbook endergonic one, in standard conditions and in the cell.

As a reaction runs, Q moves toward **Keq** and ΔG creeps toward zero; at equilibrium, going nowhere net, ΔG = 0 and Q = Keq. Put those into the card:

> **ΔG°′ = −RT ln Keq**

The more negative ΔG°′, the larger Keq: ΔG°′ < 0 means Keq > 1, and ΔG°′ > 0 means Keq < 1. Work an example: ΔG°′ = −17 kJ/mol, so Keq = e^(−ΔG°′/RT). Convert kJ to J first: 17,000 / (8.314 × 298) = 6.86, and e^6.86 ≈ 950, roughly a thousand-to-one in favor of products. Quicker: every 5.7 kJ/mol is a factor of ten (the next section shows why), so 17 kJ/mol is about three. Because the relationship is logarithmic, a modest change in free energy moves the equilibrium a lot.

## How fast it gets there: the height of the hill

Now the ridge. Without the enzyme, molecules must cross a high pass. The enzyme opens a lower pass over the same ridge: still a highest point, just a lower one. (The film's "long way around" means that higher pass, not a longer road.) Molecules cross far more often.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/enzyme-action-basics/lower-pass-same-valleys.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two valleys separated by a mountain ridge. A grey dashed trail crosses the ridge at a high notch; a gold trail crosses the same ridge at a lower notch, with many more travellers on it. An inset shows that the floors of both valleys stay at the same height with or without the enzyme." caption="An enzyme opens a lower pass over the same ridge. The valleys, free substrate and free product, stay exactly where they were, so the equilibrium is unchanged; only the height of the crossing, and so the speed, changes." />

Each chemical step passes through a **transition state**, written X‡: atoms with bonds half-broken and half-formed, neither substrate nor product. It lives for femtoseconds at the very top of the pass: a peak, not a molecule you can isolate (a stable stop between two peaks is an intermediate). The height of that top above the substrate is the **activation energy**, ΔG‡, and it sets the rate.

## How an enzyme lowers the top: grip the transition state

How does a protein lower the top? The active site, a small cleft built from amino acids brought together from distant parts of the chain, fits the distorted transition state better than the starting molecule. Binding releases energy (**binding energy**), and the extra that goes to the transition state, beyond what goes to the substrate, comes off the climb from the bound substrate. A thermodynamic cycle (Going deeper) turns that into a rule:

> **speed-up from the bound substrate = how many times more tightly the enzyme holds the transition state than the substrate**

Written with dissociation constants, Kd (the lower the Kd, the tighter the grip): kcat / kuncat = Kd(substrate) / Kd(transition state), where kcat is the rate of the step from the bound substrate and kuncat the rate with no enzyme. The 1.9 × 10⁸ enzyme above holds its transition state about 190 million times more tightly than its substrate, worth the same 47 kJ/mol.

An inset shows a stable look-alike of the transition state fitting just as snugly without reacting." caption="The active site is built for the transition state, not the substrate. The extra grip on the transition state is what lowers the top of the barrier, and a stable look-alike that copies its shape binds more tightly than the real substrate." />

**Predict.** A team designs an enzyme whose active site holds the substrate exactly as tightly as it holds the transition state. How much faster does the reaction go once the substrate is bound?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not at all. The bound substrate and the top drop by the same amount, so the climb from the bound substrate is as high as before. Worse, tightening the substrate's grip alone shrinks that speed-up: take its Kd from 1 mM to 1 µM, a thousandfold tighter, with the transition state's grip fixed, and the ratio falls a thousandfold, so a 10⁸ speed-up becomes 10⁵. The loss bites whenever the enzyme is saturated with substrate. Enzymes hold substrates moderately and transition states extremely tightly.

</details>

Binding is not the only tool. Enzymes also hold reacting groups close together and lined up, pass protons to and from the substrate (acid-base catalysis), form brief covalent bonds with it (covalent catalysis), and use metal ions. Each makes the transition state easier to reach. On paper any speed-up can still be written as extra grip on the transition state, but covalent catalysis in particular changes the transition state itself, so the simple rule then compares two different transition states.

## The evidence: transition-state analogs bind tighter

If the active site is built for the transition state, it should grip a stable look-alike of it harder than the real substrate. Proline racemase, which interconverts the two mirror forms of proline, passes through a planar transition state, its central carbon going flat as it flips, and binds its substrate, L-proline, with a Kd of 3.6 mM. Chemists made pyrrole-2-carboxylate, a stable flat look-alike the enzyme cannot turn over.

**Predict.** Which will the enzyme hold harder, its own substrate or the flat look-alike?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The look-alike: Kd 0.022 mM, 160 times tighter than the substrate it actually works on. The enzyme grips the shape it is built to stabilize.

</details>

Count it: 160-fold is about 2.2 factors of ten, or 12.6 kJ/mol, small next to the 47 to 97 kJ/mol that enzymes like those above take off their barriers. An analog is an imperfect mimic of so fleeting a structure, so it captures only part of the grip; the tightest designed analogs, made for other enzymes, bind far more tightly than this one. Read 160× as a down payment, not the full account.

## A separate question: does the active site change shape?

What the active site fits best and whether it changes shape are different questions. Emil Fischer's 1894 lock-and-key picture explained why an enzyme acts on one sugar and ignores its near-twin. But a rigid lock built for the substrate would bind the substrate most tightly, and enzymes don't: the site fits the transition state best, an idea from Haldane (1930) and Pauling (1940s), not from the shape models.

Flexibility is the second question. In 1958 Daniel Koshland proposed **induced fit**: substrate binding changes the enzyme's shape and brings its catalytic groups into line. His central argument was hexokinase and water (scientist panel). Later work showed that many enzymes also shift between shapes before the substrate arrives, and the substrate picks the one that fits (**conformational selection**). Real enzymes often use a mix of both.

## Tight binding as a drug strategy

A molecule that binds the active site more tightly than the substrate does, and that the enzyme cannot turn over, can shut the enzyme down. Drugs shaped like a transition state, as in the film's last panel, exploit the same logic: they bind the active site far more tightly than the substrate does. Statins get there another way. They block HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme of cholesterol synthesis. They are **competitive inhibitors** (they sit in the active site and keep the substrate out), not true transition-state analogs: part of each statin mimics the HMG part of the substrate and fills its pocket, and extra contacts nearby let the drug bind with nanomolar affinity, far more tightly than the substrate. Slowing the pathway makes the liver pull more LDL cholesterol out of the blood, lowering blood cholesterol as intended. Some patients report muscle symptoms; the cause is unproven (Going deeper). Inhibitors return in [enzyme kinetics](/lessons/enzyme-kinetics/).

That is the lesson's spine: the ends of the hill decide where a reaction goes, and an enzyme lowers only the top, by gripping the transition state harder than the substrate.

## Naming enzymes and their helpers

An enzyme is a reusable catalyst, usually a protein, with one important exception: some enzymes are RNA (**ribozymes**), and the ribosome that builds every protein in your body is itself one. An enzyme's activity depends on temperature, pH, and often on a helper molecule, and its name usually ends in *-ase*.

There are seven official classes, sorted by the chemistry they do. **Oxidoreductases** move electrons. **Transferases** move a chemical group from one molecule to another. **Hydrolases** use water to break a bond. **Lyases** add or remove groups across a double bond (simplified; Going deeper has the official definition). **Isomerases** rearrange a molecule into its isomer. **Ligases** join two molecules and usually spend ATP to do it. **Translocases**, added in 2018, move ions and molecules across membranes. Each enzyme gets a four-part EC number whose first digit is its class: hexokinase, a transferase, is EC 2.7.1.1.

**Predict.** Fumarase adds a water molecule across the double bond of fumarate. It uses water. Is it a hydrolase?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No, a lyase. A hydrolase uses water to cut one molecule into two; fumarase adds water across a double bond and leaves one molecule. Ask what happens to the bonds, not whether water appears.

</details>

Many enzymes cannot work alone. A **cofactor** is a small helper required for activity, of two kinds. A **coenzyme** is an organic molecule, usually derived from a vitamin (niacin becomes NAD⁺; thiamine becomes TPP). A **metal ion** is the inorganic kind (Zn²⁺, Mg²⁺). When a cofactor, organic or metal, binds tightly and permanently rather than coming and going, we call it a **prosthetic group**; the heme buried in catalase, an organic ring holding an iron ion, is the classic example. The pair of terms that trips students up most: an enzyme missing its cofactor is an **apoenzyme** and it does not work; the assembled, working complex is the **holoenzyme**. Apo means away; holo means whole.

## Going deeper

### The bookkeeping behind the rule

The speed-up rule comes from a thermodynamic cycle: going from free substrate to bound transition state costs the same free energy whichever way round you go. It compares kcat with kuncat and assumes the same kind of transition state and the same frequency factor in both. Written for kcat/KM (free enzyme plus free substrate) instead, the same cycle measures how tightly the enzyme holds the transition state outright. That free-end speed-up is the one that is equal forward and reverse: (kcat/KM) forward over (kcat/KM) reverse equals Keq (the Haldane relation), which is why no enzyme can move the equilibrium.

It applies to the highest barrier of a multistep reaction, which has several transition states and intermediates (the 7TIM structure holds an intermediate analog). Once an enzyme is limited by how fast substrate diffuses in, as triosephosphate isomerase nearly is, extra grip on the chemical transition state no longer helps. And it does not cover coupling, which changes the reaction itself (the hexokinase answer above).

### Fischer, Haldane, Pauling and shape

Fischer called lock-and-key a hypothesis that could only be tested once someone could hold a pure enzyme and see its shape; when crystallographers finally did, many active sites turned out to flex. Shape changes also continue after the chemistry: in an induced-fit enzyme, the site reopens to help the product leave.

### The full class definitions and EC numbers

Hexokinase's EC 2.7.1.1 reads as transferase, phosphate group, alcohol acceptor, first on the list. Strictly, EC 2.7 is "transferring phosphorus-containing groups", so "phosphate group" is a fair gloss. The official lyase definition is broader than "add or remove groups across a double bond": lyases break bonds by means other than hydrolysis or oxidation, often forming a double bond or a ring. The short version is enough for the MCAT.

### Statins and muscle symptoms

The classic worry: a patient starts a cholesterol drug and within weeks their muscles ache and a blood marker called creatine kinase (CK) climbs. The pathway statins slow also makes CoQ10 for mitochondrial energy and the isoprenoids muscle cells need. One proposed explanation for statin muscle symptoms is that blocking the pathway lowers CoQ10 and isoprenoids in muscle and harms its mitochondria. It is not proven. Other mechanisms are being studied, CoQ10 supplements have not reliably helped, and in blinded trials most muscle aches reported on statins also occur on placebo. True statin muscle damage with a large CK rise is rare.
