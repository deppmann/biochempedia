    story: >-
      Knudson treated retinoblastoma as a counting problem. The tumor is rare, it strikes young
      children, and it comes in two forms: some children have a family history and often tumors in
      both eyes, while others have no family history and usually a single tumor in one eye. In a
      1971 paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences he analysed 48 cases
      together with published series and asked how many rate-limiting events could produce those
      patterns. His answer was two. In the hereditary form the first event is present in the
      germline, so every retinal cell starts one step along; in the nonhereditary form both events
      must happen in the same somatic cell. He estimated that carriers of the first event develop
      about three tumors on average. The paper named no gene and no molecule. It set out a
      prediction about the number of events, and it gave
      later workers a reason to look for genes whose loss, rather than whose activation, lets a
      tumor grow.

techniques:
  - name: "Restriction fragment length polymorphism comparison"
    blurb: >-
      A probe detects DNA fragments whose sizes differ between the two homologs of a chromosome.
      Comparing a patient's normal DNA with tumor DNA shows whether the tumor has lost one of the
      two alleles. Cavenee and colleagues used it to detect loss of heterozygosity on chromosome 13
      in retinoblastoma.
  - name: "Gene transfer and functional rescue"
    blurb: >-
      A cloned normal gene is introduced into tumor cells that lack it, and their behavior is
      compared with controls, for example by colony growth in soft agar and tumor formation in
      nude mice. Suppression after restoration is functional evidence that the gene is a tumor
      suppressor, as Huang and colleagues showed for RB in 1988.
  - name: "Tumor and matched-normal sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      DNA from the tumor and from normal tissue of the same person is sequenced and compared.
      Variants in both are inherited; variants only in the tumor are somatic. The fraction of
      reads carrying each variant, read with the tumor purity, shows whether a mutation is in all
      tumor cells or only a subclone.
  - name: "Karyotyping and fluorescence in situ hybridization"
    blurb: >-
      Banded metaphase chromosomes show gains, losses and translocations large enough to see under
      the microscope, such as the 9;22 translocation in chronic myeloid leukemia. Fluorescent
      probes then test a chosen locus, for example whether two genes have been brought together.

**Every cell in a child who inherits one broken copy of the gene *RB1* carries that broken copy. Yet most such children develop only a few tumors, mostly in the retina of the eye, and often in both eyes.**

If the inherited allele were enough to make a cell cancerous, the whole retina would be tumor. The way out is that the inherited mutation is the first of two steps, and the second is a rare event in only a few cells. That idea, worked out from clinic records in 1971, organizes much of what is known about how mutations make cancer.

Cancer is a genetic disease because tumor cells carry altered DNA that they pass to their daughter cells. That does not make it inherited. Most changes in a tumor are **somatic**: they arise in body cells during life and are not passed to children.

## Two kinds of cancer gene: accelerators and brakes

Think of a car that will not stop: either the accelerator is stuck or the brakes have failed.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/cancer-genetics/accelerator-and-brake-genes.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: three panels, each with a cell holding two gene copies. With one altered oncogene copy the cell divides; with one working copy of a tumor suppressor the cell is held back; with both tumor-suppressor copies lost the cell divides. Simple pedal shapes mark a stuck accelerator and a working or cracked brake." caption="An oncogene is an accelerator stuck on, so one altered copy is usually enough. A tumor suppressor is a brake, and one working copy usually still holds, so a cell must normally lose both copies before it divides unchecked." />

A **proto-oncogene** is a normal gene whose product drives growth or division. An **oncogene** is an altered, overactive form: a gain of function. Because the altered protein acts on its own, one mutant allele (one copy of the gene) is usually enough, so the mutation is dominant within the cell. RAS is a switch that is on while it holds GTP and off once it cuts GTP to GDP; the [oncogenes lesson](/lessons/oncogenes/) and [the RAS-to-MAPK lesson](/lessons/ras-to-mapk/) cover the signaling. In 1982 several laboratories, among them those of Mariano Barbacid, Robert Weinberg and Michael Wigler, reported in *Nature* that the transforming gene from a human bladder carcinoma line differed from normal HRAS by one base change, which put valine in place of glycine at codon 12. A purified Val12 protein hydrolyzed GTP several times more slowly than normal HRAS protein made in *E. coli* in a 1984 assay.

MYC is usually activated another way: by extra copies (amplification) or by a translocation that moves it next to a highly active gene. In Burkitt lymphoma the commonest change is t(8;14), which places MYC beside an immunoglobulin heavy-chain gene. Riccardo Dalla-Favera and colleagues mapped c-myc to chromosome 8 in 1982 (see [chromosome changes](/lessons/chromosome-changes/)).

A **tumor-suppressor gene** is a brake: its product restrains division or stops a damaged cell, and cancer follows its loss of function. One working copy usually gives enough brake, so a cell must normally lose both alleles. RB1 encodes RB, which in its underphosphorylated form binds E2F transcription factors that switch on genes for DNA replication. TP53 encodes p53, which accumulates after DNA damage and can halt the cell cycle or trigger cell death (see [stress signals](/lessons/stress-signals/) and [death receptors and BCL-2](/lessons/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases/)). Returning a cloned RB gene to RB-deficient human tumor cells suppressed their tumor-like growth in a 1988 test.

Now the paradox. An inherited tumor-suppressor allele shows **autosomal dominant** inheritance in a family: about half a carrier's children inherit the risk, and affected people appear generation after generation (see [reading pedigrees](/lessons/pedigrees/)). Yet the allele is recessive in the cell. Both hold because a pedigree tracks risk, and a carrier has a vast number of cells that each already have one hit, so a second hit in at least one is likely. **Penetrance**, the fraction of carriers who show the trait, is therefore high but below 100%.

p53 warns against tidy rules. It acts as a tetramer, and many TP53 missense mutant proteins can disable wild-type subunits they join, a dominant-negative effect. In mice with one p53-null allele, many tumors kept a functional wild-type allele, so reduced dose may suffice in that model.

## Retinoblastoma and the two-hit arithmetic

Retinoblastoma is a tumor of the developing retina. Cases with a family history tend to be bilateral (both eyes) and diagnosed earlier; cases without one usually involve a single tumor in one eye. Alfred Knudson analysed 48 cases with published series and proposed in 1971 that two mutational events are needed. In the hereditary form the first is inherited through the germline and present in every cell; in the nonhereditary form both must happen somatically, in the same cell. He estimated that carriers develop about three tumors on average. Knudson did not find the gene: a cDNA from the *RB1* locus, on chromosome 13, was isolated by Stephen Friend and colleagues in 1986.

**Worked problem.** Assume that in a carrier of one mutant *RB1* allele each tumor arises from an independent second hit, so the number of tumors per eye follows a Poisson distribution with mean m = 1.5 (3 per person, Knudson's approximate figure), and the eyes are independent. What fractions of carriers have no tumor, a tumor in one eye only, and tumors in both eyes?

Step 1. The Poisson probability of zero events is e^(−m).

> **P(no tumor in one eye) = e^(−1.5) = 0.223**

Step 2. So P(at least one tumor in an eye) = 1 − 0.223 = 0.777.

Step 3. No tumor in either eye: 0.223 × 0.223 = 0.050, which equals e^(−3). About 5% of carriers stay tumor free, so penetrance is about 95%.

Step 4. Tumors in both eyes: 0.777 × 0.777 = 0.604.

Step 5. A tumor in one eye only, where either eye can be the affected one: 2 × 0.223 × 0.777 = 0.347.

Check: 0.050 + 0.604 + 0.347 = 1.001, which is 1 within rounding. Most affected carriers have bilateral disease, and about one carrier in twenty has no tumor yet can pass the allele on.

Now a sporadic case. Both rare hits must fall in one cell, so the expected number of tumors per eye is far below 1. If it were 0.0001, a tumor in each eye would have a chance of about 1 in 100 million. Sporadic tumors are therefore single and one-sided, and because two events must accumulate in one cell lineage, they tend to be diagnosed later.

## How the second hit happens: loss of heterozygosity

A carrier is **heterozygous** at *RB1*: one mutant allele, one wild-type. The second hit can be a new point mutation in the wild-type allele, or silencing of its promoter by DNA methylation (see [eukaryotic regulation](/lessons/eukaryotic-regulation/)). Often it is something larger. **Loss of heterozygosity** (LOH) means that a tumor cell has lost the wild-type allele and kept only the mutant one. It can happen by deletion, by loss of the whole chromosome through nondisjunction, by loss then duplication of the remaining chromosome, or by **mitotic recombination**, a crossover between homologs in a dividing body cell that can leave a daughter cell with two copies of the mutant region.

In 1983 Webster Cavenee, Thaddeus Dryja and colleagues tested this with DNA markers that differ between the two copies of chromosome 13. In several patients whose normal tissue was heterozygous at those markers, the tumor showed only one allele. The patterns fitted mitotic recombination, or nondisjunction with loss of the wild-type homolog. The second hit was often chromosomal, and LOH in tumor DNA became a way to find suppressor genes in other cancers.

## Instability and clonal evolution

A tumor is usually a **clone**, the descendants of one cell. In 1976 Peter Nowell proposed that tumors evolve: acquired genetic variability lets more aggressive sublines be selected, and treatment can fail when a resistant subline emerges. As in bacteria under an antibiotic, mutation supplies variants and selection keeps those that divide more or die less.

**Genome instability** raises the supply. Mutations in DNA-repair genes tend to cause further mutations in other genes. Cells lacking mismatch repair, as in Lynch syndrome tumors, accumulate copying errors in short repeats. Loss of BRCA1 or BRCA2 impairs repair of double-strand breaks by homologous recombination (see [DNA repair and recombination](/lessons/dna-repair-and-recombination/) and [mutation](/lessons/mutation/)). Faulty chromosome segregation produces whole-chromosome gains and losses, and chromothripsis can produce many clustered rearrangements in one catastrophic event.

Colorectal tumors show the accumulation. In a 1988 series of 172 specimens, Bert Vogelstein and colleagues found RAS mutations in 9% of adenomas smaller than 1 cm, 58% of larger adenomas and 47% of carcinomas, and the changes accumulated as tumors progressed. Eric Fearon and Vogelstein's 1990 model stressed the accumulation of changes over their exact order; it is a colorectal model, not a fixed sequence for every tumor.

A **driver** gives its cell a selective advantage; a **passenger** happened to be present when a clone expanded. Across 2,658 whole cancer genomes from 38 tumor types, each with matched normal tissue, the Pan-Cancer Analysis of Whole Genomes consortium identified an average of 4.6 drivers per tumor with current knowledge, a figure that depends on the rules used to call drivers. Selection also works before cancer, in clones of normal human esophagus.

## Reading a tumor genome

To call a variant somatic, you sequence the tumor and a **matched normal** sample, often blood, from the same person (the [sequencing lesson](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/) covers the method). A variant in both is germline, usually inherited (rarely new in the egg or sperm); one only in the tumor is somatic. The **variant allele fraction** (VAF) is the fraction of reads that carry the variant. A tumor sample also contains normal cells, so its **purity**, the fraction of tumor cells, must enter the reading.

> **expected VAF (heterozygous, all tumor cells, two copies) = purity × 0.5**

Genotype now shapes diagnosis: in chronic myeloid leukemia the 9;22 translocation makes the BCR-ABL kinase that imatinib inhibits (see [drugs against a part](/lessons/drugs-against-a-part/)). In a 2021 study of acute myeloid leukemia and myelodysplastic syndromes, whole-genome sequencing in 117 consecutive patients took a median of five days and changed the genetic risk category for 19 of them.

### Design problem

A colorectal tumor sample is estimated at 60% tumor cells. Tumor and matched-normal sequencing gives: KRAS G12D at VAF 0.30 in the tumor; TP53 R175H at VAF 0.60, with the other TP53 allele lost; PIK3CA at VAF 0.08; and a BRCA2 frameshift at VAF 0.50 in normal tissue and 0.80 in the tumor. Assume two copies of each region unless the data say otherwise. Which variants are inherited, which are in every tumor cell, and which are in a subclone? Why is the matched normal needed?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**KRAS.** A heterozygous variant in every tumor cell predicts 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.30, as seen: KRAS G12D is heterozygous and clonal.

**TP53.** If every tumor cell had only mutant copies, the VAF would be 0.6 × 1.0 = 0.60. That fits: the mutation is clonal and the wild-type allele is gone, a second hit by LOH. The value also says what kind of LOH. If the wild-type allele had been deleted, leaving one copy in each tumor cell while normal cells keep two, the VAF would be mutant copies over all copies: (0.6 × 1) / (0.6 × 1 + 0.4 × 2) = 0.43. A VAF of 0.60 fits copy-neutral LOH, such as mitotic recombination.

**PIK3CA.** If heterozygous, its VAF divided by the clonal expectation is 0.08 / 0.30 = 0.27, so about 27% of tumor cells carry it: a subclone that arose later.

**BRCA2.** A VAF of 0.50 in normal tissue means a heterozygous inherited allele. In the tumor, 0.6 × 1.0 + 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.80 fits tumor cells that have lost the wild-type allele while normal cells keep it: a second hit in a gene inherited in one-hit form.

**The matched normal.** Tumor data alone cannot separate an inherited BRCA2 allele from a somatic one, or set aside the many harmless inherited variants every person carries. These are inferences about one sample, not clinical advice.

</details>
