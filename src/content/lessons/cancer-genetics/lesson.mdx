scientists:
  - name: "Alfred Knudson"
    contribution: >-
      Proposed in 1971, from the ages and laterality of retinoblastoma cases, that the tumor needs
      two mutational events, the first inherited through the germline in hereditary cases and both
      acquired somatically in nonhereditary ones.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Knudson, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 68:820 (1971); AACR In Memoriam: Alfred G. Knudson Jr. (2016)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Knudson treated retinoblastoma as a counting problem. The tumor is rare, it strikes young
      children, and it comes in two forms: some children have tumors in both eyes and are diagnosed early,
      sometimes with a family history, while most have a single tumor in one eye. In a
      1971 paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences he analysed 48 cases
      together with published series and asked how many rate-limiting events could produce those
      patterns. His answer was two. In the hereditary form the first event is present in the
      germline, so every retinal cell starts one step along; in the nonhereditary form both events
      must happen in the same somatic cell. He estimated that carriers of the first event develop
      about three tumors on average. The paper named no gene and no molecule. It set out a
      prediction about the number of events, and it gave
      later workers a reason to look for genes whose loss, rather than whose activation, lets a
      tumor grow.
  - name: "Webster Cavenee"
    contribution: >-
      With Thaddeus Dryja and colleagues, compared DNA markers in blood and tumor from the same
      retinoblastoma patients and found tumors that had become homozygous for chromosome 13
      regions, showing that the second hit can be a chromosomal event.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Cavenee, Dryja, Phillips, Benedict, Godbout, Gallie et al., Nature 305:779 (1983)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the early 1980s the retinoblastoma locus had been placed on chromosome 13, but the gene
      itself was unknown. Cavenee and his colleagues did not need the gene to test Knudson's idea.
      They used restriction fragment length polymorphisms, positions where the two copies of a
      chromosome differ in where a restriction enzyme cuts, so that a probe gives fragments of
      different sizes from the two homologs. They compared each patient's constitutional DNA,
      from normal tissue, with DNA from the tumor. In several patients heterozygous at chromosome 13
      markers, the tumor showed only one of the two alleles. The patterns fitted a tumor cell that had
      kept the chromosome carrying the mutant allele and lost the normal homolog's copy of the
      region, by mitotic recombination or by mitotic nondisjunction. The 1983 paper in Nature
      linked a recessive cellular change to a visible chromosomal mechanism, and comparing tumor
      with normal DNA for loss of heterozygosity became a standard way to search for other
      tumor-suppressor genes.
  - name: "J. Michael Bishop and Harold Varmus"
    contribution: >-
      With Dominique Stehelin and Peter Vogt, showed that DNA related to the transforming gene of
      an avian sarcoma virus is present in normal bird DNA, so a viral oncogene has a normal
      cellular counterpart.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Stehelin, Varmus, Bishop & Vogt, Nature 260:170 (1976); Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1989 (nobelprize.org)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Rous sarcoma virus, a retrovirus of chickens, turns infected cells cancerous, and genetic
      work had traced that ability to one viral gene, src. Bishop and Varmus, working together at
      the University of California, San Francisco, asked where the gene came from. With
      Dominique Stehelin and Peter Vogt, they made a radioactive DNA
      copy of the src sequence and asked whether it would pair with DNA from cells that had never
      been infected. It did. In the 1976 Nature paper, DNA related to the transforming gene was
      present in normal DNA of chickens and of other birds. The interpretation was that the virus
      had picked up a normal cellular gene and carried an altered copy. That turned the search for
      cancer genes inward, toward normal genes that regulate growth, now called proto-oncogenes.
      Bishop and Varmus shared the 1989 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery of
      the cellular origin of retroviral oncogenes.
  - name: "Bert Vogelstein"
    contribution: >-
      With colleagues, measured RAS mutations and chromosome losses across colorectal tumors at
      different stages and, with Eric Fearon, proposed a genetic model in which mutations
      accumulate as colorectal tumors progress.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Vogelstein et al., N. Engl. J. Med. 319:525 (1988); Fearon & Vogelstein, Cell 61:759 (1990)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Colorectal cancer suited a genetic study of progression because the tissue can be sampled at
      many stages, from small benign polyps (adenomas) to invasive carcinoma. In a 1988 study in
      the New England Journal of Medicine, Vogelstein and colleagues at Johns Hopkins examined 172
      colorectal tumor specimens. RAS mutations were found in 9 percent of adenomas smaller than
      1 cm, 58 percent of larger adenomas and 47 percent of carcinomas, and the molecular changes
      they measured, including losses on several chromosomes, accumulated in parallel with
      clinical progression. In 1990, with Eric Fearon, he set out a genetic model for colorectal
      tumorigenesis in Cell. In that model tumors arise by mutations that activate oncogenes and
      inactivate tumor-suppressor genes, and the accumulation of changes matters more than their
      exact order. The model gave cancer biology a testable picture of a tumor as the product of
      several mutations acquired over time, in one tissue.

techniques:
  - name: "Restriction fragment length polymorphism comparison"
    blurb: >-
      A probe detects DNA fragments whose sizes differ between the two homologs of a chromosome.
      Comparing a patient's normal DNA with tumor DNA shows whether the tumor has lost one of the
      two alleles. Cavenee and colleagues used it to detect loss of heterozygosity on chromosome 13
      in retinoblastoma.
  - name: "Gene transfer and functional rescue"
    blurb: >-
      A cloned normal gene is introduced into tumor cells that lack it, and their behavior is
      compared with controls, for example by colony growth in soft agar and tumor formation in
      nude mice. Suppression after restoration is functional evidence that the gene is a tumor
      suppressor, as Huang and colleagues showed for RB in 1988.
  - name: "Tumor and matched-normal sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      DNA from the tumor and from normal tissue of the same person is sequenced and compared.
      Variants in both are usually germline; variants only in the tumor are usually somatic. The fraction of
      reads carrying each variant, read with the tumor purity, suggests whether a mutation is in all
      tumor cells or only a subclone.
  - name: "Karyotyping and fluorescence in situ hybridization"
    blurb: >-
      Banded metaphase chromosomes show gains, losses and translocations large enough to see under
      the microscope, such as the 9;22 translocation in chronic myeloid leukemia. Fluorescent
      probes then test a chosen locus, for example whether two genes have been brought together.

    choices:

      - "Loss of the whole normal chromosome 13 alone"
      - "A new point mutation in the wild-type allele"
      - "Amplification of the region with the mutant allele"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      A crossover between homologs in a dividing somatic cell can give a daughter cell two copies
      of the mutant region while keeping two chromosomes, a copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity.
      Losing the normal chromosome alone leaves one copy, not two. A new point mutation leaves the
      cell with two different mutant alleles, so nearby markers stay heterozygous. Amplification
      adds copies of the mutant region but does not remove the wild-type allele.
  - stem: "Why does an inherited RB1 mutation show dominant inheritance in families when it is recessive in the cell?"
    choices:
      - "The mutant allele makes each retinal cell a tumor"
      - "The mutant RB protein blocks the wild-type protein"
      - "A second hit is likely in at least one of many cells"
      - "The wild-type allele is silenced in every carrier"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Every retinal cell in a carrier already has one hit, so a second, rare hit in at least one of
      the very many cells is likely, and most carriers develop tumors. If the inherited allele alone
      made each cell a tumor, the whole retina would be tumor. A dominant-negative protein would make
      the allele dominant in the cell, the opposite of the premise. The wild-type allele is lost
      only in the few cells that become tumors, not in every cell of every carrier.
  - stem: "A patient's tumor shrinks on a targeted drug and then regrows. Sequencing of the regrowth finds a resistance-conferring change that was present in only a small fraction of the cells before treatment. Which explanation fits the clonal-evolution view of tumors?"
    choices:
      - "The drug selected a pre-existing subclone that divides in its presence, and that subclone expanded"
      - "The drug caused the specific resistance change in each cell it reached"
      - "Every cell in the tumor acquired the change at the same moment after treatment began"
      - "Resistance in a tumor must have been inherited through the germline"
    answer: 0
    rationale: "Mutation supplies variants and selection keeps those that divide more or die less, as in bacteria under an antibiotic. A subclone with a resistance change survives treatment and expands, so treatment can fail when a resistant subline emerges. A change present in only a small fraction of cells beforehand was not acquired by all cells at once, and nothing in the scenario says the drug produced it. What fraction of tumor cells carries the variant?"
    choices:
      - "About 10% of tumor cells, so it is subclonal"
      - "About 12% of tumor cells, so it is subclonal"
      - "About 25% of tumor cells, so it is subclonal"
      - "Every tumor cell, so the variant is clonal"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      A heterozygous variant in every tumor cell would give 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.40 of reads, so a fraction
      of 0.10 means 0.10 / 0.40 = 0.25 of tumor cells carry it, a subclone. Reading 10% directly
      ignores both the normal cells and the second, wild-type allele in each tumor cell. Dividing
      0.10 by the purity alone gives about 12% but still forgets that a heterozygous cell
      contributes only half its reads. A clonal heterozygous variant would sit near 0.40, not 0.10.

  - level: "highschool"

    text: >-

  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      Oncogenes arise by gain of function in proto-oncogenes, through point mutations (RAS codon

The first film followed one somatic change through a dividing lineage. A **somatic** change arises in a body cell during life and passes to its daughters, but usually not to the person's children. A **germline** change comes through the egg or sperm, is usually in every cell, and can be inherited. A **driver** gives its cell a selective advantage. A **passenger** gives none.

<Support>**The one idea:** Cell lineages expand when their DNA changes improve growth or survival. Every change a lineage carries, driver or passenger, spreads with it. **Scope:** This rule describes selection among somatic cell lineages within a tissue. It does not say that every DNA change helps, that every expanding lineage owes its growth to selection, that every selected clone becomes cancer, or that every cancer follows one fixed sequence.</Support>

## Two kinds of cancer gene: accelerators and brakes

The first film set the one-copy and two-copy rules; here are the molecules behind them, then the exceptions. One change alone usually does not make a malignant cancer.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/cancer-genetics/accelerator-and-brake-genes.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: three panels, each with a cell holding two gene copies. With one altered oncogene copy the cell divides; with one working copy of a tumor suppressor the cell is held back; with both tumor-suppressor copies lost the cell divides. Simple pedal shapes mark a stuck accelerator and a working or cracked brake." caption="An oncogene is an accelerator stuck on, so one altered copy is usually enough. A tumor suppressor is a brake, and one working copy usually still holds, so a cell must normally lose both copies before it divides unchecked." />

A **proto-oncogene** is a normal gene whose product drives growth or division; an **oncogene** is its altered, overactive form, a gain of function dominant within the cell.

RAS is a switch: on while it holds GTP, off once it hydrolyzes GTP to GDP. A one-base change in HRAS from a human bladder carcinoma line replaces glycine with valine at codon 12. Purified Val12 protein hydrolyzed GTP several times more slowly than normal HRAS made in *E. coli*. The larger effect: a GTPase-activating protein, or GAP, sped hydrolysis by normal RAS more than 200-fold but did not speed codon 12 mutants (signaling in the [oncogenes](/lessons/oncogenes/) and [RAS-to-MAPK](/lessons/ras-to-mapk/) lessons).

**Predict.** If a RAS mutant ignores the GAP that normally speeds its off reaction, does it spend more or less time in the GTP-bound state?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

More time. Without GAP speeding its off reaction, the mutant stays GTP-bound, and on, longer.

</details>

MYC is usually activated another way: by extra copies (amplification) or by a translocation that puts it under the control of a highly active gene's regulatory DNA. In Burkitt lymphoma the commonest change is t(8;14), which places MYC beside an immunoglobulin heavy-chain gene (see [chromosome changes](/lessons/chromosome-changes/)).

A **tumor-suppressor gene** encodes a brake: its product restrains division or stops a damaged cell. Losing both alleles, a change recessive within the cell, can help a cell become cancerous, usually together with other changes.

RB1 encodes RB. In its underphosphorylated form, RB binds E2F transcription factors that turn on genes for DNA replication. Returning a cloned RB gene to RB-deficient human tumor cells suppressed their tumor-like growth in a 1988 test. TP53 encodes p53, which accumulates after DNA damage and can halt the cell cycle or trigger cell death (see [stress signals](/lessons/stress-signals/) and [death receptors and BCL-2](/lessons/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases/)).

The first film's paradox, an *RB1* allele dominant in a family yet recessive in a cell, rests on pedigree terms: the allele shows **autosomal dominant** inheritance: about half a carrier's children inherit the risk, and affected people can appear generation after generation (see [reading pedigrees](/lessons/pedigrees/)). Both hold because a pedigree tracks risk, and a carrier has very many cells that each already have one hit, so a second hit in at least one is likely. **Penetrance**, the fraction of carriers who show the trait, is therefore high but below 100%.

p53 shows two exceptions to the copy rules. It acts as a tetramer, and many TP53 missense mutant proteins can disable wild-type subunits they join, a dominant-negative effect. In mice with one p53-null allele, many tumors kept a functional wild-type allele, so reduced dose may suffice in that model.

**Predict.** If mutant p53 disables a tetramer that also contains wild-type subunits, must both TP53 alleles be lost before function falls?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not necessarily. A mutant subunit can interfere with wild-type subunits in the same tetramer.

</details>

## Retinoblastoma and the two-hit arithmetic

Retinoblastoma is a tumor of the developing retina. Hereditary cases tend to be bilateral (both eyes) and diagnosed earlier; nonhereditary cases usually involve one tumor in one eye. Most hereditary cases lack a family history because the mutation arose new, most often in the father's sperm, and some carriers have a tumor in only one eye.

The first film's two routes come from Alfred Knudson. In 1971 he analysed 48 cases with published series and concluded that two events are needed. He estimated that carriers develop about three tumors on average, not a whole retina of tumor. Knudson did not find the gene; Stephen Friend and colleagues isolated a cDNA from the chromosome 13 *RB1* locus in 1986.

A simple model shows what the two hits predict. If each tumor in a carrier arises from an independent second hit, tumors per eye follow a Poisson distribution. With about three tumors per carrier and independent eyes, about 5% of carriers have no tumor, so penetrance is about 95%, and most affected carriers have bilateral disease (full calculation under Going deeper).

**Predict.** Under this model, is a carrier more likely to have tumors in both eyes or in one eye only?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Both eyes: about 60% of carriers, against about 35% with one eye only.

</details>

In a nonhereditary case, both rare hits must fall in one lineage, so the expected number of tumors per eye is far below 1. Such tumors tend to be single, one-sided and diagnosed later.

## How the second hit happens: loss of heterozygosity

A carrier is **heterozygous** at *RB1*: one mutant allele and one wild-type allele. A second hit can be a new point mutation in the wild-type allele or silencing of its promoter by DNA methylation (see [eukaryotic regulation](/lessons/eukaryotic-regulation/)).

Often it is larger. **Loss of heterozygosity** (LOH) means that a site no longer has two different allele identities. In a carrier's tumor, only the mutant identity remains. The cell may keep one physical copy, after a deletion or chromosome loss, or two matching copies, after loss and duplication of the mutant chromosome or after **mitotic recombination**, a crossover between homologs in a dividing body cell that leaves a daughter cell with two copies of the mutant region.

**Predict.** A tumor retains two copies of chromosome 13 but shows only the mutant marker near *RB1*. Does that pattern require a deletion?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Mitotic recombination or loss followed by duplication can leave two copies with one allele identity.

</details>

In 1983 Webster Cavenee, Thaddeus Dryja and colleagues tested this with DNA markers that differ between the two copies of chromosome 13. In several patients heterozygous at those markers in normal tissue, the tumor showed only one allele. The patterns fitted mitotic recombination, or nondisjunction with loss of the wild-type homolog. The second hit was often chromosomal, and LOH in tumor DNA became a way to find suppressor genes in other cancers.

## Instability and clonal evolution

The same lineage logic scales from one retinal cell to competing subclones in a tumor. A tumor is usually a **clone**, the descendants of one cell. In 1976 Peter Nowell proposed that tumors evolve: acquired genetic variability lets more aggressive sublines be selected. Treatment can fail when a resistant subline emerges. As in bacteria under an antibiotic, mutation supplies variants and selection keeps those that divide more or die less.

**Genome instability** raises the supply. Mutations in DNA-repair genes tend to cause further mutations in other genes. Cells lacking mismatch repair, as in Lynch syndrome tumors, accumulate copying errors in short repeats. Loss of BRCA1 or BRCA2 impairs repair of double-strand breaks by homologous recombination (see [DNA repair and recombination](/lessons/dna-repair-and-recombination/) and [mutation](/lessons/mutation/)). Faulty chromosome segregation produces whole-chromosome gains and losses, and chromothripsis can produce many clustered rearrangements in one catastrophic event.

**Predict.** Does losing mismatch repair directly make every new mutation advantageous, or does it mainly change the supply of variants?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It raises the supply. Selection favors lineages whose drivers improve growth or survival, while passengers carried by those lineages can hitchhike with them.

</details>

Colorectal tumors show the accumulation. RAS mutations were found far more often in larger adenomas and in carcinomas than in small adenomas, and the changes accumulated as tumors progressed (Vogelstein and colleagues, 1988). Eric Fearon and Vogelstein's 1990 model stressed the accumulation of changes over their exact order; it is a colorectal model, not a fixed sequence for every tumor.

Back to the one idea: instability creates more candidates, and growth or survival decides which lineages expand. In whole cancer genomes from many tumor types, each with matched normal tissue, the Pan-Cancer Analysis of Whole Genomes consortium identified an average of 4.6 drivers per tumor with current knowledge. That figure depends on the rules used to call drivers. Selection also works before cancer, in clones of normal human esophagus; a selected clone is not automatically a cancer.

## Reading a tumor genome

All those competing lineages end up mixed in one tumor sample. Can its reads still show which lineage carries a change?

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The second film compared the tumor with a **matched normal** sample, often blood, from the same person, and read each variant's **variant allele fraction** (VAF), the fraction of reads carrying it, against the sample's **purity**, the fraction of cells that are tumor cells (method in the [sequencing lesson](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/)).

A variant in both samples is usually germline, most often inherited and rarely new in the egg or sperm, but blood is not a perfect normal. Blood-cell clones that expand with age carry somatic mutations, and those cells can also enter the tumor sample.

> **expected VAF (heterozygous, all tumor cells, two copies) = purity × 0.5**

When any of the shortcut's four conditions fails (heterozygous, in every tumor cell, absent from normal cells, two copies), count mutant copies over all copies in the mixed sample, as the design problem does.

Genotype now shapes diagnosis. In chronic myeloid leukemia, the 9;22 translocation makes the BCR-ABL kinase that imatinib inhibits (see [drugs against a part](/lessons/drugs-against-a-part/)). In a 2021 study of acute myeloid leukemia and myelodysplastic syndromes, whole-genome sequencing was fast enough to change the genetic risk category for some patients.

### Design problem

A colorectal tumor sample is estimated at 60% tumor cells. Tumor and matched-normal sequencing gives: KRAS G12D at VAF 0.30 in the tumor; TP53 R175H at VAF 0.60, with the other TP53 allele lost; PIK3CA at VAF 0.08; and a BRCA2 frameshift at VAF 0.50 in normal tissue and 0.80 in the tumor. Assume two copies of each region unless the data say otherwise. Which variants are inherited, which are in every tumor cell, and which are in a subclone? Why is the matched normal needed?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

In this simplified problem, reads sample DNA copies evenly.

**KRAS.** If every tumor cell has one mutant and one wild-type copy, and normal cells have none, the expected VAF is 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.30, as observed: a heterozygous, clonal variant.

**TP53.** If tumor cells retain two mutant copies, every tumor-cell read is mutant: 0.6 × 1.0 = 0.60, as observed. Had the wild-type allele been deleted instead, tumor cells would carry one copy and normal cells two, giving mutant over all copies: (0.6 × 1) / (0.6 × 1 + 0.4 × 2) = 0.43. So 0.60 fits clonal, copy-neutral LOH, such as mitotic recombination, under these assumptions.

**PIK3CA.** If it is heterozygous, a clonal variant would sit at 0.30; 0.08 / 0.30 = 0.27, so about 27% of tumor cells carry it: a subclone that arose later.

**BRCA2.** A VAF of 0.50 in normal tissue fits a heterozygous germline allele, most likely inherited; testing the parents would show whether it is new. In the tumor, 0.6 × 1.0 + 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.80 fits two mutant copies in tumor cells and one of each in normal cells: copy-neutral LOH, as for TP53. A deletion of the wild-type copy would instead give (0.6 × 1 + 0.4 × 1) / (0.6 × 1 + 0.4 × 2) = 0.71. Either route supplies a second hit in a gene already present in one-hit form.

**The matched normal.** Tumor data alone cannot separate an inherited BRCA2 allele from a somatic one, or set aside the many harmless inherited variants every person carries. These are inferences about one sample, not clinical advice.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The two-hit arithmetic in full

**Worked problem.** Assume that in a carrier of one mutant *RB1* allele each tumor arises from an independent second hit, so the number of tumors per eye follows a Poisson distribution with mean m = 1.5 (3 per person, Knudson's approximate figure), and the eyes are independent. What fractions of carriers have no tumor, a tumor in one eye only, and tumors in both eyes?

Step 1. The Poisson probability of zero events is e^(−m).

> **P(no tumor in one eye) = e^(−1.5) = 0.223**

Step 2. So P(at least one tumor in an eye) = 1 − 0.223 = 0.777.

Step 3. No tumor in either eye: 0.223 × 0.223 = 0.050, which equals e^(−3). About 5% stay tumor free, so penetrance is about 95%.

Step 4. Tumors in both eyes: 0.777 × 0.777 = 0.604.

Step 5. A tumor in one eye only, where either eye can be the affected one: 2 × 0.223 × 0.777 = 0.347.

Check: 0.050 + 0.604 + 0.347 = 1.001, which is 1 within rounding. About one carrier in twenty has no tumor yet can pass the allele on.

If a nonhereditary case's expected number of tumors per eye were 0.0001, a tumor in each eye would have a chance of about 1 in 100 million.

### Who found what, and when

In 1982 several laboratories, among them those of Mariano Barbacid, Robert Weinberg and Michael Wigler, reported in *Nature* the codon 12 valine-for-glycine change described above; the slower hydrolysis was measured in 1984 and the GAP effect in 1987. Riccardo Dalla-Favera and colleagues mapped c-myc to chromosome 8 in 1982.

### Cohort sizes and percentages

In a 1988 series of 172 specimens, Bert Vogelstein and colleagues found RAS mutations in 9% of adenomas smaller than 1 cm, 58% of larger adenomas and 47% of carcinomas. The Pan-Cancer Analysis of Whole Genomes consortium examined 2,658 whole cancer genomes from 38 tumor types, each with matched normal tissue. In the 2021 leukemia study, whole-genome sequencing in 117 consecutive patients took a median of five days and changed the genetic risk category for 19 of them.
