## The big idea

Turn over a log after rain and you may find earthworms. Turn it over on a hot, dry day and they are often gone. That is a clue about what an earthworm needs.

Earthworms breathe through their skin, and it only works while the skin is moist. They dry out fast in sun and dry air. They have no eyes, but cells in their skin sense light. Most earthworms move away from bright light and dry ground, toward damp, dark places. A behavior that helps a living thing survive is called a **behavioral adaptation**. The worm does not plan this. Its body responds to light and dryness.

Like all animals, earthworms need air, food, water and shelter. Damp soil gives them all four. Earthworms also have a job in the soil. They eat dead leaves and bits of soil, mix the soil, and leave tunnels that let in air and water. Many people count them as **decomposers**, living things that break down dead plants and animals.

This activity lets your child test the worms’ responses with a fair, repeated test, then return them safely.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time. Children stand back about 6 feet while you lift any log or rock, tilting it toward you with a stick, not bare hands, so it stays between you and anything underneath. Look for snakes, wasps, ants and spiders. Look at, but never touch, any snake. You do any digging with the trowel.
- Handle worms gently, briefly and with wet hands. Try not to handle them right after using lotion, sunscreen or hand sanitizer. Never cut, squeeze or drop a worm, and keep worms out of the sun. Babies and toddlers should not touch worms or soil, because they put things in their mouths.
- Put yard worms back where you found them. Do not let bait-shop worms go outside. They are usually not native and can harm forests.
- If a worm thrashes like a snake and has a smooth, milky-white band, it may be a jumping worm, an invasive kind. Do not carry it, or soil from that spot, to another place, and do not put it back in a different garden. Take a photo of its band and ask your local Extension office what to do.
- Use plain water only. If you use a spray bottle, use a new one, never one that held cleaner. Keep any lamp and its cord away from the water.
- Wash hands with soap and water afterwards. After digging in leaves or tall grass, check skin and clothes for ticks and shower soon. Do not touch plants with leaves in groups of three, shiny or dull (poison ivy). Wipe up spilled water so the floor is not slippery.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Find worms.** Look after rain, or dig gently in a damp garden bed or under a pile of leaves. Collect 6 to 10 worms. Keep them in a container of damp soil in the shade, and use them the same day.
2. **Look closely.** Wet your hands and your child’s hands. Place one worm on a damp paper towel. Find the band, called the **clitellum**, which is closer to the front end. Young worms do not have one yet. Let the worm crawl and listen. You may hear a faint scratching. That is its tiny bristles, called **setae**, gripping the paper. Keep each look to a minute or two.
3. **Set up damp and dry.** Line the tray with paper towels. Wet one half so it is damp but not puddled. Leave the other half dry. Cover the whole tray with cardboard so both sides are equally dark. Ask, "Where do you think the worms will go?"
4. **Run the test.** Lay the worms across the middle line. Cover the tray, wait 10 minutes, then count the worms on each side. A worm on the line counts as "middle".
5. **Swap and repeat.** Turn the tray around so the damp side is now where the dry side was. Put the worms back in the middle and test again. Add the two damp counts together, and the two dry counts together.
6. **Test light and dark (optional).** Make the whole tray damp. Cover one half with cardboard. Cover the other half with clear plastic wrap or a clear lid, so both halves stay equally damp and only the light is different. Light the clear half with bright room light or a desk lamp with an LED bulb about 2 feet above, which stays cool. Keep the tray out of direct sun. Predict, place the worms in the middle, wait 10 minutes and count. Then swap the two covers and repeat.
7. **Return the worms.** Mist yard worms with water and take them back to where you found them. Lay them on damp soil in the shade and watch them burrow. Keep bait-shop worms for fishing, or bag them for the trash.

Expect most worms to end up on the damp side and under the cover, but not every worm. Some barely move in 10 minutes.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder which end is the front. How could we tell?"
- "Why do you think the worm’s skin feels wet?"
- "Where do you think the worms will go, the damp side or the dry side?"
- "What do you think will happen if we move the cardboard to the other end?"
- "I wonder how a worm knows it is in the light without eyes."
- "Why might worms be hard to find on a hot, dry day?"

## Why it works

An earthworm has no lungs. Oxygen passes through its thin, moist skin into tiny blood vessels. Mucus keeps that skin wet. On a dry surface or in sunlight, the skin dries and the worm cannot take in enough oxygen. Long exposure to light can harm or kill a worm, so keep tests short.

Light-sensing cells in the skin let a worm tell light from dark. Moving toward damp, dark soil keeps it where its skin stays moist. Over many generations, worms that responded this way were more likely to survive and leave young.

Many earthworms in Virginia yards came from Europe or Asia, including the nightcrawlers sold as bait. Virginia also has native earthworms. Jumping worms from Asia are spreading in Virginia. They strip the leaf layer from forest floors and gardens.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Do only the damp and dry test, with four to six worms. Your child places the worms, then draws the tray at the end with a dot for each worm. Ask, "Which side has more dots?" Skip the counting table and just compare. Let your child hold a worm only on a wet, open palm, for a few seconds.

### Ages 8 to 10
Run both tests twice and record counts in a table. Make a bar graph of damp versus dry and dark versus light. Ask your child to name the one thing that changed in each test and the things kept the same. Then ask them to write a claim, such as "Most worms moved to the damp side," and the evidence for it.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If the worms barely move, they may be too cold or too dry. Mist them, let them rest in damp soil for 10 minutes, and try again in a room-temperature spot. If you cannot find worms, look early in the morning after rain, or under boards and leaf piles in shady beds. Spring and fall are usually easier than hot summers.
