## The big idea

Give earthworms a choice of damp or dry paper. Your child can predict where they’ll go, then count the worms to check.

**Say it together:** "Earthworms have no lungs and no eyes. They breathe through wet skin, and their skin feels light. Most crawl toward damp, dark soil, where they eat dead leaves and help make soil."

Turn over a log after rain and you may find earthworms. Check on a hot, dry day and they are often gone. That is a clue to what earthworms need.

Earthworms breathe through moist skin and dry out fast in sun or dry air. Cells in their skin sense light, so most move away from bright light and dry ground toward damp, dark shelter. A behavior that helps a living thing survive is a **behavioral adaptation**. The worm does not plan it; its body responds to light and dryness.

Like all animals, earthworms need air, food, water and shelter, and damp soil gives them all four. They eat dead leaves and soil, mix the earth, and leave tunnels for air and water. Many people count them as **decomposers**, living things that break down dead plants and animals.

<SafetyNote>
- **Supervision and tools:** Stay with your child the whole time. You dig with the trowel, tear any plastic wrap, handle any glass magnifying glass (plastic is safer), and set up any cool LED desk lamp, keeping lamp and cord away from water.
- **Choking and mouthing:** Keep worms, soil bits, pebbles and plastic wrap away from children under 4. **Call 911 if choking.** If a child swallows something harmful, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222.
- **Outdoors and wildlife:** Dig only in your yard or with permission. Stay away from roads, go out only after storms pass, and dress for sun and weather. Have children stand back about 6 feet while you lift any log or rock. Tilt it toward you with a stick so it stays between you and anything underneath. Look for snakes, wasps, ants and spiders; never touch snakes or any biting or stinging animal. Avoid poison ivy (leaves in groups of three, shiny or dull) and never taste outdoor plants, berries or mushrooms. Check skin and clothes for ticks and shower soon.
- **Worm care and return:** Handle worms gently, briefly, with wet hands, not right after lotion, sunscreen or hand sanitizer. Never cut, squeeze or drop a worm, and keep worms out of sun. Return yard worms where found; never release bait-shop worms outside, as they are usually non-native and can harm forests. If a worm thrashes like a snake and has a smooth, milky-white band, it may be an invasive jumping worm—do not move it or its soil elsewhere; photo its band and ask your local Extension office.
- **Water and cleanup:** Use plain water only, in a cup or new spray bottle that never held cleaner. Wipe up spills. Wash hands with soap and water afterwards, and wash the tray well before food use.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

Test one change at a time: damp versus dry first (both halves dark), then optionally dark versus light (both halves damp).

1. **Find worms.** Look after rain, or you dig gently with the trowel in a damp garden bed or under leaves. Collect 6 to 10 worms. Keep them in a container of damp soil in the shade and use them the same day.
2. **Look closely.** Wet your hands and your child’s hands. Set one worm on a damp paper towel for a minute or two. Notice its moist skin and fine rings. Look for eyes together. Ask, *"I wonder which end is the front. How could we tell?"* Look for the lighter, thicker band (**clitellum**) closer to the front. Young worms do not have one yet. Say, *"Hold still and listen. What do you hear?"* You may hear a faint scratch as tiny bristles (**setae**) grip the paper.
3. **Set up damp and dry.** Line the tray with paper towels. Wet one half until damp, not puddled; leave the other dry. Cover the whole tray with cardboard so both sides stay dark. Ask, *"Both sides are dark, one damp and one dry. Where do you think the worms will go?"*
4. **Run the test and check.** Lay the worms across the middle line. Cover the tray, wait 10 minutes, then count each side (on the line counts as "middle") and check your child’s guess.
5. **Swap and repeat.** Ask, *"What do you think will happen if we turn the tray around?"* Turn the tray so the damp half is on the other side. Put the worms back in the middle and cover the whole tray again. Wait 10 minutes and count each side. Add the two damp counts together and the two dry counts together.
6. **Test light and dark (optional).** Dampen the whole tray. Cover one half with cardboard and the other with clear plastic wrap or a clear lid. Both covers keep the halves equally damp so only the light differs. Keep the tray out of direct sun. Use bright room light or have the adult set a cool LED desk lamp about 2 feet above the clear half. Ask, *"Where will they go now?"* Start the worms in the middle. Wait 10 minutes and count. Then swap the covers and repeat.
7. **Return the worms.** Mist yard worms and lay them on shady damp soil where you found them. Say, *"Let’s watch what they do first,"* and watch them burrow. Keep bait-shop worms for fishing, or have an adult bag them for the trash.

Expect most worms to end up on the damp side and under the cover, but not every worm. Some barely move in 10 minutes.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "Why do you think the worm’s skin feels wet?"
- "What do you think will happen if we move the cardboard to the other end?"
- "I wonder how a worm knows it is in the light without eyes."
- "Why might worms be hard to find on a hot, dry day?"

**Two-question wrap-up:**
1. "What did we count on each side? What happened after we swapped sides?"
2. "Knowing earthworms breathe through wet skin and feel light with their skin, why does that side fit them?"

## Why it works

An earthworm has no lungs. Oxygen passes through its thin, moist skin into tiny blood vessels, and mucus keeps that skin wet. On dry paper or in sunlight, the skin dries and the worm cannot take in enough oxygen. Long exposure to light can harm or kill a worm, so keep tests short.

Light-sensing cells in the skin let a worm tell light from dark. Moving toward damp, dark soil keeps its skin moist. Over many generations, worms that responded this way were more likely to survive and leave young.

Many earthworms in Virginia yards came from Europe or Asia, including bait nightcrawlers. Virginia also has native earthworms. Jumping worms from Asia are spreading in Virginia and strip the leaf layer from forest floors and gardens.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Do only the damp-and-dry test with four to six worms. Your child places the worms. At the end of the test, have them draw a dot on the tray sketch where each worm ended up. Ask, *"Which side has more dots?"* Skip the table. Let your child hold a worm only on a wet, open palm for a few seconds.

### Ages 8 to 10
Run both tests twice, record counts in a table, and make a bar graph of damp versus dry and dark versus light. Ask your child to name the one thing changed and what stayed the same, then write a claim—such as *"Most worms moved to the damp side"*—with their evidence.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If worms barely move, they may be too cold or dry. Mist them, rest them in damp soil for 10 minutes, and retry at room temperature. If you cannot find worms, look early in the morning after rain, or under boards and leaf piles in shady beds. Spring and fall are usually easier than hot summers.
