techniques:
  - name: "Thin-section electron microscopy with an electron-dense tracer"
    blurb: >-
      A tracer that scatters electrons, such as hemoglobin or a heavy-metal salt, is placed on one
      side of an epithelium, the tissue is fixed and sectioned, and the electron microscope shows
      how far the tracer got between cells. It localizes the barrier to that tracer, not to ions.
  - name: "Freeze-fracture electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Frozen tissue is cracked so the fracture runs through the middle of membranes, and a
      platinum-carbon replica of the exposed face is imaged. Tight junctions appear as a network
      of branching strands, so you can count strands and see whether they exist at all.
  - name: "Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER)"
    blurb: >-
      Cells are grown as a sheet on a porous filter, electrodes on each side pass a small current,
      and the resistance of the sheet is read after subtracting a blank filter and multiplying by
      area. It reports how freely ions cross the whole sheet, through and between the cells (in
      leaky sheets the route between often dominates), but not which route or which ions, or
      whether larger molecules leak.
  - name: "Uncharged tracer flux"
    blurb: >-
      A labelled molecule that carries no charge, such as mannitol or a fluorescent dextran of
      known size, is added to one side and sampled from the other over time. Comparing sizes tells
      a narrow claudin pore from larger routes: the tight junction's leak pathway, which passes
      molecules as large as 4 kDa dextran, or a break in the sheet, which has no sharp size cutoff.

  - stem: "A scientist engineers intestinal epithelial cells so that the two glucose carriers swap faces: SGLT1 sits only on the basolateral membrane and GLUT2 only on the apical membrane. The sodium pump stays basolateral. Compared with normal cells, what is the most likely effect on glucose movement?"

    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Carrier placement and the gradients together set the direction. Under typical gut gradients, basolateral SGLT1
      would use the sodium gradient to take glucose in from the blood side, and apical GLUT2 would

      glucose here, and the question is about which carrier sits on which face.

The film followed glucose through one gut cell. The lining is an **epithelium**, a sheet of cells that covers a surface or lines a tube. In a simple epithelium, one cell thick like this, each cell's **apical** face looks toward a lumen (the space inside a tube) or the outside world, and its **basolateral** face toward its neighbours and the basement membrane, a mat of extracellular matrix ([adhesion and the extracellular matrix](/lessons/adhesion-and-ecm/)). The faces' different proteins and lipids are its **apicobasal polarity**.

> **The one idea.** For glucose in the gut under the usual gradients, apical SGLT1 and basolateral GLUT2 bias transport toward the blood. The seal does not orient that pathway; it decides what can slip between the cells, and so what can leak back.
>
> *Scope:* glucose across a simple vertebrate epithelium. Other epithelia also secrete and place their carriers differently, and a carrier's direction also depends on the gradients it meets. The film's glucose-leaking seal was a thought experiment; the real one is in The seal, below.

The rest of the page asks how far uphill the carriers can push and at what cost, then what the real seal passes and how it is measured, then what keeps each carrier on its face and holds the sheet together. Each is about the carriers, the seal or the faces, so each comes back to the film. Plants close the page as a contrast.

## How far uphill, and at what cost

The film showed sodium paying for glucose; here are the limit and the price. The turnstile is **SGLT1**, a sodium-glucose **symporter** (one carrier moving two solutes the same way); for human SGLT1 expressed in frog oocytes, coupled transport moves two sodium ions per sugar. The exit door, **GLUT2** in the standard account, is sodium-independent and works by **facilitated diffusion**, letting glucose run down its gradient. The **sodium-potassium pump** never touches glucose. Glucose takes the **transcellular** route, through the cells; the **paracellular** route runs between them ([membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/) works out the forces; [digestion](/lessons/digestion/) covers nutrition).

### How far uphill?

For an ideally coupled carrier, with the sodium levels and voltage held fixed, net transport stops when the energy sodium releases coming in just matches the energy needed to push glucose up its gradient. Sodium pays twice: it is far more concentrated outside the cell than inside, and as a positive ion it is pulled in by the cell's negative inside. If the formula is new, start with the worked numbers.

    <p>Two sodium per glucose: the same factor squared, 61 × 61 ≈ 3,700-fold.</p>

</EquationCard>

A second sodium ion squares the ceiling. An absorbing gut cell is unlikely to approach it: the exit door keeps draining glucose, and real gradients are not fixed. But it means SGLT1 can keep taking glucose long after the gut holds less than the cell. The 2:1 ratio is SGLT1's, not the family's: its kidney relative SGLT2 coupled one sodium per glucose in a study in HEK293T cells, the 60-fold row.

### What does it cost?

Each glucose brings two sodium ions in, and the pump throws out three sodium ions for each ATP it spends. In this simple accounting, absorbing one glucose costs about two-thirds of an ATP, against the roughly 30 ATP the body later makes by oxidizing it.

A passive carrier moves glucose down whatever gradient it meets; on the basolateral face GLUT2 is an exit under the usual gradient, but it can reverse if the gradient does.

## The seal: what passes between the cells

The film's seal was the simple case, a thought experiment in which glucose slipped between the cells. Here is the fuller picture: much of the water and some of the salt that follow glucose do cross between the cells, through a seal that is choosy.

The seal is the **tight junction**, the most apical junction between neighbours. In 1963 Marilyn Farquhar and George Palade surveyed epithelia from rat and guinea-pig organs by electron microscopy ([microscopy and images](/lessons/microscopy-and-images/)). In rat kidney tubules filled with hemoglobin, the tracer stopped at the zonula occludens, the junction now called the tight junction. That placed a barrier there for a protein-sized tracer. It did not show that the junction is closed to ions.

Freeze-fracture, which splits membranes down the middle and replicates the inside face in metal, shows tight junctions as branching strands. In 1998 Mikio Furuse, Shoichiro Tsukita and colleagues showed that claudin-1 or claudin-2 alone built such strands in mouse L fibroblasts, cells that normally have none.

You might expect more strands to make a tighter seal. Two strains of a dog kidney cell line, MDCK, test that. They have similar numbers of strands, yet MDCK I resists electric current well and MDCK II is leaky.

**Predict.** What could make one sheet far leakier to ions than another with the same number of strands?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

What the strands are made of. In 2001 Furuse's group found that MDCK II cells make claudin-2 and MDCK I cells do not. When they put dog claudin-2 into MDCK I cells, resistance fell more than twentyfold, and strand number did not change significantly.

</details>

So the seal is choosy: for small ions, which claudins a tissue makes largely decides what passes. This is the **gate** function. Claudin-2 is thought to form a **paracellular pore**, a channel through the strands that favours cations; the 2001 experiment fits that idea but does not test it (the design problem below shows how).

The small intestine is a **leaky** epithelium, like the kidney's proximal tubule: its seal strongly restricts bacteria and large molecules but lets sodium and water through claudin pores. The distal colon, urinary bladder and collecting duct are **tight**. A leaky epithelium uses both routes at once. Claudin pores are narrow, though: claudin-2 pores do not pass even mannitol, an uncharged molecule about glucose's size. So for glucose the seal mainly governs the ions and water that travel with it, not the direction; only a larger, glucose-permeable leak would let much glucose slip back, as in the film.

## Measuring the seal: TEER

Saying how leaky a seal is takes a number, and the claudin results above rest on this one. TEER, transepithelial electrical resistance, is measured on cells grown as a sheet on a porous filter insert. Here are made-up numbers. MDCK monolayers grow on inserts of area 1.12 cm². A blank insert with no cells reads 120 ohm. A control MDCK I monolayer reads 3,000 ohm, and one expressing claudin-2 reads 250 ohm.

1. Subtract the blank, because the filter and fluid add resistance of their own. Control: 3,000 − 120 = 2,880 ohm. Claudin-2: 250 − 120 = 130 ohm.

**Predict.** The next step corrects for the size of the insert. Picture each patch of sheet as a resistor, so a larger sheet has more of them side by side. Does it read a higher or a lower raw resistance? So do you multiply by area, or divide?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Lower: resistors in parallel pass more current together. So multiply by area, never divide.

</details>

2. Multiply by area. Control: 2,880 × 1.12 = 3,225.6 ohm·cm². Claudin-2: 130 × 1.12 = 145.6 ohm·cm².
3. Compare: 3,225.6 / 145.6 = 22.2-fold drop, the size of effect Furuse and colleagues reported.
4. Know the limit. TEER measures how easily ions carry current through and between the cells (often mostly between, in leaky sheets). It does not say which route or ion, or whether sugars or proteins leak.

> **TEER (ohm·cm²) = (R monolayer − R blank) × area of insert**

### Design problem: pore or leak?

Claudin-2 lowered MDCK I resistance more than twentyfold without changing strand number. TEER alone cannot say why. Design experiments to decide whether it makes a selective cation pore or a general leak.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with selectivity. In parental and claudin-2 MDCK I cells on filters, measure a dilution potential: halve the salt on one side, read the voltage across the sheet, and calculate the sodium-to-chloride permeability ratio. A cation pore predicts a high ratio in claudin-2 cells; a nonselective leak, little change. Bi-ionic potentials, replacing sodium with other cations, add a size and charge profile.

Then test size with uncharged tracers: mannitol, then 4 kDa and larger dextrans. A narrow pore predicts little change in any of them, since even mannitol is too big for claudin-2 pores. A rise points to a larger route: the tight junction's leak pathway, which is size-selective, or a break in the sheet, which has no sharp size cutoff. Checking for dying or missing cells helps tell those two apart.

Then test the protein. Mutate charged residues in claudin-2's extracellular loops; if neutralizing a negative charge lowers cation preference, that supports the idea that the loops line the pore.

Controls: parental cells and an empty-vector clone, matched expression, cell density and days in culture, and more than one clone. Limit: this is one dog kidney cell line, so a result about pore behaviour in MDCK I is not yet a result about any intact tissue.

</details>

## Is the seal also a fence?

The film's idea needs each carrier to stay on its own face. The tight junction's other textbook job, as a **fence**, is said to do that: to stop membrane proteins and lipids drifting between the two faces.

**Predict.** In 2019 Tetsuhisa Otani, Furuse and colleagues disrupted five major claudins in MDCK II cells. The strands vanished and resistance fell. Did the cells lose their polarity?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Polarity was not disorganized. They did not rule out other claudins, and membrane contacts containing JAM-A remained, so a fence made by other junction proteins was not excluded. Still, in these cells polarity did not need claudin strands. The same study found that it did depend on other junction parts: cells lacking the scaffold proteins ZO-1 and ZO-2 had no tight-junction structure, and their polarity was disorganized.

</details>

So the case for the fence is weaker than the slogan, and something else keeps the faces apart.

## How each cell knows its top from its bottom

What keeps SGLT1 on the apical face and the pump on the basolateral one? In the standard scheme, which comes largely from fruit-fly genetics and mammalian cell lines, three groups of proteins mark the sides. The **Crumbs complex** sits apically. The **Par complex**, which includes the kinase aPKC, sits at the boundary where apical meets lateral. The **Scribble complex** sits basolaterally. The groups are thought to restrain one another, so each holds its own territory; aPKC, for example, phosphorylates basolateral proteins and pushes them off the apical membrane. Polarized membrane traffic then delivers the right proteins to each face, as described in [the lesson on vesicle traffic](/lessons/vesicle-traffic/).

## Holding the sheet together: the rest of the stack

The faces and the seal matter only while the sheet stays in one piece. Below the seal, more junctions are stacked down the side of the cell in a characteristic order; all but the gap junction link inside to the cytoskeleton.

| Junction, top to bottom | Transmembrane protein | Inside partner | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tight junction (the seal) | Claudins, with occludin among others | ZO proteins, to actin | Seal the gaps; decide which ions pass |
| Adherens junction | E-cadherin, a calcium-dependent adhesion protein | Catenins, to a belt of actin | Couple neighbours' actin mechanically |
| Desmosomes (spot welds) | Desmogleins and desmocollins, cadherins of their own family | Desmoplakin, to keratin intermediate filaments | Resist stretching and tearing |
| Gap junctions, scattered | Connexins: two hemichannels per channel, one from each cell, each of six connexins in the human connexin 26 crystal structure | No major cytoskeletal anchor in the standard scheme | Pass ions and small molecules from cytosol to cytosol |
| Hemidesmosome, at the base | Integrin alpha6 beta4 | Plectin, to keratin | Anchor the base to the basement membrane |

The order is not universal: many insect epithelia seal with **septate junctions**, which lie below the adherens junction (the figure's inset).

## Plants: walls and plasmodesmata

Plant cells have no tight or adherens junctions, so this section is a contrast, not part of the gut story: a plasmodesma differs in kind from a gap junction (figure). Each plant cell is boxed in a cell wall, an extracellular matrix rich in cellulose, and neighbouring walls are glued together. The route between plant cells is the **plasmodesma**: a channel through the wall lined by plasma membrane that is continuous from one cell to the next. Down its middle runs the **desmotubule**, a narrow strand of endoplasmic reticulum continuous with the ER of both cells. Cytosol fills the sleeve between the two.

## Going deeper

### Junction details

Gap junctions are not alike. Channels made of different connexins pass the messenger cAMP very differently relative to potassium: in pairs of cultured cells made to express one connexin, the permeability ratio was 0.180 for connexin 43, 0.027 for connexin 26 and 0.018 for connexin 40. Two cells can be equally coupled electrically and still share a messenger very differently. Even within the fly the order of junctions can flip: in the midgut the adherens junctions lie below the septate junctions.

### The claudin knockout numbers

In the five-claudin knockout in MDCK II cells, resistance fell from 52.5 to 14.5 ohm·cm² in one clone. Removing JAM-A along with the claudins caused sporadic polarity defects.

### A pore with no visible sleeve

Appearance can mislead at plasmodesmata. In 2019 Dawei Yan and colleagues studied an Arabidopsis root mutant, *plm-2*. At one interface, between the phloem-pole pericycle and the endodermis, they saw no plasmodesmata of the type with a visible cytoplasmic sleeve. Yet GFP moved across that interface faster than in wild-type roots, by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching. A pore with no visible sleeve still passed protein, at least at this interface.
