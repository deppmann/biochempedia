## Explain: the parts and their jobs

<WordBank terms={['organelle', 'nucleus', 'cell membrane', 'cytoplasm', 'endoplasmic reticulum', 'mitochondria', 'vacuole', 'metabolism', 'photosynthesis', 'cellular respiration']} />

Many cells contain organelles. An **organelle** is a small part inside a cell that does a special job. Here are the main parts. Plant and animal cells share the first five. The last, the vacuole, is large in plant cells; many animal cells have only small sacs instead.

- The **nucleus** holds the cell’s instructions. These instructions guide what the cell makes and does.
- The **cell membrane** is the thin boundary around the cell. It controls what enters and leaves, and it lets the cell sense its surroundings.
- The **cytoplasm** is everything inside the cell membrane except the nucleus: a jelly-like fluid and the organelles in it. Many reactions happen in the fluid.
- The **endoplasmic reticulum** (ER) is a network of folded membranes. It helps make and move many proteins and fats.
- **Mitochondria** release much of the energy stored in food such as sugar (glucose) and fats, in a form the cell can use.
- A **vacuole** is a sac that stores water, food and wastes. In animal cells, small sacs called lysosomes break down wastes and worn-out parts.

Each job uses more than one part.

| Job | Parts that help |
|---|---|
| Using energy | Mitochondria, cytoplasm |
| Making molecules | Nucleus (instructions), ER, cytoplasm |
| Removing wastes | Cell membrane, vacuoles and other small sacs |
| Responding to the environment | Cell membrane, nucleus |

Not every cell has every part. A mature human red blood cell has no nucleus and no mitochondria. Bacteria have no nucleus, as you will see in [that lesson](/lessons/domains-kingdoms-and-dna/). Plant cells add a cell wall and a large central vacuole, and green plant cells also have chloroplasts, as shown in [that lesson](/lessons/plant-and-animal-cells/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Each organelle has a special job, and each job of the cell uses several organelles.</Support>

## Explain: the parts work as a team

A cell must repair itself, and many cells also grow and reproduce. Cells also exchange gases, carry out metabolism and respond. The organelles do all of this together.

- **Growth and repair.** Instructions from the nucleus guide which proteins and fats are made. The ER makes and moves many of them. Mitochondria release the energy for this building.
- **Reproduction.** Before a cell divides, it grows and copies its instructions. You will see how in [that lesson](/lessons/cells-divide/).
- **Gas exchange.** In a cell using oxygen, oxygen moves in across the cell membrane, and carbon dioxide moves out. Mitochondria use the oxygen. A leaf cell in light can do the reverse. [That lesson](/lessons/diffusion-and-osmosis/) shows how gases cross.
- **Response.** A signal touches the cell membrane, and the cell changes what it does.

**Metabolism** means all the chemical reactions inside the cell, such as building molecules up and breaking them down. Two examples matter most in this course. In **photosynthesis**, plant cells use radiant energy (light) to make sugar (glucose), as in [that lesson](/lessons/photosynthesis-feeds-the-world/). In **cellular respiration**, cells break down sugar and release its stored energy, as in [that lesson](/lessons/releasing-energy-from-food/).

A cell is a bit like a sports team, where each player has a role. The comparison breaks in one way: no part decides or plans anything. Each part simply follows chemistry.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Organelles work as one system, so trouble in one part slows many life processes.</Support>

## Explain: same parts, different amounts

Look again at the two cells from the start. Both have a nucleus, a membrane, cytoplasm, ER and mitochondria. But the amounts are very different.

A heart muscle cell works without a break, so it releases a lot of energy, mostly from fats. Mitochondria fill a large share of it. A typical white fat cell stores fat. One fat drop fills most of the cell, and the other parts sit in a thin rim.

## Elaborate: how much space do mitochondria fill?

Scientists took very thin slices of cells and photographed them with an electron microscope. Then they laid a grid of points over each picture and counted the points on mitochondria.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/inside-a-cell/counting-points-on-mitochondria.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a grey picture of a thin slice of a cell with oval mitochondria, covered by an even grid of dots. Dots that land on mitochondria are filled gold and the rest are hollow. A zoom shows one mitochondrion with gold dots on it and a hollow dot beside it." caption="Point counting: lay an even grid over a picture and count the points that land on mitochondria. The share of points estimates the share of the cell's volume they fill." />

| Cells measured | Mitochondria (% of cell volume) |
|---|---|
| Hummingbird flight muscle (rufous hummingbird) | 34.5 |
| Heart muscle, 10 kinds of mammals | 22 to 37 (range) |
| Human arm muscle, elite skiers | 9.0 |
| Human leg muscle, elite skiers | 8.6 |
| Guinea pig pancreas cell that makes digestive enzymes | 8.1 |
| Chicken brown-like fat cell, grown in a dish for 22 days | 4.2 |
| Chicken white fat cell, embryo | 0.7 |
| Human red blood cell, mature and healthy | 0 (none) |

Sources: Mathieu-Costello and colleagues, 1992 (hummingbird); Barth and colleagues, 1992 (heart, ten species including humans); Ørtenblad and colleagues, 2018 (skiers, Norway); Bolender, 1974 (pancreas, five guinea pigs); Mezentseva and colleagues, 2008 (chicken cells). The red blood cell row is a textbook fact, not a measurement. Most rows come from different studies; the two skier rows share one study, and so do the two chicken rows.

1. Looking only at rows with a single value, which measured cell had the largest share of mitochondria? Which had the smallest share above zero?
2. Find the mean of the two skier values.
3. How many percentage points apart are the hummingbird muscle and the pancreas cell?

### Design problem

Scientists count points on a grid to measure space inside a cell. Does the spacing of the grid change your answer? Plan a test using a printed electron microscope picture of a cell and clear grids you draw yourself.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does grid spacing change the measured share of a cell filled by mitochondria?
- **Independent variable:** spacing between grid points, such as 10 mm, 5 mm and 2 mm.
- **Dependent variable:** percent of points that land on mitochondria.
- **Constants:** the same printed picture, the same rule for a point on an edge, and the same lighting.
- **Control:** the finest grid (2 mm) is the comparison for the others.
- **Repeated trials:** place each grid in three different spots on the picture and count each time. Find the mean for each spacing.
- **Safety:** use a ruler and pencil only; no sharp tools needed.
- **What it means:** if the coarse grid gives values far from the fine grid, it needs more points to be trusted.

</details>
