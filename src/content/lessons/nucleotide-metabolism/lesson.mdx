**Lose one enzyme that recycles purines, and the body builds purines far faster than normal and makes several times more uric acid.**

That is Lesch-Nyhan syndrome; a classic early sign is orange, sand-like urate crystals in a baby's diaper. A broken recycling enzyme ought to leave the cell short of purines. Why does it make a flood instead, and why does the flood leave as uric acid?

*About the film above:* it covers purines only; its "roughly six ATP" counts making the PRPP (in ATP equivalents, explained below); and uricase, which it says converts uric acid to allantoin, does only the first of three steps.

Nucleotides are the letters of DNA and RNA, the energy carriers ATP and GTP and the core of cyclic AMP, and NAD⁺, FAD and coenzyme A each contain one. Their rings cost a small fortune in ATP, so the cell recycles much of what it has.

## Three words first: base, nucleoside, nucleotide

A purine **base** is a two-ring molecule on its own (a pyrimidine base has one ring). Add a ribose and it is a **nucleoside**; add a phosphate to that ribose and it is a **nucleotide**.

| Base | + ribose = nucleoside | + phosphate = nucleotide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hypoxanthine | inosine | IMP |
| guanine | guanosine | GMP |
| adenine | adenosine | AMP |

IMP is the first purine nucleotide the cell builds, and the parent of both AMP and GMP.

## Two doors in, one way out

Nucleotides do not last: RNA is made and broken down all day, and nucleotides are turned over constantly, so a cell is always releasing free bases.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A cell's purine nucleotides are a pool on a loop, with two doors in (build and recycle) and one way out. Salvage puts a large share of freed bases straight back (how large depends on the tissue); whatever it misses leaves the loop, almost all of it as uric acid. De novo synthesis refills the pool through a gate that PRPP pushes open and finished nucleotides push shut. So, at steady state, the cell builds what it fails to recycle, and the uric acid the body makes equals what it builds plus the purine it absorbs from food.

Two exceptions: a growing tissue builds extra to grow, and a sudden loss of many cells sends their nucleic acids and nucleotides into the exit (the last prediction). Food purine is mostly broken down in the gut wall and liver rather than reused, so it adds to the exit, not the pool. Pyrimidines have their own build and recycle routes (recycling runs through different enzymes) and a harmless exit, covered below.

### The molecule both doors use: PRPP

Both doors start from **PRPP**, 5-phosphoribosyl-1-pyrophosphate: ribose-5-phosphate (from the pentose phosphate pathway) carrying a **pyrophosphate**, two linked phosphates, on carbon 1. PRPP synthetase takes that pyrophosphate from ATP, leaving AMP. PRPP is "activated": when something attaches to carbon 1 the pyrophosphate leaves, and its rapid breakdown makes the attachment effectively one-way. Costs here are counted in **ATP equivalents**: one high-energy phosphate bond spent, as when ATP becomes ADP (a GTP becoming GDP counts the same). PRPP uses one ATP *molecule*, but turning it into AMP spends two bonds, and the AMP must be recharged twice to become ATP again. So one PRPP costs **2 ATP equivalents**.

### Door 1, build: de novo synthesis

The cell assembles the purine ring atom by atom directly on carbon 1 of PRPP, and about ten enzymatic steps later **IMP** appears. The ring's nine atoms come from glycine (three), glutamine (two), aspartate (one), CO₂ (one) and two one-carbon (formyl) groups carried by **tetrahydrofolate (THF)**, the working form of the folate vitamin. Remember those two folate carbons.

| What building spends | ATP equivalents |
| --- | --- |
| Making the PRPP | 2 |
| Four steps that each turn ATP into ADP (adding glycine, adding the second glutamine nitrogen, closing the first ring, adding aspartate) | 4 |
| **IMP, total** | **6** |
| IMP → AMP (a GTP → GDP, +1) | 7 for AMP |
| IMP → GMP (an ATP → AMP, +2) | 8 for GMP |

### Door 2, recycle: salvage

Salvage attaches an existing base to PRPP in a *single* step, by **phosphoribosyltransferases**, which move the phosphoribose from PRPP onto the base. **HGPRT** (hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase) turns hypoxanthine into IMP and guanine into GMP; **APRT** (adenine phosphoribosyltransferase) turns adenine into AMP.

The only cost is the PRPP, 2 ATP equivalents, with no amino acids or folate: three to four times cheaper than building. In many tissues salvage is a major source, not a backup; how much a cell relies on it also depends on how fast it is growing.

### The gate: where the cell decides to build

PRPP itself can still go to salvage, to pyrimidines or to NAD⁺. The first step that sends it to purines *only* is **glutamine-PRPP amidotransferase**, which swaps PRPP's pyrophosphate for an amino group from glutamine. That makes it the **committed step**, and so the place for control.

- **Is there PRPP to spend?** PRPP is the gate's substrate and also switches it on. More PRPP, more building.
- **Are the shelves full?** IMP, AMP and GMP bind the enzyme away from its active site and switch it off (**feedback inhibition**). AMP and GMP together shut it down more than either alone.

**Predict.** A cell already has plenty of AMP and GMP, and it is handed free hypoxanthine. What happens to its rate of building?

It falls, for two reasons. Salvaging the hypoxanthine uses up PRPP, so the gate has less to spend, and it makes IMP, which adds to the feedback. Recycling does more than save ATP: it turns building down.

## The way out: uric acid

A purine that is not recycled is taken apart in three moves.

1. **Strip the phosphate and the amino group:** AMP and adenosine funnel through to inosine (losing adenine's amino group is what turns the adenosine into inosine, whose base is hypoxanthine).
2. **Remove the ribose:** inosine becomes the free base **hypoxanthine**, which salvage can still catch.
3. **Oxidize twice:** **xanthine oxidase** turns hypoxanthine into **xanthine**, and xanthine into **uric acid**. Guanine joins at xanthine, by losing its amino group.

Most mammals go further: their **uricase** oxidizes uric acid, the first of three steps to highly soluble allantoin. Humans and the other apes lost a working uricase during evolution, so for us sparingly soluble uric acid *is* the end product.

That missing enzyme is why humans are prone to **gout**, though who gets it depends mostly on how well the kidneys and gut excrete urate. Once serum urate (uric acid's ionized form, the one in blood) climbs past roughly 6.8 mg/dL, it is supersaturated and can slowly crystallize as monosodium urate in and around joints. A flare is the body's inflammatory attack on those needle-sharp crystals.

One fix is to make less urate. **Allopurinol**, a hypoxanthine look-alike, inhibits xanthine oxidase, so urate production falls and, over months, the crystals can dissolve. It came out of Gertrude Elion and George Hitchings's lab, but not as a gout drug: they first tested it as a xanthine oxidase blocker to slow the breakdown of their leukemia drug 6-mercaptopurine, and its urate-lowering effect led to its use in gout. Other drugs instead help the kidneys excrete more urate.

## When recycling breaks: Lesch-Nyhan syndrome

Back to the opening puzzle. In Lesch-Nyhan syndrome the HGPRT gene, on the X chromosome, does not work, so nearly all patients are boys.

## Pyrimidines: ring first

Pyrimidines (cytosine, uracil, thymine) mirror the purine build door: they build the **ring first**, from carbamoyl phosphate and aspartate, and take the ribose-phosphate from PRPP only afterward. The carbamoyl phosphate comes from **CPS-II**, not the urea cycle's **CPS-I**. CPS-I is mitochondrial, takes free ammonia and needs N-acetylglutamate; CPS-II is cytosolic, takes glutamine's nitrogen and is shut off by UTP, a finished pyrimidine. Different places and signals let nitrogen disposal and nucleotide building run independently.

The exit differs too: pyrimidines break down to soluble products (β-alanine, β-aminoisobutyrate, CO₂ and ammonia), so they have no gout.

## DNA's building blocks

DNA needs *deoxy*ribonucleotides, a branch off the pool outside the loop's balance. In a dividing cell most new dNTPs (deoxynucleoside triphosphates) come through one gateway, **ribonucleotide reductase**, which reduces ribonucleotides; cells can also salvage ready-made deoxynucleosides. Its activity is therefore tied tightly to cell division, and **hydroxyurea** inhibits it (used in sickle-cell disease and some leukemias).

Thymine needs one more step. **Thymidylate synthase** adds a carbon, carried by a folate, to dUMP (uracil's deoxynucleotide) to make thymidylate (**dTMP**), leaving the folate as dihydrofolate (DHF). **Dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR)** must turn DHF back into THF before it can carry another carbon, to thymidylate or to the purine ring.

## Counterfeits at each door

The drugs here run on Emil Fischer's old principle: a molecule's *shape* decides what an enzyme will accept. A counterfeit that fits the lock well enough to turn it, or jam it, is a medicine: an **antimetabolite**. The dividing cell is the most exposed, because it needs nucleotides fastest, though fast-dividing normal tissues are hit too.

- **Methotrexate** mimics folate and jams DHFR, so THF is not regenerated, starving both the purine ring's two folate carbons and the carbon that makes dTMP.
- **5-fluorouracil** mimics uracil. Inside the cell it becomes FdUMP, which traps thymidylate synthase itself, so it too starves the cell of the dTMP it needs to copy its DNA.
- **6-mercaptopurine** (6-MP), a fake purine, enters through the *recycle* door: HGPRT turns it into counterfeit nucleotides. These shut down purine synthesis and, once built into DNA, damage the leukemia cells that took it up.

The thread runs from Fischer naming the purine ring to Hitchings and Elion turning it into leukemia therapy, transplant drugs, the gout drug above and the antiviral acyclovir. Most aim at the doors, the exit or the DNA branch of one loop; acyclovir, a guanosine look-alike, instead jams the herpes virus's own DNA polymerase.

## Going deeper

### The fine print of the tally

PRPP to IMP is ten steps in vertebrates (eleven counting PRPP synthetase). The CO₂ step uses no ATP in vertebrates; bacteria spend one there. IMP dehydrogenase, on the way to GMP, uses an NAD⁺ and is the target of the transplant drug mycophenolate. That AMP needs GTP and GMP needs ATP helps balance the two pools.

### Variations on the loop

- An overactive **PRPP synthetase** pushes the gate open even with working HGPRT: urate overproduction and gout by the same logic.
- **Partial HGPRT deficiency** still causes urate overproduction, with gout and kidney stones, but milder or no neurological disease.
- In Lesch-Nyhan the established driver is the rise in PRPP. Weaker feedback from lower IMP and GMP may add to it, but that is secondary and debated.
- With working HGPRT, allopurinol tends to lower total purine output, not just urate, because the hypoxanthine it leaves behind is salvaged. In Lesch-Nyhan it only swaps urate for hypoxanthine and xanthine, with a risk of xanthine stones.

### Pyrimidine and dNTP control

Besides UTP feedback, CPS-II is switched on by PRPP and ATP. In mammals it sits in one large protein, CAD, with the next two steps; a later enzyme, DHODH, makes orotate. In orotic aciduria, an inherited block after orotate, orotate spills into the urine. Mammalian ribonucleotide reductase works on ribonucleoside *di*phosphates; one allosteric site sets its overall activity and another sets which substrate it reduces, keeping the four dNTPs balanced (mis-tuning is mutagenic).

### More on the drugs

- Inside cells methotrexate gains a chain of glutamates, and these forms also inhibit the purine formyl-adding enzymes directly, most strongly AICAR transformylase.
- **The leucovorin paradox.** Leucovorin, a ready-made reduced folate, bypasses DHFR and rescues normal cells from methotrexate. With 5-FU it does the opposite: it supplies the folate partner that locks FdUMP onto thymidylate synthase.
- TPMT (thiopurine methyltransferase) inactivates 6-MP, so people with low TPMT build up toxic amounts of its nucleotides. Xanthine oxidase also breaks 6-MP down, so giving allopurinol with 6-MP means cutting the 6-MP dose.
