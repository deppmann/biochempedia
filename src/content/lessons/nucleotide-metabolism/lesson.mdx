**A nucleotide is costly for a cell to build, so the cell recycles much of what it has.** Nucleotides are the letters of DNA and RNA, the carriers of energy (ATP, GTP), the cores of signaling molecules (cyclic AMP), and the backbones of key cofactors (NAD⁺, FAD, coenzyme A). Building their ring systems from scratch costs a small fortune in ATP. The result is a metabolism organized around thrift — and the places where that thrift fails, or where a drug imitates a building block, are where this chapter pays off in the clinic.

## Two ways to make a purine

There are two routes to a purine nucleotide, and the difference is mostly about cost.

**De novo synthesis** builds the purine ring atom by atom directly onto an activated ribose called **PRPP**. It runs about ten enzymatic steps and spends roughly six ATP to produce the first finished purine nucleotide, **IMP**, from which both AMP and GMP branch. The committed, regulated step is **glutamine-PRPP amidotransferase** — the cell's decision to spend.

**Salvage** does the opposite of expensive. It takes a free base that already exists — hypoxanthine, guanine, adenine — and reattaches it to PRPP in a *single* step. **HGPRT** salvages hypoxanthine and guanine; **APRT** salvages adenine. Recycling is so much cheaper that many cells recover a large share of their free purines rather than rebuilding them. In many tissues salvage is not a backup plan but a major source; how much a cell relies on it depends on the tissue and on how fast it is growing.

The cost difference is not academic. When the salvage enzyme HGPRT is missing — an X-linked defect called **Lesch-Nyhan syndrome** — two bad things happen at once. Free bases that should have been reclaimed are instead dumped into the catabolic pathway and turned into uric acid, and the PRPP that salvage would have consumed piles up and pushes de novo synthesis even higher, making still more purines to degrade. The consequence is severe hyperuricemia alongside profound neurological disease. Losing the recycling program is both wasteful and toxic.

## The human endgame: uric acid

When a purine is finally retired, it is broken down in a few steps: AMP and adenosine funnel through inosine to **hypoxanthine** (guanine joins the route one step later, at xanthine), and then the enzyme **xanthine oxidase** oxidizes hypoxanthine to **xanthine** and xanthine to **uric acid**. For most mammals that is not the end — they carry an enzyme called **uricase** that converts uric acid into highly soluble allantoin. Humans and the other apes lost a working uricase during evolution. So for us, uric acid *is* the end product, and it is only sparingly soluble.

That missing enzyme is why humans are prone to **gout**, though who actually gets it depends mostly on how well the kidneys and gut excrete urate. Once serum urate climbs past roughly 6.8 mg/dL, it is supersaturated and can slowly crystallize as monosodium urate in and around joints. A flare is the body's inflammatory attack on those needle-sharp crystals. One fix is to make less of it: **allopurinol**, a hypoxanthine look-alike, inhibits xanthine oxidase, so urate production falls and, over months, the crystals can dissolve. Other drugs instead help the kidneys excrete more urate. It came out of Gertrude Elion and George Hitchings's lab: they first tested it as a xanthine oxidase blocker to slow the breakdown of their leukemia drug 6-mercaptopurine, and its urate-lowering effect led to its use in gout.

## Pyrimidines: the ring comes first

Pyrimidine synthesis is worth one contrast. Where purines are assembled directly on the ribose-phosphate scaffold, pyrimidines build the **ring first** — from carbamoyl phosphate and aspartate — and only attach ribose-phosphate afterward. The carbamoyl phosphate here is made by **CPS-II**, a cytosolic enzyme that draws its nitrogen from glutamine, and it is deliberately a different enzyme from the mitochondrial CPS-I of the urea cycle. Keeping the two apart lets the cell dispose of nitrogen and build nucleotides without the two jobs interfering.

One more enzyme deserves a name. DNA needs *deoxy*ribonucleotides, and in a dividing cell most of them are made by reducing ribonucleotides through a single gateway, **ribonucleotide reductase**. Cells can also salvage ready-made deoxynucleosides. Because most new dNTPs for DNA pass through that one enzyme, its activity is tied tightly to cell division — which makes it, like the rest of this pathway, a target.

## Where the chemistry meets the clinic

Nucleotide metabolism is unusually rich in drugs, and they all run on Fischer's old principle: a molecule's *shape* decides what an enzyme will accept. Make a counterfeit that fits the lock well enough to turn it, or to jam it, and you have a medicine.

The dividing cell is the most exposed, because it needs nucleotides fastest. **Methotrexate** mimics folate and blocks dihydrofolate reductase, the enzyme that regenerates tetrahydrofolate, the one-carbon carrier that thymidylate synthase needs to make thymidylate (dTMP). **5-fluorouracil** mimics uracil; inside the cell it becomes FdUMP, which traps thymidylate synthase itself. Both starve a cell of the thymidylate it needs to copy its DNA. **6-mercaptopurine**, a fake purine, is picked up by the salvage enzyme HGPRT and turned into counterfeit nucleotides. These shut down purine synthesis and, once built into DNA, damage the leukemia cells that swallowed it.

These are the **antimetabolites**, and the thread runs straight from Emil Fischer naming the purine ring, to Hitchings and Elion turning that ring into leukemia therapy, transplant drugs, the gout drug above, and the antiviral acyclovir. Understand how the cell builds and recycles its nucleotides, and you understand why these drugs work — and why the anticancer ones hit the fastest-dividing cells hardest of all.
