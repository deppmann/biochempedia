techniques:
  - name: "Radioimmunoassay"
    blurb: >-
      Mix a fixed, limiting amount of antibody with a fixed amount of radiolabelled hormone and
      the sample. Unlabelled hormone in the sample displaces label from the antibody, so the
      bound radioactivity, read against standards of known concentration, gives the amount of
      hormone.
  - name: "SDS-PAGE, western blot and immunoprecipitation"
    blurb: >-
      Unfold proteins in SDS, separate them by size in a polyacrylamide gel, move them to a
      membrane and detect one with an antibody. With a phosphospecific antibody the blot reads
      one phosphorylated site. Immunoprecipitation uses an antibody on beads to pull a protein,
      and whatever is bound to it, out of an extract.
  - name: "Tandem mass spectrometry of phosphopeptides"
    blurb: >-
      Digest proteins with trypsin, weigh the peptides, then fragment each one and weigh the
      pieces. A phosphate adds about 80 daltons, and the fragments that carry the extra mass
      can place it on one residue, with no need to know the site beforehand.
  - name: "Analog-sensitive kinase"
    blurb: >-
      Replace the gatekeeper residue in a kinase's ATP pocket with glycine or alanine so that it
      accepts a bulky ATP analog that other kinases use poorly. Proteins labelled from that
      analog are candidates for direct substrates of the engineered kinase.

    choices:
      - "It falls, because unlabelled hormone takes the sites"
      - "It rises, because more hormone is there to be bound"
      - "It holds steady, because the antibody is always saturated"
      - "It doubles, because each antibody has two binding arms"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Hormone from the sample carries no label and competes with the labelled hormone for a
      limited number of antibody sites, so the more of it there is, the less label is bound.
      Bound radioactivity rising would be right only if the sample's own hormone were
      radioactive, and it is not. Holding steady confuses how full the sites are with which
      molecules fill them: the sites can stay full while label is replaced by unlabelled
      hormone. Doubling because an antibody has two arms mistakes the shape of the antibody for
      the readout, since the number of arms is the same in every tube.
  - stem: "Why is a cell extract heated in the detergent SDS before it is run on a polyacrylamide gel?"
    choices:
      - "So that each protein unfolds and runs by its size"
      - "So that each protein is cut into short peptides"
      - "So that each protein gains a phosphate at one site"
      - "So that each protein stays bound to its partners"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      SDS unfolds proteins and coats them with negative charge roughly in proportion to length,
      so they separate by size. Staying bound to partners is the opposite of what SDS does, and
      it is the reason immunoprecipitation uses an extract that has not been heated in SDS.
      Cutting into peptides is the job of trypsin before mass spectrometry, not of a detergent.
      Gaining a phosphate needs a kinase and ATP; SDS adds charge by sticking to the protein,
      not by modifying it.
  - stem: "A blot with a phosphospecific antibody against one serine of Akt shows a darker band after insulin. Which added experiment separates more phosphate per Akt from more Akt?"
    choices:
      - "Run the same samples for longer on a larger gel"
      - "Heat the same samples in SDS for twice as long"
      - "Blot the same samples with twice as much of the antibody"
      - "Blot the same samples with an antibody to total Akt"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      An antibody to total Akt binds the protein with or without the phosphate, so it shows
      whether the amount of Akt changed; if it did not, the darker phosphospecific band means
      more phosphate. Using twice as much of the phosphospecific antibody darkens every lane and
      still cannot tell the two explanations apart. A longer run on a larger gel spreads the
      bands but measures neither quantity. Heating longer in SDS changes nothing about how much
      Akt or phosphate is present.
  - stem: "An analog-sensitive version of a kinase is added to a cell extract with an ATP analog that has a benzyl group on its adenine and a radioactive gamma phosphate. Why are only the direct substrates of that kinase labelled?"
    choices:
      - "The analog blocks the pockets of all other kinases"
      - "The mutant kinase works faster than normal kinases"
      - "The enlarged pocket alone accepts the bulky analog"
      - "The benzyl group binds to the substrates directly"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The benzyl group makes the analog a poor fit for a normal ATP pocket, and only the
      engineered kinase, whose gatekeeper has been made small, can use it, so any labelled
      protein took its phosphate from that kinase. The analog does not block other kinases: it
      fits them poorly, and they carry on using ordinary ATP without becoming radioactive. Speed
      is not the point, and a faster kinase using ordinary ATP would still be lost among hundreds
      of others. The benzyl group is on the adenine, which is not transferred; only the
      gamma phosphate ends up on the substrate.
  - stem: "A compound inhibits kinase X and, at the dose used, at least ten other kinases. In cells, that dose abolishes the activity of X, yet the cells respond to the hormone as before. What follows?"
    choices:
      - "The activity of X is sufficient for the response"
      - "The activity of X is not needed for the response"
      - "The activity of X is needed, with ten other kinases"
      - "The activity of X cannot be judged with this compound"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The response survived with X shut off, so the activity of X is not needed; an unselective
      inhibitor can still exclude. Sufficiency would be shown by turning X on alone and seeing
      the response without hormone, which was not done. Saying X is needed along with ten other

**Fasting blood holds insulin at a few tens of picomolar: about one insulin molecule for every trillion molecules of water, with the blood protein albumin outnumbering it roughly ten million to one.** Every pathway in this course rests on someone measuring a thing that scarce, or picking one modified protein out of the thousands in a cell. This lesson is about how that is done and how much each kind of result is allowed to prove.

## Measuring almost nothing: let the hormone compete with a labelled copy

The method grew out of an unexpected by-product of a different experiment. In the mid-1950s Rosalyn Yalow, a physicist, and Solomon Berson, a physician, worked at the Veterans Administration hospital in the Bronx. They were testing the proposal that people with adult-onset diabetes destroy insulin too fast. They tagged insulin with radioactive iodine, injected it, and followed the radioactivity as it left the blood.

It left more slowly, but only in one group: people who had already been treated with insulin. When their serum was sorted by paper electrophoresis, the radioactivity travelled with a globulin, the class of serum protein that includes antibodies. Something in the blood of treated patients was holding the injected insulin. Immunologists of the day doubted that a protein as small as insulin, 51 amino acids, could raise antibodies. *Science* rejected the paper. The *Journal of Clinical Investigation* rejected it at first, then printed it in 1956 with the word "antibody" removed from the title.

Yalow and Berson then used the unwelcome antibody as a scale. Put a fixed, small amount of antibody in a tube with a fixed, small amount of radioactive insulin, and add the blood sample. The sample's own insulin carries no label, and it competes with the labelled insulin for the same few binding sites. The more insulin the patient had, the less radioactivity ends up on the antibody. Separate bound from free, count each, and read the concentration from a curve built with known amounts.

This is the radioimmunoassay. Depending on the antibody and the tracer, it can measure a hormone from nanomolar down to a fraction of a picomolar, and the binding obeys the rules in [binding and dose-response](/lessons/binding-and-dose-response). Its selectivity is the antibody's: Yalow pointed out in her Nobel lecture that an antibody can tell cortisol from corticosterone, two steroids that differ by one hydroxyl group. The bioassays in [finding a signal](/lessons/finding-a-signal) measured what a hormone does. This one counts how much hormone the antibody recognises, which is usually, but not always, the same as how much active hormone is there.

## Seeing one protein among thousands: sort by size, then ask an antibody

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

A cell extract holds thousands of different proteins. Prof. Deppmann asks his students to describe three methods that follow one of them.

**SDS-PAGE** sorts. Heat the extract in SDS, a detergent that unfolds each protein and coats it with negative charge roughly in proportion to its length. Pull the mixture through a polyacrylamide gel with an electric field, and small proteins travel farther than large ones.

**The western blot** finds. Move the sorted proteins from the gel onto a membrane, then soak the membrane in an antibody against your protein. A second antibody, which carries an enzyme that makes light or colour, marks where the first one stuck. You see a band at one size.

**Immunoprecipitation** fishes. Here the extract is not heated. An antibody fixed to beads pulls its protein out of the mixture, and partners bound to that protein come out with it. Blot the catch for a suspected partner and you learn that the two were bound together in the extract, most likely as one complex. A tube with an unrelated antibody is the control that shows the partner did not stick to the beads on its own.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/detecting-a-signal/immunoprecipitation-fishing.webp" width={1200} height={623} alt="AI-generated illustration: three steps. Beads coated with Y-shaped antibodies drop into a tube of mixed proteins; an antibody catches the gold protein with its partner still attached; the bead is lifted out carrying both while the other proteins stay behind." caption="Immunoprecipitation uses an extract that has not been heated. An antibody fixed to beads pulls its protein out of the mixture, and a partner bound to that protein comes out with it." />

Among what he calls "other ways to detect phosphorylation events" is the tool behind much of this course's evidence: the phosphospecific antibody. It is raised against a short piece of a protein with a phosphate on one residue, and chosen so that it binds only when the phosphate is present. A blot with it reads one site, from cells, minutes after a signal arrives. Blot the same samples with an antibody to the whole protein as well, or you cannot tell more phosphate from more protein.

An antibody has limits. It answers the question you built it for, so it cannot find a site nobody suspected. It reports that a band got darker, not what fraction of the molecules carry the phosphate. It can bind the wrong protein, which is why a careful blot includes a sample in which the protein or the phosphate is missing. And a blot averages millions of cells; watching one living cell takes the reporters of the next lesson.

## "How do we determine where the protein is modified?" Weigh the pieces

Prediction tools such as Scansite search a sequence for the patterns that known kinases prefer, and databases such as UniProt list sites that have been reported. They are a start. A predicted site is a guess until someone finds the phosphate on it.

Mass spectrometry finds it by weighing. Cut the protein into peptides with trypsin, an enzyme that cuts after lysine and arginine, usually not when proline follows. The instrument weighs each peptide (strictly, it measures mass divided by charge) closely enough that one phosphate shows.

> **mass of a phosphopeptide = mass of the peptide + about 80 daltons for each phosphate**

In tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) the instrument then selects one peptide, breaks it along its backbone and weighs the fragments. The fragments spell out the sequence, and the ones carrying the extra 80 daltons show which residue holds the phosphate, provided enough fragments break between the candidate residues. Sometimes they do not, and the site can only be narrowed to two or three residues. No antibody is needed and no site has to be known in advance. Once the rare phosphopeptides have been enriched, one experiment on a whole extract can report thousands of sites. A site that is not reported may still exist, because some peptides are never detected.

### A worked case: BATF

Prof. Deppmann's 2003 paper in the *Biochemical Journal*, with Tina Thornton, Fransiscus Utama and Elizabeth Taparowsky, asks these questions of the transcription factor BATF. BATF from cells ran on a gel as shifted bands that calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase removed, which showed phosphorylation in vivo. A gel run with reversed polarity showed that serine 43 and threonine 48, both in the DNA-binding domain, carry phosphate. The paper reports phosphate on several serines and threonines and on at least one tyrosine; phosphoamino acid analysis on a thin-layer plate, which shows what kind of amino acid carries the phosphate, and kinase assays were among its methods. Then serine 43 was changed to aspartate, whose negative charge partly imitates a phosphate, to model what the modification does. Such phosphomimetic mutants are imperfect: aspartate carries less charge than a phosphate and has a different shape, so a mutant that does nothing is weak evidence. Each method is explained in [the phosphorylation switch](/lessons/phosphorylation-switch).

## Which kinase? Give one kinase an ATP of its own

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

"What if you have a kinase but don’t know the substrates?" The approaches he lists each have a weakness. A purified kinase in a test tube shows what it can phosphorylate, which is more than it does in a cell. Prediction from sequence gives candidates. Finding the kinase bound to a protein shows contact, which is not catalysis. Removing the kinase with an inhibitor, RNA interference or a knockout changes many proteins, and the direct substrates look the same as those three steps downstream.

Kevan Shokat's laboratory found a way through, which Prof. Deppmann introduces as "organic chemistry meets protein manipulation". Kinases share an ATP pocket, and one bulky residue in it, the gatekeeper, limits what fits. Replace the gatekeeper with glycine or alanine and the pocket gains a hole. An ATP with a bulky group added to its adenine fits the enlarged pocket and fits normal kinases poorly. Such a kinase is called analog-sensitive. The laboratory introduced the approach in 1997 with the tyrosine kinase v-Src.

### Design problem

The human genome encodes about five hundred protein kinases, a cell expresses hundreds of them, and you want the direct substrates of one. Say why adding radioactive ATP to a cell extract cannot give the answer. Then design a version of your kinase that can use a form of ATP that the other kinases cannot, and say what control proves that the labelled proteins are its direct substrates.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Every kinase in the extract uses ordinary ATP, so radioactive ATP labels the substrates of every active kinase at once. Nothing marks which kinase put each phosphate on.

Find the gatekeeper of your kinase by lining its sequence up with kinases whose pockets are known, and change it to glycine. Check that the mutant still phosphorylates a known substrate. Make an ATP analog with a benzyl group on the N6 nitrogen of adenine and a radioactive gamma phosphate, the one that is transferred. Add the analog to an extract that contains the mutant kinase. Only the mutant uses it well, so a labelled protein received its phosphate from your kinase in one step.

The control is the same extract with the normal kinase in place of the mutant. It should give no labelled bands. A band that appears there comes from a kinase that can use the analog unaided, and is discarded. Each candidate still has to be confirmed in living cells. The enlarged pocket helps there too: a bulky inhibitor built for it shuts off the mutant kinase and spares the normal ones.

## What an inhibitor proves, and what "necessary" and "sufficient" mean

"How do we determine what effect a phosphorylation event is having on a protein?" Prof. Deppmann's answer has two parts: mutate the site to imitate the gain or the loss of the phosphate, and inhibit or activate the kinase. The second part has misled many laboratories.

Most kinase inhibitors work by plugging the ATP pocket, and about five hundred kinases share that pocket. Staurosporine, a natural product, binds most of the kinases it has been tested against. In 2000 Philip Cohen's group in Dundee took compounds sold as selective inhibitors of named kinases and tested each against a panel of purified kinases. Apart from rapamycin and PD 184352, even the most selective inhibited at least one other kinase. Jenny Bain led further rounds in 2003 and 2007. The 2007 paper judged many widely used compounds too non-specific for any conclusion except one: ruling a kinase out. In 2008 Mazen Karaman, Patrick Zarrinkar and colleagues at the company Ambit Biosciences measured thirty-eight inhibitors against 317 kinases and concluded that "small assay panels do not provide a robust measure of selectivity."

So an inhibitor result has a direction. If a dose that shuts off kinase X leaves the response intact, the activity of X is not needed. If the response stops, X or any other target of the compound may be the cause. To pin it on X, use two inhibitors of unlike structure, which are unlikely to share their other targets. Better, express a mutant of X that the drug cannot bind: if the response returns while every other target stays blocked, the drug was acting through X. Then add genetics, a knockout or a knockdown, which goes wrong in different ways.

With normal kinase X, the drug sits on X and on another target and the response stops. With a drug-resistant mutant of X, the drug cannot bind X, the other target stays blocked, and the response returns." caption="A mutant of X that the drug cannot bind tests the drug's effect. Two words organise all of this. A step is **necessary** if removing it removes the response: the knockout, the controlled inhibitor, the site changed to alanine. A step is **sufficient** if turning it on by itself produces the response without the signal: a kinase that is always active, or, more weakly, a site changed to aspartate, which imitates the phosphate only in part. A step can be one without being the other. Every method has its own way of misleading you, so a claim earns belief when two methods that fail differently agree.
