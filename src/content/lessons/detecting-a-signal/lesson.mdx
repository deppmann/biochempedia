techniques:
  - name: "Radioimmunoassay"
    blurb: >-
      Mix a fixed, limiting amount of antibody with a fixed amount of radiolabelled hormone and
      the sample. Unlabelled hormone in the sample displaces label from the antibody, so the
      bound radioactivity, read against standards of known concentration, gives the amount of
      hormone.
  - name: "SDS-PAGE, western blot and immunoprecipitation"
    blurb: >-
      Unfold proteins in SDS, separate them by size in a polyacrylamide gel, move them to a
      membrane and detect one with an antibody. With a phosphospecific antibody the blot reads
      one phosphorylated site. Immunoprecipitation uses an antibody on beads to pull a protein,
      and whatever is bound to it, out of an extract.
  - name: "Tandem mass spectrometry of phosphopeptides"
    blurb: >-
      Digest proteins with trypsin, weigh the peptides, then fragment each one and weigh the
      pieces. A phosphate adds about 80 daltons, and the fragments that carry the extra mass
      can place it on one residue, with no need to know the site beforehand.
  - name: "Analog-sensitive kinase"
    blurb: >-
      Replace the gatekeeper residue in a kinase's ATP pocket with glycine or alanine so that it
      accepts a bulky ATP analog that other kinases use poorly. Proteins labelled from that
      analog are candidates for direct substrates of the engineered kinase.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "In a radioimmunoassay, every tube holds the same amount of antibody and the same amount of radioactive hormone. What happens to the radioactivity bound to the antibody as the hormone in the sample increases?"
    choices:
      - "It falls, because unlabelled hormone takes the sites"
      - "It rises, because more hormone is there to be bound"
      - "It holds steady, because the antibody is always saturated"
      - "It doubles, because each antibody has two binding arms"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Hormone from the sample carries no label and competes with the labelled hormone for a
      limited number of antibody sites, so the more of it there is, the less label is bound.
      Bound radioactivity rising would be right only if the sample's own hormone were
      radioactive, and it is not. Holding steady confuses how full the sites are with which
      molecules fill them: the sites can stay full while label is replaced by unlabelled
      hormone. Doubling because an antibody has two arms mistakes the shape of the antibody for
      the readout, since the number of arms is the same in every tube.
  - stem: "Why is a cell extract heated in the detergent SDS before it is run on a polyacrylamide gel?"
    choices:
      - "So that each protein unfolds and runs by its size"
      - "So that each protein is cut into short peptides"
      - "So that each protein gains a phosphate at one site"
      - "So that each protein stays bound to its partners"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      SDS unfolds proteins and coats them with negative charge roughly in proportion to length,
      so they separate by size. Staying bound to partners is the opposite of what SDS does, and
      it is the reason immunoprecipitation uses an extract that has not been heated in SDS.
      Cutting into peptides is the job of trypsin before mass spectrometry, not of a detergent.
      Gaining a phosphate needs a kinase and ATP; SDS adds charge by sticking to the protein,
      not by modifying it.
  - stem: "A blot with a phosphospecific antibody against one serine of Akt shows a darker band after insulin. Which added experiment separates more phosphate per Akt from more Akt?"
    choices:
      - "Run the same samples for longer on a larger gel"
      - "Heat the same samples in SDS for twice as long"
      - "Blot the same samples with twice as much of the antibody"
      - "Blot the same samples with an antibody to total Akt"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      An antibody to total Akt binds the protein with or without the phosphate, so it shows
      whether the amount of Akt changed; if it did not, the darker phosphospecific band means
      more phosphate. Using twice as much of the phosphospecific antibody darkens every lane and
      still cannot tell the two explanations apart. A longer run on a larger gel spreads the
      bands but measures neither quantity. Heating longer in SDS changes nothing about how much
      Akt or phosphate is present.
  - stem: "An analog-sensitive version of a kinase is added to a cell extract with an ATP analog that has a benzyl group on its adenine and a radioactive gamma phosphate. Why are only the direct substrates of that kinase labelled?"
    choices:
      - "The analog blocks the pockets of all other kinases"
      - "The mutant kinase works faster than normal kinases"
      - "The enlarged pocket alone accepts the bulky analog"
      - "The benzyl group binds to the substrates directly"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The benzyl group makes the analog a poor fit for a normal ATP pocket, and only the
      engineered kinase, whose gatekeeper has been made small, can use it, so any labelled
      protein took its phosphate from that kinase. The analog does not block other kinases: it
      fits them poorly, and they carry on using ordinary ATP without becoming radioactive. Speed
      is not the point, and a faster kinase using ordinary ATP would still be lost among hundreds
      of others. The benzyl group is on the adenine, which is not transferred; only the
      gamma phosphate ends up on the substrate.
  - stem: "A compound inhibits kinase X and, at the dose used, at least ten other kinases. In cells, that dose abolishes the activity of X, yet the cells respond to the hormone as before. What follows?"
    choices:
      - "The activity of X is sufficient for the response"
      - "The activity of X is not needed for the response"
      - "The activity of X is needed, with ten other kinases"
      - "The activity of X cannot be judged with this compound"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The response survived with X shut off, so the activity of X is not needed; an unselective
      inhibitor can still exclude. Sufficiency would be shown by turning X on alone and seeing
      the response without hormone, which was not done. Saying X is needed along with ten other
      kinases reads the result backwards, because nothing was lost. Saying X cannot be judged
      is the right caution when an unselective compound blocks a response, but here the
      response was not blocked, and that outcome is the one such a compound can settle.

With no unlabelled insulin, 5,000 counts per minute are bound to the antibody. Standards of 20, 60 and 180 pM unlabelled insulin leave 4,000, 2,500 and 1,250 counts per minute bound. A patient's plasma is mixed with an equal volume of buffer, and this diluted sample leaves 2,500 counts per minute bound. What is the insulin concentration in the undiluted plasma?"
    choices:
      - "180 pM"
      - "30 pM"
      - "60 pM"
      - "120 pM"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The diluted sample leaves 2,500 counts bound, the same as the 60 pM standard, so the
      diluted sample holds 60 pM. Mixing with an equal volume of buffer halved the
      concentration, so the plasma held 120 pM. Answering 60 pM reads the curve correctly but
      forgets the dilution. Answering 30 pM applies the dilution in the wrong direction,
      halving where it should double. Answering 180 pM comes from halving the counts to 1,250
      and reading that standard, which treats counts and concentration as if they moved
      together when they move in opposite directions.
  - stem: "A protein is isolated from resting and from stimulated cells and digested with trypsin. One peptide, which contains two serines and no threonine or tyrosine, weighs 1,500.7 Da from resting cells and 1,580.7 Da from stimulated cells. When the heavier peptide is fragmented, every fragment that includes the second serine is about 80 Da heavier than its sequence predicts, and fragments that include only the first serine are not. Which conclusion do the data support?"
    choices:
      - "One phosphate, located on the first serine"
      - "Two phosphates, one located on each serine"
      - "One phosphate, located on the second serine"
      - "No phosphate, since trypsin cut at a new site"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The peptide gained 80 Da, the mass of one phosphate, and the extra mass travels only with
      fragments that contain the second serine, so that serine carries it. A phosphate on the

      would not follow one serine from fragment to fragment.

      - "Evidence that K is sufficient to phosphorylate S"
      - "Evidence that compound C acts on S by inhibiting K"
      - "Evidence that the eleven kinases all lie upstream of K"
      - "Evidence that compound C cannot enter these cells"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      In the mutant cells the eleven other kinases are still inhibited, yet S is phosphorylated
      again, so the block seen with normal K came from inhibiting K and not from the other

**Fasting blood holds insulin at a few tens of picomolar: about one insulin molecule for every trillion molecules of water, with albumin outnumbering it roughly ten million to one.** Every pathway in this course rests on measuring signals that scarce, picking one modified protein from thousands, and proving which enzyme acted. The first film animated the three detection strategies; here are the controls and limits behind each, then how you prove cause.

<Support>**The one idea.** Competition, antibodies, and mass spectrometry detect scarce signals, each with blind spots; causal claims earn belief when two methods that fail differently agree. **Scope:** Applies to bulk biochemical, mass-spectrometric, chemical-genetic and pharmacological assays; single-cell reporters come next, and agreeing methods still need controls and leave unexamined steps open.</Support>

## Measuring almost nothing: let the hormone compete with a labelled copy

A **radioimmunoassay** measures a scarce hormone by letting unlabelled sample hormone compete with a fixed radiolabelled tracer for a fixed, limiting amount of antibody, separating bound from free label and reading the concentration off known standards. The antibody sites must be limiting, fewer than the labelled hormone molecules, or every labelled molecule would find a site and a little sample hormone would barely change the count.

The first film showed the high-affinity saturation limit, where bound label tracks tracer divided by total hormone. Because affinity and concentration rarely keep sites completely full across the whole range, real assays always read unknowns against an empirical standard curve; binding obeys [binding and dose-response](/lessons/binding-and-dose-response).

Rosalyn Yalow and Solomon Berson built this assay from an insulin-binding serum globulin found by accident in the mid-1950s at the Bronx Veterans Administration hospital (Going deeper). Depending on antibody and tracer, it reaches from nanomolar to a fraction of a picomolar. Selectivity is the antibody's: Yalow noted in her Nobel lecture that an antibody distinguishes cortisol from corticosterone, two steroids differing by one hydroxyl group.

**Predict.** A blood sample holds an inactive hormone fragment that keeps the short surface the antibody recognises. Will a radioimmunoassay detect it, and will it match a bioassay?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes, the fragment competes for antibody sites and lowers bound radioactivity. Bioassays ([finding a signal](/lessons/finding-a-signal)) measure what a hormone *does*; a radioimmunoassay counts what the antibody *recognises*, which is usually, but not always, active hormone.

</details>

## Seeing one protein among thousands: sort by size, then ask an antibody

The first film animated size sorting and blotting; three methods follow one protein in a cell extract, and they treat protein structure in opposite ways.

**SDS-PAGE** (sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis) sorts: heating the extract in SDS unfolds each protein and coats it with negative charge roughly in proportion to length, so an electric field pulls smaller proteins farther through the gel.

**The western blot** finds: transfer the sorted bands to a membrane, add an antibody to your protein, then a second enzyme-linked antibody that makes light or colour, giving a band at one size.

**Immunoprecipitation** fishes from an unheated, native extract: an antibody on beads pulls out its target together with bound partners. Blotting the catch for a partner shows the two were associated in the extract, most likely as one complex; a parallel tube with an unrelated antibody rules out sticking to the beads.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/detecting-a-signal/immunoprecipitation-fishing.webp" width={1200} height={623} alt="AI-generated illustration: three steps. Beads coated with Y-shaped antibodies drop into a tube of mixed proteins; an antibody catches the gold protein with its partner still attached; the bead is lifted out carrying both while the other proteins stay behind." caption="Immunoprecipitation captures a native protein and its bound partners on antibody beads, so the extract cannot be heated in SDS." />

**Predict.** To test whether protein A binds protein B by immunoprecipitating A, what happens if you heat the extract in SDS first?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

SDS and heat unfold the proteins and break non-covalent interactions, separating A from B before the beads touch them. Protein B stays behind, so the interaction is missed.

</details>

A **phosphospecific antibody**, raised against a short phosphorylated peptide and chosen to bind only when that phosphate is present, reads one known site minutes after a signal arrives. Always blot the same samples for total protein too, or you cannot tell more phosphate per molecule from more protein.

Antibody blots have four limits: they cannot discover an unsuspected site; a darker band does not tell you what fraction of molecules carry phosphate; antibodies can bind the wrong protein (requiring a sample lacking the protein or phosphate as a control); and blots average millions of cells, whereas single living cells need the reporters of the next lesson.

## Where is the protein modified? Weigh the pieces

Finding an unknown site takes a method that needs no antibody. Prediction tools such as Scansite scan sequences for kinase motifs and UniProt lists reported sites, yet a predicted site stays a guess until the phosphate is physically detected.

**Mass spectrometry** cuts the protein into peptides with trypsin (after lysine and arginine, usually not before proline) and weighs each peptide (strictly mass divided by charge) closely enough that one phosphate stands out:

> **mass of a phosphopeptide = mass of the peptide + about 80 daltons for each phosphate**

In **tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS)** the instrument selects one peptide, breaks its backbone and weighs the fragments. Fragments spell out the sequence, and those carrying the extra 80 daltons pinpoint the residue—provided cuts fall between candidate residues; otherwise the site narrows only to two or three residues. After enriching rare phosphopeptides, one run on a whole extract can report thousands of sites with no prior guess.

**Predict.** A whole-extract MS/MS scan reports two thousand phosphorylation sites, but a suspected site on your protein is missing. Does that prove the site is unphosphorylated?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Some peptides are never detected because of cleavage, enrichment, ionisation or fragmentation, so an unreported site may still exist.

</details>

Locating a phosphate shows what changed, not which of the cell's roughly five hundred kinases put it there or whether that step causes the response.

## Which kinase? Give one kinase an ATP of its own

The second film showed why ordinary radioactive ATP and a knockout fail to isolate direct substrates; removing the kinase with an inhibitor or RNA interference fails the same way. Sequence prediction gives only candidates.

**Predict.** A purified kinase phosphorylates protein Y in a test tube, and the same kinase comes down with protein Z when it is immunoprecipitated from cells. Why does neither prove a direct substrate in cells?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A test tube shows what a kinase *can* phosphorylate, which is more than it does in a cell: concentrations are high and the cell's scaffolds and compartments are gone. Finding the kinase bound to a protein shows contact, which is not catalysis.

</details>

Kevan Shokat's laboratory solved this where organic chemistry meets protein engineering, introducing the method in 1997 with the tyrosine kinase v-Src. In the shared ATP pocket, a bulky **gatekeeper** residue limits what fits; replacing it with glycine or alanine opens a hole, making an **analog-sensitive** kinase. An ATP analog with a bulky benzyl group on its adenine fits this enlarged pocket and fits normal kinases poorly. Because the bulky group stays on adenine and only the radioactive gamma phosphate transfers, labelled proteins are candidates for direct substrates of that kinase.

## What an inhibitor proves, and what "necessary" and "sufficient" mean

Testing what phosphorylation *does* has two parts: mutate the site to mimic loss or gain of phosphate, and inhibit or activate the kinase. The second part has misled many laboratories.

Most kinase inhibitors plug the ATP pocket shared by about five hundred kinases. The natural product staurosporine binds most kinases tested against it. In 2000 Philip Cohen's group in Dundee tested "selective" inhibitors against purified kinase panels; apart from rapamycin and PD 184352, even the best hit at least one other kinase. Larger surveys followed (Going deeper), and the 2007 paper judged many widely used compounds too non-specific for any conclusion except ruling a kinase out.

As the second film showed, an inhibitor's logic is one-sided: if shutting off kinase X leaves the response intact, X is not needed; if the response stops, X or any off-target may be responsible.

**Predict.** Two structurally unrelated inhibitors both block kinase X in vitro and both stop a hormone response. Why is that stronger than doubling one inhibitor's dose, and what could still mislead you?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Doubling one dose only widens its off-target list, whereas unlike structures rarely share the same off-targets. Still, shared off-targets remain possible, so a drug-resistant mutant of X—plus independent genetics—gives a cleaner test.

</details>

Expressing a drug-resistant mutant of X sharpens the proof: if the response returns while other targets stay blocked, the drug acted through X. Adding genetics—a knockout or knockdown—tests the claim with a method that fails differently.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/detecting-a-signal/drug-resistant-mutant-rescue.webp" width={1200} height={796} alt="AI-generated illustration: two panels with the same signal and drug. With normal kinase X, the drug sits on X and on another target and the response stops. With a drug-resistant mutant of X, the drug cannot bind X, the other target stays blocked, and the response returns. Arrows are schematic and do not establish pathway order." caption="If a drug-resistant mutant of kinase X restores the response while other targets stay blocked, the drug acted through X—though rescue alone does not order other targets or prove a direct link." />

Two words organise these tests. A step is **necessary** if removing it removes the response: a knockout, controlled inhibitor, or mutating the site to **alanine** (no hydroxyl to accept phosphate). A step is **sufficient** if turning it on alone triggers the response without the signal: an always-active kinase, or—more weakly—mutating the site to **aspartate**, which mimics phosphate only in part. A step can be one without the other.

| Test | Outcome | Proves | Does *not* prove |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Unselective inhibitor (X + off-targets) | Response intact | X is not necessary (rules X out) | Anything about kinases the dose left active |
| Unselective inhibitor (X + off-targets) | Response stops | Ambiguous (X or off-target) | That X caused the block |
| Inhibitor + drug-resistant X | Response returns | Drug acted through X (necessary) | Pathway order, direct link or sufficiency |
| Site mutated to alanine | Response stops | Site phosphorylation is necessary | That the site alone is sufficient |
| Always-active kinase (no signal) | Response occurs | Kinase activity is sufficient | That X is necessary for the signal |
| Site mutated to aspartate (no signal) | Active (or silent) | Weak evidence of sufficiency if active | Negative result is weak (partial mimic) |

Keep the rule in view: each detection method has its own blind spots, and causal claims earn belief when two methods that fail differently agree.

### Design problem

The human genome encodes about five hundred protein kinases, a cell expresses hundreds of them, and you want the direct substrates of one. Say why adding radioactive ATP to a cell extract cannot give the answer. Then design a version of your kinase that can use a form of ATP that the other kinases cannot, and say what control proves that the labelled proteins are its direct substrates.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Every kinase in the extract uses ordinary ATP, so radioactive ATP labels the substrates of every active kinase at once. Nothing marks which kinase put each phosphate on.

Find the gatekeeper of your kinase by lining its sequence up with kinases whose pockets are known, and change it to glycine. Check that the mutant still phosphorylates a known substrate. Make an ATP analog with a benzyl group on the N6 nitrogen of adenine and a radioactive gamma phosphate, the one that is transferred. Add the analog to an extract that contains the mutant kinase. Only the mutant uses it well, so a labelled protein received its phosphate from your kinase in one step.

The control is the same extract with the normal kinase in place of the mutant. It should give no labelled bands. A band that appears there comes from a kinase that can use the analog unaided, and is discarded. Each candidate still has to be confirmed in living cells. The enlarged pocket helps there too: a bulky inhibitor built for it shuts off the mutant kinase and spares the normal ones.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How the insulin antibody was found

In the mid-1950s Rosalyn Yalow, a physicist, and Solomon Berson, a physician, worked at the Veterans Administration hospital in the Bronx. They were testing the proposal that people with adult-onset diabetes destroy insulin too fast. They tagged insulin with radioactive iodine, injected it, and followed the radioactivity as it left the blood.

It left more slowly, but only in one group: people who had already been treated with insulin. When their serum was sorted by paper electrophoresis, the radioactivity travelled with a globulin, the class of serum protein that includes antibodies. Something in the blood of treated patients was holding the injected insulin. Immunologists of the day doubted that a protein as small as insulin, 51 amino acids, could raise antibodies. *Science* rejected the paper. The *Journal of Clinical Investigation* rejected it at first, then printed it in 1956 with the word "antibody" removed from the title.

### A worked case: BATF

A 2003 paper in the *Biochemical Journal* by Christopher Deppmann, Tina Thornton, Fransiscus Utama and Elizabeth Taparowsky asks where a protein is modified, and what the modification does, of the transcription factor BATF. BATF from cells ran on a gel as shifted bands that calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase removed, which showed phosphorylation in vivo. A gel run with reversed polarity showed that serine 43 and threonine 48, both in the DNA-binding domain, carry phosphate. The paper reports phosphate on several serines and threonines and on at least one tyrosine; phosphoamino acid analysis on a thin-layer plate, which shows what kind of amino acid carries the phosphate, and kinase assays were among its methods. Then serine 43 was changed to aspartate, whose negative charge partly imitates a phosphate, to model what the modification does. Such phosphomimetic mutants are imperfect: aspartate carries less charge than a phosphate and has a different shape, so a mutant that does nothing is weak evidence. Each method is explained in [the phosphorylation switch](/lessons/phosphorylation-switch).

### The inhibitor surveys

In 2000 Philip Cohen's group in Dundee took compounds sold as selective inhibitors of named kinases and tested each against a panel of purified kinases. Apart from rapamycin and PD 184352, even the most selective inhibited at least one other kinase. Jenny Bain led further rounds in 2003 and 2007. In 2008 Mazen Karaman, Patrick Zarrinkar and colleagues at the company Ambit Biosciences measured thirty-eight inhibitors against 317 kinases and concluded that "small assay panels do not provide a robust measure of selectivity."
