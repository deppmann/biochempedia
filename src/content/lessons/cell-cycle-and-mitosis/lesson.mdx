## Explain: the cell cycle

<WordBank terms={['cell cycle', 'interphase', 'mitosis', 'cytokinesis', 'chromosome', 'sister chromatids', 'centromere', 'spindle fibers', 'prophase', 'metaphase', 'anaphase', 'telophase']} />

The **cell cycle** is the series of stages a cell goes through from the time it forms until it divides into two cells. In plants, animals, fungi and other eukaryotes, a typical dividing cell passes through four main phases: G1, S, G2 and M. Some cells stop dividing and leave the cycle.

**Interphase** is the part of the cycle between divisions, made of G1, S and G2. It is not a rest.

- **G1:** the new cell grows, makes proteins and does its everyday work.
- **S:** the cell copies its DNA. "S" stands for synthesis, which means making.
- **G2:** the cell makes the proteins it will need to move its chromosomes, and gets ready to divide.

DNA is stored in **chromosomes**, long strands of DNA wrapped around proteins. A typical human body cell with a nucleus has 46 chromosomes. Red blood cells lose their nucleus as they mature. After S phase, each chromosome is two identical copies joined together.

Why copy first? Picture two groups that each need the whole recipe book. Tearing one book in half gives each group half the recipes; copying it first gives each a full book. The comparison breaks because a cell does not decide anything; proteins copy and share the DNA.

M phase is division itself, in two parts. **Mitosis** is the division of the nucleus into two nuclei, each with a full set of chromosomes. **Cytokinesis** is the division of the cytoplasm, which splits one cell into two. Usually cytokinesis begins as mitosis is ending, and the result is two new cells with the same DNA as each other and as the cell they came from.

When the controls on the cycle fail, cells can keep dividing when they should not. That uncontrolled division is a key feature of cancer.

## Explain: the stages of mitosis

After S phase, a copied chromosome is made of two **sister chromatids**, the two identical copies of one chromosome. They are held together at the **centromere**, a narrow region of the chromosome. **Spindle fibers** are thin protein fibers that attach to the chromosomes and move them.

| Stage | What the chromosomes do |
|---|---|
| **Prophase** | The chromosomes coil up and become thick enough to see in a light microscope. Spindle fibers form, and near the end of this stage the envelope around the nucleus breaks down. |
| **Metaphase** | Spindle fibers line the chromosomes up in a single row across the middle of the cell. |
| **Anaphase** | The sister chromatids of each chromosome separate and are pulled to opposite ends of the cell. Each one is now its own chromosome. |
| **Telophase** | A full set of chromosomes reaches each end. A new nuclear envelope forms around each set, and the chromosomes loosen again. |

{/* DIAGRAM: one cell with two chromosome pairs shown at 1 prophase, 2 metaphase, 3 anaphase and 4 telophase, then 5 cytokinesis in an animal cell beside a plant cell */}

Cytokinesis often starts during late anaphase or telophase, and it looks different in plants and animals. In an animal cell, a ring at the middle pinches the membrane inward, making a furrow that deepens until the cell splits in two. A plant cell has a stiff wall and cannot pinch. Instead, a **cell plate** forms across the middle of the cell and grows outward until it becomes a new wall between the two cells.

## Explain: why cells divide and stay small

Cells divide for several reasons:

- **Growth.** A tree or a teenager grows by adding cells, and many of those new cells then get bigger.
- **Repair.** Skin cells divide behind a wound to replace those that were lost.
- **Replacement.** The lining of your gut and the outer layer of your skin are replaced over and over.
- **Asexual reproduction.** A single-celled amoeba or yeast cell can make a new organism by dividing. Bacteria divide too, by a simpler process covered in How Microbes Multiply.

Why not grow bigger cells instead? Oxygen, food molecules and wastes all cross the cell membrane, which you study in [Crossing the Membrane](/lessons/crossing-the-membrane/). The **surface-area-to-volume ratio** compares how much outside surface a cell has with how much inside it has. Model a cell as a cube:

| Side of cube (units) | Surface area (square units) | Volume (cubic units) | Surface area ÷ volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | 1 | 6 |
| 2 | 24 | 8 | 3 |
| 4 | 96 | 64 | 1.5 |

These come from geometry (area = 6 × side × side, volume = side × side × side), not from measuring cells. Each time the side doubles, the volume grows eight times but the surface only four times. A large cell would have less membrane for each bit of its inside.

Real cells are not cubes, and long, thin or flat cells keep more surface. The model works best for comparing cells of similar shape. Some cells are very large, such as egg cells and long nerve cells, and cells also control their own size, so surface area is one limit among several.

The onion root shows both ideas together. Near the tip, cells divide. Just behind that zone, the new cells grow longer, and that lengthening pushes the root through the soil. So a root grows by making new cells and by letting them stretch.

### Design problem

Does the temperature an onion root grows at change how many of its tip cells are dividing? Plan an investigation. Name your variables, constants, control, sample size and repeated trials, and say how you will stay safe.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do onion roots grown at a warmer temperature have a larger fraction of tip cells in mitosis?
- **Independent variable:** the growing temperature, for example a cool room and a warmer incubator, checked with a thermometer.
- **Dependent variable:** the fraction of tip cells in any stage of mitosis.
- **Constants:** one onion variety, the same water and days of growth, tips cut to the same length at the same time of day, the same stain and sorting rules.
- **Control:** roots grown at ordinary room temperature, to compare with.
- **Sample size and trials:** several roots from each of several onions at each temperature. On each slide, count every cell in a row of fields, not only interesting ones, until you have a few hundred. Have two people count one slide to check agreement.
- **Safety:** goggles and gloves. The stain and the acid used to soften roots are handled by your teacher, and blades are used with care.

A larger fraction in mitosis at the warmer temperature would suggest that temperature changes the cycle. It would not give the cycle length, which needs a separate measurement.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How spindle fibers move chromosomes

### Where the onion counts come from

The counts come from The Biology Project, University of Arizona, onion root-tip cell cycle activity (page dated 1997, revised August 2004). A 2018 study that gathered published estimates for roots grown from onion bulbs reported values from about 13.5 to 23 hours, so 24 hours is a round assumption, not a measurement.

### Wound repair in mouse skin

In mouse skin, the wound also shrinks as its edges are pulled in, and how much each step matters depends on the kind of wound.
