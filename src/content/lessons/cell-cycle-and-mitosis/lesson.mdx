## Explain: the cell cycle

<WordBank terms={['cell cycle', 'interphase', 'mitosis', 'cytokinesis', 'chromosome', 'sister chromatids', 'centromere', 'spindle fibers', 'prophase', 'metaphase', 'anaphase', 'telophase']} />

<Support>**The one idea:** Copy each chromosome first; separate the copies so each nucleus gets one. **Scope:** This is the normal outcome for a typical eukaryotic cell completing mitosis. Cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm, and some cells stop dividing and leave the cycle.</Support>

The **cell cycle** is the series of stages a cell goes through from the time it forms until it divides into two cells. In plants, animals, fungi and other eukaryotes, a typical dividing cell passes through four main phases: G1, S, G2 and M. **Interphase** is the part between divisions. It has three parts. In G1 the cell grows, makes proteins and does its everyday work. In S it copies its DNA ("S" stands for synthesis, which means making). In G2 it makes the proteins that will move the chromosomes and gets ready to divide.

DNA is stored in **chromosomes**, long strands of DNA wrapped around proteins. A typical human body cell with a nucleus has 46 chromosomes. Red blood cells lose their nucleus as they mature.

After S phase, each chromosome has been copied. Each copy is a **sister chromatid**. The two stay joined, so they count as one copied chromosome until they separate.

| Moment | Model cell | Typical human body cell with a nucleus |
|---|---:|---:|
| Before S phase | 4 chromosomes, 4 chromatids | 46 chromosomes, 46 chromatids |
| After S phase | 4 copied chromosomes, 8 sister chromatids | 46 copied chromosomes, 92 sister chromatids |
| In each new nucleus after mitosis | 4 chromosomes, 4 chromatids | 46 chromosomes, 46 chromatids |

**Predict.** A typical human body cell has finished S phase but has not started mitosis. How many chromosomes and sister chromatids should you count?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Count 46 copied chromosomes and 92 sister chromatids. Copying doubled the DNA molecules, not the number of joined chromosome units.

</details>

Copying first is like copying a recipe book before two groups share it, instead of tearing it in half. The comparison breaks because a cell does not decide anything; proteins copy and share the DNA.

M phase is division itself, in two parts. **Mitosis** is the division of the nucleus into two nuclei, each with a full set of chromosomes. **Cytokinesis** is the division of the cytoplasm, which splits one cell into two. The result is two new cells that usually have the same DNA as each other and as the cell they came from.

## Explain: the stages of mitosis

A stained slide shows only still shapes, so read each shape as evidence. Sister chromatids are held together at the **centromere**, a narrow region of the chromosome. **Spindle fibers** are thin protein fibers that attach to chromosomes and move them.

| Stage | Clue in a still cell | What the clue means |
|---|---|---|
| **Prophase** | Thick, separate chromosomes become visible. | The chromosomes have coiled up. Spindle fibers form, and near the end the nuclear envelope breaks down. |
| **Metaphase** | Copied chromosomes form one row across the middle. | Spindle fibers have lined them up before separation. |
| **Anaphase** | Two matching groups sit apart, heading toward opposite ends. | Sister chromatids have separated and are being pulled to opposite ends. Each is now its own chromosome. |
| **Telophase** | A chromosome set sits at each end as two nuclei appear. | A new nuclear envelope forms around each set, and the chromosomes loosen again. |

## Explain: why cells divide and stay small

A small cut on your finger usually closes on its own in roughly a week or a little longer. Near an onion root tip, cells divide as the root pushes deeper into the soil. Both depend on adding cells, and cells divide for several reasons.

- **Growth.** A tree or a teenager grows by adding cells, and many of those new cells then get bigger.
- **Repair.** Skin cells divide behind a wound to replace those that were lost.
- **Replacement.** The lining of your gut and the outer layer of your skin are replaced over and over.
- **Asexual reproduction.** A single-celled amoeba or yeast cell can make a new organism by dividing. Bacteria divide too, by a simpler process covered in How Microbes Multiply.

Why not grow only bigger cells? Oxygen, food molecules and wastes all cross the cell membrane, which you study in [Crossing the Membrane](/lessons/crossing-the-membrane/). The **surface-area-to-volume ratio** compares how much outside surface a cell has with how much inside it has.

**Predict.** A cube's side length doubles. Does its surface area, its volume, or both grow by the larger factor?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Volume grows by the larger factor. Surface area becomes four times larger, while volume becomes eight times larger.

</details>

Model a cell as a cube:

| Side of cube (units) | Surface area (square units) | Volume (cubic units) | Surface area ÷ volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | 1 | 6 |
| 2 | 24 | 8 | 3 |
| 4 | 96 | 64 | 1.5 |

These values come from geometry (area = 6 × side × side, volume = side × side × side), not from measuring cells. Each time the side doubles, volume grows eight times but surface only four times, so a large cell has less membrane for each bit of its inside.

Real cells are not cubes, and long, thin or flat cells keep more surface. The model works best for comparing cells of similar shape. Some cells, such as egg cells and long nerve cells, are very large. Cells also control their own size, so surface area is one limit among several.

The onion root shows both ideas together. Near the tip, cells divide. Just behind that zone, the new cells grow longer, and that lengthening pushes the root through the soil.

The limits are real: these are 36 photographs from a teaching set that does not say how they were picked. They hold far more dividing cells than a random field of a real root tip would, so the times for the stages of mitosis come out much too long. And 24 hours is an assumption, not a measured cycle time."

### Design problem

Does the temperature an onion root grows at change how many of its tip cells are dividing? Plan an investigation. Name your variables, constants, control, sample size and repeated trials, and say how you will stay safe.

**Predict.** If warmer roots show a larger fraction of cells in mitosis, can that result alone tell you the total cycle length?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The result would suggest that temperature changes the cycle, but a total cycle length needs a separate measurement.

</details>

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do onion roots grown at a warmer temperature have a larger fraction of tip cells in mitosis?
- **Independent variable:** the growing temperature, for example a cool room and a warmer incubator, checked with a thermometer.
- **Dependent variable:** the fraction of tip cells in any stage of mitosis.
- **Constants:** one onion variety, the same water and days of growth, tips cut to the same length at the same time of day, the same stain and sorting rules.
- **Control:** roots grown at ordinary room temperature, to compare with.
- **Sample size and trials:** several roots from each of several onions at each temperature. On each slide, count every cell in a row of fields, not only interesting ones, until you have a few hundred. Have two people count one slide to check agreement.
- **Safety:** goggles and gloves. The stain and the acid used to soften roots are handled by your teacher, and blades are used with care.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The copy-then-separate rule explains how each new nucleus receives a full chromosome set; cytokinesis then splits the cell. Experiments on mouse skin suggest that new skin covers a wound as cells near the edge move into the gap, while cells farther back divide to replace them (see Going deeper for wound shrinkage and differences among wound types). An onion root grows deeper because cells at the tip divide and the cells behind them stretch. Living things add cells instead of growing giant ones partly because small cells have more membrane for each bit of their inside.

## Going deeper

### How spindle fibers move chromosomes

You can picture spindle fibers as ropes, but they do not reel anything in. They get shorter by losing pieces at their ends.

### Where the onion counts come from

The counts come from The Biology Project, University of Arizona, onion root-tip cell cycle activity (page dated 1997, revised August 2004). A 2018 study that gathered published estimates for roots grown from onion bulbs reported values from about 13.5 to 23 hours, so 24 hours is a round assumption, not a measurement.
