## Explain: germ theory changed how people stop disease

<WordBank terms={['germ theory', 'microbe', 'pathogen', 'sanitation', 'aseptic technique', 'pasteurization', 'sterilization', 'vaccine', 'antigen', 'memory cells', 'antibiotic', 'antibiotic resistance']} />

A **microbe** is a living thing too small to see without a microscope, such as a bacterium. The **germ theory** of disease says that many diseases are caused by microbes that get into the body. A microbe or virus that causes disease is called a **pathogen**. Germ theory explains infectious diseases, not every illness; most heart disease is not an infection.

Later in the 1800s, experiments by Louis Pasteur, Robert Koch and others linked particular microbes to particular diseases. Once people accepted germ theory, they had a reason for practices that block microbes from passing from one person to another:

- **Sanitation** is keeping places clean and removing sewage and waste, so pathogens do not reach drinking water or food.
- **Safe handling of food and water** includes cooking, refrigeration and treating drinking water. **Pasteurization** is heating a food such as milk for a set time to kill harmful bacteria.
- **Aseptic technique** is a set of steps that keep microbes out of a wound or sterile area, such as clean hands, sterile gloves and sterile tools.
- **Vaccination** prepares the immune system before a pathogen arrives (next section).
- **Chemicals and processes that destroy microbes.** Disinfectants such as chlorine kill many microbes on surfaces, though some bacterial spores survive. **Sterilization** is a process that removes all forms of microbial life from an object, often with high-pressure steam.

Some of these habits came first. Edward Jenner's smallpox vaccine (1796) and many sanitation reforms came before germ theory, and Semmelweis began chlorine washing in 1847 without knowing which microbe was involved. Germ theory explained why these practices worked and led to new ones, such as vaccines made in the laboratory.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** germ theory says many diseases are caused by microbes, so stopping microbes from spreading or destroying them prevents disease.</Support>

## Explain: vaccines and immune memory

A **vaccine** is a preparation that teaches the immune system to recognize a pathogen without causing the full disease. It works through an **antigen**, a substance the immune system can recognize and respond to, often a protein on a pathogen's surface. The immune system responds by making **antibodies**, proteins that stick to one antigen and help destroy or block it.

| Kind of vaccine | What is in it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Live weakened | A weakened form of the virus or bacterium | MMR (measles, mumps, rubella) |
| Killed (inactivated) | Whole microbes killed by heat or chemicals | Polio shot (IPV), hepatitis A |
| Toxoid | A bacterial toxin made harmless | Tetanus, diphtheria |
| Subunit | One piece, such as a protein or sugar | Some pneumonia vaccines |
| mRNA | Instructions your cells use to make one viral protein | COVID-19 mRNA vaccines |

The first time your body meets an antigen, the response is slow, often taking a week or more to build. During it, some immune cells become **memory cells**, which stay in the body long after. Some long-lived cells also keep making antibody, so a lower level of antibody stays in the blood for years. On a later exposure, memory cells recognize the antigen quickly, and memory B cells turn into cells that make large amounts of antibody. A vaccine gives you that first meeting without the disease, so the real pathogen meets antibody already waiting and a faster response.

A wanted poster is one way to picture this, but the immune system does not look at anything; memory cells carry receptors that fit one antigen.

Vaccines do not work perfectly in everyone: a small share of vaccinated people still get measles.

## Explain: antibiotics and why they stop working

An **antibiotic** is a drug that kills bacteria or stops them from growing and dividing. Many antibiotics block the building of the bacterial cell wall. Others jam bacterial ribosomes, so the cell cannot make proteins. Your cells have no cell wall, and their ribosomes differ enough that these drugs mostly leave them alone.

A virus has no cell wall and no ribosomes of its own. It uses the ribosomes of the cell it infects, as [Living or Not?](/lessons/living-or-not/) explains. So antibiotics do not treat colds, flu or other viral infections.

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/germs-vaccines-antibiotics/what-antibiotics-hit.webp" width={1200} height={800} keyItems={["cell wall", "bacterial ribosomes", "virus", "your cells"]} given={[0, 2]} alt="AI-generated illustration: on the left a rod-shaped bacterium with an outer wall and ribosomes, with gold block marks showing an antibiotic hitting the wall and the ribosomes; on the right one of your own cells, with only a thin membrane, its own ribosomes and tiny virus particles inside, and no gold marks. Four numbered circles mark parts for the student to name." caption="Antibiotics hit parts that bacteria have. A virus has no cell wall or ribosomes of its own and works inside your cells, so there is nothing there for the drug to hit." />

**Antibiotic resistance** is the ability of some bacteria to survive a drug that kills others of their kind. It belongs to the bacteria, not to the person. In a large population of bacteria, a few may already carry a gene that lets them survive. An antibiotic kills or stops the sensitive ones, and the resistant ones are left to multiply. This is **natural selection**, the process in which individuals with a helpful trait survive and reproduce more, covered in [Variation and Survival](/lessons/variation-and-survival/). Resistant bacteria can also pass resistance genes to other bacteria.

Health agencies say misuse of antibiotics, such as using them for a cold, can add to resistance: the bacteria in the body meet the drug with no benefit, which favors resistant ones. How long a course should last is decided by doctors, and for some infections, studies have found that shorter courses work as well as longer ones. The simulation's "stop early" switch is a model of selection, not medical advice.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** antibiotics hit parts that bacteria have and viruses lack, and each use favors any resistant bacteria present.</Support>

### Design problem

A company has a new vaccine against a made-up disease that spreads in schools. Plan a fair test of whether it reduces cases of the disease. Name your groups, what you keep the same, what you count, how many people you need, and how you protect the volunteers.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Groups:** chance assigns volunteers to the vaccine or to a look-alike placebo shot, which tends to make the groups alike in age, health and habits.
- **Independent variable:** vaccine or placebo.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of confirmed cases in each group, defined before the trial starts (for example, symptoms plus a positive lab test).
- **Constants:** same dose schedule, follow-up time and illness checks in both groups.
- **Blinding:** neither volunteers nor the people judging illness know who got which shot, so expectations do not change the count.
- **Sample size:** thousands of volunteers, because a small trial may see too few cases of an uncommon disease to tell the groups apart.
- **Ethics and safety:** every volunteer gives informed consent and can leave at any time. If children take part, a parent gives permission and the child also agrees. An independent group watches for side effects and can stop the trial. Results are reported either way.
- **What would mean what:** far fewer cases in the vaccine group than the placebo group supports the vaccine. Similar numbers would mean no clear effect.

</details>

**Science practices you used:** making predictions and testing them in a model, reading data tables and calculating ratios, comparing groups fairly, planning a blinded trial with a control group, and naming the limits of historical data.
