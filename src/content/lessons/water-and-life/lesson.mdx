## Explain: a molecule with charged ends

<WordBank terms={['polar', 'covalent bond', 'hydrogen bond', 'solvent', 'cohesion', 'surface tension', 'adhesion', 'capillary action', 'specific heat', 'heat capacity']} />

A water molecule has one oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms. Each hydrogen is joined to the oxygen by a **covalent bond**, a bond in which two atoms share electrons. The molecule is bent, a little like a wide letter V, with oxygen at the point.

Oxygen pulls the shared electrons closer to itself than hydrogen does. So the oxygen end carries a slight negative charge, and each hydrogen end carries a slight positive charge. A molecule with a slightly negative end and a slightly positive end is **polar**. These are partial charges, smaller than the full charge on a particle of salt, and the molecule as a whole has no net charge.

Opposite charges attract. The slightly positive hydrogen of one water molecule is drawn to the slightly negative oxygen of a neighbor. That attraction is a **hydrogen bond**, a weak attraction between molecules. In liquid water, hydrogen bonds form and break constantly as the molecules jostle.

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/water-and-life/water-polar-bent-blocks.webp" width={1200} height={800} keyItems={["oxygen end", "hydrogen end", "covalent bond", "hydrogen bond"]} given={[0, 1]} alt="AI-generated illustration: three bent block shapes, each a water molecule drawn as one wide body with two small tip blocks, in a row; dashed gold lines link neighbouring molecules and short solid bars join the tips to the bodies. Four numbered circles mark parts for the student to name." caption="One water molecule is bent, and the links inside it are not the same kind as the links between neighbours. Name the four numbered parts." />

Keep the two kinds of bond apart. Covalent bonds hold the atoms of one molecule together. Hydrogen bonds hold neighboring molecules near each other, and they are much weaker.

## Explain: dissolving, sticking and climbing

Almost every special property of water comes from those charged ends and hydrogen bonds, and these properties support the chemical reactions of metabolism that keep cells alive.

### Water as a solvent

A **solvent** is a liquid that dissolves other substances. When you stir salt into water, the charged ends of water molecules surround each charged particle of salt and pull it away from the crystal. Sugar molecules have polar parts of their own, so water dissolves sugar too. Oil molecules are nonpolar. Water molecules attract one another more strongly than they attract oil, so the oil is left out in its own layer. Water is sometimes called the universal solvent, but it does not dissolve everything.

### Cohesion and surface tension

**Cohesion** is the attraction of water molecules to other water molecules. At the surface, each molecule is pulled sideways and downward by its neighbors, so the surface resists being stretched. That resistance is **surface tension**. It is still liquid water at the surface, not a separate skin.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/water-and-life/strider-surface-tension.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a water strider standing on a still pond with small dimples under its feet, and a zoom circle showing a top row of abstract water-molecule blocks with arrows pulling sideways and downward." caption="At the surface each water molecule is pulled sideways and downward by its neighbours. That pull, together with legs covered in tiny water-repellent hairs, is why a small strider can stand on a pond; the water is still liquid, not a skin." />

### Adhesion and capillary action

**Adhesion** is the attraction of water to a different substance. Paper is made of cellulose fibers, and plant cell walls contain cellulose. Cellulose has many polar parts that water clings to. When water climbs a narrow space or a fiber because of adhesion and cohesion working together, that is **capillary action**.

| Property | What causes it | Living example |
|---|---|---|
| Solvent | Charged ends surround charged and polar particles | Plants carry dissolved sugar in the sap of their phloem |
| Cohesion | Hydrogen bonds between water molecules | A column of water holds together inside a plant’s xylem tubes |
| Surface tension | Cohesion at the water’s surface | A water strider stands on a pond |
| Adhesion | Attraction of water to other polar materials | Water clings to the cellulose in the walls of plant cells |

In a tall tree, water evaporating from the leaves pulls the next molecules up behind it, and cohesion keeps the column from breaking. Most of the lift comes from that evaporation. Adhesion to the tube walls helps hold the column in place.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/water-and-life/tree-water-column.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a tall tree with a cut-away trunk showing a column of water running from the roots to a leaf where vapour leaves, plus a zoom of a narrow tube filled with touching water-molecule blocks." caption="Water leaving a leaf pulls the next molecules up behind it, and cohesion keeps the column from breaking. Adhesion to the tube walls helps hold it in place." />

One more oddity: ice floats. As water freezes, hydrogen bonds lock the molecules into an open pattern with more space between them, so ice is less dense than liquid water. A frozen pond that is deep enough keeps liquid water underneath, where fish can survive the winter.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Because water molecules are polar, water dissolves charged and polar substances, sticks to itself (cohesion), and sticks to other polar materials (adhesion).</Support>

## Explain: why water warms slowly

**Specific heat** is the energy needed to warm one gram of a substance by one degree Celsius. For liquid water it is about 4.18 joules per gram per degree. For dry sand it is about 0.8, roughly a fifth as much. **Heat capacity** is the same idea for a whole object, so a big body of water has an enormous heat capacity.

Why so high? When you add heat to water, part of the energy goes into stretching, bending and breaking the hydrogen bonds between molecules, so less of it shows up as faster motion and a rise in temperature. When water cools, those bonds re-form and release energy back.

> heat added (joules) = mass (grams) × specific heat × temperature change (°C)

For equal masses that take in equal energy, water warms about one fifth as much as dry sand.

This slow warming matters to life. Large bodies of water such as lakes and the Bay change temperature slowly, so the fish, crabs and grasses in them usually face gentler daily temperature swings than the air above them does. Water makes up roughly half of your body’s weight, a bit more or less depending on age and body build. Blood, which is mostly water, carries heat from warm working muscles to cooler parts of the body, and the water in your tissues helps slow changes in your temperature, along with sweating and other ways your body controls it.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Water needs a lot of energy to change its temperature, so watery places and watery bodies warm and cool slowly.</Support>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The air over the Bay swung by several degrees while the water barely moved. The data fit the idea that each gram of water needs a lot of energy to warm and that the Bay holds a vast amount of water. Water’s polar molecules, held near one another by hydrogen bonds, help explain that slow warming. They also explain the strider on the pond and the water climbing the towel.

### Design problem

A paper towel wicks water upward. Does the width of a strip of paper change how high the water climbs? Plan a fair test you could do with paper, a ruler, a tray of water and a timer.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Question and hypothesis: If strips are wider, then water will climb higher in 10 minutes, because the edges of a narrow strip may slow the water more.
- Independent variable: strip width, for example 5, 10 and 20 millimeters.
- Dependent variable: height the water reaches after 10 minutes, measured in millimeters from the water line.
- Constants: the same kind of paper cut from one sheet, the same strip length, the same depth dipped into the water, the same water at the same temperature, the same timer.
- Comparison: the 5 mm strip serves as the baseline the wider strips are compared with.
- Repeated trials: test at least five strips of each width and calculate the mean height for each width.
- Safety: wipe up spills so no one slips.

A 2020 study did a version of this with laboratory filter paper. It ran five repeats at each of three widths, 5, 10 and 20 mm, and the wider strips showed greater rise in the range tested. A simple formula did not predict every measured height, which is a reminder that paper is more complicated than a model of tiny tubes.

## Going deeper

### The heat calculation, step by step

To find the temperature change, divide the heat by the mass times the specific heat. For example, 100 grams of water that takes in 836.8 joules warms by 836.8 ÷ (100 × 4.184) = 2 °C. The same energy would warm 100 grams of dry sand by about 10 °C.

### How the buoy numbers were gathered

The table shows eight readings, every third hour. The full day had 190 paired readings; across all of them the air ranged by 8.1 °C and the water by 1.4 °C.
