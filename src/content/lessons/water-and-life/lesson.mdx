## Explain: a molecule with charged ends

## Explain: dissolving, sticking and climbing

Why cells care: dissolved substances meet and react in water, the setting for the chemical reactions of metabolism that keep cells alive.

### Water as a solvent

A **solvent** is a liquid that dissolves other substances. The first film's salt **ions**, charged particles, are held by the pull of opposite charges, not by hydrogen bonds. Sugar has no ions, but its molecules carry slightly charged hydrogens and oxygens like water's, so water hydrogen-bonds to sugar and dissolves it. The film's oil is **nonpolar**: its molecules share their electrons about evenly. Water is sometimes called the universal solvent, but it does not dissolve everything.

### Cohesion, adhesion and capillary action

**Cohesion** is the attraction of water molecules to each other; at the surface it gives **surface tension**, the surface's resistance to being stretched.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/water-and-life/strider-surface-tension.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a water strider standing on a still pond with small dimples under its feet, and a zoom circle showing a top row of abstract water-molecule blocks with arrows pulling sideways and downward." caption="At the surface each water molecule is pulled sideways and downward by its neighbours. That pull, together with legs covered in tiny water-repellent hairs, is why a small strider can stand on a pond; the water is still liquid, not a skin." />

**Adhesion** is the attraction of water to a different substance. It is strong on polar surfaces such as cellulose, in plant cell walls and paper, and weak on water-repellent ones such as the strider's leg hairs, where water beads up. When adhesion and cohesion together pull water up a narrow space or a fiber, that is **capillary action**.

**Predict.** When water freezes, hydrogen bonds lock each molecule in place. Will ice float or sink in liquid water? Why would a fish in a pond care?

## Explain: why the Bay barely warmed

The second film defined **temperature** (the average energy of motion of a substance's particles), **specific heat** (the energy to warm **one gram** by 1 °C: about 4.18 joules per gram per degree for liquid water, about 0.8 for dry sand) and **heat capacity** (the energy to warm a **whole object** by 1 °C; for one substance, mass times specific heat). One equation ties them:

> heat added (joules) = mass (grams) × specific heat × temperature change (°C)

Read it two ways. Swap the substance: equal energy warms water about one fifth as much as an equal mass of dry sand, as in your simulation. Grow the mass: the same energy in more grams gives a proportionally smaller rise.

**Predict.** Two ponds of the same area get the same sunshine all day. One is 10 cm deep and one is 2 m deep, and each mixes its warmth through its whole depth. Which warms more, and about how many times more?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The shallow pond, about twenty times as much: the same energy goes into a twentieth of the mass (10 cm against 200 cm), as long as neither pond loses much heat to the ground or air.

</details>

Those conditions matter on the Bay, where the buoy cannot separate the second film's three causes, so specific heat is a necessary part of the story, not the whole of it. Your body, largely water, is steadied the same way (see Going deeper).

This is one buoy on one day, and two gusts of cool wind lowered the air temperature, so the data fit the idea but do not prove heat capacity is the only cause."

### Design problem

A paper towel wicks water upward. Does the width of a strip of paper change how high the water climbs? Plan a fair test you could do with paper, a ruler, a tray of water and a timer.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Question and hypothesis: If strips are wider, then water will climb higher in 10 minutes, because the edges of a narrow strip may slow the water more.
- Independent variable: strip width, for example 5, 10 and 20 millimeters.
- Dependent variable: height the water reaches after 10 minutes, measured in millimeters from the water line.
- Constants: paper cut from one sheet, strip length, dipping depth, the water and its temperature, and the timer.
- Comparison: the 5 mm strip is the baseline for the wider strips.
- Repeated trials: test at least five strips of each width and calculate the mean height for each.
- Safety: wipe up spills so no one slips.

A 2020 study did a version of this with laboratory filter paper. It ran five repeats at each of three widths, 5, 10 and 20 mm, and the wider strips showed greater rise in the range tested. A simple formula did not predict every measured height, which is a reminder that paper is more complicated than a model of tiny tubes.

## Going deeper

### The heat calculation, step by step

Temperature change = heat ÷ (mass × specific heat). For example, 100 grams of water that takes in 836.8 joules warms by 836.8 ÷ (100 × 4.184) = 2 °C. The same energy would warm 100 grams of dry sand by about 10 °C.

### Where the energy really goes

The second film's orange dots are a sketch, not separate packets of energy. In liquid water and solid sand at this lesson's temperatures, added energy both makes particles move or vibrate faster and changes the energy stored in how they are arranged; in water, rearranging the web of hydrogen bonds is a big part of the high specific heat. The film's cooling answer assumes both lose energy at the same rate; outdoors, wind, surface area and evaporation set those rates.

### Water in your body

Water makes up roughly half of your body’s weight, a bit more or less depending on age and body build. Blood, which is mostly water, carries heat from warm working muscles to cooler parts of the body, and the water in your tissues helps slow changes in your temperature, along with sweating and other ways your body controls it.
