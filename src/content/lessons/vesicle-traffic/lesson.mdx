    story: >-
      Schekman and his graduate student Peter Novick asked whether yeast, a cell they could mutate
      at will, would reveal the parts of secretion one gene at a time. In 1979 they described a
      temperature-sensitive mutant, sec1, that kept making secretory enzymes at 37 °C but could not
      release them. Its internal pool rose up to eightfold, held in membrane-bound vesicles, and on

      SEC18, turned out to encode the yeast form of NSF. Schekman shared the 2013 Nobel Prize in
      Physiology or Medicine with James Rothman and Thomas Südhof.

    story: >-
      Rothman attacked secretion with test tubes instead of mutants. In 1980, with Fries, he

      The 1984 version, by Balch, Dunphy, Braell and Rothman, used donor Golgi from clone 15B, a

    story: >-
      Barlowe's best-known work makes an ER vesicle from parts. Working with Schekman and
      colleagues, he added three soluble yeast components, Sar1p, the Sec23p complex and the Sec13p
      complex, to washed ER membranes and obtained vesicles 60 to 65 nm across. The 1994 paper named
      the coat COPII. A control mattered as much as the yield. Vesicles made with GMP-PNP, a GTP

    story: >-
      Vesicles with a bristly coat had been seen in electron micrographs before anyone knew what the
      coat was made of. In 1975 Pearse reported a method to purify coated vesicles from pig brain.
      In 1976 she compared coated vesicles purified from brain, adrenal medulla and a lymphoma cell
      line that does not secrete. In each, a single major protein with an apparent molecular weight
      of 180,000 formed the coat, and peptide mapping suggested that its sequence was conserved
      across the tissues and species she studied. She called it clathrin. The coats were closed
      networks of hexagons and pentagons, built from variable numbers of clathrin subunits in
      vesicles of different sizes. Because a cell line that does not secrete still held a large
      amount of clathrin, she argued that coated vesicles move substantial amounts of membrane
      inside cells, not only during secretion. A purified coat protein gave later workers something
      to image, reconstitute and link to the adaptors that choose its cargo.

techniques:
  - name: "Temperature-sensitive mutant screens and double mutants"
    blurb: >-
      Yeast mutants that work at 25 °C and fail at 37 °C are screened for secretory enzyme trapped
      inside the cell. Complementation sorts them into groups, and double mutants, read by electron
      microscopy, show which block comes first.
  - name: "Cell-free transport assays"
    blurb: >-
      Donor membranes, acceptor membranes, cytosol and ATP are mixed in a tube. Cargo that reaches
      the acceptor gains a sugar only the acceptor can add, so transport becomes a number, and each
      ingredient can be removed, inhibited or added back.
  - name: "Liposome and proteoliposome reconstitution"
    blurb: >-
      Purified proteins are added to synthetic lipid vesicles to ask what they can do alone: bud a
      coat, tether, or fuse. Fusion is read as lipid mixing and lumenal content mixing, with
      controls for leakage.
  - name: "Electron microscopy of arrested cells"
    blurb: >-
      Thin sections of mutant cells at the restrictive temperature show which membrane piles up:
      ER, Golgi-like stacks or vesicles of a given size. The accumulated organelle marks where the
      pathway stopped.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "What two jobs does a vesicle coat such as COPII perform?"
    choices:
      - "It selects cargo and bends the membrane into a bud"
      - "It tethers the vesicle and fuses it with the target"
      - "It hydrolyzes ATP and pumps cargo across the bilayer"
      - "It labels the vesicle and later recycles its SNAREs"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      A coat gathers cargo, through binding sites such as those on Sec24, and its assembly curves
      the membrane into a bud; purified COPII proteins bud chemically defined liposomes. A coat does
      not pump cargo across the bilayer or use ATP to do so; cargo is already in the lumen or the
      membrane. Tethering and fusion are later steps carried out by Rabs, tethers and SNAREs, though COPII
      begins to come off after Sar1 hydrolyzes GTP, and parts of it can stay on long enough to help the vesicle be caught at the Golgi. Recycling SNAREs is the work of NSF and SNAP, not of a coat.
  - stem: "Which description of the SNARE bundle that drives fusion is correct?"
    choices:
      - "Four helices that assemble in one membrane before docking"
      - "A Rab bound to its effector on the target membrane surface"
      - "Four helices, Qa, Qb, Qc and R, contributed by two membranes"
      - "Two helices, one from each membrane, held together by NSF"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      A fusion bundle has four SNARE motifs, classed Qa, Qb, Qc and R, and while it bridges two
      membranes (a trans complex) its zippering pulls them together. Two helices is too few, and NSF
      takes bundles apart rather than holding them together. A bundle assembled in one membrane is a
      cis complex, the state after fusion, and cannot pull two membranes together. A Rab with its
      effector is part of tethering, not the fusion bundle.
  - stem: "After fusion, the SNAREs that zippered now sit together in one membrane. What returns them to a usable state?"
    choices:
      - "Dynamin with AP2, using GTP to cut the bundle loose"
      - "NSF with SNAP, using ATP to pull the bundle apart"
      - "Sar1 with Sec23, using GTP to coat the bundle anew"
      - "A Rab with GDI, using GTP to tether the bundle again"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      NSF (Sec18 in yeast) is an ATPase that, with its adaptor SNAP (Sec17), binds the cis-SNARE
      complex and uses ATP to separate the helices so they can be used again. Sar1 and Sec23 build
      the COPII coat at the ER and do not act on SNARE bundles. GDI removes GDP-bound Rabs from
      membranes and does not unwind SNAREs. Dynamin pinches off clathrin-coated buds and AP2 binds
      cargo; neither disassembles SNAREs.
  - stem: "An ER membrane protein carries a C-terminal KKXX motif on its cytosolic tail. If both lysines are changed to serines, where is more of the protein found at steady state?"
    choices:
      - "Further along the pathway, since COPI can no longer retrieve it"
      - "In the nucleus, since the lysines had been an export signal"
      - "In the ER, since COPII can no longer pick it up for export"
      - "In the cytosol, since it can no longer insert into the bilayer"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The dilysine motif is read by the α-COP and β′-COP subunits of coatomer, which return escaped
      proteins from the Golgi to the ER; without it the protein that leaves the ER is not brought
      back and builds up in later compartments. The motif is a retrieval signal, not a COPII export
      signal, so losing it does not hold the protein in the ER. The tail is cytosolic and plays no
      part in membrane insertion. The lysines are not a nuclear signal, and the protein is a
      membrane protein of the secretory pathway.
  - stem: "Yeast mutant a alone accumulates ER at 37 °C. Mutant b alone accumulates small vesicles near the ER. The a b double mutant accumulates ER and no vesicles. What is the best conclusion?"
    choices:
      - "Genes a and b act in parallel, since their phenotypes differ"
      - "Gene b acts before gene a, since vesicles are smaller than ER"
      - "Gene a acts before gene b, if the two act on one linear route"
      - "Genes a and b encode the same protein, since they interact"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The double mutant shows the a phenotype, so cargo never reaches the step that needs b; on a
      linear pathway the earlier block wins, which places a before b. Kaiser and Schekman used this
      logic to separate vesicle-forming from vesicle-fusion genes. Vesicle size says nothing about
      order. A double-mutant phenotype tests order, not whether two genes encode one protein.
      Parallel pathways would predict an additive or mixed phenotype, not a clean copy of one
      single mutant.

  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Cells move proteins between compartments in small membrane bubbles called vesicles. A protein
      coat on the outside picks which cargo goes in and bends the membrane into a bud. The vesicle
      is then recognized by proteins on the target compartment: a switch protein called a Rab
      recruits long tethering proteins that catch it. Finally, SNARE proteins, some on the vesicle

      emptying the cargo into the target. An enzyme called NSF uses ATP to unwind the SNAREs so they
      can be used again.

    label: "College"

      Budding starts when a small GTPase or a lipid recruits a coat. At the ER, Sar1-GTP recruits

      At the Golgi, Arf1-GTP recruits coatomer (COPI), whose α- and β′-COP subunits bind KKXX

**A yeast mutant called sec1 grows at 25 °C, but at 37 °C it keeps making secretory enzymes and cannot release them.** Novick and Schekman reported it in 1979. The first film above follows those enzymes along the route and through the vesicles that never fuse with the surface; the three jobs it names are **budding** (pick up cargo and pinch off), **targeting** (be caught by the right membrane) and **fusion** (merge with it).

**Predict.** In sec1 cells at 37 °C, which of the three jobs worked for the last vesicle on the route, and which could have failed?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Budding worked: the vesicles exist. Targeting or fusion failed, and a pile of vesicles alone cannot say which.

</details>

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A vesicle's trip is three handoffs, and each can fail on its own. A **coat** buds the vesicle and picks its cargo. A **Rab-GTP with a tether** catches it at the right membrane (targeting). A **SNARE bundle, guided by an SM protein**, fuses it. On a linear route, when one handoff fails, cargo piles up in the last place it reached, and a fast, specific inhibitor stops working once the state it targets is past.

Each can fail on its own, which is why mutants and inhibitors can tell them apart." />

This picture fits the coat-dependent routes in this lesson. Some endocytosis uses other budding machinery, and tubular carriers and lipid transfer without a vesicle fall outside it.

A few terms. The **cytosol** is the cell's fluid outside organelles; the **lumen** is the inside of a membrane-bound compartment. A **vesicle** is a small membrane sphere, often 50 to 100 nm across, that carries lumenal cargo and membrane proteins from a donor compartment to a target. Its lumen never mixes with the cytosol (see [membrane topology](/lessons/membrane-organization-topology/)), so every protein that steers it works on its cytosolic face. A coat grabs a membrane protein's cytosolic tail directly. A soluble cargo, such as the secretory enzyme invertase measured below, cannot touch the coat: it is caught by a membrane-spanning receptor that the coat grabs (Erv29 in yeast, ERGIC-53 in mammals), or rides along in the fluid a bud encloses.

## Step 1, budding: a coat picks the cargo and bends the membrane

A **coat** is a lattice of cytosolic proteins that assembles on a patch of membrane. The film's cookie cutter with hands is a simplification: the cutter shapes the bud and the hands grab the cargo, but different parts of a coat often do each job.

Take **COPII**, the ER's coat. It is started by a switch, **Sar1**, a small GTPase (see [GTPase switches](/lessons/gtpase-switches/)). Sar1-GTP grabs the ER membrane and pulls in an inner layer, **Sec23/24**, which holds cargo and in turn recruits an outer cage, **Sec13/31**. Cargo choice sits mainly in Sec24: yeast mutant studies support a model in which Sec24 has several independent cargo-binding sites. Sar1's GTP is also a timer. When Sar1 hydrolyzes it, the coat begins to let go, and the vesicle can go on to be caught and fused.

| Coat | Started by | Where it works | Cargo signal it reads |
|---|---|---|---|
| COPII | Sar1-GTP | ER to Golgi | signals read by Sec24 |
| COPI | Arf1-GTP, recruiting coatomer (seven subunits) | within the Golgi, and Golgi back to ER | dilysine (KKXX) tails, read by α- and β′-COP |
| Clathrin with AP2 | the lipid PI(4,5)P2, which opens AP2 | plasma membrane | motifs read by AP2; clathrin binds no cargo |

A KKXX tail is a return label: COPI fetches escaped ER membrane proteins back from the Golgi. Crystal structures of recombinant AP2 show it locked, part of its β2 subunit covering both cargo sites; it opens on membranes with PI(4,5)P2, a phosphoinositide concentrated in the plasma membrane (see [lipid anchors and addresses](/lessons/lipid-anchors-and-addresses/)), so the lipid and the cargo together tell it where it is (compare the locked and open structures on this page). **Dynamin**, a GTPase, pinches the finished clathrin bud off.

## Step 2, targeting: a Rab and a tether catch the vesicle

A budded vesicle must find its target among many membranes. Part of the answer is **Rab** proteins, a large family of small GTPases. A Rab has a lipid tail (prenyl groups) that can anchor it in a membrane. When the Rab carries GDP, a protein called **GDI** (GDP dissociation inhibitor) can wrap that tail and carry the Rab in the cytosol. At the right membrane the Rab leaves GDI, its tail enters the bilayer, and a GEF (an exchange factor) there loads GTP, which holds it on. Rab-GTP recruits proteins called effectors, which generally prefer the active, GTP-bound form. The film's Rab "anchored in a membrane" and "held in the cytosol by GDI" are these two states, simplified: a Rab-GDP can also sit briefly on a membrane.

Among the effectors are **tethers**: long coiled-coil proteins or large complexes that capture a vesicle before its SNAREs engage. In yeast, the tether Uso1p works with the Rab Ypt1p on ER-to-Golgi vesicles. On the yeast vacuole, a six-subunit tether called HOPS binds the Rab Ypt7.

## Step 3, fusion: SNAREs zip, an SM protein guides them, NSF resets them

A caught vesicle still has to merge, and bilayers resist merging: their water-coated surfaces repel at close range. **SNAREs** supply the pull. Most have a **SNARE motif** of about 60 to 70 amino acids next to a membrane anchor; **v-SNAREs** sit in the vesicle and **t-SNAREs** in the target. A lone motif is floppy. Four of the right kind wind into a parallel four-helix bundle. The strands are named by the amino acid each places at the bundle's centre: three give a glutamine (Q) and one an arginine (R), so they are **Qa, Qb, Qc and R**. Often the vesicle gives R and the target gives the three Q strands, though not at every step. At a nerve terminal, syntaxin-1 gives Qa, SNAP-25 gives both Qb and Qc, and synaptobrevin on the vesicle gives R.

The film shows the zip, which runs from the N-terminal end toward the membrane anchors. Four strands are not four anchors: SNAP-25 gives two strands and attaches by lipid chains on the loop between them. While the strands come from two membranes, the bundle is a **trans-SNARE complex**. Afterwards the whole bundle sits in one membrane: a **cis-SNARE complex**.

**Predict.** Does the moment of fusion use ATP?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Four floppy strands folding into one tight rope releases free energy, and the zip turns it into a pull on the membranes. The zip itself uses no ATP; ATP pays to get SNAREs ready. A cis bundle is stable and useless, since both ends are already in one membrane. **NSF** (Sec18 in yeast), an ATPase, binds cis bundles through its adaptor **SNAP** (Sec17) and spends ATP to pull the strands apart, after one fusion and before the next. NSF cocks the spring that the next zip releases.

</details>

In a cell, SNAREs do not work alone. The main fusion steps that have been studied also need an **SM protein**, named for its first two members, Sec1 and Munc18, which binds SNAREs and helps the right four zip together. On the yeast vacuole the SM protein Vps33 is built into the tether HOPS, so one complex both catches and helps fuse.

That answers the opening puzzle. **Sec1 is the SM protein for the last step of secretion: fusion of secretory vesicles with the plasma membrane.** Upstream, invertase-laden vesicles still budded from the Golgi at 37 °C. They piled up, then fused after Sec1 worked again at 25 °C.

Sec1's known job, not the pile, names fusion. The same pile of 80 to 100 nm vesicles appears when the secretory-vesicle Rab (Sec4), its tether (the exocyst) or a SNARE (Sec9, the yeast relative of SNAP-25) fails; a pile cannot tell a vesicle never caught from one caught but unable to fuse. Test tubes split them; imaging single vesicles in living cells can too. Before that, the steps had to be found and put in order.

## Putting the handoffs in order: mutants

In 1980 the density screen from the second film used a Ludox gradient, and Novick, Field and Schekman sorted 188 clones that trapped invertase at 37 °C, the restrictive temperature, into 23 complementation groups (see [complementation and pathways](/lessons/complementation-and-pathways/)): the screen's yield, not the number of genes secretion needs. The SEC numbers carry no information about order; order came from double mutants in 1981.

Now the numbers, made up for practice. Invertase is measured in arbitrary units after 1 h at 37 °C.

> **fraction secreted = external / (external + internal)**

**Step 1.** Wild type: external 180, internal 20, total 200, so 180/200 = 90% secreted. Mutant secX: external 12, internal 188, total 200, so 12/200 = 6%. The block is real.

**Step 2.** The recovery test: shift secX back to 25 °C with cycloheximide to stop new synthesis. After 60 min external is 150 and internal 50, total still 200. The pool released is (188 − 50)/188 = 138/188 = 73.4%. External rose by the same 138 units, so the trapped material was deliverable.

**Step 3.** Electron microscopy at 37 °C shows secX accumulates ER, secY accumulates Golgi-like stacks (Berkeley bodies), and secZ accumulates 80 to 100 nm vesicles. The secX secZ double mutant accumulates ER. The secY secZ double accumulates Golgi-like membranes.

**Predict.** Put X, Y and Z in order. Which double mutant would test the one link the data leave open?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Cargo piles up at the first block it meets, so a double mutant looks like the earlier single block: X acts before Z, and Y before Z. ER precedes Golgi on the secretory route, which places X before Y; a secX secY double accumulating ER would test that directly. Order: ER (X), Golgi (Y), vesicles (Z), surface.

</details>

The rule assumes a linear pathway with arrest states you can tell apart. It orders where transport is blocked, not when a protein acts: a gene product could still act early and be needed only later. A gene needed at several steps shows only its earliest blocked one. NSF resets SNAREs at many steps, and in classical sec18 experiments at 37 °C, ER and small ER-derived vesicles piled up, not late secretory vesicles.

## Splitting catch from fuse: test tubes

Mutants show where cargo stops. Cell-free assays mix donor membranes, acceptor membranes, cytosol and ATP so that individual steps can be timed, removed or rebuilt. In the assays of the second film, docking means vesicles bound to the acceptor membrane, the tethered state.

**Predict.** In 1998 Cao, Ballew and Barlowe ran a yeast ER-to-Golgi assay and added GDI, which pulls Rabs off membranes, at different times. Added at the start, GDI blocked fusion. What happens to its effect if it is added after the vesicles have docked?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It fades. Once vesicles had docked, fusion was no longer sensitive to GDI, so the GDI-sensitive step is passed by docking. That does not show the Rab left or stopped mattering: GDI pulls off GDP-bound Rabs, and an active Rab-GTP resists it. In general, losing sensitivity to a fast, specific inhibitor shows that an inhibitor-sensitive state has been passed; it times the end of a whole step only if the inhibitor can still reach and act on its target later, which is why budding, docking and fusion are measured separately. The authors proposed that this first docking needs the tether Uso1p and the Rab Ypt1p but not SNARE proteins.

</details>

Purified yeast HOPS shows that catching is not fusing: separate forms lacking the beta-propeller domain of Vps11 or Vps18 still tethered Ypt7-bearing liposomes but failed to promote fusion.

### Design problem

You have this cell-free assay, with yeast ER as donor and Golgi as acceptor. You want to know whether GDI acts before or after vesicles dock, and whether a purified tether is enough for fusion. Design the experiment.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use three readouts. Budding: vesicles released from ER, collected after spinning out large membranes. Docking: vesicles that sediment with the acceptor Golgi. Fusion: cargo that gains an acceptor-only sugar, or lumenal content mixing.

Start the reaction and add GDI at 0, 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes. Plot fusion still blocked against time of addition, with docking measured on the same schedule. If GDI acts at docking, sensitivity is lost as docking completes, as Cao, Ballew and Barlowe found. If it acts at fusion itself, additions made after docking but before fusion still block. Measure when fusion itself happens on the same schedule, or hold vesicles docked but unfused before adding GDI, to tell the two apart (Going deeper, "Why the fusion time course matters").

For the tether, run docking and fusion separately with the purified tether, matched SNARE proteoliposomes and an active Rab, since tethering without fusion is possible. Controls: no ATP, no cytosol, no tether, and a leakage check so that content released by broken membranes is not read as fusion.

</details>

In one line: coat buds, Rab and tether catch, SNAREs with an SM protein fuse, NSF resets; where cargo stops and when an inhibitor stops mattering point to the failed handoff.

## Going deeper

### A coat from three parts

With purified yeast proteins, Matsuoka and colleagues showed in 1998 that Sar1, Sec23/24 and Sec13/31 alone bud coated vesicles from chemically defined liposomes that contain PI4P or PI(4,5)P2, so the coat can bend membrane without help from other ER proteins. After Sar1 hydrolyzes GTP, parts of the coat can stay on long enough to help the vesicle be caught at the Golgi.

### Coats that curve late, and tubes as well as spheres

In cultured BSC-1 cells, clathrin lattices began to curve only at around 70% of their final clathrin content, and raised membrane tension stalled the change, so a coat does not always curve from the start. And in mammalian cells, whole-cell imaging in 2021 found ER export through an interwoven tubular network, not only through separate small spheres.

### HOPS up close

The purified six-subunit HOPS complex is about 43 nm tall, and a structural working model places its Vps39 and Vps41 subunits at the Ypt7 contacts. Its SM protein, Vps33, is held in the complex by Vps16 and binds SNAREs.

### Paying for fusion

Assembly of one neuronal SNARE bundle releases about 65 kT, about 170 kJ/mol at body temperature (Gao and colleagues, 2012). In a single-molecule assay, NSF took one complex apart in one round of hydrolysis of the ATP already bound to it, at most six (Ryu and colleagues, 2015); other assays report more ATP per complex. As an illustration, six ATP at 50 to 57 kJ/mol each supply 300 to 342 kJ/mol, about 116 to 133 kT at 310 K, more than this construct's assembly releases. Reversing the same assembly under the same conditions must cost at least what it released, but these separate experiments do not measure a matched reset cycle or a universal ATP budget.

### Can SNAREs fuse membranes alone?

In 1998 Weber and colleagues put recombinant v-SNAREs and t-SNAREs into separate liposomes, and the docked liposomes mixed their lipids at physiological temperature, which the authors read as fusion. That shows SNAREs can merge those bilayers on their own, though lipid mixing alone cannot tell full fusion from hemifusion. It does not show they supply specificity or speed in a cell.

### What NSF might do before fusion

NSF may do more than recycle SNAREs. In a 2024 yeast vacuole reconstitution, Sec18 still promoted fusion with Sec17 fixed to the membrane; whether this holds at other fusion steps is not yet known.

### Why the fusion time course matters

Take a simple model: a batch of vesicles, all undocked at time zero, docks with a first-order half-time of 10 min (75% docked by 20 min), each docked vesicle then fuses with its own first-order half-time, and each inhibitor instantly and completely blocks its step. If the fusion half-time is about 2 min, a docking inhibitor and a fusion inhibitor added at 20 min block about 25% and 31% of eventual fusion: nearly the same. With a 15 min fusion half-time they block about 25% and 69%, and the steps separate clearly.
