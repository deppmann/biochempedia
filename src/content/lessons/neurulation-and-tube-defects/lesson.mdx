techniques:
  - name: "Targeted microinjection in frog embryos"
    blurb: >-
      Messenger RNA for a normal or mutant protein is injected into one chosen cell of an early
      Xenopus embryo. The fate map says which tissue that cell's descendants will form, so the
      protein ends up in a known region, such as the midline or the sides of the neural plate.
  - name: "Explants (Keller sandwiches)"
    blurb: >-
      Two pieces of the dorsal region of an early frog embryo are cut out and pressed together,
      inner faces touching, and grown in a dish. The tissue elongates on its own, so its
      convergent extension can be measured apart from the rest of the embryo.
  - name: "Positional cloning of a mouse mutation"
    blurb: >-
      A gene known only from a mutant animal is located by tracking the mutation against marked
      stretches of chromosome over many crosses, then narrowing to one gene. This is how the
      loop-tail mutation was traced to Vangl2.
  - name: "Randomized controlled trial"
    blurb: >-
      Participants are assigned to a treatment or a comparison by chance, so differences that
      people bring with them tend to be spread evenly between groups. A difference in outcome can then be
      credited to the treatment, after allowing for chance, within the population the trial enrolled.

**In a human embryo, the tissue that becomes the brain and spinal cord starts as a flat sheet of cells, and by the end of about the fourth week after fertilization all but its lowest part has rolled into a closed tube. That is often before a woman knows she is pregnant.**

This lesson asks one question: what happens if the tube does not close? To answer it you need to know how closing works, where it can fail, and when it happens.

## From a flat plate to a tube

We will look at the plate two ways: from above, to see its length and width, and in cross-section, as if sliced across the back, to see it fold.

Three tissues come first. The **ectoderm** is the outermost of the embryo's three germ layers. After neural induction (the signals that make ectoderm neural), a band of it down the centre of the back becomes the **neural plate**, the sheet that will form the brain and spinal cord. The ectoderm on either side becomes **surface ectoderm**, which forms the epidermis, the future skin, and in the head the sensory placodes. Beneath the midline runs the **notochord**, a rod of mesoderm.

Primary neurulation turns the plate into a tube in overlapping steps, worked out mostly in chick and mouse embryos:

1. **Shaping.** The plate cells grow taller, so the plate thickens. At the same time the plate narrows from side to side and lengthens from head to tail. Hold on to this step.
2. **Bending.** The plate creases along hinge points. In chick and mouse a medial hinge point lies over the notochord, and paired dorsolateral hinge points form at some levels of the body. Cells at a hinge become wedge-shaped. The edges rise as the neural folds.
3. **Meeting and fusion.** The folds move toward the midline, meet and fuse.
4. **Separation.** The tube detaches from the epidermis, which seals over it.

Neural crest cells arise at the border where the neural plate meets the epidermis. In the mammalian head they leave the folds before the folds fuse; in the trunk, and in chick, they leave around or after fusion. They form much of the peripheral nervous system, and how they leave is the subject of [the lesson on neural crest](/lessons/neural-crest/).

Now look from above. Closure does not happen all at once. In the mouse, fusion begins at several separate sites and spreads from each like a zipper. The first, called **Closure 1**, is at the boundary between the hindbrain and the spinal cord. The open stretches left between and beyond the zipped regions, called **neuropores**, close in turn: the head-end neuropore, then the tail-end one last. Whether human closure starts from the same set of sites is debated, so the mouse map should not be read straight onto the human embryo.

The lowest part of the cord is built another way. There, cells of the tail bud gather into a solid rod that then hollows out and joins the tube made above it. This is **secondary neurulation**, and in humans it is thought to form the lowest sacral and tail-end segments of the cord.

## Where closure fails

If one stretch of the tube does not close, it stays open for the rest of pregnancy. Where it stays open gives the defect its name.

**Predict.** Use the zipper picture. What would you expect at birth in each case? (a) The head end never closes. (b) The lower end never closes. (c) Closure 1 never forms, so the zipper that should spread from it never starts. (d) The tube closes everywhere, but the bony arch of one vertebra fails to close over it, under normal skin.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

(a) is anencephaly, (b) is myelomeningocele and (c) is craniorachischisis, all open defects, described below; in (c) the forebrain can still close from its own sites.

(d) is the trap: spina bifida occulta, which is not a failure of the tube at all. The tube closed; the bone over it did not. Open and closed defects are easy to blur because both can carry the name spina bifida.

</details>

**Open defects: the tube itself never closed.**

- **Anencephaly.** The head end fails to close. The exposed brain tissue degenerates, and most of the forebrain and the top of the skull are missing. Affected babies are stillborn or die soon after birth.
- **Myelomeningocele (open spina bifida).** The lower end fails to close, and the cord itself is in the open lesion. It commonly brings weakness or paralysis below the lesion, loss of bladder and bowel control, and often hydrocephalus.
- **Craniorachischisis.** The tube is open from the midbrain down the spine. In mouse terms, Closure 1 never happens, as in the loop-tail mouse you will meet below.

The map is schematic." />

**Closed defects: the cord is not exposed.** Two forms of spina bifida are usually covered by skin and are generally less severe than myelomeningocele, though not always harmless.

- **Spina bifida occulta.** One or more vertebral arches are unclosed, but the cord lies in place under normal skin. It is often found by chance and is, strictly, a defect of bone over a closed tube.
- **Meningocele.** The meninges, the membranes around the cord, bulge through the gap in the bone in a sac, while the cord stays in place.

The next question is why closure fails at all.

## Narrow first, then fold

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Narrow first, then fold. The two neural folds can close only a gap their bending can cover, and the midline between them sets that gap. Convergent extension narrows the midline; bending, rising and fusion close what is left. The steps overlap: narrowing starts before the folds rise and keeps going while they rise.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which tube.** Primary neurulation: the brain and all of the spinal cord except its lowest part.
- **Which failures.** Defects in which the folds rise but start too far apart, as in the loop-tail mouse below. Failures of bending, fusion or sealing are other routes. The rule says only that the midline must become narrow enough, in time, for the folds to meet.
- **Which width.** The midline is the middle strip of the plate, over the notochord, between the two edges that rise as folds.
- **Not folate.** It says nothing about how folic acid lowers risk.

**Why the midline?** Put your hands flat on a table, palms down, thumbs touching. Roll each hand up onto its thumb edge until it stands upright, palms facing. The little-finger edges, now on top, meet, however broad your hands are. Now lay a book between your thumbs and do it again. The hands stop apart by the width of the book, and broader hands help only if you lean them inward past upright. Your hands are the folds, your thumbs are where the folds rise, and the book is the midline: whatever width it still has when the folds rise is a gap they must cover. The cartoon is rigid. In the embryo the midline creases at the medial hinge, and fold size, hinge bending and forces from surrounding tissue also matter, but a broader midline still leaves the folds farther apart.

**How the midline narrows.** Cells in the plate crawl in between their neighbours on either side, a move called **intercalation**. It rearranges cells without adding or removing them. In an idealized patch with no cell division, 40 cells across and 50 long is 2,000 cells; the same 2,000 cells at 20 across are 100 long. This is **convergent extension**: converge across, extend along. In the real plate cells also divide and change shape, and cells growing taller, which makes each take up less of the sheet's surface, also narrow the plate; the share of each differs by species and level of the body. Convergent extension is the narrowing the experiments below test.

**How the cells know which way is across.** Look at a fly's wing. Every cell grows one hair, and all the hairs point toward the wingtip. In flies mutant for genes such as *frizzled* or *Van Gogh*, the hairs swirl. The proteins these genes make sort to opposite sides of each cell: Frizzled and Dishevelled gather on one side, and Van Gogh (called Vangl in vertebrates) and Prickle on the other. This **planar cell polarity (PCP)** is a sense of direction within the plane of a sheet. In the neural plate it tells each cell which way is side to side, and so which neighbours to crawl between. Two of these proteins matter here: Dishevelled in the frog and Vangl2 in the mouse.

**Predict.** In frog embryos (*Xenopus laevis*) the fate of each early cell is mapped, so messenger RNA injected into one chosen cell ends up in a known part of the neural plate. In 2002 John Wallingford and Richard Harland injected a form of Dishevelled built to block its planar-polarity signaling into cells that would form either the midline of the plate or its sides, where the folds rise and fuse. Two species and two PCP proteins both tie closure to convergent extension of the midline.

**Narrowing is not the whole job.** The plate still has to bend at its hinges. At some hinges, cells constrict their apical face, the face on the top surface of the plate that will line the inside of the tube; other hinges bend without it (see Going deeper). Signals from the surface ectoderm beside the plate contribute, and the fold tips must stick together where they meet. The share of each differs by species and by level of the body. Mice lacking the actin-binding protein Shroom3, which takes part in apical constriction, fail to close the head folds, and some also develop spina bifida.

## When the tube closes

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Embryologists date human embryos by **Carnegie stages**, a series of 23 stages defined by structure, not by age, covering the first eight weeks after fertilization. Closure runs over a few of them:

| Event | Carnegie stage |
| --- | --- |
| Neural folds first meet | about 10 |
| Head-end neuropore closes | about 11 |
| Tail-end neuropore closes, completing primary neurulation | about 12 to 13 |

Exact days differ between sources. Now put the end of primary closure on two clocks. Embryologists count from fertilization. Doctors usually count pregnancy from the first day of the last menstrual period, which adds about two weeks.

| Clock | Starts counting at | Primary closure is complete by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Embryologist's | fertilization | the end of about the fourth week (about day 28) |
| Doctor's (obstetric) | the last menstrual period, about two weeks earlier | ? |

## The folic-acid trials: strong evidence

Folate is a B vitamin. Folic acid is the synthetic form used in tablets and fortified food. Two randomized trials, in which women were assigned to a supplement or a comparison by chance, carry most of the evidence that it prevents neural tube defects.

**Recurrence.** The first trial enrolled women who had already had an affected pregnancy, whose risk of another was much higher than average. In the Medical Research Council Vitamin Study (1991), 4 milligrams of folic acid a day, started before conception, cut recurrence by about 72 percent.

**Predict.** Does "about 72 percent fewer" mean that 72 of every 100 women were spared an affected pregnancy? What else would you need to know?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Seventy-two percent is a **relative** reduction; to know how many women benefit you need the starting risk. In the trial, about 3.5 in 100 pregnancies were affected in the groups without folic acid, against about 1 in 100 with it. That is a relative fall of about 72 percent but an **absolute** fall of about 2.5 percentage points: roughly 40 high-risk women (100 divided by 2.5) took folic acid for each affected pregnancy prevented. Where the starting risk is far lower, as in women with no earlier affected pregnancy, the same relative cut spares far fewer per 1,000.

</details>

**First occurrence.** Most babies with a neural tube defect are born to women who have never had an affected pregnancy, so a second question remained. In Hungary, through a family-planning service, women planning a pregnancy took a multivitamin with 0.8 milligrams of folic acid or a trace-element supplement, starting at least a month before conception. There were no affected pregnancies in the multivitamin group and six in the trace-element group (Czeizel and Dudás, 1992). The numbers are small, and the supplement held several vitamins, so this trial alone could not credit folic acid by itself. Read beside the British trial, it is evidence that a first occurrence can also be prevented.

Since many pregnancies are unplanned, the United States and Canada began requiring folic acid in enriched grain products in 1998, and many countries have since done the same. Recorded rates fell after fortification, though the size of the fall depends on how and where defects are counted.

## What is still open

Folic acid lowers the risk; it does not remove it. Much remains unexplained.

- **How folate works.** Its reduced forms carry one-carbon units for making thymidylate and purines and for methylation reactions, as [that lesson](/lessons/nucleotide-metabolism/) explains, and either could limit a fast-growing plate. How folate prevents defects is not settled. Most mothers of affected babies are not folate deficient by ordinary tests, and some defects persist despite fortification.
- **Defects folate does not fix.** In mice, some mutants respond to folate and others do not. The curly tail mouse develops spina bifida that folic acid does not prevent, and another nutrient, inositol, lowered its rate (see the anecdote below).
- **Other routes.** Valproic acid, an anticonvulsant taken in early pregnancy, raises the risk of spina bifida, and so does diabetes in the mother before pregnancy.
- **Human PCP genes.** Rare variants in human genes for planar cell polarity have been reported in some affected people.

### Design problem

Two mouse mutants both have open neural tubes. In the first, the neural plate is short and wide from the start. In the second, the plate narrows normally, but the folds never meet. Say what failed in each, and propose one measurement and one experiment that would tell them apart.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The first mutant's short, wide plate suggests that convergent extension failed, the PCP-dependent narrowing. If its midline is wide too, its folds start too far apart to meet; whole-plate shape alone does not show that, so the measurement below checks it. The second points to a failure after shaping: the folds do not bend at the hinges, rise or move inward far enough to meet.

The measurement: in staged embryos fixed just before the folds rise, measure the width of the midline and the width and length of the neural plate against wild-type littermates. A convergent extension mutant has a wider midline and a wider, shorter plate before folding begins; the second mutant matches wild type at that stage.

The experiment: culture embryos and film the folds, or label midline cells with a dye and follow them. In the first mutant, labelled cells fail to intercalate and the midline stays wide while the folds, once raised, still move inward. In the second, the plate narrows on schedule but the folds stall, and you can then ask whether hinge cells constrict their apical faces or whether the folds rise but fall short of the midline.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Hinge cells: what squeezes and what does not

A hinge cell's wedge is narrow at the apical face and wide at the base. At the midline hinge of chick and mouse, much of that wedging comes from nuclei sitting near the base of the cells during part of the cell cycle. Actin and myosin help squeeze the apical face at some hinges and levels, with Rho kinase helping in chick, as [that lesson](/lessons/integrins-and-rho-gtpases/) describes for other cells, and Shroom- and Rho kinase-dependent apical constriction matters in the head and in frogs. Yet chick midline hinge cells wedge even without actin filaments, and mouse spinal hinges still bend without them.

### Planar polarity proteins in a living plate

In vertebrates, planar polarity is the branch of Wnt signaling that runs through Dishevelled without beta-catenin and reaches the cytoskeleton, partly through Rho; [that lesson](/lessons/wnt-signaling/) covers the branches. In his own laboratory, John Wallingford and Mitchell Butler later filmed fluorescent planar-polarity proteins in living frog neural plates and found them enriched at shrinking cell junctions (Butler and Wallingford, 2018). The imaging links PCP protein enrichment to junction shrinkage and actomyosin, without settling which drives which.

### Trial details and policy dates

Before the British trial, a non-randomized study led by Richard Smithells had suggested that women who took a multivitamin before conceiving had fewer repeat defects; the women who chose vitamins might have differed in other ways. An earlier trial in Cardiff was too small to give an answer. The MRC trial was led by Nicholas Wald and used a factorial design, folic acid alone, other vitamins alone, both, or neither; the other vitamins showed no clear effect. In 1992 the US Public Health Service advised 0.4 milligrams of folic acid a day for women who could become pregnant.
