## The big idea

Your child has probably noticed that some birds disappear in winter and others stay at the feeder. Winter is hard for animals mainly because food runs short, and staying warm uses energy. Each kind of animal has its own way of getting through.

Some animals travel to places with more food and come back later. This is **migration**. Some rest deeply in a sheltered spot and live on stored fat. In **hibernation**, an animal's body cools and its heartbeat slows far below normal sleep. Turtles and frogs go **dormant**: their bodies cool with the weather and they barely move until spring. Other animals stay active and change: thicker coats, food stored in fall, or a new diet.

Grade 1 children only need the plain words "travel," "rest" and "stay and change." Grade 2 adds the terms migrate, hibernate and dormancy, and asks how food supply changes behavior through the year.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child for the whole activity, and beside them for any outdoor watch. Nobody touches or picks up a wild animal, alive or dead.
- Never touch a bat, even one that seems asleep or hurt. Bats can carry rabies, and a bite can be too small to see. If a bat touches anyone, or is found in a room with a sleeping person or a child alone, wash any scratch with soap and water and call a doctor or your local health department. Do not let the bat go until you have talked to them.
- Leave resting animals alone. A turtle or frog found in leaves or soil in winter stays where it is; pile the leaves back over it and walk away. Step back from any snake; copperheads live in Virginia, and no snake should be touched. Do not go into caves, mines or attics where bats may be resting.
- Bird feeders are optional and an adult job. Virginia's wildlife agency advises against feeders from April 1 to November 1 because bears come to them, and says feeding a bear, even by letting it eat birdseed, is illegal. If a bear visits, take the feeder down for 3 to 4 weeks. Dirty feeders spread disease among birds, so at least every two weeks, and more often after rain or when many birds visit, an adult scrubs a feeder with soap and water, rinses it well and lets it dry (Clemson Extension's "Washing Bird Feeders" page explains stronger methods for adults). Wash hands with soap after handling a feeder, seed or anything outdoors.
- Never look at the sun through binoculars.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Make the cards.** Draw, print or cut out 10 animals: ruby-throated hummingbird, monarch butterfly, tundra swan, groundhog, bat, eastern box turtle, wood frog, white-tailed deer, gray squirrel and northern cardinal. Write the name on each card.
2. **First guess.** Lay out the three sheets: Travel, Rest and Stay and change. Ask your child to sort the cards with no help. Ask, "How did you decide?" Write down their reasons. Don't correct anything yet.
3. **Check each animal.** Go through the cards together, using the list below. Let your child move cards as they learn. Ask before each one, "Where do you think this one goes?"
4. **Travel.** Hummingbirds leave Virginia in late summer and fall and spend winter from Mexico to Central America. Monarchs fly to mountain forests in central Mexico. Tundra swans travel the other way: they nest in the Arctic and fly about 3,000 miles to spend winter on Virginia's rivers and bays.
5. **Rest.** Groundhogs hibernate in burrows. Many Virginia bats hibernate in caves, mines or buildings, though some kinds fly south instead. Box turtles dig into soil. Wood frogs, found mostly in northern and western Virginia, lie under dead leaves and can partly freeze.
6. **Stay and change.** Deer stay and grow a grayish-brown winter coat. Gray squirrels bury nuts in fall and dig them up in winter. Cardinals stay all year and fluff their feathers in the cold.
7. **Window watch.** Sit at a window or outside for 15 minutes. Before you start, ask, "How many kinds of birds do you think we'll see?" Count how many birds of each kind you see and note what they are doing. The count depends on the season, the time of day and what grows nearby. An empty yard is a result too.
8. **Watch again later.** Repeat the watch in another season, at the same time of day. Compare the two sheets. Which birds were there both times? Which ones came or went?

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder what a hummingbird would eat here in January. What do you think?"
- "Why do you think the cardinal can stay when the hummingbird leaves?"
- "What do you think a groundhog eats all winter?"
- "If Virginia is 'south' for tundra swans, where do you think their summer home is?"
- "Which birds do you think we'll see this time that we didn't see last time?"
- "What do you think would happen to a squirrel that forgot where it buried its nuts?"

## Why it works

Winter cuts the food supply for many animals. Insects, nectar and fresh leaves disappear. At the same time, staying warm burns more energy. Each strategy solves that problem a different way.

Migrating animals move to where food is. Shorter days and a shrinking food supply both act as signals to leave, so many birds go before food runs out. Monarchs that fly south in fall are a special late-summer generation that lives for months; in spring they start back north and lay eggs, and their descendants finish the trip over several generations.

True hibernators, like groundhogs and many bats, lower their body temperature and heart rate and live on stored fat. Groundhogs still wake briefly a dozen or more times each winter. Cold-blooded animals, such as box turtles and wood frogs, cool to the temperature around them and become dormant. Wood frogs survive partial freezing because their liver releases sugar into the blood, which protects their cells from ice.

Animals that stay rely on thick coats, fat, stored food and switching to foods like seeds and bark.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use six cards: hummingbird, monarch, groundhog, box turtle, squirrel and deer. Use the plain words "travel," "rest" and "stay." Let your child act each one out: flap away, curl up, or scurry and dig. For the window watch, draw the birds you see and make a tally mark each time one lands.

### Ages 8 to 10
Use all 10 cards and add the words migrate, hibernate and dormant. Add a fourth pile, "comes here for winter," for tundra swans. Make a bar graph of how many birds of each kind you saw. Repeat the watch in winter and in spring at the same time of day, then compare the graphs and explain one difference using food.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If no birds come, try early morning or the hour before sunset, when many birds feed. Sit still and quiet for the first five minutes. If your child can't name a bird, have them describe it: size compared with a sparrow or a crow, main color, and what it was doing. A drawing is enough. If cards end up in "wrong" piles, ask what evidence would settle it rather than giving the answer.
