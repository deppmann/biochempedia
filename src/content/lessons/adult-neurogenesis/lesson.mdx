techniques:
  - name: "Tritiated thymidine autoradiography"
    blurb: >-
      A radioactive form of thymidine is injected, and cells copying their DNA at that moment build
      it into their chromosomes. A photographic emulsion over a tissue section shows labelled nuclei
      as silver grains. It marks cells born after the injection, but by light microscopy alone it is
      hard to tell a labelled neuron from a glial cell beside it.
  - name: "BrdU labelling with cell-type markers"
    blurb: >-
      Bromodeoxyuridine, a thymidine analogue, is given and later detected with an antibody, in the
      same cell as a neuron protein such as NeuN or a glial protein such as GFAP. A cell positive for
      both BrdU and NeuN was most likely born after the dose and is now a neuron, though DNA repair or abortive DNA synthesis in dying neurons can also add BrdU.
  - name: "Bomb-pulse carbon-14 dating"
    blurb: >-
      Nuclei are isolated from post-mortem tissue and sorted into neuronal and non-neuronal by flow
      cytometry with a NeuN antibody, and the carbon-14 in their DNA is measured by accelerator mass
      spectrometry. Comparing the value with the atmospheric curve shows whether the DNA was made at birth or
      later, and turnover models turn it into birth-date estimates, with no label given to the person.
  - name: "Antimitotic ablation and regeneration"
    blurb: >-
      A drug that kills dividing cells is infused, which removes fast-cycling precursors and their
      daughters. The cells that survive and then rebuild the population are candidates for the stem
      cell. In the adult mouse subventricular zone those cells were astrocytes.

      - "The neuron's DNA is replaced each year with newly made strands"

    answer: 1

**Adult rodents make new neurons all their lives in two places, from stem cells that look like astrocytes, and the new neurons join working circuits in the olfactory bulb and the hippocampus.**

For most of the twentieth century the working rule was simple: the neurons you are born with are the neurons you keep, and those you lose are not replaced. This lesson is about the exceptions. It starts with where adult neurons are made, which cell makes them and what they do. Then it shows how anyone can tell that a neuron is new. It ends with people, where the hippocampus may keep making some neurons and the question is still argued over.

## Two places adult rodents keep making neurons

Adult rodents make neurons all their lives in two places.

The first is the **subventricular zone** (SVZ), a thin layer in the wall of the lateral ventricles. Neuroblasts, young neurons that can still migrate, line up in chains and travel forward along the **rostral migratory stream** to the olfactory bulb, where they become interneurons. Carlos Lois and Arturo Alvarez-Buylla showed this long-distance migration in adult mice in 1994; the stream returns as a case of tangential migration in [that lesson](/lessons/tangential-migration/).

The second is the **subgranular zone** (SGZ) at the inner edge of the dentate gyrus. Its stem cells are also astrocyte-like, and their progeny become dentate granule neurons.

Which cell in the SVZ is the stem cell? In 1999 Fiona Doetsch, Alvarez-Buylla and colleagues described four cell types there: ependymal cells lining the ventricle, astrocytes (B cells), rapidly dividing immature precursors (C cells, the transit-amplifying cells), and migrating neuroblasts (A cells). They infused an antimitotic drug that killed the C and A cells. The astrocytes survived, began dividing, and regenerated C cells and then neuroblasts. In untreated mice, a retrovirus that could enter only astrocytes labelled new olfactory bulb neurons. So the lineage runs:

> **B cell (astrocyte-like stem cell) → C cell (transit-amplifying) → A cell (neuroblast) → olfactory bulb interneuron**

The adult stem cell looks like a glial cell, which fits development: the embryonic progenitors of the brain, the radial glia, are glial in character too ([that lesson](/lessons/radial-glia-progenitors/)). The dentate gyrus follows the same plan: astrocyte-like stem cells make dividing precursors, and those make young neurons.

Joseph Altman first saw new neurons at both sites in rats in the 1960s. How he did it, and why it took decades to be believed, comes next.

## How anyone can tell that a neuron is new

To find a newborn cell you need a mark that only newborn cells carry. A cell copies its DNA once per division, in S phase, and a neuron that has stopped dividing normally does not copy its genome again. Give an animal a labelled form of thymidine, the DNA building block used only in DNA, and every cell in S phase at that moment builds the label into its chromosomes. A strongly labelled neuron found weeks later most likely descends from a cell that divided after the injection; DNA repair can add a little label too, so studies need controls for it.

Think of it as a stamp put on every page printed on one day: a stamped page was printed that day.

Joseph Altman did this in rats in the 1960s with tritiated thymidine, thymidine carrying radioactive hydrogen. He found labelled new neurons in the dentate gyrus and in cells moving from the wall of the lateral ventricle to the olfactory bulb. The claims were largely set aside for years, because with a light microscope it is hard to prove that a labelled cell is a neuron and not a glial cell lying against one.

Songbirds reopened the question. In adult canaries, label given to dividing cells appeared first in the ventricular zone and weeks later in neurons of the song nucleus HVC, and electron microscopy confirmed that they were neurons. So the new neurons were born at the ventricle and migrated in (Goldman and Nottebohm, 1983).

Then the label changed. Bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) is a thymidine look-alike that an antibody can detect, so the same cell can be stained for BrdU and for a neuron-only protein such as NeuN. That answered the old objection in a routine way.

## New neurons join working circuits

A new neuron is useful only if it is wired in. In the dentate gyrus, new granule neurons grow dendrites and send axons into the hippocampal circuit over several weeks. For a time while they mature, young granule neurons fire more readily than older ones in response to weak input (reviewed by Miller and Sahay, 2019).

The first sign that new adult neurons join working circuits came from songbirds. In 1984 John Paton and Fernando Nottebohm recorded from new neurons in the canary song nucleus HVC and found that some of them responded to sound.

What are the new neurons for? In rodents the answers are clearest. Young dentate granule neurons are thought to help with pattern separation, telling apart similar places or events: mice in which adult neurogenesis was blocked did worse at distinguishing nearby locations (Clelland and colleagues, 2009). In the rodent olfactory bulb, the steady supply of new interneurons has been linked to odour learning.

## Signals and experience set the output

Local signals set the balance between resting and dividing. Notch signalling helps keep stem cells in reserve ([that lesson](/lessons/notch-signaling/)), and in adult rats extra Wnt3 increased neuron production while blocking Wnt signalling nearly abolished it (Lie and colleagues, 2005) ([that lesson](/lessons/wnt-signaling/)).

Experience changes the output: mice with a running wheel had more new dentate gyrus neurons than mice in standard cages (van Praag, Kempermann and Gage, 1999), and mice housed in larger cages with toys and companions had more hippocampal neurons (Kempermann, Kuhn and Gage, 1997).

Experience can act at different steps. More new neurons can come from more division of precursors, or from more young cells surviving to become neurons. In rodents, learning tasks that depend on the hippocampus mostly change how many new neurons survive, and the effect depends on when the cells were born (reviewed by Ming and Song, 2011). So a study that reports more new neurons has to say which step changed.

### Design problem

You suspect that running helps mice tell apart two similar places because it adds new dentate granule neurons. Design experiments to test the three links in that claim: that running adds new neurons, that the better discrimination needs them, and that the effect is specific.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

First, show that running adds neurons, not just dividing cells. Give BrdU to runners and controls, and several weeks later count cells positive for both BrdU and NeuN. Count BrdU cells one day after the dose too, so you know whether running changed division, survival, or both.

Second, test the behaviour. Train runners and controls to tell apart two nearby locations, and also two far-apart ones. If new neurons help with pattern separation, runners should do better mainly when the locations are close.

Third, test whether the new neurons are needed. Block adult neurogenesis in runners and ask whether their advantage on nearby locations goes away. Check that the block does not stop the mice running, harm other cells, or impair the easy far-apart task; otherwise a loss could have another cause.

</details>

## New neurons in the adult human brain

Do adult humans do this too? Recent reviews read the evidence as increasingly favouring some adult neurogenesis in the human hippocampus, from several independent methods, but its rate, how much it varies between people and what it does are still unsettled.

BrdU cannot be given to healthy people for research. Jonas Frisén's group at the Karolinska Institute found a label already in the atmosphere. Nuclear weapons tests in the 1950s and early 1960s sharply raised the carbon-14 in the air, and the level fell after the 1963 treaty that banned tests in the atmosphere. Carbon from the air ends up in the DNA of every cell that divides, so the DNA of a cell that never divides again keeps the carbon-14 of its birth year.

> **carbon-14 in the DNA of a cell = carbon-14 in the air in the year that DNA was made**

Sorting nuclei from post-mortem brain into neurons and other cells, the group found that neurons at the back of the cerebral cortex are as old as the person (Spalding and colleagues, 2005). So are olfactory bulb neurons (Bergmann and colleagues, 2012), unlike in rodents; in human infants the stream of young neurons toward the front of the brain thins out during the first 18 months of life and is nearly gone by adulthood (Sanai and colleagues, 2011). Hippocampal neurons, by contrast, carry carbon-14 showing that some were added after birth (Spalding and colleagues, 2013). A sample averages many nuclei, so turning the reading into a rate needs a model of how neurons are added; the estimate and its assumptions are under Going deeper. A later study reported new interneurons in the adult striatum, beside the ventricle wall (Ernst and colleagues, 2014), a site that is less established.

Staining tissue for proteins of young neurons, doublecortin (DCX) and PSA-NCAM, has given opposite answers. In 2018 Sorrells, Alvarez-Buylla and colleagues found that young neurons in the human dentate gyrus fell sharply in childhood and were not detected in adults. The next month Boldrini and colleagues reported thousands of immature neurons in healthy people from adolescence into old age, and in 2019 Moreno-Jiménez, Llorens-Martín and colleagues, working with tissue fixed under tightly controlled conditions, reported thousands of DCX-positive neurons into old age, with fewer as Alzheimer's disease advanced.

Why would careful laboratories disagree? Two limits matter most. DCX staining weakens with time after death and with long fixation, so a negative result may mean the antigen was lost. And in humans it is debated whether DCX and PSA-NCAM mark only newborn neurons, so a marker count does not by itself say when a cell was born. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing has pointed both ways (see Going deeper).

None of the rodent functions above transfers to people automatically. Whatever adult neurons humans make, the carbon-14 data point to the hippocampus and striatum, not the olfactory bulb where rodents put most of theirs.

## Going deeper

Each piece below is detail behind a claim made above.

### The discovery, step by step

Altman's method was autoradiography: a photographic emulsion laid over a thin brain section shows tritiated-thymidine-labelled nuclei as black silver grains. In 1965, with Gopal Das, he reported labelled granule cells in the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus in rats after birth, and in 1969 he described dividing cells in the wall of the lateral ventricle that moved forward into the olfactory bulb. Besides the light-microscope problem there was contrary evidence from primates: in 1985 Pasko Rakic reported that thymidine given to rhesus monkeys after puberty, examined from 3 days to 6 years later, labelled glial cells but no neurons. In rats the identity objection had already been answered by electron microscopy: in 1977 Michael Kaplan and James Hinds showed that thymidine-labelled cells in the adult dentate gyrus and olfactory bulb were neurons.

The canary experiment started from a hormone effect. Adult female canaries given testosterone start to sing, and HVC grows. In 1983 Steven Goldman and Fernando Nottebohm injected tritiated thymidine into adult female canaries and found that 1.8 to 4.0 percent of HVC neurons were labelled, with or without the hormone, so the hormone was not needed for the new neurons to appear.

In 1999 two groups using BrdU reported new dentate gyrus neurons in adult monkeys (Gould and colleagues, and Kornack and Rakic, both in macaques), with Kornack and Rakic estimating a rate about ten times lower than in rodents.

### Reading the bomb curve

Carbon-14 is made at a nearly steady rate in the upper atmosphere. Above-ground nuclear weapons tests, at their height between 1955 and 1963, nearly doubled the level in the northern hemisphere air. After the 1963 treaty the level fell year by year as the extra carbon-14 moved into the oceans and the biosphere. Plants fix that carbon dioxide, and people eat the plants or animals that ate them. Carbon-14 decays slowly (half-life about 5,730 years), so over a lifetime the decay is negligible; the clock is the changing level in the air.

In 2005 Kirsty Spalding, Frisén and colleagues showed that DNA carbon-14 tracks the atmospheric curve. They sorted nuclei from post-mortem brain into neuronal and non-neuronal with an antibody to NeuN, measured the carbon-14 by accelerator mass spectrometry, and found that occipital cortex neurons were as old as the person while non-neuronal cells were younger.

Take one example: "This person is born in 1972." Their neuronal DNA holds the carbon-14 of 1976 on the falling side of the curve. Because a sample holds many nuclei, the value is an average:

> **measured carbon-14 = (fraction born at birth × level at birth) + (fraction born later × average level when they were born)**

So some of the DNA was made after birth. Because the sample averages carbon-14, not years, 1976 is not simply the mean birth year, and the reading cannot say whether all neurons are a little younger or a few are much younger. Turning it into birth dates needs a model of how neurons are added.

### Two donors on the curve

A worked problem on the curve. Try it before opening the answer.

Two people donate hippocampus and cortex: one born in 1950, before the bomb peak, and one born in 1972, after it. In the person born in 1950, the carbon-14 of cortical neuron DNA matches the air of 1950, but the hippocampal neuron DNA holds more. What does each result mean, and why is the person born before the peak the more decisive test?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The cortical value matches the birth year, so essentially all cortical neurons were born around birth. The hippocampal value is higher, and in 1950 the air held the low pre-bomb level. Every year from about 1955 through the decades these donors lived had more carbon-14 than 1950, so, unless DNA repair, extra DNA copies or stray non-neuronal nuclei add bomb carbon, the value can rise only through DNA made after 1955, when this person was at least five. Most likely some hippocampal neurons were born after early childhood.

In the person born in 1972, neurons born later carry less carbon-14 than birth-year neurons, so they lower the value. How much depends on how many were added, when, and how fast the curve was falling, and the shortfall has to be larger than measurement error. In the person born in 1950, the baseline is the flat pre-bomb level and every later year sits above it, so any rise is easy to interpret; its size still depends on how many neurons were added and when.

</details>

### The hippocampal turnover estimate

Spalding and colleagues (2013) fitted the carbon-14 of hippocampal neurons from donors born across the twentieth century to turnover models. They estimated that about one third of hippocampal neurons are subject to exchange, with about 700 new neurons added per hippocampus per day, a turnover of 1.75 percent a year within the renewing fraction. The estimate rests on the model chosen, on pure neuronal sorting, and on DNA repair adding little carbon.

### The human studies in detail

In March 2018 Sorrells, Alvarez-Buylla and colleagues stained human dentate gyrus for dividing progenitors and for young neurons, using DCX and PSA-NCAM. The young neurons fell sharply in the first year of life, a few remained at 7 and 13 years, and none were detected in adults aged 18 to 77, either in post-mortem controls or in tissue removed during epilepsy surgery. They saw a similar decline in juvenile macaques and concluded that adult human hippocampal neurogenesis does not continue or is extremely rare.

The next month, Boldrini and colleagues reported the opposite from whole post-mortem hippocampi of healthy people aged 14 to 79. They found similar numbers of intermediate progenitors and thousands of immature neurons at every age, with a smaller pool of resting stem cells in the front of the dentate gyrus in older people. In 2019 Moreno-Jiménez, Llorens-Martín and colleagues reported DCX-positive neurons in healthy people into their ninth decade.

The collections also differ in their donors: age, cause of death, epilepsy and medication all vary between them.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing has not ended the argument. Franjic and colleagues (2022) found signs of neurogenesis in adult macaques, pigs and mice but not in adult humans, and found DCX in many human neurons that were not immature. Dumitru, Frisén and colleagues (2025) combined single-nucleus RNA sequencing, the proliferation marker Ki67 and machine learning, and reported dividing neural progenitors in the adult human dentate gyrus.
