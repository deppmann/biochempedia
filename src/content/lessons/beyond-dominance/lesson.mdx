## Explain: when neither allele hides the other

<WordBank terms={['allele', 'heterozygous', 'homozygous', 'genotype', 'phenotype', 'incomplete dominance', 'codominance', 'multiple alleles', 'pedigree', 'carrier', 'karyotype', 'autosome']} />

An **allele** is one version of a gene. A **homozygous** organism has two of the same allele, and a **heterozygous** organism has two different alleles. Your **genotype** is your pair of alleles, and your **phenotype** is the trait that shows.

When neither allele is dominant, we write both with capital letters. For snapdragon flower color, R is the red allele and W is the white allele.

**Incomplete dominance** means the heterozygote shows a phenotype in between the two homozygotes. RR snapdragons are red, WW are white, and RW are pink. Think of mixing paint. The analogy breaks at once: paint stays mixed, but a pink plant passes on a separate R or W to each sex cell.

Cross two pink snapdragons and the Punnett square gives:

| | R (1/2) | W (1/2) |
|---|---|---|
| **R (1/2)** | RR, red | RW, pink |
| **W (1/2)** | RW, pink | WW, white |

> genotype ratio 1 RR : 2 RW : 1 WW, and phenotype ratio 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white

Each genotype has its own color, so the ratios match. Red and white come back: the alleles never blended.

**Codominance** means both alleles show fully in the heterozygote. In Shorthorn cattle, RR is red, WW is white, and RW is roan. Each hair is red or white, and the two kinds grow side by side. A roan × roan cross gives 1 red : 2 roan : 1 white on average.

Human ABO blood type is the best-known case. Red blood cells can carry an A marker, a B marker, both or neither. The gene has three common alleles, IA, IB and i. A gene with more than two alleles in a population has **multiple alleles**, though each person still carries only two.

| Blood type | Genotypes in the usual model | Markers on red blood cells |
|---|---|---|
| A | IA IA or IA i | A |
| B | IB IB or IB i | B |
| AB | IA IB | A and B |
| O | ii | neither |

IA and IB are codominant, and each is dominant to i.

The environment can change a phenotype too. In many bigleaf hydrangeas, the same plant has bluer flowers in acidic soil and pinker flowers in less acidic soil.

## Explain: reading a family pedigree

Geneticists cannot set up crosses between people, so they study existing families. A **pedigree** is a chart of a family that shows who has a trait across generations.

The usual symbols:
- A square is a male, and a circle is a female.
- A shaded symbol is a person who shows the trait. The legend always says what the shading means.
- A horizontal line joins two partners. A vertical line drops from it to a line holding their children.
- Roman numerals (I, II, III) label generations, and numbers label people within a generation.

To read a pedigree, test each model against every person. Two clues help, for genes on the autosomes.
- Recessive clue: two unshaded parents can have a shaded child. Each parent must be a **carrier**, a heterozygote who carries a recessive allele but does not show the trait.
- Dominant clue: a dominant trait usually appears in every generation, and a shaded child usually has a shaded parent. Once in a while, a new change in DNA starts a dominant trait in a family with no history of it.

These are clues, not proofs. A small family may fit both models.

{/* DIAGRAM: a three-generation pedigree, with numbered parts: 1 square and circle symbols, 2 shaded affected people, 3 the partner line, 4 the line to the children, 5 generation numerals I to III, 6 two unshaded carrier parents with a shaded child */}

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A pedigree lets you test whether a dominant or a recessive model fits a family, and a shaded child of two unshaded parents points to a recessive trait.</Support>

## Explain: a karyotype counts whole chromosomes

A **karyotype** is a picture of a cell’s chromosomes, sorted into matched pairs by size and shape. Most human body cells have 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs. Twenty-two pairs are **autosomes**, the chromosomes that are not sex chromosomes. The last pair, the sex chromosomes, is usually XX or XY.

A karyotype makes an extra or missing chromosome easy to count.
- Three copies of chromosome 21, instead of two, is called trisomy 21. A cell with it has 47 chromosomes. It is the most frequent cause of Down syndrome. The CDC estimates that about 1 in 640 babies born in the United States has Down syndrome.
- A cell with only one X and no second sex chromosome has 45 chromosomes, written 45,X. It is linked to Turner syndrome.

A karyotype cannot show single genes, such as someone’s ABO alleles.

Nearly every cell in your body has the same DNA, with the same genes. A skin cell and a muscle cell still look and act differently, because each cell uses, or expresses, a different set of its genes. Mature red blood cells are an exception: they lose their nucleus, so they have no chromosomes to count. You can read how cells copy DNA in [that lesson](/lessons/dna-structure-and-copying/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A karyotype shows the number and shape of whole chromosomes, so it can reveal an extra chromosome 21, while nearly every body cell carries the same genes and uses them differently.</Support>

### Design problem

A plant breeder has pink snapdragons. One idea is that pink is the heterozygote in an incomplete dominance pattern. Another is that pink is a third, true-breeding color. Plan crosses that would tell these ideas apart.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Cross pink plants with other pink plants, and also cross pink plants with white plants.

- **Independent variable:** the type of cross, pink × pink or pink × white.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of red, pink and white offspring.
- **Constants:** the same soil, water, light, pot size and growing time for all plants.
- **Control of pollen:** remove each flower’s pollen-making parts before they ripen, brush on pollen from the chosen parent, and cover the flower so insects cannot bring other pollen.
- **Repeated trials:** use several different pink parents.
- **Sample size:** grow at least 100 offspring per cross, because small counts swing widely.

If pink is RW, pink × pink should give about 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white, and pink × white about 1 pink : 1 white. If pink is true-breeding, pink × pink gives only pink.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The pink snapdragon and the roan calf are both heterozygotes. In snapdragons the RW plant shows an in-between color, and in Shorthorns the RW calf shows both colors, hair by hair. The alleles stay separate, so red and white offspring return in the next generation.

**Science practices you used:** predicting before testing, using a Punnett square and a pedigree as models and naming their limits, telling model predictions from measured data, and planning controlled crosses with enough offspring.
