## Explain: when neither allele hides the other

<WordBank terms={['allele', 'heterozygous', 'homozygous', 'genotype', 'phenotype', 'incomplete dominance', 'codominance', 'multiple alleles', 'pedigree', 'carrier', 'karyotype', 'autosome']} />

An **allele** is one version of a gene. A **homozygous** organism has two of the same allele, and a **heterozygous** organism has two different alleles. Your **genotype** is your pair of alleles, and your **phenotype** is the trait that shows.

For snapdragon flower color, `R` is the red allele and `W` is the white allele. Both are capitals, so neither symbol suggests that one hides the other.

**Incomplete dominance** means the heterozygote shows a phenotype in between the two homozygotes, as in pink `RW` snapdragons. Pink looks like mixed paint, but paint stays mixed. A pink plant still passes a separate R or W to each sex cell.

The first film's pink × pink grid:

| | R (1/2) | W (1/2) |
|---|---|---|
| **R (1/2)** | RR, red | RW, pink |
| **W (1/2)** | RW, pink | WW, white |

> genotype ratio 1 RR : 2 RW : 1 WW, and phenotype ratio 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white

Red and white return: the alleles never blended.

**Predict.** Cross a pink `RW` snapdragon with a white `WW` snapdragon. Which colors should appear, and in what ratio?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The pink parent passes `R` or `W`; the white parent passes `W`. Expect pink and white offspring `1:1`, on average.

</details>

**Codominance** means the heterozygote shows the effects of both alleles. Snapdragon color is not codominant, so the first film switched to roan Shorthorn cattle; roan × roan gives `1` red : `2` roan : `1` white on average.

ABO blood type adds **multiple alleles**: a population can have more than two versions of a gene, although each person carries only two. The ABO gene has three common alleles, IA, IB and i. The table adds the genotypes the first film skipped.

| B | IB IB or IB i | B |
| AB | IA IB | A and B |
| O | ii | neither |

IA and IB are codominant, and each is dominant to i.

## Explain: reading a family pedigree

The second film showed how to test a **pedigree**, a chart showing who has a trait across generations. The full key adds what the film left out: which shape is which sex, and how people are numbered.
- A square is a male, and a circle is a female.
- Shading marks a person who shows the trait. The legend always says what the shading means.
- A horizontal line joins two partners. A vertical line drops from it to their children.
- Roman numerals (I, II, III) label generations, and numbers label people within a generation.

The film's two clues apply to genes on the **autosomes**, the chromosomes that are not sex chromosomes. In the recessive clue, each unshaded parent of a shaded child must be a **carrier**, a heterozygote who carries a recessive allele but does not show the trait. In the dominant clue, a shaded child usually has a shaded parent. Once in a while, though, a new change in DNA starts a dominant trait in a family with no history of it. These are clues, not proofs. A small family may fit both models.

## Explain: a karyotype counts whole chromosomes

A **karyotype** is a picture of a cell’s chromosomes sorted into matched pairs by size and shape. A typical human body cell with a nucleus has 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes and one pair of sex chromosomes, usually XX or XY.

**Predict.** A karyotype shows one X and no second sex chromosome. How many chromosomes does that cell have?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It has 45 chromosomes, written 45,X. This chromosome pattern is linked to Turner syndrome.

</details>

Having three copies of chromosome 21, instead of two, is called trisomy 21. A cell with it has 47 chromosomes. It is the most frequent cause of Down syndrome. The CDC estimates that about 1 in 640 babies born in the United States has Down syndrome.

As the second film showed, nearly every body cell with a nucleus has the same genes, and skin and muscle cells differ because they use, or **express**, different sets of them. Mature red blood cells, which lose their nucleus, are the most common cells in your body. To see how cells copy DNA, read [that lesson](/lessons/dna-structure-and-copying/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A karyotype shows the number and shape of whole chromosomes, so it can reveal an extra chromosome 21. Nearly every body cell with a nucleus carries the same genes but uses them differently.</Support>

### Design problem

A plant breeder has pink snapdragons. One idea is that pink is the heterozygote in an incomplete dominance pattern. Another is that pink is a third, true-breeding color. Plan crosses that would tell these ideas apart.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Cross pink plants with other pink plants, and also cross pink plants with white plants.

- **Independent variable:** the type of cross, pink × pink or pink × white.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of red, pink and white offspring.
- **Constants:** the same soil, water, light, pot size and growing time for all plants.
- **Control of pollen:** remove each flower’s pollen-making parts before they ripen, brush on pollen from the chosen parent, and cover the flower so insects cannot bring other pollen.
- **Repeated trials:** use several different pink parents.
- **Sample size:** grow at least 100 offspring per cross, because small counts swing widely.

If pink is RW, pink × pink should give about 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white, and pink × white about 1 pink : 1 white. If pink is true-breeding, pink × pink gives only pink.

</details>
