## The big idea

Ask your child to find a grasshopper in tall grass, and they will soon see why it is hard. Many animals are coloured like the place where they live. That match is called **camouflage**: colours or patterns that help an animal blend into its background, so animals that might eat it pass it by.

In this game your child is the hungry bird. Yarn worms that match the grass are hard to see, so they get "eaten" last. On a different background, a different colour wins. The point is not that green is a good colour. The point is that a colour hides an animal only when it matches where the animal lives.

Virginia's grade 2 standard asks children to see camouflage as one way animals survive. Grade 3 adds that a **population**, a group of the same kind of living thing in one place, can change over a long time. The game shows the start of that idea. Hidden worms survive, and in real life survivors have young that look like them.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time. Outdoors, choose a fenced yard or a spot well away from roads.
- Before you start, walk the patch and check for animal droppings, broken glass, ant mounds, poison ivy (leaves of three, let it be), and bees or wasps. In late summer, yellowjackets can nest in the ground; skip any spot where insects fly in and out of a hole. Do not use a lawn that was recently sprayed or fertilised; the product label says when children may go back on it.
- Use only soft yarn pieces. Do not use toothpicks, sticks or any other pointed pieces as worms, at any age: they can poke skin or eyes, can be swallowed, and are hard to find again in grass. Children hunt with shoes on.
- Count every piece back at the end, and keep looking until all 20 are found. Yarn left in the grass can be swallowed by a pet (swallowed string can block or tear a cat’s or dog’s gut) or tangled in a mower. Keep cats and dogs indoors during the game, do not mow the patch until every piece is found, and keep yarn away from children under 4.
- Wash hands with soap and water after hunting in grass or dirt. After time in long grass, check skin and clothes for ticks.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Make the worms.** Cut 20 pieces of yarn about 5 cm long: 5 green, 5 brown, 5 red and 5 yellow. Show your child one of each colour.
2. **Predict.** Ask, "If you were a bird, which colour would you find first? Which would you miss?" Write down the guess.
3. **Set the scene.** While your child turns away, scatter all 20 worms over the marked patch of lawn. Lay them on top of the grass, not pushed under it.
4. **Hunt.** Say "Go," and give your child 30 seconds to pick up as many worms as they can, one at a time, and drop them in the cup. Expect some worms to be left over; how many depends on the grass and the size of the patch. The red and yellow worms usually go first, and green and brown ones are often left.
5. **Count and record.** Tip out the cup and count each colour. Fill in the table. Then help find every worm still in the grass, until you have all 20.
6. **Repeat.** Do the same hunt twice more on the grass. Three rounds give a fairer answer than one. Add up the totals for each colour.
7. **Change the background.** Now scatter the same 20 worms on bare dirt, a rug or newspaper. Ask your child to predict again before hunting. On brown dirt, brown worms often win. On a busy-patterned rug, the winner can surprise you.
8. **Talk it over.** Ask, "Which worms would still be alive at the end of the day? Which ones would grow up and have baby worms?"

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "Which colour do you think you'll find first? Why?"
- "I wonder which worms a real bird would miss on our grass."
- "What do you think will happen if we play on the dirt instead?"
- "If the hidden worms have babies the same colour, what will the next group of worms look like?"
- "Can you think of a real animal near our house that is hard to see? Where does it live?"
- "Why might some animals be bright and easy to see?"

## Why it works

Many predators hunt by sight. In the game, a worm's colour sets how likely it is to be found. Worms with less contrast against the background get found less often, as camouflaged animals do.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/camouflage-hunt/same-worms-two-backgrounds.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: yarn pieces of four colours lying on grass, where the red and yellow ones stand out and the green and brown ones blend in, and a small inset of the same yarn on bare brown dirt, where the brown pieces blend in." caption="The same yarn worms on two backgrounds. On grass, the red and yellow ones usually go first and green and brown are often left; on brown dirt, brown worms often win." />

Over many generations, that difference can change a population. Animals pass traits such as colour to their young. If well-matched animals survive more often and have more young, the matching colour slowly becomes more common in the group. No single animal has to change. Scientists call this process **natural selection**. One well-known case is the peppered moth in England, where darker moths became more common for a time on soot-darkened trees.

Camouflage is one strategy. Its opposite is **warning colour**, bright colours on animals that sting or taste bad. A monarch butterfly carries toxins from the milkweed it ate as a caterpillar. Many birds that eat one get sick and learn to avoid that pattern.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use two colours only, 10 green and 10 red, and one background. Keep it to one or two rounds. Instead of a table, your child can line up the found worms in two rows, one per colour, and see which row is longer. Then draw a bird and the worm it missed.

### Ages 8 to 10
Play three generations. After each hunt, gather the worms still in the grass: these are the survivors. Each survivor "has one baby": add one new piece of that colour, then scatter the survivors and babies together. Hunt again with the new group. Keep a tally of every piece you add, and at the end find every piece, including the extras. Record each generation in a table and make a bar graph of how many of each colour remain. Discuss how the population changed even though no worm changed colour.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If your child finds all 20 worms quickly, the patch is too small or the grass is too short. Make the patch bigger or cut the time to 15 seconds. If no worms are found, use shorter grass or a longer time. If green and red are found equally, check that the worms are lying on the grass, not tucked under it. If a piece is still missing after the count, search the patch again on your hands and knees in good light, and keep pets and young children off it and the mower away until you find it.
