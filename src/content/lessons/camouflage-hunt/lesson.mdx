## The big idea

Your child plays a hungry bird in this 30-minute game. They look for yarn worms and count which colours they find. Then they change the background to see which colours are harder to spot.

> **Say it together:** "Some animals have colours like the place where they live. That match is called **camouflage**. A colour hides an animal only when it matches its background."

Ask your child to spot a grasshopper in tall grass, and they soon see why it is hard. Camouflage is any colour or pattern that helps an animal blend in so predators pass it by.

In this game, your child is the hungry bird. Yarn worms that blend into the grass are harder to spot. Change the background to see whether a different colour hides best. Hidden worms survive, and real survivors have young like them—the start of how a **population** (a group of the same kind of living thing in one place) changes over time.

<SafetyNote>
- **Supervision and scissors:** Stay with your child the whole time. An adult cuts the yarn and checks the patch before the hunt. Choose another spot if you find broken glass, animal droppings, ant mounds, poison ivy (leaves of three, let it be), or bees or wasps.
- **Choking hazard:** Keep short yarn pieces away from children under 4. **Call 911 if choking.**
- **Count all 20 pieces back:** Keep looking until all 20 are found. Leftover yarn can be swallowed by a pet (swallowed string can block or tear a cat's or dog's gut) or cut by a mower. Keep pets indoors during the game, and do not mow until every piece is found.
- **Outdoors and hands:** Use your own yard or a spot where you have permission. Choose a fenced yard or a spot well away from roads. Hunt with shoes on. On hot or sunny days, play when it is cooler, use a hat or sunscreen, and bring water. Move indoors if a storm approaches. Never taste plants, berries, or mushrooms found outdoors. Leave real insects and animals alone. Never handle stinging or biting ones. Wash hands with soap and water after hunting in grass or dirt or touching plants. Check skin and clothes for ticks after time in long grass.

## What to do

1. **Make the worms and try one look first.** An adult cuts 20 yarn pieces about 5 cm long: 5 green, 5 brown, 5 red, and 5 yellow. Show your child one worm of each colour before you put any on the lawn.
2. **Predict before you hunt.** Ask, *"If you were a hungry bird, which colour would you find first? Which would you miss?"* Write down the guess.
3. **Set the scene.** While your child turns away, scatter all 20 worms on top of the marked lawn patch, not pushed under the grass.
4. **Hunt for 30 seconds.** Give one rule: pick up worms one at a time into the cup. Say, *"Ready, bird? Go!"* and start the timer. Notice what happens: red and yellow usually go first, while green and brown are often left. How many remain depends on the grass and patch size.
5. **Count, record, and check.** Tip out the cup, sort by colour, count each pile, and fill in the table. Compare with the guess. For example, if you find all the red and yellow worms and just one green worm, record **Red: 5, Yellow: 5, Green: 1**. Count the brown worms you found and fill in their row. Then find every leftover worm until all 20 are back.
6. **Repeat twice more.** Play two more rounds on grass. Three rounds give a fairer answer than one. Add up the counts across rounds for each colour.
7. **Change the background—predict, then check.** Move the 20 worms to bare dirt, a rug, or newspaper. Ask, *"What do you think will happen here? Which colour will win now?"* Write the guess, then hunt and count. On brown dirt, brown often wins; on a busy-patterned rug, the winner can surprise you.
8. **Close with two debrief questions.** Tie back to the opening lines:
   - *"Which colours were hardest to find on each background, and what do you notice about how they matched?"*
   - *"Which worms would still be alive at the end of the day, and what would their babies look like?"*
   Say the **Say it together** lines once more.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "Which colour do you think you'll find first? Why?"
- "I wonder which worms a real bird would miss on our grass."
- "What do you think will happen if we play on the dirt instead?"
- "If the hidden worms have babies the same colour, what will the next group of worms look like?"
- "Can you think of a real animal near our house that is hard to see? Where does it live?"
- "Why might some animals be bright and easy to see?"

## Why it works

Many predators hunt by sight. In the game, a worm's colour sets how likely it is to be spotted. Worms with less contrast against the background get found less often, just as camouflaged animals do.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/camouflage-hunt/same-worms-two-backgrounds.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: yarn pieces of four colours lying on grass, where the red and yellow ones stand out and the green and brown ones blend in, and a small inset of the same yarn on bare brown dirt, where the brown pieces blend in." caption="The same yarn worms on two backgrounds. On grass, the red and yellow ones usually go first and green and brown are often left; on brown dirt, brown worms often win." />

Over many generations, that difference can change a population. Animals pass traits such as colour to their young. When well-matched animals survive more often and have more young, the matching colour slowly becomes more common in the group. No single animal has to change. Scientists call this process **natural selection**. One well-known case is the peppered moth in England, where darker moths became more common for a time on soot-darkened trees.

Camouflage is one strategy. Its opposite is **warning colour**: bright colours on animals that sting or taste bad. A monarch butterfly carries toxins from the milkweed it ate as a caterpillar. Many birds that eat one get sick and learn to avoid that pattern.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use two colours only—10 green and 10 red—and one background. Keep it to one or two rounds. Skip the table: have your child line up the found worms in two rows, one per colour, and ask *"Which row is longer?"* Then draw a bird and the worm it missed.

### Ages 8 to 10
Play three generations. After each hunt, gather the worms still in the grass: these are the survivors. Each survivor "has one baby": add one new piece of that colour, then scatter the survivors and babies together and hunt again. Keep a tally of every piece you add, and at the end find every piece, including the extras. Record each generation in a table and make a bar graph of how many of each colour remain. Ask how the population changed even though no worm changed colour.

## If it doesn't go as planned

- **Found all 20 quickly:** The patch is too small or the grass is too short. Make the patch bigger or cut the time to 15 seconds.
- **No worms found:** Try shorter grass or give a longer time.
- **Green and red found equally:** Check that the worms lie on top of the grass, not tucked under it. A tie can also be chance, so write it down and play again. Ask your child if the red and green yarn look alike to them. If they do, record that observation too.
- **A piece is still missing:** Search the patch again on your hands and knees in good light. Keep pets and young children off the patch, and keep the mower away, until you find it.
