## Explain: copying a gene into messenger RNA

<WordBank terms={['protein synthesis', 'transcription', 'RNA', 'messenger RNA', 'translation', 'codon', 'ribosome', 'transfer RNA', 'amino acid', 'mutation', 'substitution', 'frameshift']} />

**Protein synthesis** is the making of a protein from the instructions in a gene, and it happens in two stages. In plant and animal cells the chromosomes stay in the nucleus, but proteins are built outside it. So the cell first makes a portable copy.

**RNA** is a nucleic acid, a chain of nucleotides like DNA, with three differences:

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Strands | two, twisted into a double helix | usually one |
| Sugar | deoxyribose | ribose |
| Bases | A, T, C, G | A, U, C, G (uracil, U, in place of T) |

**Transcription** is the first stage: an enzyme copies the base order of one gene into a strand of RNA. The enzyme unwinds a short piece of DNA and reads one strand, the template strand, pairing a new RNA base with each DNA base: DNA A with RNA U, T with A, C with G, G with C. The copy is **messenger RNA (mRNA)**, the RNA that carries a gene's instructions to the protein-building machinery.

Like DNA, RNA has direction: its two ends differ (biologists call them 5′ and 3′), and it is built and read one way only.

In cells with a nucleus, the mRNA leaves through pores in the nuclear membrane. Bacteria have no nucleus, so both stages happen in their cytoplasm.

## Explain: reading the message three letters at a time

**Translation** is the second stage: the cell reads the mRNA and builds a chain of amino acids. An **amino acid** is one of the small building blocks of proteins; living things use about twenty common kinds. A **ribosome** is the structure that reads mRNA and joins amino acids together.

The ribosome reads the mRNA in groups of three bases. Each group of three is a **codon**, and each codon stands for one amino acid or for "stop." With four bases there are 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 possible codons. In the standard code, 61 of them stand for amino acids and three, UAA, UAG and UGA, are stop codons. The codon AUG stands for methionine and is the usual start codon, where reading begins.

**Transfer RNA (tRNA)** is a small RNA that carries one amino acid and has a stretch of three bases, its anticodon, that pairs with a matching codon. As each tRNA pairs with its codon, the ribosome links its amino acid to the chain. At a stop codon no amino acid is added, and the chain is released. It folds into a shape that lets the protein do its job.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/protein-synthesis/ribosome-reads-codons.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a two-lobed ribosome sitting on an mRNA strand divided into groups of three blocks. Inside, a key-shaped tRNA fits three blocks against one codon and carries a bead that is joining a growing bead chain; another tRNA arrives. An inset shows the ribosome at a stop codon with no tRNA and the chain released." caption="Translation up close: the ribosome reads one codon at a time, each tRNA's anticodon pairs with a codon, and its amino acid joins the chain until a stop codon releases it." />

Here is part of the codon chart.

| mRNA codon | Meaning |
|---|---|
| AUG | methionine (Met), start |
| GAG, GAA | glutamic acid (Glu) |
| GUG | valine (Val) |
| UUU, UUC | phenylalanine (Phe) |
| GGC | glycine (Gly) |
| AAA | lysine (Lys) |
| UAA, UAG, UGA | stop |

Work one example. A DNA template strand, written from its 3′ end on the left to its 5′ end on the right, reads TAC CTC CTT AAA ACT.
1. Pair each base: the mRNA is AUG GAG GAA UUU UGA.
2. Translate: Met, Glu, Glu, Phe, then UGA means stop.
3. The chain is Met–Glu–Glu–Phe. The stop codon adds nothing.

Specialized cells make different proteins from the same genes: young red blood cells fill with hemoglobin, while some pancreas cells make insulin.

The **genetic code**, the set of codon meanings, is almost the same in every living thing, with a few exceptions, such as in some bacteria and in mitochondria. So a bacterium can read a suitably prepared gene for a human protein. In 1979 a team put man-made genes for the two chains of human insulin into E. coli; the bacteria made the chains, and once joined outside the cells, tests recognized them as insulin.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A ribosome reads mRNA one codon at a time, tRNA brings each matching amino acid, and a stop codon ends the chain.</Support>

## Explain: when one letter changes

A **mutation** is a change in the base order of DNA, from a rare copying error or from damage. Three kinds:

- A **substitution** swaps one base for another.
- An **insertion** adds one or more bases.
- A **deletion** removes one or more bases.

A substitution can be a **silent mutation**, one that leaves the amino acid the same because more than one codon stands for it (GAG and GAA both mean Glu). It can change one amino acid. Or it can turn a codon into a stop, cutting the chain short.

An insertion or deletion of one base causes a **frameshift**, a shift in how the bases are grouped into codons. Take THE CAT ATE THE RAT, delete the first T, and read in threes: HEC ATA TET HER AT. The analogy breaks because mRNA has no spaces; the ribosome groups letters from where it started.

Now the opening puzzle. In the usual numbering of beta-globin, a hemoglobin chain, the sixth amino acid is glutamic acid, coded by GAG. (Some databases count the starting methionine and call it the seventh.) The coding strand is the DNA strand that matches the mRNA letter for letter, with T where the mRNA has U. In the sickle allele this strand reads GTG, so the mRNA codon is GUG and valine replaces glutamic acid. Glutamic acid carries an electric charge and valine does not. Hemoglobin with valine there can stick together into stiff rods, mostly when it is low on oxygen, and the rods bend the cell into a sickle.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/protein-synthesis/sickle-one-letter.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: two rows of four frames. In the top row a DNA codon, its mRNA codon, a six-bead chain and a round red blood cell with separate hemoglobin blobs. In the bottom row the middle letter of each codon is changed, the sixth bead is different, the hemoglobin has joined into stiff rods, and the cell is bent into a sickle." caption="One letter, followed all the way up: GAG to GTG puts valine in place of glutamic acid, and that hemoglobin can stick into stiff rods, mostly when it is low on oxygen, bending the cell into a sickle." />

A person has two copies of this gene. With one sickle allele and one usual allele, a person has sickle cell trait and usually has no symptoms. Sickle cell disease happens when a person has a disease-causing change on both copies, and at least one of them is the sickle change.

### Design problem

Use the simulation as a model to test this question: which kind of mutation most often changes the amino acid chain, a substitution, an insertion or a deletion? Plan a fair test.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Hypothesis: If ribosomes read in threes, then one-base insertions and deletions will change the chain more often than substitutions.
- Independent variable: the kind of mutation (substitution, insertion or deletion of one base).
- Dependent variable: whether the chain changed, and how many amino acids changed.
- Constants: the same starting DNA, one mutation per trial, the same codon chart.
- Control: translate the starting DNA with no mutation, so you know the normal chain.
- Trials: 20 of each kind at randomly chosen spots; report the percent that changed the chain.

If insertions and deletions change the chain in almost every trial while some substitutions change nothing, the hypothesis is supported. The limit: a model of one short gene shows the chain, not whether a real folded protein still works.

</details>
