## Copy a gene into messenger RNA

<WordBank terms={['protein synthesis', 'transcription', 'RNA', 'messenger RNA', 'translation', 'codon', 'ribosome', 'transfer RNA', 'amino acid', 'mutation', 'substitution', 'frameshift']} />

The first film showed the two stages of **protein synthesis**, making a protein from a gene: **transcription** copies one gene into **messenger RNA (mRNA)**, and **translation** reads the mRNA to build a chain of amino acids.

**RNA** is a nucleic acid, a chain of nucleotides like DNA, with three differences:

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Strands | two, twisted into a double helix | usually one |
| Sugar | deoxyribose | ribose |
| Bases | A, T, C, G | A, U, C, G (uracil, U, in place of T) |

In transcription, an enzyme reads one DNA strand, the **template strand**, pairing DNA A with RNA U, T with A, C with G, and G with C. The other DNA strand, the **coding strand**, matches the mRNA letter for letter, with T where the mRNA has U.

**Predict.** A template DNA triplet reads CTT. Which mRNA triplet will pair with it?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

C pairs with G, and each T pairs with A, so the mRNA triplet is GAA. Pair with the labelled template; do not copy its letters straight across.

</details>

Like DNA, RNA has direction: its two ends differ (biologists call them 5′ and 3′), and it is built and read one way only.

Bacteria have no nucleus, so both stages happen in their cytoplasm.

## Read the message into an amino acid chain

Each **codon**, a group of three mRNA bases, means one **amino acid** or “stop.” Amino acids are the small building blocks of proteins; living things use about twenty common kinds. Here is part of the codon chart.

| mRNA codon | Meaning |
|---|---|
| AUG | methionine (Met), start |
| GAG, GAA | glutamic acid (Glu) |
| GUG | valine (Val) |
| UUU, UUC | phenylalanine (Phe) |
| GGC | glycine (Gly) |
| AAA | lysine (Lys) |
| UAA, UAG, UGA | stop |

**Predict.** A ribosome already reading an mRNA reaches UUU GGC, lined up as two codons. Which two amino acids will it add next?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The chart gives phenylalanine for UUU and glycine for GGC, in that order.

</details>

Now a complete example. A DNA template strand, written from its 3′ end on the left to its 5′ end on the right, reads TAC CTC CTT AAA ACT.

1. Pair each base: the mRNA is AUG GAG GAA UUU UGA.
2. Translate: Met, Glu, Glu, Phe, then UGA means stop.
3. The chain is Met–Glu–Glu–Phe. The stop codon adds nothing.

AUG, the usual start codon, stands for methionine. With four bases, there are 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 possible codons. In the standard code, 61 stand for amino acids, and three (UAA, UAG and UGA) are stops.

In the first film, each **transfer RNA (tRNA)** paired with a codon through its three-base **anticodon**. After the stop, the released chain can fold into a working protein.

## When one letter reaches a whole cell

The second film skipped two things: why valine makes hemoglobin stick, and why one sickle copy usually causes no symptoms.

Glutamic acid carries an electric charge and mixes well with water. Valine has no charge and avoids water. When hemoglobin is low on oxygen, the valine can fit into a water-avoiding patch on another hemoglobin, so the molecules can join into the stiff rods the second film showed.

A person has two copies of this gene.

**Predict.** One person has one sickle allele and one usual allele. Another has a disease-causing change on both copies, including the sickle change. Who usually has sickle cell disease symptoms?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The second person. One sickle allele with one usual allele is sickle cell trait, which usually causes no symptoms. Sickle cell disease needs a disease-causing change on both copies, at least one of them the sickle change.

</details>

### Design problem

Use the simulation as a model to test this question: which kind of mutation most often changes the amino acid chain, a substitution, an insertion or a deletion? Plan a fair test.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Hypothesis: If ribosomes read in threes, then one-base insertions and deletions will change the chain more often than substitutions.
- Independent variable: the kind of mutation (substitution, insertion or deletion of one base).
- Dependent variable: whether the chain differs from the control chain, and how many amino acids changed, compared position by position from the start.
- Constants: the same starting DNA, one mutation per trial, the same codon chart, and only spots inside the coding part, from the start codon up to the stop.
- Control: translate the starting DNA with no mutation, so you know the normal chain.
- Trials: 20 of each kind at randomly chosen spots, with the new base also chosen at random; report the percent that changed the chain.

If insertions and deletions change the chain in almost every trial while some substitutions change nothing, the hypothesis is supported. The limit: a model of one short gene shows the chain, not whether a real folded protein still works.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Counting amino acids in beta-globin

Beta-globin is the hemoglobin chain this gene codes for. In its usual numbering, glutamic acid is the sixth amino acid. Some databases count the starting methionine and call it the seventh.

### Silent does not always mean identical

The second film's silent swap, GAG to GAA, kept the chain the same. The story “Silent, but not always identical” adds a refinement: two messages that make the same amino acid chain can still differ in how long they last, which can change how much protein a cell makes.
