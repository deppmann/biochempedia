**Early in the hindbrain, the cells of one segment look much like those of the next. Yet r2 will make the motor neurons that work the jaw, and r4 the ones that move the face. What tells them apart is an address written in Hox transcription factors. Each rhombomere carries its own mix of Hox proteins, and segment genes such as Krox20 add to it. The mix helps set the segment's identity, including which neurons it makes and where their axons go.**

This lesson follows the address from its source. Retinoic acid, a product of vitamin A, helps switch on the front-most Hox genes, so the early embryo uses this signal to set its code. That is the likely reason a retinoid taken in early pregnancy can disturb the embryo's face and brain (the history is under Going deeper). In humans that mechanism is inferred from animal work. First, the map of the early neural tube, then the Hox genes, then retinoic acid, then the rhombomeres and the knockout that tests the code.

## The idea: the tube gets an address before it gets neurons

The neural tube is the hollow rod that forms when the edges of the neural plate fold up and fuse. It becomes the brain and spinal cord. Its front end soon swells into three vesicles: the forebrain (prosencephalon), the midbrain (mesencephalon) and the hindbrain (rhombencephalon). The forebrain then divides into the telencephalon, which makes the cerebral hemispheres, and the diencephalon, which makes the thalamus and its neighbours and pushes out the optic vesicles that become the retinas. The hindbrain divides into the metencephalon (pons and cerebellum) and the myelencephalon (medulla).

The hindbrain also shows a row of small bulges called rhombomeres, numbered r1 to r7 from front to back, with a less distinct r8 behind. Repeated units like these are called neuromeres. So, as the opening asked, the question becomes: how is the nervous system specified within neuromeres?

Think of a new street where every house is built to the same plan. Before anyone moves in, each house needs a number. The embryo's answer rests on three basic principles that will come back in the lessons on the tube's other axes:

1. Particular genes are switched on at particular places and particular times.
2. Signals that spread from a source and oppose one another form graded concentrations, and those gradients set the pattern.
3. The signals restrict where certain transcription factors are made, and those factors then regulate the genes that give each region its character.

In the hindbrain, the signal this lesson follows is retinoic acid, working alongside FGF and Wnt signals, and the transcription factors are the Hox proteins, together with segment genes such as Krox20.

## Hox genes: order on the chromosome, order along the body

The story starts in the fruit fly. A homeotic mutation turns one body part into the likeness of another: legs where antennae should be, or a second pair of wings in place of the small balancing organs called halteres. Edward Lewis spent decades on one cluster of such genes, the bithorax complex. In 1978 he proposed that its genes lie along the chromosome in the same order as the body segments they control, from front to back.

In 1984 two groups found what these genes share: a stretch of about 180 base pairs common to several fly homeotic genes. It was named the homeobox. It encodes the homeodomain, about 60 amino acids that later work showed bind DNA, with one helix lying in the major groove. One of the two groups, Gehring's in Basel, soon detected related sequences in vertebrate DNA. Body-plan genes belong to a shared animal toolkit (see [that lesson](/lessons/how-signaling-evolved/)).

Hox genes are one branch of the much larger homeobox family, and they come in clusters. The fly has eight, in two complexes on one chromosome. Mice and humans have 39, in four clusters called Hoxa, Hoxb, Hoxc and Hoxd, each on a different chromosome. Genes that sit at the same position in different clusters, such as Hoxa3, Hoxb3 and Hoxd3, form a paralog group. There are 13 groups, and no cluster has all 13. Paralogs have similar sequences and often similar expression, so they can stand in for one another, and knocking out a single Hox gene often gives a milder defect than you might expect.

The clusters obey the rule Lewis saw, called colinearity:

- **Spatial colinearity.** Genes at the 3′ end of a cluster (group 1) have the most anterior front edges of expression, reaching into the hindbrain. Genes farther toward the 5′ end have edges farther back, and group 13 is expressed near the tail.
- **Temporal co-linearity.** In mouse and chick, 3′ genes are also switched on first and 5′ genes later, as the body axis grows backward. This is a vertebrate observation. In the fly the timing rule is weak or absent.

The somites, blocks of mesoderm beside the tube that form one pair at a time (roughly every 90 minutes in chick), show temporal colinearity too, but that story belongs to them.

In the hindbrain the Hox genes with sharp front edges are mainly those of paralog groups 1 to 4, and their edges sit at rhombomere boundaries.

## Retinoic acid: the vitamin that sets the back of the code

Retinoic acid is made from retinol (vitamin A) in two oxidations, through retinaldehyde. In the early embryo the main enzyme for the second step is Raldh2, made in the mesoderm beside the future hindbrain and spinal cord. A family of enzymes called Cyp26 oxidizes retinoic acid to inactive products, and Cyp26a1 is strongly expressed toward the front of the neural plate. Picture a bath with the tap at one end and the drain at the other. Supply behind and destruction in front leave retinoic acid high toward the back of the hindbrain and low toward the front.

Retinoic acid is small and oily, so it crosses membranes and reaches the nucleus. There it binds the retinoic acid receptor (RAR), which works as a pair with the retinoid X receptor (RXR) on short DNA sequences called retinoic acid response elements (RAREs). Both are nuclear receptors, ligand-activated transcription factors of the kind covered in [that lesson](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/). Hoxa1 and Hoxb1 carry RAREs, and in cell culture 3′ Hox genes respond to lower doses of retinoic acid than 5′ genes. The gradient and the cluster order fit together: toward the front, where the signal is lower or arrives for a shorter time, fewer Hox genes come on, and those that do lie toward the 3′ end of the cluster. Retinoic acid mainly drives paralog groups 1 to 5. The more 5′ Hox genes of the spinal cord are switched on by FGF and Wnt signals instead.

Change the signal and the code moves. In 1992 Heather Marshall, Robb Krumlauf, Andrew Lumsden and colleagues reported that exposing mouse embryos to retinoic acid at an early stage changed the hindbrain Hox domains and transformed r2 and r3 toward the identity of r4 and r5. Removing the drain does something similar. Mouse embryos lacking Cyp26a1 showed a rostral hindbrain that looked partly transformed toward a more posterior character (Abu-Abed et al., 2001). In mouse embryos lacking Raldh2, the posterior hindbrain is poorly patterned.

A teratogen is an agent that causes birth defects when an embryo or fetus is exposed to it. Retinoids qualify because the early embryo uses this very signal to set its axis. In mouse and chick embryos, extra retinoic acid at an early stage shifts the Hox code of the hindbrain and disturbs the [neural crest](/lessons/neural-crest/) cells that leave it to build much of the face. In human embryos this mechanism is inferred from the animal work rather than shown directly, but it fits the ears, jaw and heart in the pattern of defects described in Going deeper. Too little vitamin A harms the embryo as well. The dose has to be right in both directions.

## Rhombomeres: Hox genes and segment genes give each segment its identity

In 1989 Andrew Lumsden and Roger Keynes mapped the motor neurons of the cranial nerves in chick embryos. Branchiomotor neurons, the motor neurons for muscles of the jaw, face and throat, arose in pairs of rhombomeres: trigeminal (nerve V, chewing) from r2 and r3, facial (nerve VII, facial expression) from r4 and r5, glossopharyngeal (nerve IX, swallowing) from r6 and r7. Each nerve left from the even-numbered member of its pair. In 1990, with Scott Fraser, they injected single cells with a tracer. Clones labelled before boundaries appeared could span two rhombomeres; clones labelled after did not. The rhombomeres are compartments. One reason cells do not mix is contact repulsion: in fish and frog embryos, r3 and r5 make the receptor EphA4 while their neighbours make ephrin-B ligands, and cells sort where the two meet.

In 1989 David Wilkinson, Krumlauf and colleagues reported that mouse Hox genes have expression domains whose front edges fall at rhombomere boundaries, often two rhombomeres apart. Later in development Hoxb1 is strong in r4 alone. Neighbouring rhombomeres share many of these genes, so the Hox code works with segment genes such as Krox20, needed for r3 and r5, and MafB (Kreisler), needed for r5, which switch on particular Hox genes in those segments. Together they give each rhombomere its character, including the motor neurons it makes.

What about r1, which makes the cerebellum? Hox expression stops at r1, kind of. Normally no Hox gene is expressed there, and the front edge of Hoxa2 sits at the r1/r2 boundary. But that edge is held, not merely inherited. In chick, a signal from the boundary between midbrain and hindbrain keeps Hoxa2 out of r1, and changing that signal changes where Hoxa2 is found. That signalling centre has [its own lesson](/lessons/local-organizers/).

## What happens if we knock out Hoxb1?

A conventional knockout is a mouse line in which one gene has been disrupted in every cell. In 1996 two groups reported Hoxb1 knockouts, one led by Studer and Krumlauf and one by Goddard and Capecchi (full author lists under Going deeper). In a normal mouse, facial branchiomotor neurons are born in r4 and migrate backward to r6, where they form the facial motor nucleus. In both mutants the facial branchiomotor neurons failed to form or to migrate normally. Studer and colleagues found that r4 lost parts of its identity and that its motor neurons behaved like trigeminal neurons of r2 and r3, a conversion toward trigeminal. Goddard and colleagues reported that the motor nucleus of nerve VII failed to form.

### Design problem

You knock out Hoxb1 in a mouse. Predict what happens to r4, to its motor neurons and to the face. Then answer a student's question: in the mutant, r4 is still there as a segment. If Hoxb1 is gone, why does r4 not simply vanish, and what happens to its motor neurons?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start from the code. Among the Hox genes, Hoxb1 is the one that most sets r4 apart from the rhombomeres in front of it, which share Hoxa2 but lack strong Hoxb1. Remove it and r4 is left with a code closer to that of r2, so r4 should take on r2-like character.

The motor neurons follow. Neurons born in r4 should no longer behave like facial neurons. They may fail to make the facial program, or make a trigeminal-like one and stop migrating backward to r6. Without a working facial motor nucleus, the muscles of facial expression lose their motor supply, so you would predict weak or absent facial movement.

Now the student's question. Here the knockout changes what r4 cells become; it does not delete the segment. That is not true of every patterning gene: r5 is missing in mice lacking Krox20 or kreisler (MafB), and mice lacking both Hoxa1 and Hoxb1 lose r4 and r5. Rhombomere 4 is still there, its progenitors still divide, and they still make motor neurons, now with a different identity. In the real mutants these neurons fail to migrate properly and the facial motor nerve is lost. So the segment survives with a changed identity, but the facial motor nucleus does not form normally: the neurons are missing, misplaced or behaving like trigeminal neurons.

## Going deeper

### Isotretinoin and the first reports

Isotretinoin, a form of retinoic acid made from vitamin A, went on sale in the United States in 1982 as a treatment for severe acne. By 1985, Edward Lammer and colleagues had described a pattern of defects in children exposed to it early in pregnancy: small or missing ears, an altered face and jaw, heart defects, and abnormalities of the brain. They called it retinoic acid embryopathy. A pill taken for the skin had reached the embryo while its head and face were being laid out. Experiments in animals suggest how, as the retinoic acid section above explains.

### Who found the homeobox

The laboratory of Walter Gehring in Basel (William McGinnis, Michael Levine, Ernst Hafen, Atsushi Kuroiwa and Gehring) and Matthew Scott and Amy Weiner in Thomas Kaufman's laboratory at Indiana University each found a stretch of about 180 base pairs common to several fly homeotic genes.

### The 1996 Hoxb1 knockouts

The two 1996 Hoxb1 knockout reports were by Michele Studer, Lumsden, Laila Ariza-McNaughton, Allan Bradley and Krumlauf in *Nature*, and by J. M. Goddard, Mireille Rossel, Nancy Manley and Mario Capecchi in *Development*.
