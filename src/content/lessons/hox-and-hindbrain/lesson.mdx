    story: >-

      genetics alone. In 1995 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Christiane
      Nüsslein-Volhard and Eric Wieschaus. The order he described gave the later molecular
      biologists a map to test, and the rule turned out to hold for Hox clusters in mice and
      humans.

    story: >-
      The bulges of the embryonic hindbrain had been described long before anyone knew whether
      they meant anything. Lumsden and Roger Keynes asked that question in chick embryos. In a
      1989 paper in Nature they mapped where the motor neurons of the cranial nerves arise and
      where their axons leave the hindbrain, and found that both line up with the rhombomeres:
      the motor neurons of a nerve come from a pair of neighbouring rhombomeres, and the nerve
      leaves from the even-numbered one. A pattern that repeats every two segments suggested the
      bulges were real units. The next year, with Scott Fraser, they injected single hindbrain
      cells with a fluorescent tracer and followed their descendants. Cells labelled before
      boundaries appeared could give clones that spanned two rhombomeres; cells labelled after
      boundaries formed gave clones that stayed inside one. The hindbrain was built of
      compartments, groups of cells that do not mix with their neighbours. That made it
      reasonable to ask what gene code each compartment carried.

    story: >-
      Lumsden's compartments needed a molecular label, and Krumlauf's papers supplied one in
      three steps. In 1989, with David Wilkinson, Sangita Bhatt, Martyn Cook and Edoardo
      Boncinelli, he reported that several mouse Hox genes are switched on in the hindbrain in
      domains whose front edges fall at rhombomere boundaries. An edge that matches a boundary is
      a correlation, so the next papers changed the code and watched what followed. In 1992, with
      Heather Marshall, Lumsden and others, he reported that exposing mouse embryos to retinoic
      acid at an early stage changed the hindbrain Hox domains and transformed rhombomeres 2 and 3
      toward the identity of 4 and 5. In 1996 Studer, Lumsden, Laila Ariza-McNaughton, Allan
      Bradley and Krumlauf described mice lacking Hoxb1, in which rhombomere 4 lost features of
      its identity and its motor neurons migrated abnormally. Together the papers made the Hox
      code a cause of segment identity, not only a marker of it.

techniques:
  - name: "In situ hybridization"
    blurb: >-
      A labelled strand of RNA or DNA that matches a gene's message is applied to a whole embryo
      or a section, and it sticks where that message is present. It shows where and when a gene
      is switched on. Here it revealed Hox domains whose edges sit at rhombomere boundaries.
  - name: "Single-cell lineage tracing"
    blurb: >-
      One cell is injected with a dye that cannot pass to its neighbours, and its descendants are
      found later. Where the clone ends up tells you whether its cells were free to cross a
      boundary. In the chick hindbrain, clones labelled after boundaries formed stayed in one
      rhombomere.
  - name: "Gene knockout by targeting"
    blurb: >-
      A gene is disrupted in mouse embryonic stem cells, and the cells are used to make a line of
      mice that lacks the gene in every cell. What goes wrong shows what the gene is needed for.
      Hoxb1 knockouts showed that one Hox gene is needed for the identity of rhombomere 4.
  - name: "Timed retinoid exposure"
    blurb: >-
      Embryos are given extra retinoic acid, or a drug that blocks its synthesis, at a chosen
      stage. Shifts in gene expression and anatomy show how the embryo responds to too much or too little signal at that stage; blocking synthesis is the better guide to what the normal signal does. Extra
      retinoic acid pushed anterior hindbrain segments toward a more posterior identity.

**Early on, the segments of the embryonic hindbrain look alike. Yet one will make the motor neurons that work the jaw, and its neighbour two doors down the ones that move the face.**

What tells them apart is a code of transcription factors that each segment carries. The code helps set which neurons the segment makes and where their axons go, and a signal made from vitamin A helps write it. This lesson shows the code, tests whether it causes a segment's identity, and then follows it back to the signal. That signal is one likely reason a retinoid taken in early pregnancy can disturb the embryo's face and brain.

## Two segments that look alike

The neural tube is the hollow rod that forms when the edges of the neural plate fold up and fuse. It becomes the brain and spinal cord. Its front end soon swells into three vesicles: the forebrain, the midbrain and the hindbrain. The hindbrain goes on to make the pons, the cerebellum and the medulla.

Look at the early hindbrain from above and you see a row of small bulges called **rhombomeres**, numbered r1 to r7 from front to back, with a less distinct r8 behind. They look like repeats of one design, like a new street where every house is built to the same plan.

They do not behave like repeats. In 1989 Andrew Lumsden and Roger Keynes mapped, in chick embryos, where the **branchiomotor neurons** arise: the motor neurons for the muscles of the jaw, face and throat. The motor neurons of each cranial nerve came from a pair of rhombomeres.

| Rhombomeres | Motor neurons they make | Muscles they supply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| r2 and r3 | trigeminal (nerve V) | first-arch muscles: the jaw |
| r4 and r5 | facial (nerve VII) | second-arch muscles |
| r6 and r7 | glossopharyngeal (nerve IX) | third-arch muscles: the throat, for swallowing |

Each nerve left the hindbrain from the even-numbered member of its pair. (Cranial nerves are numbered I to XII from front to back.) Birds have no muscles of facial expression. In mammals the matching second-arch neurons move the face, which is why the mouse experiments below speak of facial movement.

So the hindbrain is built of units that keep their cells. That makes the question sharp. Like each house on that street, each unit needs a number before anyone moves in: some label that tells its cells what to become. What is it?

## The code each segment carries

The label is a set of transcription factors, proteins that switch other genes on and off. Most come from **Hox genes**, a family met properly [further on](#order-on-the-chromosome-order-along-the-body). For now, two facts are enough. Hox genes are numbered in paralog groups, 1 to 13, by where they sit in a cluster on the chromosome: group 1 at the end called 3′, group 13 at the 5′ end (3′ and 5′ name the two ends of a DNA strand). And in the hindbrain, the Hox genes switched on in domains with sharp front edges are mainly those of groups 1 to 4.

In 1989 David Wilkinson, Robb Krumlauf and colleagues reported that mouse Hox genes have expression domains whose front edges fall at rhombomere boundaries, often two rhombomeres apart. Read it column by column. Every rhombomere has a different combination, but neighbours share many genes. Rhombomeres r3 and r4 share Hoxa2 and Hoxb2, so those two cannot tell them apart. What does is a segment gene, Krox20 in r3, and one Hox gene that, later in development, is strong in r4 alone: Hoxb1.

The segment genes are transcription factors too, each made in particular segments: **Krox20** in r3 and r5, and **MafB** (Kreisler) in r5 and r6. They switch on particular Hox genes in those segments. Together, Hox genes and segment genes give each rhombomere its character, including the motor neurons it makes.

The first column is empty. No Hox gene is expressed in r1, and the front edge of Hoxa2 sits at the r1/r2 boundary. That edge is held, not merely inherited: in chick, a signal from the boundary between midbrain and hindbrain keeps Hoxa2 out of r1, and changing that signal changes where Hoxa2 is found. That signalling centre has [its own lesson](/lessons/local-organizers/).

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** More early retinoic acid, a more posterior code; the code helps set the neurons. Early on, over the range tested, more retinoic acid, a product of vitamin A, shifts a stretch of hindbrain toward the code of segments farther back, and less shifts it the other way. The code a rhombomere ends up with, sharpened by segment genes such as Krox20, helps decide which motor neurons it builds.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which genes.** The Hox genes of paralog groups 1 to 4 in the hindbrain, early, before the segments sharpen.
- **Which claim.** The direction of change, often by moving Hox domains forward. It does not give the exact edges or how many Hox genes a segment carries, so the table above does not break it, and retinoic acid can switch some Hox genes off as well as on. Segment genes and local signals refine the code.
- **Helps, not decides alone.** The code is needed for a segment's identity, as the knockout below shows, but it works with segment genes, not by itself.

## Remove one letter: Hoxb1

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

A conventional knockout is a mouse line in which one gene has been disrupted in every cell. What goes wrong shows what the gene is needed for.

In a normal mouse, facial branchiomotor neurons are born in r4 and migrate backward to r6, where they form the facial motor nucleus. That nucleus supplies the muscles of facial expression through nerve VII.

**Predict.** Knock out Hoxb1. Does r4 disappear, or does it stay and become something else? Use the table to decide, and say what r4's motor neurons should do.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Start from the code. Hoxb1 is the clearest Hox difference between r4 and r3. Take it out, and in this simplified table r4 is left with r3's Hox genes, Hoxa2 and Hoxb2. But the code is a network, not a list. Hoxb1 helps keep Hoxb2 on in r4, so in the mutant Hoxb2 drops there too. And r4 never had Krox20, the r3 segment gene. So r4 should stay, but take on trigeminal character closer to that of r2.

The motor neurons follow. Neurons born in r4 should no longer behave like facial neurons. They may fail to make the facial program, or make a trigeminal-like one and stop migrating backward to r6. Without a working facial motor nucleus, the muscles of facial expression lose their motor supply, so you would predict weak or absent facial movement.

In 1996 two groups reported Hoxb1 knockouts, one led by Studer and Krumlauf and one by Goddard and Capecchi. In both mutants the facial branchiomotor neurons failed to form or to migrate normally, and the facial motor nerve was lost. Studer and colleagues found that r4 lost parts of its identity and that its motor neurons behaved like trigeminal neurons of r2 and r3. Goddard and colleagues reported that the motor nucleus of nerve VII failed to form.

Rhombomere 4 is still there. Its progenitors still divide, and they still make motor neurons, now with a different identity.

</details>

Not every patterning gene works this way. There are two outcomes to tell apart:

- **Territory lost.** In mice lacking Krox20, r3 and r5 fail to persist as distinct territories, and r5 is missing in kreisler (MafB) mutants. Fate mapping shows that some Krox20-lacking cells survive, mix with neighbours and take on the identity of even-numbered segments. Krox20 couples a segment's identity to keeping its cells apart.
- **Identity changed, segment kept.** Without Hoxb1, r4 stays recognizable but loses important parts of its identity.

These are different outcomes, not two separate classes of gene. Either way, the code acts as a cause, not just a marker. The next question is where its edges come from.

## Where the edges come from: a tap at the back, a drain at the front

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Retinoic acid is made from retinol (vitamin A) in two oxidations, through retinaldehyde. In the early embryo the main enzyme for the second step is Raldh2, made in the mesoderm (the middle tissue layer) beside the posterior hindbrain and the spinal cord. A family of enzymes called Cyp26 oxidizes retinoic acid to inactive products, and Cyp26a1 is strongly expressed toward the front of the neural plate.

Picture a bath with the tap at one end and the drain at the other. Supply behind and destruction in front leave retinoic acid high toward the back of the hindbrain and low toward the front. The picture works for something that spreads slowly, like dye running in with the tap water, which is darkest near the tap. It does not work for the water level, which evens out.

Retinoic acid is small and oily, so it crosses membranes and reaches the nucleus. There it binds the retinoic acid receptor (RAR), which works as a pair with the retinoid X receptor (RXR) on short DNA sequences called retinoic acid response elements (RAREs). Both are nuclear receptors, ligand-activated transcription factors of the kind covered in [that lesson](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/). Hoxa1 and Hoxb1 carry RAREs.

In cell culture, 3′ Hox genes respond to lower doses of retinoic acid than 5′ genes. In a simple threshold model, then, fewer Hox genes would come on toward the front, where the signal is lower or arrives for a shorter time, and those that did would lie toward the 3′ end of the cluster. The real hindbrain bends this model, as shown below. Retinoic acid mainly drives paralog groups 1 to 5, which include the hindbrain's groups 1 to 4. The more 5′ Hox genes of the spinal cord depend mainly on FGF and Wnt signals.

If that is right, changing the amount of signal should move the code.

**Predict.** Expose mouse embryos to extra retinoic acid at an early stage. Which way should the Hox edges move, and what should happen to r2 and r3?

Extra signal shows how the embryo responds to too much. It does not by itself show what the normal signal does.

</details>

Less signal pushes the other way. In mouse embryos lacking Raldh2, and in quail embryos deprived of vitamin A, the posterior hindbrain is poorly patterned: its rhombomeres, roughly r4 to r7 in quail, are reduced, lost or misspecified, so posterior character is lost (Niederreither et al., 2000; Maden et al., 1996). Blocking synthesis like this is the better guide to what the normal signal does.

### What the gradient cannot do

The table shows where the rule stops.

- **Edge order.** Hoxa2 and Hoxb2, in group 2, reach farther forward than Hoxb1, in group 1, though 3′ genes need less signal. Other inputs help set the group 2 edges: the midbrain–hindbrain signal that keeps Hoxa2 out of r1 in chick, and in mouse Krox20 and Hoxb1, which regulate Hoxb2 in r3, r5 and r4.
- **Narrowing.** A threshold rule in which retinoic acid only switches genes on predicts that each gene stays on from its edge all the way back. Hoxb1 instead ends up in r4 alone. Loops by which Hox genes and segment genes regulate one another do that, along with a second RARE in Hoxb1 that represses it in r3 and r5 (Studer and colleagues, 1994). So the signal takes part in the narrowing too.
- **Sharpness.** A smooth signal does not by itself say how sharp or stable an edge will be. Segment genes, feedback between genes and cell sorting (shown in fish and frog) make the boundaries in the table sharp and keep them that way.

Whether cells read how much signal they get or how long they get it is still argued (the Graduate explanation on this page says more).

### Retinoids as teratogens

A teratogen is an agent that causes birth defects when an embryo or fetus is exposed to it. Retinoids qualify because the early embryo uses this very signal to set its axis. In mouse and chick embryos, extra retinoic acid at an early stage shifts the Hox code of the hindbrain and disturbs the [neural crest](/lessons/neural-crest/) cells that leave it to build much of the face. In human embryos this mechanism is inferred from the animal work rather than shown directly, but it fits the ears, jaw and heart in the pattern of defects described in [Going deeper](#isotretinoin-and-the-first-reports). Too little vitamin A harms the embryo as well. The dose has to be right in both directions.

## Order on the chromosome, order along the body

Why are Hox genes switched on in an ordered series at all? The answer begins in the fruit fly.

A homeotic mutation turns one body part into the likeness of another: legs where antennae should be, or a second pair of wings in place of the small balancing organs called halteres. Edward Lewis spent decades on one cluster of such genes, the bithorax complex. In 1978 he proposed that its genes lie along the chromosome in the same order as the body segments they control, from front to back.

In 1984 two groups found what these genes share: a short stretch of DNA common to several fly homeotic genes. It was named the homeobox. It encodes the homeodomain, a part of the protein that later work showed binds DNA, with one helix lying in the major groove. One of the two groups, Gehring's in Basel, soon detected related sequences in vertebrate DNA. Body-plan genes belong to a shared animal toolkit (see [that lesson](/lessons/how-signaling-evolved/)).

Hox genes are one branch of the much larger homeobox family, and they come in clusters. Mice and humans have 39, in four clusters called Hoxa, Hoxb, Hoxc and Hoxd, each on a different chromosome. Genes that sit at the same position in different clusters, such as Hoxa3, Hoxb3 and Hoxd3, form a paralog group. There are 13 groups, and no cluster has all 13. Paralogs have similar sequences and often similar expression, so they can stand in for one another, and knocking out a single Hox gene often gives a milder defect than you might expect.

The clusters obey the rule Lewis saw, called colinearity.

- **Spatial colinearity.** Along the body as a whole, genes at the 3′ end of a cluster (group 1, then group 2 and so on) have the most anterior front edges of expression, reaching into the hindbrain. Genes farther toward the 5′ end have edges farther back, and group 13 is expressed near the tail.
- **Temporal colinearity.** In mouse and chick, 3′ genes are also switched on first and 5′ genes later, as the body axis grows backward. This is a vertebrate observation. In the fly the timing rule is weak or absent.

**Predict.** By the whole-body rule, which should reach farther forward in the hindbrain: Hoxb1, in group 1, or Hoxb2, in group 2? Check the table before you open this.

The rule says Hoxb1. The table says otherwise: Hoxb2 starts at r3 and Hoxa2 at r2, while Hoxb1 starts at r4. In the hindbrain the order bends, because other inputs help set the group 2 edges. Colinearity is a trend along the whole body, not a law for every boundary.

</details>

### What this example teaches about every axis

The hindbrain shows three principles that will come back in the lessons on the tube's other axes.

1. Particular genes are switched on at particular places and particular times.
2. Signals that spread from a source, and are destroyed or opposed elsewhere, form graded concentrations, and those gradients help set the pattern. Here the source is Raldh2 and the drain is Cyp26.
3. The signals restrict where certain transcription factors are made, and those factors then regulate the genes that give each region its character.

In the hindbrain, the signal is retinoic acid, working alongside FGF and Wnt signals, and the transcription factors are the Hox proteins, together with segment genes such as Krox20.

### Design problem

An embryo has extra Cyp26 activity in the posterior hindbrain. Using the guiding idea and its scope, predict which way the Hox edges move and say what the rule cannot tell you about which genes go first. Say what you would expect for the segments and motor neurons of r4 to r7. Then say how you would tell a segment that has changed identity from one that is missing.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Direction.** If the extra drain acts during the early patterning window, it should lower retinoic acid in the posterior hindbrain. By the rule, posterior segments should shift toward a more anterior identity, the direction seen when Raldh2 or vitamin A is lacking.

**What the rule cannot tell you.** It cannot say which genes go first or how far each edge moves. Even in a simple threshold model, lowering the signal by a factor k moves every edge back by the same distance only if the signal falls off exponentially (the shift is then λ ln(1/k), where λ is the distance over which the signal falls e-fold); with other profiles the shifts differ, and edges in front of the region with extra Cyp26 need not move at all. Nor does the gradient set every edge: the midbrain–hindbrain signal holds Hoxa2 out of r1, and loops and repression confine Hoxb1 to r4, so some Hox domains may move less than the gradient alone predicts. To find out, you would measure retinoic acid activity and Hox expression over time.

**Segments and neurons.** If the code shifts back, posterior segments would carry a code more like that of the segments in front of them. By the logic of the Hoxb1 knockout, their motor neurons might then take a more anterior program. That is a prediction from the rule, not a result in this lesson.

**Identity or lost territory.** Ask first whether each segment is there: its boundaries, its width, dividing progenitors and segment genes such as Krox20 in r3 and r5. A missing boundary or marker shows that a recognizable territory has failed, but not why. Cells may have changed identity, mixed with neighbours or died. To tell these apart, combine the shape of the tissue with several independent markers, lineage tracing, and counts of dividing and dying cells. If the segment is there, ask what it makes: which motor neurons, where they migrate, and which even-numbered rhombomere their axons leave from. A segment that is present but makes the wrong neurons has changed identity, as r4 does without Hoxb1.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The regions of the early tube

The three vesicles have formal names: the forebrain (prosencephalon), the midbrain (mesencephalon) and the hindbrain (rhombencephalon). The forebrain then divides into the telencephalon, which makes the cerebral hemispheres, and the diencephalon, which makes the thalamus and its neighbours and pushes out the optic vesicles that become the retinas. The hindbrain divides into the metencephalon (pons and cerebellum) and the myelencephalon (medulla).

Repeated units such as rhombomeres are called neuromeres. The question this lesson answers for the hindbrain can be put for all of them: how is the nervous system specified within neuromeres?

### The homeobox in numbers

The homeobox is about 180 base pairs long and encodes a homeodomain of about 60 amino acids. It was found in 1984 by the laboratory of Walter Gehring in Basel (William McGinnis, Michael Levine, Ernst Hafen, Atsushi Kuroiwa and Gehring) and by Matthew Scott and Amy Weiner in Thomas Kaufman's laboratory at Indiana University. The fly has eight Hox genes, in two complexes on one chromosome.

### The somites keep time too

The somites, blocks of mesoderm beside the tube that form one pair at a time (roughly every 90 minutes in chick), show temporal colinearity too, but that story belongs to them.

### Two Hox genes lost together

Mice lacking both Hoxa1 and Hoxb1 have been described as losing r4 and r5. The published descriptions differ in their wording, and some describe a severe reduction and remodelling of the r4–r5 region rather than a clean loss of both segments.

### Isotretinoin and the first reports

Isotretinoin, a synthetic form of retinoic acid (13-cis-retinoic acid), went on sale in the United States in 1982 as a treatment for severe acne. By 1985, Edward Lammer and colleagues had described a pattern of defects in children exposed to it early in pregnancy: small or missing ears, an altered face and jaw, heart defects, and abnormalities of the brain. They called it retinoic acid embryopathy. A pill taken for the skin had reached the embryo while its head and face were being laid out.

### The 1996 Hoxb1 knockouts

The two 1996 Hoxb1 knockout reports were by Michele Studer, Lumsden, Laila Ariza-McNaughton, Allan Bradley and Krumlauf in *Nature*, and by J. M. Goddard, Mireille Rossel, Nancy Manley and Mario Capecchi in *Development*.
