techniques:
  - name: "Competitor removal with a randomized, untouched comparison"
    blurb: >-
      Remove one species from around marked individuals of another in a portion of each plot,
      chosen at random, and leave the rest untouched. A difference in survival or growth between
      the portions measures the effect of the removed competitor at that site.
  - name: "Predator exclusion cages"
    blurb: >-
      Cover plots with mesh that keeps out a predator, and compare caged with open plots. This
      shows how many deaths the predator causes, provided the cage itself does not change conditions
      and the mesh actually excludes the predator. Crossing cages with competitor removal also shows
      whether the predator weakens competition by eating the competitor, as Connell found at Millport.
  - name: "Monoculture and coculture in laboratory microcosms"
    blurb: >-
      Grow each species alone and in mixture under the same conditions, from several starting
      ratios. A species that persists alone but disappears in mixture is being excluded by its
      competitor, and different starting ratios show whether the outcome depends on where you start.
  - name: "Long-term monitoring of a natural experiment"
    blurb: >-
      Measure the same wild population for many years, so that an unplanned event such as the
      arrival of a competitor can be compared with the years before it. It gives a before-and-after
      comparison without random assignment or replication.

    choices:
      - "Species 1 wins, since K2 is less than alpha21 K1"
      - "Species 2 wins, since only its coefficient changed"
      - "Both coexist, since alpha12 is still below one"
      - "Either wins, depending on the starting numbers"

    rationale: >-
      Coexistence needs both K1 > alpha12 K2 and K2 > alpha21 K1. With alpha21 = 1.0, K2 = 80 is
      less than 1.0 x 100, so species 2 cannot increase when rare: its per-capita rate at N1 = 100
      is r2(1 - 100/80), negative for any r2 (-0.0625 per day with the worked problem's r2 = 0.25), while species 1 can still invade. Species 1 excludes

**Two species eat the same limited food, and one grows twice as fast. Must the faster one win?**

Not in the model below. The first film built the test that decides; this text puts numbers on it.

> **The one idea.** Two competitors coexist stably when each can grow when it is rare and the other sits at its **carrying capacity**, the population size it would settle at alone. That is the **invasion test**, and in the Lotka-Volterra model below it decides the outcome.
>
> *Scope:* a model of two species in one place, both present at the start, with fixed parameters, an unchanging, well-mixed environment and no other enemies or resources. Exclusion means loss from that one place, not from everywhere. Exact ties, where a rare species' growth is exactly zero, are knife-edge cases this lesson sets aside.

## Kinds of competition

**Competition** is an interaction in which individuals use a resource in short supply, so that each lowers the survival, growth or reproduction of the others. The resource can be food, water, light, nutrients or space.

Competition within a species, **intraspecific competition**, is already built into the logistic model in [population growth](/lessons/population-growth/): growth slows as numbers approach the carrying capacity K. **Interspecific competition** is the same pressure between species that share a resource.

Competition also differs by route. In **exploitation competition** the effect is indirect: each competitor uses up a resource, leaving less for the other. In **interference competition** one individual acts directly on another, by fighting, overgrowing, or holding a territory. Think of two flatmates and one fridge: exploitation is eating the food first, interference is a lock on the door.

## Lotka-Volterra: the invasion test in numbers

The model named for Alfred Lotka and Vito Volterra extends the logistic to two species, each slowed by its own numbers and by the other's, converted into its own units:

> **dN1/dt = r1 N1 [1 - (N1 + alpha12 N2) / K1]**
> **dN2/dt = r2 N2 [1 - (N2 + alpha21 N1) / K2]**

Here N is a species' number of individuals, r its maximum **per-capita growth rate** (growth per individual, per day), and K its carrying capacity (individuals). The **competition coefficient** **alpha12**, read *on 1, from 2* as in the first film, is the effect of one species 2 individual on species 1, measured in species 1 individuals. If alpha12 = 0.5, two individuals of species 2 crowd species 1 as much as one of its own. Swapping the subscripts is the usual mix-up.

**Worked problem.** These values are invented for practice. Take r1 = 0.5 per day, r2 = 0.25 per day, K1 = 100 individuals, K2 = 80 individuals, alpha12 = alpha21 = 0.5, and start with N1 = N2 = 20. Assume constant parameters, no age structure (every individual counts the same, whatever its age) and no migration.

1. *Growth at the start.* For species 1, dN1/dt = 0.5 x 20 x [1 - (20 + 0.5 x 20) / 100] = 10 x (1 - 30/100) = 7 individuals per day. For species 2, dN2/dt = 0.25 x 20 x [1 - (20 + 0.5 x 20) / 80] = 5 x (1 - 30/80) = 3.125 individuals per day. Both grow.
2. *Isoclines.* An **isocline** is the line where a species' growth is zero. For species 1 it is N1 + 0.5 N2 = 100, running from N1 = 100 on its own axis to N2 = 200. For species 2 it is 0.5 N1 + N2 = 80, running from N2 = 80 to N1 = 160. Substitute N1 = 100 - 0.5 N2 into the second: 50 - 0.25 N2 + N2 = 80, so 0.75 N2 = 30, N2* = 40 and N1* = 80. Check: 80 + 0.5 x 40 = 100 and 0.5 x 80 + 40 = 80.
3. *The invasion test.* Species 1, rare among species 2 at its carrying capacity, grows at 0.5 x (1 - 0.5 x 80/100) = 0.3 per day. Species 2, rare among species 1 at N1 = 100, grows at 0.25 x (1 - 0.5 x 100/80) = 0.094 per day. Both pass. The two coexistence conditions are this same test, rearranged: K1 > alpha12 K2 is 100 > 0.5 x 80 = 40, and K2 > alpha21 K1 is 80 > 0.5 x 100 = 50. The model predicts stable coexistence at 80 and 40.

**Predict.** Raise alpha21 from 0.5 and change nothing else. How large can alpha21 get before species 2 fails the invasion test?

### The rule

The first film sorted the outcomes by who passes. In parameters:

- Both conditions hold: the species settle where the isoclines cross, **stable coexistence**.
- Only one holds: the species that passes **excludes** the other from this place.
- Both are reversed, K1 < alpha12 K2 and K2 < alpha21 K1: each species wins from some starts, so the **starting numbers decide** (Going deeper).

The film's words, each species limits itself more than it limits the other, say the same thing. Read "limits" as the share of a species' room that one individual fills. One individual of species 1 fills 1/K1 of its own room and alpha21/K2 of species 2's room. Species 1 limits itself more when 1/K1 > alpha21/K2; multiply both sides by K1 K2 to get K2 > alpha21 K1, the condition for species 2 to pass.

Since r is not in the conditions, it cannot change the verdict (Going deeper covers where r still matters).

## Exclusion is a model conclusion

In the 1930s Georgii Gause grew Paramecium species alone, where each grew well, and together, where P. aurelia persisted and P. caudatum declined toward disappearance. Results like this were later summed up as the **competitive exclusion principle**, a name popularized by Garrett Hardin in 1960: two species limited by exactly the same resource cannot coexist indefinitely.

Treat that as a model conclusion, not a law: it is the case where at least one species fails the invasion test. It follows when there is a single limiting resource, a constant and well-mixed environment, populations that settle to a steady state, and nothing else holding the winner down. Real places often break those conditions with several resources, changing seasons, patchy habitat, or predators that hit the commoner species.

To catch a competitor keeping a species out of a place it could otherwise live, take the competitor away and watch.

## Partitioning and character displacement

**Resource partitioning** is the division of resources among species by type, place or time. Less resource overlap can, all else equal, lower the competition coefficients, and in the model lower alphas make it easier for each species to pass the invasion test. Robert MacArthur's 1958 study of five warbler species in northeastern conifer forests found that they foraged in different parts of the same trees. The study was observational: no species was removed.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/competition/warblers-divide-the-spruce.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a single spruce tree in side view with one small warbler perched near the top and another among the dense middle branches, each surrounded by a gold shaded band marking where it fed." caption="Warblers share one spruce by feeding in different parts of it, Cape May warblers mostly in the tops and black-throated green warblers in the dense middle branches; watching shows partitioning, but it does not test whether competition caused it." />

Partitioning has more than one possible cause. Past competition might have driven species apart. Each species might shift its behaviour when the other is present, which is **plasticity**. Or only species that already differed could settle together, **ecological sorting**: no species changed; the place admitted only those that differed.

**Character displacement** is evolutionary divergence between species caused by their interactions. Here it means ecological character displacement: divergence in a trait caused by competition for limited resources. A claim needs four kinds of evidence:

1. The trait difference has an inherited basis, not just a response to surroundings. A difference that persists when populations are raised in the same environment, a **common garden**, supports this; heritability within one population does not by itself show that a difference between populations is genetic. Heritability of a trait like beak size is the subject of [complex traits and GWAS](/lessons/complex-traits-and-gwas/).
2. The trait affects how the organism gets its resource.
3. Sorting is ruled out.
4. Competition is the cause, not another change in the environment.

On Daphne Major in the Galapagos, Peter and Rosemary Grant reported that the medium ground finch, Geospiza fortis, diverged in beak size from the large ground finch, G. magnirostris, 22 years after magnirostris arrived, when the two species together severely depleted the food supply. It is a natural experiment: one island, one event, no random assignment, and a drought that coincided with the competition (see [testing evolutionary explanations](/lessons/testing-evolutionary-explanations/)). Against the list: beak size is highly heritable, and the change was close to what that heritability predicted, which supports an evolved change (item 1); large beaks crack the large hard seeds (item 2); sorting is a poor fit, because one resident population changed (item 3). Item 4, competition rather than drought, is the hardest.

**Predict.** In a drought in 1977, before magnirostris had arrived, which fortis beaks did selection favour? And in 2004, with magnirostris taking the large hard seeds?

### Design problem

Connell also fitted cages over some patches to keep out the whelk Thais, which was present in the open patches. How do you read the caged against the open plots alongside the removal, and what two additions would you make?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The cages test a second cause. If Thais were eating Chthamalus directly, Chthamalus kept free of Balanus would survive worse in open patches than in caged ones. Connell found little sign of that except at the lowest level. But Thais preferred the larger Balanus, so in open patches at and below mid-tide it thinned the competitor and weakened the competition. Predation and competition interact, so read the cage and removal comparisons together: they still leave Balanus setting Chthamalus's lower limit at Millport. Cages may not exclude every predator, so any conclusion should say how well the mesh worked.

What to add: a handling control, in which you prod around Chthamalus with the needle without removing anything, would check that disturbance alone does not change survival. That is your improvement, not part of Connell's design. Then repeat the removal on other shores, with other wave exposure and tidal ranges, before claiming the result holds beyond this one coast.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Mortality and r

Abreu and colleagues (2019) saw a slower grower win in soil-bacteria microcosms at low dilution. Raising the dilution rate, which adds a death rate, reversed the winner. In this model an equal per-capita death rate d, smaller than both growth rates, shrinks each K by the fraction d/r, more for the slow grower; the alphas stay the same, so added mortality favours the faster grower. If d reaches a species' r, it cannot persist even alone. They also found a coexistence-or-exclusion bistability that the basic model cannot produce.

### When neither species passes

The line dividing the starts each species wins from is not equal numbers: with unequal K and alpha, a species can win from behind. This line also moves when the growth rates change, so here r can decide the winner from a given start. A start exactly on the line settles at the unstable crossing; any nudge tips it.

### Species names

The Cumbrae Chthamalus stellatus is now identified as C. montagui; Balanus balanoides is now usually called Semibalanus balanoides; Thais is now Nucella lapillus.

### Clusters, not spacing

Model work by D'Andrea and colleagues (2019) adds a twist to partitioning: in their simulations, competition for resources sometimes produced clusters of similar species rather than evenly spaced ones.
