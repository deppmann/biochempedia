## Explain: two divisions make half

<WordBank terms={['meiosis', 'gamete', 'homologous chromosomes', 'diploid', 'haploid', 'sister chromatids', 'allele', 'crossing over', 'independent assortment', 'fertilization', 'genetic diversity']} />

Most of your body cells have 46 chromosomes, in 23 pairs. You got one chromosome of each pair from your mother and one from your father. The two chromosomes of a pair are **homologous chromosomes**: they carry the same genes in the same order. They do not have to carry the same versions. An **allele** is one version of a gene, such as the allele for purple or for white flowers in a pea plant.

A cell with two sets of chromosomes, one set from each parent, is **diploid**. A cell with one set is **haploid**. A **gamete** is a sex cell, an egg or a sperm, and in humans it is haploid, with 23 chromosomes.

**Meiosis** is the kind of cell division that makes haploid cells from a diploid cell. In animals those cells become gametes. (In plants, meiosis makes spores, which grow and later make gametes.) Before meiosis starts, the cell copies its DNA once. Each chromosome is then two **sister chromatids**, identical copies joined together. Then come two divisions:

1. **Meiosis I.** The homologous chromosomes line up in pairs. Then the two chromosomes of each pair are pulled apart into two cells. Each cell now has one chromosome of each pair, still in two copies.
2. **Meiosis II.** The sister chromatids of each chromosome separate. There is no DNA copying in between.

The result is usually four cells, each with one chromosome from every pair. In a woman, the divisions are uneven. One large egg forms, and the other products are small cells called polar bodies.

{/* DIAGRAM: one cell with two chromosome pairs (mother's color, father's color) through 1 DNA copying, 2 pairs lining up, 3 pairs separating in meiosis I, 4 copies separating in meiosis II, 5 four haploid cells */}

**Fertilization** is when an egg and a sperm join. Their two sets of 23 make a diploid cell with 46 again. Because a sex cell gets only one chromosome from each pair, if a parent has two different alleles of a gene, each sex cell has a 50 percent chance of getting either one.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Meiosis copies the DNA once and divides twice, first separating the pairs and then the copies, so each sex cell gets one chromosome from each pair.</Support>

## Explain: two ways meiosis mixes alleles

Meiosis I mixes alleles in two ways.

**Crossing over** is the swapping of matching pieces between the two chromosomes of a pair while they lie side by side in meiosis I. After a swap, one chromosome can carry some alleles from the grandmother and some from the grandfather. Studies that read the DNA of single human sperm count an average of about 22 to 28 crossovers per sperm, depending on the man.

**Independent assortment** means each chromosome pair lines up and separates on its own, without regard to the other pairs. A deck of cards gives a picture: shuffle well, and each hand you deal is a new mix. The picture breaks because a sex cell always gets exactly one card from each pair, never two from one pair.

Count the choices. Each pair can send either of its two chromosomes, so every pair doubles the number of combinations.

> number of chromosome combinations in a sex cell = 2ⁿ, where n is the number of chromosome pairs

For humans, n = 23, so one parent can make 2²³ = 8,388,608, or about 8.4 million, chromosome combinations. Two parents together give 8,388,608 × 8,388,608, which is more than 70 trillion. These are possibilities across many meioses, not children anyone could have, and they ignore crossing over, which adds far more.

One limit: genes that sit close together on the same chromosome tend to travel together. Independent assortment applies to genes on different chromosomes.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Crossing over swaps pieces between paired chromosomes, and independent assortment shuffles the pairs, so nearly every sex cell carries a new mix of alleles.</Support>

## Explain: mitosis and meiosis side by side

You met **mitosis**, the division that makes two cells with the same chromosomes as the parent cell, in [The Cell Cycle and Mitosis](/lessons/cell-cycle-and-mitosis/).

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Growth, repair, asexual reproduction | Making gametes (in animals) |
| DNA copied | Once | Once |
| Divisions | One | Two |
| Cells made | Usually two | Usually four (one egg in females) |
| Chromosome sets | Diploid stays diploid | Diploid becomes haploid |
| Genetic sameness | Cells match the parent cell | Each cell is a new mix |

The "match" in mitosis is a close match. Rare copying mistakes, called mutations, can change a cell's DNA. Mutations are the other source of new alleles, and recombination by crossing over and shuffling mixes them into new combinations.

Asexual reproduction, with one parent and mitosis-like division, makes offspring that are genetically the same as the parent. Sexual reproduction makes offspring that differ. **Genetic diversity** is the variety of alleles and allele combinations in a population. When the environment changes, some combinations may do better than others, so a varied population is more likely to have some members that survive and reproduce. Diversity raises the chances; it does not guarantee survival.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Mitosis makes two matching cells for growth and repair; meiosis makes four varied haploid cells, and that variety helps a population face a changing environment.</Support>

### Design problem

Does sexual reproduction help a population adapt to a harsh environment? Plan a lab investigation with yeast, which can reproduce with or without meiosis. Name your variables, constants, controls, sample size and repeated trials, and say how you will stay safe.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do yeast populations that go through meiosis and mating adapt faster to a hot, salty environment than populations that cannot?
- **Independent variable:** whether the population can reproduce sexually. Use a strain that can and a closely matched strain that cannot.
- **Dependent variable:** fitness after many generations, measured by growing each evolved population mixed with its ancestor and seeing which one increases.
- **Constants:** the same growth medium, number of generations, number and timing of starvation rounds that trigger meiosis and spore formation, and transfer schedule.
- **Controls:** run both kinds of population in a comfortable environment too. If sex helps only in the harsh one, the benefit depends on the environment. Also keep frozen ancestors to compare against.
- **Sample size and trials:** several separate populations of each kind in each environment, since one population can change by chance.
- **Safety:** use lab yeast strains, wear goggles, handle heat and sterilizing equipment with your teacher, and follow the lab's rules for disposing of cultures.

A larger gain for the sexual populations in the harsh environment only would support the idea that mixing alleles helps most when conditions change.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

Brothers and sisters differ because each grows from a different egg and a different sperm. Meiosis makes every sex cell a new mix: crossing over swaps pieces of paired chromosomes, and independent assortment shuffles 23 pairs into millions of combinations. Identical twins come from one fertilized egg, which is why they match so closely.
