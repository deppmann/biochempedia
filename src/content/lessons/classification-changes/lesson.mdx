## Explain: how the sorting system has changed

<WordBank terms={['classification', 'kingdom', 'domain', 'phylogeny', 'embryology', 'protist', 'ribosomal RNA', 'Archaea', 'Bacteria', 'Eukarya']} />

**Classification** is the sorting of living things into groups based on how they are related. The groups are nested, like folders inside folders. A **kingdom** is a large group, such as plants or animals. A **domain** is the largest group of all, above kingdom.

Biologists build these groups from several kinds of evidence:
- **Structure:** body parts and cell parts, such as a nucleus or a cell wall.
- **Embryology:** how an organism develops before birth or hatching. **Embryology** is the study of that early development.
- **Phylogeny:** the history of ancestors and descendants. A **phylogeny** is that family history, often drawn as a branching tree.
- **DNA and RNA:** the order of the bases in an organism’s genes and other molecules.

The system has been rebuilt several times. Each date below is the year a scheme was published, not the year everyone agreed.

| Year | Scheme | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| 1735 | Linnaeus, *Systema naturae* | Living things sorted as plants or animals (minerals too) |
| 1866 | Haeckel | Protista added as a third living kingdom |
| 1938 | Copeland | A four-kingdom system, with bacteria apart |
| 1969 | Whittaker | Five kingdoms, with Fungi apart from plants |
| 1977 | Woese and Fox | RNA evidence for three major lines of descent |
| 1990 | Woese, Kandler and Wheelis | Three domains placed above the kingdoms |

Many US textbooks use six kingdoms: Archaebacteria, Eubacteria, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia. A **protist** is a eukaryote that is not a plant, animal or fungus. The SOL keeps Protista as a kingdom, and you will use it. Newer trees divide protists into many separate groups, because some protists are closer to animals or plants than to other protists.

## Explain: a molecule every cell shares

Every cell builds proteins on ribosomes. **Ribosomal RNA** (rRNA) is the RNA that forms much of a ribosome. Because every cell has it, rRNA can be compared across all cellular life. Its sequence changes slowly, so even very distant groups still share enough of it to compare. Shared does not mean identical: the small differences are the evidence.

Woese and Fox cut rRNA into short pieces and listed the pieces from each organism. Then they scored how much two lists overlapped, from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (the same). Methane-making microbes scored high with each other and low with typical bacteria. In 1977 they read this as three major lines of descent.

In 1990, Woese, Otto Kandler and Mark Wheelis proposed the rank of domain, above kingdom, with three names:
- **Bacteria:** the domain of familiar single-celled prokaryotes, such as *E. coli*.
- **Archaea:** the domain of single-celled prokaryotes that differ from bacteria in their RNA and membranes.
- **Eukarya:** the domain of organisms whose cells have a nucleus, including animals, plants, fungi and protists. The 1990 paper spelled it Eucarya.

Archaea are not only microbes of hot springs. The 1977 samples included one that grows best at moderate temperatures. In 1992, archaea were found in ordinary coastal seawater.

The tree is still being tested. Genome studies published in 2023 and 2025 placed eukaryotes inside one branch of the archaea. Scientists still use the three domain names, but they disagree about the exact branch.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Comparing ribosomal RNA, which all cells share, revealed that archaea form their own domain, separate from bacteria.</Support>

## Explain: other groups that moved

DNA and protein comparisons have moved other groups too.

- **Fungi and animals.** In 1993, a survey of 25 proteins strongly supported fungi being closer to animals than to plants. The sampled animals and fungi shared four insertions or deletions that plants lacked. This does not mean animals came from mushrooms. It means animals and fungi share a more recent common ancestor.
- **Two African elephants.** In 2010, a study of 39,763 DNA bases from 375 places in the nuclear genome supported treating forest and savanna elephants as two species. The study had only one or two animals of each kind, so it could not settle every detail.
- **Whales.** DNA comparisons placed whales among the even-toed hoofed mammals, with hippos as their closest living relatives. Some biologists use one group name, Cetartiodactyla, for them all, and others simply widen Artiodactyla to include whales.
- **Protists.** Gene sequences split the old kingdom Protista into many groups that are not each other’s closest relatives.

Why is it fine for science to change its mind? A classification is a claim about ancestry. A good claim can be tested, and some tests show it needs fixing. The older groups were reasonable for the evidence of their time.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Reclassification shows science testing its claims against new evidence, which makes the system more accurate over time.</Support>

### Design problem

You think a newly found microbe from a pond might be an archaeon, not a bacterium. Plan how you would test this with sequence comparisons.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Is the pond microbe’s rRNA more similar to known archaea or to known bacteria?
- **Independent variable:** the comparison organism (several known archaea and several known bacteria).
- **Dependent variable:** the similarity of the matching rRNA gene, as a percent of matching bases.
- **Constants:** compare the same gene and the same length of sequence, and use the same computer method for every pair.
- **Control:** include two known bacteria compared with each other, and two known archaea, so you know what a within-group score looks like.
- **Repeated trials and sample size:** sequence the pond microbe more than once, and use at least five organisms from each domain, not one.
- **Second line of evidence:** check a different gene or the membrane fats, because one marker can mislead.
- **Safety:** treat unknown microbes as possible germs; grow them only in a supervised lab.

If the pond microbe scores clearly higher with every archaeon than with every bacterium, and the second marker agrees, the evidence supports calling it an archaeon.

</details>
