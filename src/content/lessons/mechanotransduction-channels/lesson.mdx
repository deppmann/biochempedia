  Piezo2 is a principal channel for touch and limb position. How does force open it? One idea carries
  the lesson: tension pays for area. A channel that takes up more membrane when open is pushed open by
  membrane tension, and in a two-state model that idea gives both numbers in a current-tension fit a

  sixty-fold gap between that area and the structural one, and to weigh the lipid and tether routes.

  - name: "Bertrand Coste"
    contribution: >-
      First author of the 2010 Science paper that identified Fam38A/Piezo1 as necessary for
      mechanically activated current in cultured mouse Neuro2A cells, and first author of the 2013
      PNAS study showing that human PIEZO2 variants found in distal arthrogryposis change the
      channel's inactivation.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Coste B, Mathur J, Schmidt M, Earley TJ, Ranade S, Petrus MJ, Dubin AE, Patapoutian A, Science 330:55-60 (2010); Coste B et al., PNAS (2013), doi 10.1073/pnas.1221400110"
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The problem in 2010 was that nobody knew which protein carried the current that appears when
      you poke a mammalian cell. Working in Ardem Patapoutian's group at The Scripps Research Institute, Coste
      took a cultured mouse neuroblastoma line, Neuro2A, that reliably gave such a current, and
      knocked down candidate genes one at a time while recording whole-cell current during
      indentation by a separate glass probe. Knocking down Fam38A, renamed Piezo1, sharply reduced the
      current. Expressing Piezo1, or the related Piezo2, in heterologous cells produced mechanically
      activated currents whose kinetics differed from one another. The paper did not claim the pore:
      it kept open the possibility that Piezo proteins were non-conducting subunits of something
      else. Three years later Coste and colleagues took human PIEZO2 variants from families with
      distal arthrogryposis, expressed them in HEK293T cells, and found changed inactivation. That
      pair of papers gave the field a gene, an assay and a disease handle at once.

    contribution: >-
      Co-author of the 2015 cryo-EM reconstruction of purified mouse Piezo1, and senior author of the 2019 mouse
      PIEZO2 structure that counted 38 transmembrane helices per protomer in a curved dome, and of

    profileSourceAnchor: "Ge J et al., Nature (2015), doi 10.1038/nature15247; Wang L et al., Nature 573:225-229 (2019); Yang X, Lin C, Chen X, Li S, Li X, Xiao B, Nature 604:377-383 (2022)"
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Xiao's laboratory in Beijing has pushed one question for a decade: what shape is a Piezo
      channel, and does that shape change when it works. The 2015 reconstruction of purified mouse
      Piezo1, with Jingpeng Ge as first author, reached 4.8 angstroms and showed a trimer with three propeller-like
      arms. In 2018 the group resolved the arms into repeating four-helix units curved into blades,
      and found that mutating single residues of the long intracellular beam impaired mechanical
      activation. In 2019, with first author Li Wang, they counted 38 transmembrane helices per
      protomer of mouse PIEZO2, 114 in the trimer, bowing the membrane into a dome about 28 nm across
      and 10 nm deep. In 2022, with first author Xuzhong Yang, they put purified PIEZO1 into liposome
      vesicles and resolved curved and flattened classes of the same protein, reporting about 300
      square nanometres of in-plane expansion. Structure stopped being a static portrait and became a
      measurement of how much area gating costs.
  - name: "Christoph A. Haselwandter"
    contribution: >-
      With Roderick MacKinnon, published a 2018 continuum-elasticity calculation in which the
      membrane deformation caused by a Piezo dome extends well beyond the protein itself, so that the
      energy of gating is paid partly by the surrounding bilayer.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Haselwandter CA, MacKinnon R, eLife 7:e41968 (2018), doi 10.7554/eLife.41968; Guo YR, MacKinnon R, eLife 6:e33660 (2017)"
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The dome idea came out of structures, but its consequences are a problem in elasticity, and
      Haselwandter, a physicist at the University of Southern California, worked them out with
      MacKinnon. If a trimer with curved blades sits in a bilayer, the bilayer cannot stay flat where
      the protein ends: it bends away, and that bent skirt is a membrane footprint far larger than the
      channel. Their 2018 calculation took the closed-dome geometry from the purified mouse Piezo1
      structure, with a cap surface area of about 390 square nanometres and a radius of curvature of
      10.2 nanometres, and computed how far the deformation reaches and what it costs. The result
      reframed the gating question. In the model, membrane tension does work on the
      footprint as well as on the protein, so the energy available to flatten the dome is larger than
      the channel's own area would suggest. These are model inputs and model outputs, not recordings,
      and the paper says so. What it gave the field was a way to put numbers on the lipid argument.
  - name: "Alexander T. Chesler"
    contribution: >-
      Led the 2016 New England Journal of Medicine report of two people with compound inactivating
      PIEZO2 variants, who had impaired discriminative touch and profound proprioceptive impairment
      that worsened when visual cues were removed.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Chesler AT, Szczot M, Bharucha-Goebel D, Čeko M, Donkervoort S et al., N Engl J Med (2016), doi 10.1056/NEJMoa1602812; Szczot M et al., Sci Transl Med (2018), doi 10.1126/scitranslmed.aat9892"
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Mouse conditional genetics had tied Piezo2 to light touch, but mouse skin is not human skin and
      a floxed allele is not a human genotype. Chesler's group at the National Institutes of Health
      worked with two people who carried compound inactivating PIEZO2 variants, and tested them rather
      than inferring from the mice. Discriminative touch was impaired. Proprioception was affected
      profoundly, and performance fell further when the participants could not see their own limbs,
      which is the clinical signature of a proprioceptive rather than a motor problem. Abilities
      remained: the deficits were selective, not a loss of all mechanical sensation. In 2018, with
      Mark Szczot as first author, the group added an experimental skin-inflammation protocol and
      found that four other people with PIEZO2 loss of function did not develop the painful
      responses to gentle touch that controls develop. Human loss of function became a measurement instead of an extrapolation.

techniques:
  - name: "Candidate-gene knockdown with whole-cell patch clamp"
    blurb: >-
      Short interfering RNA reduces one transcript at a time in a cultured cell line while a glass
      probe indents the cell and a patch pipette records whole-cell current. If the current
      disappears, that gene is required for it in that preparation. It does not show which protein
      forms the pore.
  - name: "Pressure-clamp recording with differential interference contrast imaging"
    blurb: >-
      A cell-attached patch is pressurised through the pipette while the patch is imaged, so its
      radius of curvature is measured rather than assumed. With the radius and the pressure
      difference you compute membrane tension, which turns a plot of current against pressure into a
      plot of current against tension.
  - name: "Purification and reconstitution into lipid bilayers"
    blurb: >-
      The channel is purified away from every other cellular component and placed in an artificial
      bilayer, such as a droplet bilayer or a liposome. Mechanical activation there shows that the
      protein plus lipid is sufficient. Absence of a cellular feature in the reconstituted system is
      informative too.
  - name: "Single-particle cryo-electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Purified protein is vitrified (frozen so fast that ice crystals cannot form), imaged as thousands of individual particles and averaged into a
      three-dimensional map. Distinct classes within one data set can represent distinct
      conformations. A map is a static reconstruction, not a time course of gating.

  - stem: "Read as an equilibrium two-state switch, a current-tension fit with slope factor k = 0.8 mN/m implies an effective area change of about 5 nm². Under the same assumptions, a second fit gives k = 0.4 mN/m. What effective area change does it imply?"
    choices:
      - "About 2.5 nm², because a smaller slope factor means less area"
      - "About 10 nm², because the slope factor is thermal energy divided by the area"
      - "About 5 nm², because the slope factor does not depend on area"
      - "About 20 nm², because the area scales with the square of the slope factor"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The slope factor is thermal energy divided by the area, so the area is thermal energy divided by
      the slope factor: (4.1 × 10⁻²¹ J)/(0.4 × 10⁻³ N/m), about 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ m² or 10 nm². Halving the slope factor doubles the area and makes the curve steeper. Reading a

      inverse, not inverse-square. Either way the answer is an effective two-state area, not a
      structural measurement.

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm five"
    text: >-
      Some proteins in your skin are shaped like tiny shallow bowls pushed into the cell's wrapper.
      When something presses or stretches the wrapper, the bowl gets pulled flat. When it flattens enough, a
      hole in the middle can open and charged particles rush through, and that is the start of the
      feeling of being touched. Scientists found the gene for these bowls by switching genes off one
      at a time until poking a cell stopped working.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Piezo1 and Piezo2 are ion channels that open when a membrane is pulled. Researchers found the first of them
      in 2010 by silencing candidate genes in a mouse cell line and recording the electrical current
      produced when a glass probe pressed the cell. Pictures taken later with an electron microscope
      show three curved arms that bow the membrane into a dome about 28 nanometres wide. Because a
      flat shape covers more area than a curved one, stretching the membrane favours the flat shape,
      and flattening is thought to help open the pore. Mutations in the human PIEZO2 gene change how quickly the current
      shuts off, and people who lack working PIEZO2 have specific problems with touch and with
      sensing where their limbs are.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      Piezo1 was identified in 2010 by candidate-gene knockdown in cultured mouse Neuro2A cells,
      where knockdown of Fam38A sharply reduced the current evoked by probe indentation; Piezo2 was the related
      protein reported in the same paper, and the paper left open whether Piezo proteins form the pore. Cryo-EM
      later showed a trimer whose blades, built from repeating four-helix units, curve the bilayer
      into a dome, with the carboxy-terminal domains lining the central pore. Mouse PIEZO2 has 38
      transmembrane helices per protomer, 114 per trimer. The force-from-lipid model holds that
      membrane tension favours flattening because a flatter dome projects more area onto the membrane
      plane, and purified Piezo1 is mechanically gated in artificial bilayers. A complementary
      force-from-filament route through cadherin, beta-catenin and actin has been proposed from
      cultured-cell work. Human PIEZO2 variants alter inactivation kinetics, and loss of function
      produces selective touch and proprioceptive deficits.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      The quantitative core is an area-tension energy. For a state with larger projected area, the
      tension-dependent contribution is minus T times ΔA, with T the tension, so tension biases the ensemble
      toward the flatter, larger-footprint state. Yang and colleagues (2022) resolved curved and
      flattened mouse PIEZO1 in liposome vesicles, reported roughly 300 square nanometres of in-plane
      expansion, and calculated a half-maximal activation tension near 1.9 pN/nm using a previously
      measured stiffness; the primary abstract hedges the link between those rearrangements and
      gating. Haselwandter and MacKinnon (2018) showed by continuum elasticity that the deformation
      footprint extends past the dome, so the effective area doing work on the channel exceeds the
      protein's own; their closed-dome inputs, a cap area near 390 square nanometres and a 10.2 nm
      radius of curvature, are model parameters rather than the structural 300 square nanometres.
      Empirically, Lewis and Grandl (2015) measured patch geometry during pressure clamp in mouse
      Piezo1-expressing HEK293t cells and fitted T50 = 2.7 ± 0.1 mN/m with a slope of 0.8 ± 0.1 mN/m,
      and in a separate prepulse protocol measured 1.4 ± 0.1 mN/m after a five-second +5 mmHg prepulse against 2.2 ± 0.1 mN/m without one.
      Read through an equilibrium two-state Boltzmann, where the slope factor is thermal energy over ΔA, that
      0.8 mN/m slope factor implies an effective area of about 5 square nanometres, the same order as the 6 to 20 estimated for human PIEZO1 against MscL
      (Bae and colleagues, 2013) and some sixty-fold below the structural area. If the channels are
      independent two-state switches that differ only in half-activation tension, spread among them can
      only make the population curve shallower, and the slope-derived area then underestimates their
      common area; outside those assumptions it is an effective area, not a guaranteed bound.

      What is open: whether a tether is required for gating at a sensory ending in vivo, and what besides tension licenses opening. In 2026 Vaisey and MacKinnon reported that Piezo1 does not conduct in the kind of lipids used for the vesicle structures, but in cell-derived membranes it flattens further, with changes around the pore; they propose that force is necessary but not sufficient. Purified
      Piezo1 is sufficient in droplet bilayers but does not reproduce rapid whole-cell inactivation,
      and a cadherin-dependent route was reported in cultured cells. Deciding the question needs
      acute, afferent-specific perturbation with recordings at the ending, not a germline deletion
      and not indentation without measured geometry.

**Mouse PIEZO2 threads 114 transmembrane helices through the membrane to build a single ion channel. The pore it opens sits at the centre.**

Why would a channel need so much membrane-spanning protein around one pore? Piezo channels turn mechanical force directly into ionic current, and Piezo2 is a principal channel for touch and limb position: mice lacking it in sensory neurons lose almost all light-touch behaviour, and two people lacking working PIEZO2 had selective deficits in touch and especially limb position. [Touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) names Piezo2 as the channel mice need for gentle touch, and [transduction strategies](/lessons/transduction-strategies/) places direct mechanical gating among the ways a stimulus becomes a current. This lesson asks the mechanical question those lessons leave alone.

## Three questions and one idea

Three questions run through the lesson. Which protein carries the current when a cell is poked? What shape is it? And how does force open it: through the lipid around it, through a protein tether, or both?

The leading structural model, still debated, answers with a shape. The channel is a shallow dome, force favours flattening it, and flattening is thought to help open the pore. One idea turns that picture into numbers you can compute yourself.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson. Tension pays for area.** If a channel takes up more membrane area when it is open than when it is closed, membrane tension pushes it open. The push is an energy: tension times the area gained, T·ΔA. In a two-state model, that energy gives both numbers in a current-tension fit a meaning, and more area means a steeper switch. Piezo's curved dome is a way to gain a lot of area when it flattens; in the leading model, that is what the 114 helices are for.

## Which protein? A screen, and the claim it refused to make

The 2010 experiment was built around a cultured mouse neuroblastoma line, Neuro2A, that gave a reliable current when a separate glass probe indented the cell while a patch pipette recorded whole-cell current, the current across the entire cell membrane. Coste and colleagues silenced candidate genes one at a time with short interfering RNA and asked which loss reduced the current.

Coste and colleagues then expressed mouse Piezo1, or the related Piezo2, in heterologous cultured cells, meaning other cultured cells given an introduced copy of the gene. Each produced mechanically activated currents, and their kinetics differed between the two. Ion-substitution experiments pointed to a non-selective cation conductance for Piezo2, and its current inactivated quickly. **Inactivation** is the decay of a mechanically activated current while the stimulus is held; human variants change it.

This logic recurs across the unit. The screen showed necessity in one cell line; expression showed sufficiency to confer a current in another. Neither shows that the protein lines the pore, and the authors said so. Necessity, sufficiency and pore identity are three separate claims, and the 2010 paper earned two.

## Blades, beam and dome

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The first map, from Ge and colleagues in 2015, was a trimer (three identical subunits, or protomers) with three propeller-like arms at 4.8 angstroms. Zhao and colleagues then resolved the arms into repeating four-helix units assembled into curved blades around the central pore. They also found that mutating single residues of the long intracellular beam impaired mechanical activation, which ties a specific part of the architecture to gating rather than to assembly.

By 2019 the picture was sharp enough to count. Mouse PIEZO2, reconstructed by Wang L and colleagues (Nature, 2019), holds 38 transmembrane helices per protomer, which means 114 in the trimer. Keep them apart: 38 describes one subunit, 114 the whole channel.

The curved blades bow the bilayer into a dome, reported as about 28 nm in diameter and 10 nm deep for purified mouse PIEZO2.

A dome has two areas, and the difference between them is the whole argument. Its **surface area** is the membrane actually in it. Its **projected area** is its shadow on the flat plane of the surrounding membrane: the area it occupies seen from above. A curved cap projects less area than its surface area. Flatten the same cap and the two become equal.

**Predict.** Treat the PIEZO2 dome as an idealized spherical cap, 28 nm across and 10 nm deep. Flatten it without stretching it. Does its projected area grow or shrink, and by roughly how much?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It grows, by about 310 nm². A spherical cap whose rim has radius a and whose depth is h has a surface area of π(a² + h²). Here a = 14 nm and h = 10 nm, so the surface is π(196 + 100) nm² ≈ 930 nm². Its shadow is the disc under the rim, πa² = π × 196 nm² ≈ 616 nm². Flattened, all 930 nm² lies in the plane, so the projected area rises by about 930 − 616 ≈ 310 nm².

The gain is simply πh² (π × 10² ≈ 314 nm²). It depends on how deep the dome is, not on how wide. This is idealized geometry, not a measurement: the real dome is protein blades and bent lipid, not a smooth cap of membrane.

</details>

## Tension pays for area: from an energy to a curve

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Take the simplest picture: the channel is either closed or open, and the open state has a larger projected area, by an amount ΔA. Membrane **tension**, T, is an in-plane stretch: force per unit length of membrane. Stretching a membrane does work on anything in it that gets wider, and that work is the tension times the area gained. So tension lowers the free energy of the open state relative to the closed one:

To turn the energy into a curve, use the Boltzmann relation from statistical physics. For a channel with two states at equilibrium, the ratio of open to closed is exp(−ΔG/k<sub>B</sub>T<sub>abs</sub>), where k<sub>B</sub>T<sub>abs</sub> is thermal energy, about 4.1 × 10⁻²¹ J at room temperature (k<sub>B</sub> is Boltzmann's constant and T<sub>abs</sub> absolute temperature; T stays tension, and neither is the fitted slope factor k). Written with energies, the same curve is P(open) = 1 / [1 + exp((ΔG₀ − T·ΔA)/k<sub>B</sub>T<sub>abs</sub>)] (the card's optional fold shows the steps). At T = T50 the energy difference ΔG is zero, so half the channels are open.

The tension form is the one fitted to patch data, usually as a normalized current. Reading a current fit this way assumes that normalized current tracks the fraction of channels open: constant conductance and availability, and a plateau where the available channels are open. With that assumption, matching the tension form with the energy form gives each fitted number a physical meaning:

- **T50 = ΔG₀ / ΔA.** The half-activation tension is the channel's resting preference for closed, divided by the area it gains on opening.
- **k = k<sub>B</sub>T<sub>abs</sub> / ΔA.** The slope factor is thermal energy divided by that area. A larger area gives a smaller k and a steeper switch.

One unit note before the numbers. Tension is force per length, so 1 pN/nm and 1 mN/m are the same quantity. Structural papers tend to use the first and patch papers the second.

### Worked problem: from patch pressure to predicted current, and back to an area

Take an illustrative spherical patch of radius R = 2.0 micrometres under a pressure difference of magnitude 2000 Pa. The radius and pressure are invented round numbers. The fit parameters are from the paper's pressure-clamp fit for these recordings: a half-activation tension T50 = 2.7 ± 0.1 mN/m and a Boltzmann slope factor k = 0.8 ± 0.1 mN/m.

1. Tension: T = (2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m)(2000 N/m²)/2 = 0.002 N/m = 2.0 mN/m. Units: metres times newtons per square metre gives newtons per metre.
2. Predicted normalized current: I/Imax = 1 / [1 + exp((T50 − T)/k)] = 1 / [1 + exp((2.7 − 2.0)/0.8)] = 1 / (1 + e^0.875). e^0.875 is about 2.40 (between e^0 = 1 and e^1 ≈ 2.72; see [e and ln](/math-toolkit/#e-and-ln)), so I/Imax = 1/3.40 = 0.294, about 29 percent of the fitted plateau.

The fit also hides an area. Before you read on, use k = k<sub>B</sub>T<sub>abs</sub>/ΔA to find it.

3. Run the fit backwards, reading it as a two-state switch. Rearrange k = k<sub>B</sub>T<sub>abs</sub>/ΔA by multiplying both sides by ΔA and dividing both by k: ΔA = k<sub>B</sub>T<sub>abs</sub> / k = (4.1 × 10⁻²¹ J) / (0.8 × 10⁻³ N/m) = 5.1 × 10⁻¹⁸ m², an effective area of about **5 nm²**. Units: joules divided by newtons per metre gives square metres, and 1 nm² is 10⁻¹⁸ m².
4. Under the same reading, the effective resting preference for closed is ΔG₀ = T50 × ΔA = (2.7 × 10⁻³ N/m)(5.1 × 10⁻¹⁸ m²) = 1.4 × 10⁻²⁰ J, about 3.4 k<sub>B</sub>T<sub>abs</sub>. This number is only as good as the two-state reading: it is computed from the fitted area, so it is not an independent measurement of how strongly the channel prefers closed.

An independent route gives the same order. Calibrated against the bacterial channel MscL in the same patch, human PIEZO1's Boltzmann slope implies about 6 to 20 nm² on opening (Bae and colleagues, 2013). Both estimates assume a two-state switch, and the next section shows why the area actually coupled to opening could be larger.

Two cautions travel with the answer to step 2. It is a normalized current for mouse Piezo1 in a human cell line at room temperature in the cell-attached configuration, not the fraction of channels or of neurons doing anything. And protocol matters. The same paper ran a separate prepulse protocol. Within it, T50 was 2.2 ± 0.1 mN/m without a prepulse and 1.4 ± 0.1 mN/m after a five-second +5 mmHg prepulse. Compare those two with each other, not with 2.7, which belongs to the other protocol. The authors' reading is that resting tension inactivates some channels; the prepulse relieves it, they recover, and less added tension is needed. That shift is the finding: Piezo1's apparent sensitivity is not a fixed property; it depends on the tension the channel was already under.

## The gap between structure and current

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

Yang and colleagues (2022) put purified mouse PIEZO1 into liposome vesicles and resolved curved and flattened classes from the same sample. Between them they reported roughly 300 nm² of in-plane areal expansion. From that area together with a previously measured stiffness, they calculated a half-maximal activation tension of about 1.9 pN/nm, which is 1.9 mN/m. Their abstract hedges whether the rearrangements are the gating transition itself.

**Predict.** Using k = k<sub>B</sub>T<sub>abs</sub>/ΔA, how steep would the current-tension curve be if all 300 nm² were coupled to opening? Compare it with the measured k of 0.8 mN/m.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

k = (4.1 × 10⁻²¹ J)/(300 × 10⁻¹⁸ m²) ≈ 0.014 mN/m. The channel would go from 10 to 90 percent open over about 0.06 mN/m of tension (in this curve the 10-to-90 span is about 4.4 slope factors, 2 × ln 9). The measured fit takes about 3.5 mN/m. The half-activation tensions are of the same order (1.9 mN/m calculated from structure and a stiffness, 2.7 from a patch fit), but the slope factors differ about sixty-fold.

</details>

Geometry alone makes a change of that order plausible. The idealized PIEZO2 cap you flattened earlier gained about 310 nm², the same order as Yang's 300 nm², although that was a different paralog (Piezo2, not Piezo1) and a calculation rather than a measurement. So why is the measured switch so shallow? The question is open; each candidate answer is testable:

- **The dome may not be a two-state switch.** If it flattens gradually, like a spring, only the part of the flattening coupled to the pore sets the slope, and a single two-state ΔA no longer describes the channel.
- **A patch curve is a population curve.** It sums many channels, the published fit also pools different patches, and uneven tension across a patch could spread their T50s. If the channels are independent two-state switches that differ only in T50, spread can only make the population curve shallower. As an illustration, identical 300 nm² switches whose T50s follow a normal spread with a standard deviation of about 1.4 mN/m give a curve that a single Boltzmann fits with k ≈ 0.8 mN/m. That spread is wider than the 0.8 mN/m prepulse shift, so whether real patches are that uneven is open.
- **The flattened class may not be the open channel,** which is the hedge Yang and colleagues make.

What the patch number means depends on the answer. Under the second, the slope-derived area underestimates the common area of the switches. Under the first and third, the area actually coupled to opening could be far smaller than 300 nm², near the patch number. Either way it is an effective area, not a measurement: "Piezo1 gains 5 nm² on opening" is not something anyone has shown, and neither is "hundreds of nm²".

The membrane around the channel adds to the puzzle. Haselwandter and MacKinnon (2018) worked out the elasticity with a continuum calculation. A bilayer cannot stay flat where a curved protein ends; it bends away in a skirt, a membrane footprint reaching well past the dome, so tension does work on the footprint as well as on the protein. Their closed-dome inputs were a cap surface area of about 390 nm² and a radius of curvature of 10.2 nm. Those are model parameters. By now you have met five area numbers. Keep them apart:

## Lipid or tether?

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

The guiding idea is an energy budget for membrane tension, not a delivery route. The −T·ΔA term is the work done by membrane tension. A tether could change the local tension, or it could pull on the channel directly, which would add its own work term, force times distance.

The lipid route has a clean test: take everything else away. Purified mouse Piezo1, reconstituted into artificial droplet bilayers with no other cellular components, remained mechanically activatable (Syeda and colleagues, 2016). A droplet bilayer forms where two lipid-coated droplets of salt solution touch, so nothing from a cell is present.

**Predict.** Suppose a tether to the cytoskeleton were strictly required for Piezo1 to open. What should the purified channel have done in a droplet bilayer? And since it did open, what can you conclude about an intact cell?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It should have stayed silent. Because it opened, protein plus lipid is sufficient in that preparation, and no tether is required there. That says nothing about whether an intact cell also uses a second route.

</details>

Force could also arrive through protein. Wang J and colleagues (Cell Reports, 2022) proposed a cadherin-beta-catenin-actin route, complementary to force-from-lipid, and reported that disrupting Piezo1-E-cadherin interactions prevented cytoskeleton-dependent gating in their cultured-cell assays. How widely that route operates beyond those cells is not yet known.

The two accounts need not be rivals. Bilayer sufficiency cannot exclude a second route in an intact cell, and a tether requirement in a cultured cell cannot abolish the lipid energetics the structures measure. The bilayer may even be missing something: the purified droplet-bilayer recordings did not reproduce the rapid inactivation seen in whole cells. That is why the field has not chosen.

Other channels show each route in its own preparation, from tether-free gating of purified TRAAK and TREK1 in lipid to a microtubule requirement for fly NOMPC; the table in Going deeper keeps each result with its preparation.

### Design problem

Purified mouse Piezo1 is mechanically active in droplet bilayers but does not reproduce the rapid inactivation of whole cells, while disrupting Piezo1-E-cadherin interactions prevented cytoskeleton-dependent gating in cultured cells. Design experiments to decide whether a tether is required for gating in a sensory neuron.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

The bilayer result is sufficiency of protein plus lipid; the cadherin result is necessity of a coupling in one cultured cell type. Neither addresses a sensory ending, so the experiment has to go there.

Measure tension, not pressure or displacement. Use cell-attached or excised pressure clamp with the patch imaged so its radius of curvature is measured, and convert with T = R(pressure difference)/2. Indentation with a probe delivers an unknown mixture of membrane tension, cytoskeletal strain and cell deformation, so a tether result from indentation alone is hard to interpret; a dose-response in tension is comparable across preparations and against the published fits.

Perturb acutely and in the right cells. A germline deletion of a cadherin or a catenin changes development, adhesion and skin structure, so any afferent deficit has several possible causes. Use an inducible, afferent-specific driver in adult mice to remove the candidate linker, or apply an acute biochemical disruption of the interaction, and record within the window where skin architecture is unchanged. Confirm that architecture with histology in the same animals.

Record at the ending. Ex vivo skin-nerve recording gives spikes from an ending whose end organ is intact; patch recording near the terminal gives currents. Pair them, because a tether could change the mechanics of force delivery to the ending without changing the channel's tension sensitivity, and the current-tension relation is the most direct way to distinguish those.

Predict the outcomes. If a tether is required, acute disruption should shift the current-tension relation or abolish the response at an ending whose Piezo2 is intact and whose structure is normal. If gating is lipid-driven, the current-tension relation should survive while the amount of tension produced by a given skin stimulus may change. A shifted curve alone is not decisive, because a linker can also alter local membrane mechanics or channel availability, so check channel abundance and patch mechanics and rescue the interaction.

A human answer needs different tools. Microneurography can record single afferents and psychophysics can measure thresholds, but neither can delete a linker, so in living people the main test would be people carrying variants in candidate adhesion proteins, with human cell models adding mechanistic evidence.

</details>

## Variants that change inactivation, and what people report

Inactivation is where human genetics meets the biophysics. Coste and colleagues expressed human PIEZO2 variants from families with distal arthrogryposis, an inherited condition in which joints of the hands and feet are fixed in bent positions from birth, in HEK293T cells. Both I802F and E2727del accelerated recovery from inactivation; only E2727del slowed inactivation during the pulse. So "gain-of-function variants inactivate more slowly" is wrong for I802F.

Loss of function in people is narrower than the mouse headline. Two individuals with compound inactivating PIEZO2 variants, meaning both copies of the gene carried inactivating variants, had impaired discriminative touch and profound proprioceptive impairment that worsened without visual cues, while retaining other abilities. In a later study using experimental skin inflammation, PIEZO2-deficient participants did not develop the painful responses to gentle touch the protocol produces. Adult mice with Piezo2 deleted from sensory neurons and Merkel cells show an almost complete light-touch deficit across behavioural assays, and most rapidly adapting mechanically activated currents disappear from their cultured dorsal-root-ganglion neurons. Those are mouse results; the human ones are selective deficits with residual abilities, and the two should not be merged. [Merkel cells](/lessons/merkel-cells/) and [the LTMR classes](/lessons/ltmr-classes/) carry the cell-biological half of this story, and [proprioception](/lessons/proprioception/) the limb-position half.

### Back to the opening question

Why would a channel need 114 transmembrane helices around one pore? In the leading, still debated model, to collect area. A curved structure that flattens gains hundreds of square nanometres, and tension pays for whatever part of that area is coupled to the pore. Which part that is, and how the flattening opens the pore, is the open problem in this field.

### Where the idea stops

- **Two states is an assumption.** A dome that flattens gradually is a spring, not a switch, and then a single ΔA is not well defined.
- **A patch slope gives an effective area.** It underestimates the coupled area only if the channels are identical two-state switches that differ in T50 alone; otherwise it is not a bound.
- **The rule is the work of membrane tension, not a delivery route.** A tether that pulls on the channel directly would add its own work term.
- **Inactivation is outside it.** The two-state model has no inactivated state, so it covers neither inactivation nor recovery from it, the kinetics the human PIEZO2 variants change.
- **Tension may not be the only requirement.** Later work in cell-derived membranes suggests lipid composition matters too, not tension alone (see Going deeper).

## Going deeper

### Numbers from the 2010 paper

In the 2010 experiments, Piezo2 showed a reported inactivation time constant of 7.3 ± 0.7 ms at −80 mV in HEK293T cells (n = 11).

### Mechanotransducers in other preparations

Mechanosensitivity is not one mechanism with one protein. Each of these results belongs to its preparation.

| Protein | Organism and preparation | What was shown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MscL and MscS | Bacteria | Where the force-from-lipid principle was first established |
| TRAAK and TREK1 | Human TRAAK and zebrafish TREK1, purified, in lipid membranes | Mechanically gated with no cellular tethers. TRAAK could also electrically oppose Piezo1 activation in cultured cells |
| OSCA1.2 | Arabidopsis, purified, in proteoliposomes | Mechanically activated |
| TMEM63C | Mouse, expression assays | No stretch-activated current in the same assays that detected currents for other family members |
| NOMPC | Drosophila S2 cells | Required microtubule association for mechanogating: a tether in a fly |
| MEC-4 | C. elegans touch receptor neurons | A mec-4 null eliminated mechanically evoked receptor currents, which are carried mostly by sodium, unlike the non-selective Piezo current |
| TMC1 | Mouse hair cells, rescued TMC1 | Cysteine modification rapidly changed permeation, supporting TMC1 as a pore-forming component of the transduction channel without showing that it alone constitutes the native complex |

Drosophila NOMPC has 29 ankyrin repeats. The microtubule requirement for mechanogating was shown in S2 cells, and the behavioural work was done in third-instar larval class III dendritic-arborization neurons. [The hair-cell transduction channel](/lessons/hair-cell-transduction-channel/) takes up the TMC1 story.

### Piezo1 variants and red blood cells

The xerocytosis-associated human PIEZO1 variants M2225R and R2456H slowed inactivation in heterologous recordings. (Hereditary xerocytosis is an inherited condition in which red blood cells lose water.) In mouse red blood cells the chemical agonist Yoda1 produced calcium influx followed by dehydration that depended on the Gardos channel, a calcium-activated potassium channel. That connects a kinetic change to a cell-volume phenotype in that species.

### Piezo2 tension sensitivity and splice variants

Piezo2's own tension sensitivity has also been measured with the same imaged pressure-clamp method, and it differs between splice variants. An alternatively spliced exon, exon 35, was enough to confer high tension sensitivity on Piezo2 in the tested splice variants (Sindoni and colleagues, 2026). This is one recent study, so treat it as an example that splice context can tune sensitivity, not as a field-wide rule.

### Piezo1 in cell-derived membranes

In 2026 Vaisey and MacKinnon reported that Piezo1 does not conduct in the kind of lipids used for the vesicle structures, but in cell-derived membranes it flattens further, with changes around the pore. They propose that force is necessary but not sufficient. This is one recent study.
