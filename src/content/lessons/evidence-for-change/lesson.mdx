## Read the fossil record

Here are the four patterns from the first film, in Virginia:

- **Existence and diversity.** Many kinds of life lived that are not alive now.
- **Extinction.** A group is **extinct** when no living members are left. Trilobites, extinct sea animals with hard shells, are found in western Virginia rocks.
- **Change.** Old forms look like, but are not the same as, living ones. The state fossil scallop is one example.
- **Environments.** Sea shells in Virginia's coastal plain show that a shallow sea once covered that land.

The early fossils in this table sit on separate branches, not on steps in one line of ancestors.

## Compare the same parts

Hold out your arm. It has one upper arm bone, two lower arm bones, wrist bones and finger bones. A cat's front leg, a whale's flipper and a bat's wing have the same set. Their shapes, sizes and jobs differ.

A **common ancestor** is an early population that two or more kinds of living things came from. Inherited changes can reshape an old set of parts, so a shared ancestor with this bone map explains the match best.

## Compare sequences

A **DNA sequence** is the order of the bases A, T, C and G along a piece of DNA. Many genes carry the code for a protein, as you saw in [DNA: The Code of Life](/lessons/dna-the-code-of-life/), so a protein difference comes from a difference in its gene's DNA.

The second film said closer relatives *usually* have more similar sequences. “Usually” matters: changes do not build up at one steady rate, and one short protein is not a complete family tree. DNA also helps sort living things into groups, as in [Domains, Kingdoms and DNA](/lessons/domains-kingdoms-and-dna/).

## Why scientists call it a theory

A **hypothesis** is a possible explanation that a test can support or reject. A **theory**, as the first film said, is a well-tested explanation backed by many lines of evidence, and it makes predictions that can be checked.

The theory of evolution says that populations change over time and share common ancestors. Natural selection is one important cause, as you saw in [Natural Selection in Action](/lessons/natural-selection-in-action/); new mutations and chance also change populations. The theory predicts broad agreement among fossils, structures and sequences. Each could have told a different story. Instead, their broad patterns largely agree; when details conflict, scientists check more evidence.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A scientific theory is a well-tested explanation. Fossils, body structures and sequences are three of the many kinds of evidence that support evolution.</Support>

## Design problem: model burial and decay

Hard remains and fast burial make fossils more likely. This model tests only short-term decay.

**Predict.** Which should break down less: an apple slice buried in damp sand, an unburied slice, or a shell?

<details>
<summary>One way to test it</summary>

- **Question:** Do apple slices (soft remains) rot less when buried in damp sand than when left unburied on top of the same damp sand? Do shells change at all?
- **Independent variable:** buried under 3 centimetres of damp sand, or laid unburied on top of the same damp sand.
- **Dependent variable:** a rot score after 7 days, from 0 (fresh) to 3 (very soft, mouldy or smelly). Mass can mislead, because slices can dry out or pick up water and sand.
- **Constants:** the same slice size, cups, water and room.
- **Control:** unburied slices. Cover every cup the same way. Add clean shells to both groups as a hard-part check.
- **Trials:** five cups of each kind; find the mean rot score.
- **Safety:** use clear covered cups, wear gloves, never taste anything, and let the teacher discard anything mouldy. Wash your hands.

If buried slices score less rotten, burial slowed decay in this model. They may not, since damp sand still lets air in. Real fossils form best when remains are buried fast in fine mud with little oxygen and no scavengers, which this model leaves out. Shells should barely change. This is not real fossil formation, which takes thousands of years or more.

</details>

## Going deeper: Virginia's changing coast

The shallow sea recorded by the state fossil was here long before the Chesapeake Bay. The Bay began to fill as sea level rose after the last ice age, about 10,000 years ago. The old shells and the younger Bay record different changes in the same region.
