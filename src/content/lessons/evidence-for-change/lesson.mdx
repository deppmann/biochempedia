## Explain: what fossils record

<WordBank terms={['evidence', 'fossil', 'fossil record', 'extinct', 'common ancestor', 'DNA sequence', 'hypothesis', 'theory']} />

**Evidence** is information from observations or measurements that supports or argues against an idea. A **fossil** is the preserved remains or traces of a living thing from the past. Shells, bones, teeth and footprints can all be fossils.

Fossils are rare. Hard parts like shells and bones last longer than soft parts. Quick burial in mud or sand helps too. So the **fossil record**, all the fossils found so far, has gaps.

Even with gaps, the fossil record shows clear patterns:
- **Existence and diversity.** Many kinds of life lived that are not alive now.
- **Extinction.** A group is **extinct** when no living members are left. Trilobites, sea animals with hard shells, are found in rocks in western Virginia. They are extinct.
- **Change.** Old forms look like, but are not the same as, living ones. The state fossil scallop is one example.
- **Environments.** Sea shells in Virginia’s coastal plain show that a shallow sea once covered that land.

Groups also show up at different times. The table lists one well-known early fossil for each group.

| Group | Example fossil and place | About how old (millions of years) |
|---|---|---|
| Fish | Haikouichthys, China | about 518 |
| Four-legged animals | Ichthyostega, Greenland | about 365 |
| Mammal relatives | Morganucodon teeth | about 205 |
| Bird-like dinosaurs | Archaeopteryx | about 150 |

Some groups have older fossils than these, and no fossil shows the day a group began. New finds can push dates back.

## Explain: same bones, similar DNA

Hold out your arm. It has one upper arm bone, two lower arm bones, wrist bones and finger bones. A cat’s front leg, a whale’s flipper and a bat’s wing have the same set. The bones differ in shape and size, and the limbs do different jobs. A **common ancestor** is an early population that two or more kinds of living things came from. A shared ancestor with these bones explains the match best.

Doing the same job is not enough. A butterfly wing and a bird wing both fly, but only the bird wing has bones. So scientists compare how parts are built, not what they do.

DNA gives a second check. A **DNA sequence** is the order of the bases A, T, C and G along a piece of DNA. Many genes carry the code for a protein, as you saw in [DNA: The Code of Life](/lessons/dna-the-code-of-life/). Small changes in DNA happen and get passed on. Species that split from a common ancestor more recently have had less time to collect changes. So closer relatives usually have more similar DNA. DNA is also used to sort living things into groups, as in [Domains, Kingdoms and DNA](/lessons/domains-kingdoms-and-dna/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** The same bones in different shapes, and similar DNA sequences, are strong signs that living things share a common ancestor.</Support>

## Explain: why scientists call it a theory

A **hypothesis** is a possible explanation that a test can support or reject. A **theory** is a well-tested explanation backed by many lines of evidence. A theory also makes predictions that can be checked.

The theory of evolution says that populations change over time and share common ancestors. Natural selection is one important cause of that change, as you saw in [Natural Selection in Action](/lessons/natural-selection-in-action/). New mutations and chance also change populations. The theory predicts that fossils, bones and DNA will tell the same family story. Each one could have told a different story. Instead, they largely agree.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A theory in science is a well-tested explanation. Fossils, bones and DNA are three of the many kinds of evidence that support the theory of evolution.</Support>

### Design problem

Fossils form more often when remains are buried fast and when they are hard. Plan a model test: does quick burial in sand slow the breakdown of soft remains, compared with hard ones?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do apple slices (soft remains) rot less when buried in damp sand than when left unburied on top of the same damp sand? Do shells change at all?
- **Independent variable:** buried under 3 centimetres of damp sand, or laid unburied on top of the same damp sand.
- **Dependent variable:** a rot score after 7 days, from 0 (fresh) to 3 (very soft, mouldy or smelly). Mass can mislead, because slices can dry out or pick up water and sand.
- **Constants:** the same slice size, the same cups, the same amount of water, the same room.
- **Control:** unburied slices. Keep every cup covered with a lid in the same way. Add clean shells to both groups as a hard-part check.
- **Trials:** five cups of each kind; find the mean rot score.
- **Safety:** use clear covered cups, wear gloves, never taste anything, and let the teacher throw out anything mouldy. Wash your hands.

If buried slices score less rotten, burial slowed decay in this model. They may not, because damp sand still lets air in. Real fossils form best when remains are buried fast in fine mud with little oxygen and no scavengers, which this model leaves out. Shells should barely change. This is a model, not real fossil formation, which takes thousands of years or more.

</details>
