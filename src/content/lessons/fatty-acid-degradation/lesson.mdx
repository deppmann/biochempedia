    answer: 0
    rationale: "Acyl-CoA synthetase splits ATP all the way to AMP plus pyrophosphate (not ADP + Pi). The pyrophosphate is then hydrolyzed to 2 Pi, which both makes the activation irreversible and consumes a second high-energy phosphoanhydride bond, so the true cost is 2 ATP equivalents.   - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: "Fat is stored as triacylglycerol, which is glycerol holding three fatty acids. When you need energy, hormones (mainly epinephrine, helped by falling insulin) trigger lipases that release the fatty acids into the blood, where they ride on a protein called albumin. To burn one, the cell first attaches coenzyme A (this costs 2 ATP), then uses a carnitine shuttle to carry it into the mitochondrion. Inside, beta-oxidation runs a four-step cycle that snips off two carbons each round as acetyl-CoA, producing FADH2 and NADH that the cell turns into lots of ATP. A 16-carbon fat (palmitate) yields about 106 ATP. When sugar runs low, the liver turns extra acetyl-CoA into ketone bodies so the brain can use them."
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: "Fatty acid degradation runs in three stages. (1) Mobilization: catecholamines act through β-adrenergic 7TM receptors → cAMP → PKA (glucagon does this mainly in rodents; low insulin permits it), which phosphorylates perilipin and HSL; phospho-perilipin releases CGI-58 to activate ATGL; ATGL, HSL, and MAG lipase sequentially release three fatty acids and glycerol. (2) Activation and transport: acyl-CoA synthetase forms acyl-CoA via an acyl-adenylate, hydrolyzing ATP to AMP + 2 Pi (a 2-ATP cost); the carnitine shuttle (CPT-I, translocase, CPT-II) imports the acyl group, and malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT-I to prevent simultaneous synthesis and degradation. (3) Beta-oxidation: acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (FAD), enoyl-CoA hydratase, 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (NAD+), and thiolase remove acetyl-CoA each cycle. Palmitate (C16) → 8 acetyl-CoA, 7 FADH2, 7 NADH, ≈106 net ATP. Unsaturated FAs need an isomerase (± 2,4-dienoyl-CoA reductase); odd-chain FAs yield propionyl-CoA → methylmalonyl-CoA (biotin) → succinyl-CoA (B12). During fasting the liver makes ketone bodies (acetoacetate, D-3-hydroxybutyrate, acetone) for extrahepatic tissues."
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: "The architecture of fatty acid catabolism is a study in compartmentation and reciprocal regulation. Activation is thermodynamically forced by pyrophosphate hydrolysis (ΔG'° driven by PPi → 2 Pi), and its AMP product is the energetic '2-ATP' charge that must enter any honest yield calculation. The carnitine shuttle is the committed, rate-controlling import step, and its inhibition by malonyl-CoA links degradation directly to the acetyl-CoA carboxylase reaction of synthesis: malonyl-CoA rises when ACC is active (high citrate, insulin signaling), closing CPT-I and enforcing reciprocity so the two opposing pathways are rarely co-active. Beta-oxidation's first three reactions are the same sequence as the succinate-to-oxaloacetate stretch of the TCA cycle (FAD oxidation to a trans double bond, hydration, NAD+ oxidation to a ketone), and the fourth, thiolysis, then cuts at the carbon that sequence has activated, exploiting β-carbonyl activation to make the C2–C3 bond thiolytically labile. The substrate-specific dehydrogenase isozymes (VLCAD→SCAD) reflect a kinetic optimization across chain length, and MCAD loss produces a chain-length-dependent metabolic block with hypoketotic hypoglycemia. The inability of mammals to achieve net gluconeogenesis from even-chain fatty acids is a stoichiometric consequence of the TCA cycle's two decarboxylations: acetyl-CoA carbon cannot accumulate as oxaloacetate. Ketogenesis (HMGCS2-gated, the regulated step) and the liver's lack of SCOT/thiophorase make the liver an obligate exporter, and the Cahill adaptation reflects a glucose-sparing reallocation of fuel to D-3-hydroxybutyrate that minimizes proteolysis during prolonged starvation."

**Angus Barbieri went 382 days without food, living on his own body fat.**

In 1965, at 27 and 456 pounds, he stopped eating under medical supervision. He lived on water, tea and coffee, with daily vitamins and, for stretches, potassium and sodium supplements, and finished at 180 pounds. Nobody measured his brain's fuel, but by Cahill's fasting data his blood ketones would have stayed high and his brain would have run largely on ketone bodies made from his own fat, with the glucose it still needed coming from gluconeogenesis. How did stored fat become fuel his tissues, and later his brain, could use?

## Why fat is the reserve: the electrons

Cells make most of their ATP by taking electrons from fuel (oxidizing it) and passing them down the electron transport chain, so a fuel is worth roughly the electrons it can give up. Nearly every carbon in a fatty acid is a CH₂ group, as electron-rich as a carbon inside a chain can be. A typical glucose carbon is H–C–OH: it already carries an oxygen, so glucose is partly oxidized before the cell starts.

Per carbon, fat gives more ATP than glucose (the ledger below counts how much). Per gram the gap is wider: a gram of fat stores more than twice the energy of a gram of dry carbohydrate, because glucose's oxygen atoms add weight without adding fuel. Storage widens it again: glycogen is kept with about twice its weight in water, fat nearly dry, so gram for gram of stored depot fat holds roughly six times as much. The cost of that density is access: fat is hidden in droplets as triacylglycerol, fatty acids do not dissolve in water, and each one must be released, ferried through blood and carried into a mitochondrion before a bond is burned.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Fat's fuel is the electrons on its CH₂ carbons, and the mitochondrion spends them two carbons at a time with one repeated cycle. Oxidize, hydrate, oxidize turns the β-carbon (carbon 3, counting from the CoA end) into a ketone, so an enzyme (thiolase) can cut right next to it. Every turn pays the same packet, one FADH₂, one NADH and one acetyl-CoA, so a fatty acid's yield is a count of turns and acetyl-CoA, minus a 2-ATP activation toll. For palmitate, with 16 carbons, that comes to about 106 ATP.

The count holds for a saturated, even-numbered chain burned all the way to CO₂. The rest of the chapter is logistics, three stages as in the film (release, activation and import through a gate, then the cycle), and the edges of the rule: double bonds, odd chains, and a fasting liver that makes more acetyl-CoA than it can burn.

## Stage 1: Mobilization, unlocking the droplet

Triacylglycerol is glycerol holding three fatty acids by ester bonds, and about 95% of its energy sits in the chains. When you fast or exercise, epinephrine and norepinephrine bind β-adrenergic receptors (7-transmembrane receptors) on the fat cell, raise cAMP and activate protein kinase A (PKA), while falling insulin takes the brake off. It is the same cAMP → PKA signal that breaks down glycogen, so both storage depots empty together. Textbooks also list glucagon here; know it for exams, but its direct effect is mainly in rodent fat cells and small in human ones, where the catecholamines do most of the work.

PKA does two things: it loosens the protein coat on the fat droplet, which frees an activator for the first lipase, and it switches on a second lipase that then moves onto the droplet. Three lipases then remove the three fatty acids one at a time. These released free (nonesterified) fatty acids, not the fat you eat or the fat you store, are what travels in blood, bound to serum albumin. The glycerol is water-soluble and goes to the liver, where glycerol kinase phosphorylates it so it can enter glycolysis or gluconeogenesis.

## Stage 2: Activation and the carnitine gate

First the fatty acid is **activated**: acyl-CoA synthetase couples it to coenzyme A. The reaction splits ATP to AMP plus pyrophosphate (PPᵢ), not to ADP, and hydrolysis of the PPᵢ makes the step irreversible.

**Predict.** How many high-energy phosphate bonds does activation spend?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Two. ATP → AMP + PPᵢ breaks one, and PPᵢ → 2 Pᵢ breaks the second, so activation costs **2 ATP**, not 1. Charge the toll against the yield at the end.

</details>

Acyl-CoA cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane, so it hands its cargo to **carnitine** (Latin *carnis*, flesh). CPT-I, in the outer mitochondrial membrane, swaps CoA for carnitine. A translocase ferries the acylcarnitine across the inner membrane, and CPT-II inside regenerates acyl-CoA.

CPT-I is the main control point for burning long-chain fatty acids. It is inhibited by **malonyl-CoA**, the first committed intermediate of fatty acid *synthesis*.

**Predict.** A drug strongly activates acetyl-CoA carboxylase, the enzyme that makes malonyl-CoA. What happens to fat burning in the mitochondria?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It falls. More malonyl-CoA shuts CPT-I, so long chains cannot get in. That is the gate's logic: while the cell is building fat, malonyl-CoA is high and the cell does not waste energy burning what it is busy making.

</details>

## Stage 3: Beta-oxidation, one cycle run again and again

Picture palmitoyl-CoA as 16 carbons with coenzyme A hooked to one end by a thioester (a sulfur-linked ester). Number from that end: carbon 1 is the thioester carbon, carbon 2 is the **α-carbon**, and carbon 3 is the **β-carbon**.

The cell has a cutting problem: a plain carbon–carbon bond between CH₂ groups gives an enzyme nothing to grab. So first it rebuilds the β-carbon into a ketone (C=O), giving CoA–S–C(=O)–CH₂–C(=O)–R: carbons 1, 2 and 3 in order, with R the rest of the chain. Carbon 2 then sits between two carbonyl groups, the thioester on one side and the new ketone on the other. Thiolase's cysteine attacks the ketone carbon, the bond between carbons 2 and 3 breaks, and the thioester steadies the negative charge left on carbon 2, so the two-carbon piece leaves as acetyl-CoA.

**Predict.** Turning a CH₂ into a C=O means taking electrons away and adding an oxygen. Using an electron carrier, then water, then an electron carrier again, what three moves would do it?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Oxidize, hydrate, oxidize: a double bond, then an OH on carbon 3, then a ketone. You have seen this sequence before: the [citric acid cycle](/lessons/citric-acid-cycle/) uses it to turn succinate into fumarate, then malate, then oxaloacetate.

</details>

The four reactions, with their carriers:

1. **Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase** removes two hydrogens from carbons 2 and 3 and makes a trans double bond. The product is a trans-Δ²-enoyl-CoA; Δ² means the double bond starts at carbon 2. The electrons go to the enzyme's **FAD**, making **FADH₂**.
2. **Enoyl-CoA hydratase** adds water across the double bond, putting an OH on the β-carbon. No carrier is used.
3. **3-Hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase** oxidizes that OH to a ketone. These electrons go to **NAD⁺**, making **NADH**.
4. **β-Ketothiolase** uses a fresh CoA to make that cut between carbons 2 and 3, releasing **acetyl-CoA**. The rest of the chain, now two carbons shorter, carries the fresh CoA straight back to step 1.

Why FAD first and NAD⁺ second? Making a carbon–carbon double bond out of two CH₂ groups releases too little energy to push electrons onto NAD⁺, so the enzyme hands them to its own tightly bound FAD, just as succinate dehydrogenase does in the citric acid cycle. Oxidizing an alcohol to a ketone is a closer match for NAD⁺, and the cut that follows pulls that step forward.

So every turn pays the same packet, **one FADH₂, one NADH and one acetyl-CoA**. Walk the cycle below and watch the carriers stack up.

## MCAD deficiency: a block in the middle stalls the spiral

**Predict.** Step 1 comes in chain-length versions, and most dietary fat is long-chain. Why would losing only the medium-chain enzyme, MCAD, stop a child from burning fat during a fast?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Every long chain becomes a medium chain on its way down the spiral, so a block at medium length stalls much of fat burning partway through. Too little acetyl-CoA reaches the liver's ketone pathway, so ketones stay low, and tissues burn glucose instead while the liver loses the energy it needs to make more. Blood sugar falls without ketones appearing: **hypoketotic hypoglycemia**.

</details>

**MCAD deficiency** affects roughly 1 in 10,000 to 20,000 births and is most common in people of northern European ancestry. A stomach bug or a skipped breakfast can set off the crash. Some deaths from it were once written off as SIDS. Now a newborn heel prick, read by tandem mass spectrometry, catches most cases, though a few babies crash before the result is back. One enzyme, one heel prick, thousands of lives. The same step is the target in **Jamaican vomiting sickness**, where a metabolite of hypoglycin A from unripe ackee fruit poisons several of the acyl-CoA dehydrogenases.

## Special cases that bend the rule

Two kinds of real fat break the rule's assumptions of an identical packet per turn and a chain that ends in acetyl-CoA.

- **Unsaturated chains.** After a few cycles, a natural **cis double bond** lands in a position (cis-Δ³, starting at carbon 3) the standard enzymes cannot process. cis-Δ³-enoyl-CoA isomerase moves it to the trans-Δ² position and the cycle resumes. The double bond is already there, so that turn skips the FAD step: stearate (C18, saturated) nets 120 ATP, oleate (C18, one cis bond at an odd-numbered position) 118.5. Some polyunsaturated chains need a second helper, 2,4-dienoyl-CoA reductase, which spends NADPH.
- **Odd chains.** The last thiolase cut leaves a three-carbon **propionyl-CoA** instead of an acetyl-CoA. A biotin enzyme converts it to methylmalonyl-CoA, an epimerase flips its arrangement at one carbon, and a vitamin B12 enzyme rearranges it into **succinyl-CoA**, a citric acid cycle intermediate. A B12 deficiency stalls this step and spills methylmalonic acid into urine.

## Why even-chain fat gives no net glucose

The odd-chain route matters because of a hard rule: **mammals cannot make net glucose from even-chain fatty acids by the usual route**. Each turn of the citric acid cycle brings in two carbons as acetyl-CoA and loses two as CO₂, so no net oxaloacetate is left over to seed [gluconeogenesis](/lessons/gluconeogenesis/). Succinyl-CoA is a net addition of a four-carbon intermediate, so that surplus can be drawn off as oxaloacetate and become glucose. Apart from a small trickle through acetone (some is metabolized back to pyruvate), only the odd-chain tail and the glycerol backbone make net sugar.

## Ketone bodies: the liver's fasting export

When glucose is scarce, low insulin floods the liver with fatty acids and opens CPT-I. The liver's β-oxidation then makes acetyl-CoA faster than its citric acid cycle can burn it. Fasting signals also raise the amount of HMGCS2 (HMG-CoA synthase), the regulated enzyme that commits acetyl-CoA to ketone bodies, and oxaloacetate drawn off for gluconeogenesis adds to this.

The liver repackages the surplus. Thiolase, HMGCS2 and HMG-CoA lyase build **acetoacetate**, which is reduced to **D-3-hydroxybutyrate** (the dominant form in blood) or decarboxylates spontaneously to **acetone**. Acetone is exhaled as the fruity "acetone breath" that can get diabetic ketoacidosis mistaken for drunkenness.

The liver keeps only the carrier part of each packet, because the acetyl-CoA leaves in the ketones instead of entering its own citric acid cycle. If it exports palmitate's eight acetyl-CoA as four acetoacetate, it keeps 7 × 4 − 2 = 26 ATP, about a quarter of the 106. The tissues that burn the ketones collect most of the rest. Each acetoacetate gives them two acetyl-CoA, worth 20, minus one GTP, because reloading it onto CoA spends a succinyl-CoA: 4 × 19 = 76. Exported as D-3-hydroxybutyrate instead, each ketone carries one of the liver's NADH with it, so the liver keeps about 16 and the tissues get about 86. Liver plus tissues total 102, not 106; the missing 4 are the four GTP the tissues give up to reload the ketones onto CoA.

The liver cannot use its own ketones. It lacks thiophorase (succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid CoA transferase), the enzyme that reloads acetoacetate onto CoA so it can be split back into two acetyl-CoA. Red blood cells cannot use ketones either, having no mitochondria. Heart, muscle, kidney and, after adaptation, the brain run on them happily. George Cahill's starvation studies measured the payoff: by day 40 of a fast, protein breakdown falls from roughly 75 to 20 grams a day as the brain shifts onto ketones, sparing muscle. The same ketotic state is the basis of the ketogenic diet, which reduces seizures in many children with drug-resistant epilepsy; how it does so is still not settled. Russell Wilder introduced the diet for epilepsy in 1921, and the Charlie Foundation revived it in 1994.

## Going deeper

The main line above does not depend on these details.

### A second route to lipolysis

The catecholamine–cAMP signal is not the only trigger: heart-derived natriuretic peptides also start lipolysis, through cGMP rather than cAMP.

### Inside the droplet: perilipin, CGI-58 and three lipases

Besides **perilipin**, whose release of CGI-58 switches on adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), PKA phosphorylates **hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL)**, which moves onto the droplet. In order, ATGL takes triacylglycerol to diacylglycerol, HSL takes that to monoacylglycerol, and monoacylglycerol lipase removes the last fatty acid. When CGI-58 is mutated and ATGL cannot be switched on, fat accumulates in tissues that should not hold it: Chanarin-Dorfman syndrome.

### Albumin's spare capacity

The fatty acids leave the fat cell on serum albumin, which has about seven fatty acid binding sites but normally carries only one or two.

### Who does step 1 at each chain length

The chain-length versions of step 1 are VLCAD, MCAD and SCAD, plus LCAD, which matters more in rodents than in people. Mind the naming trap. VLCAD acts on roughly C12 to C20 chains, palmitate included, and sits on the inner mitochondrial membrane; for those long chains, steps 2 to 4 are done by one membrane-bound complex, the mitochondrial trifunctional protein. Shorter chains use separate soluble enzymes in the matrix. "Very-long-chain fatty acids" (about C22 and longer) are first shortened in peroxisomes, where step 1 hands its electrons to O₂, making H₂O₂, so less ATP is made.

### Where step 1's electrons go

Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase's bound FADH₂ passes its electrons to ubiquinone through electron-transferring flavoprotein (ETF) and ETF:ubiquinone oxidoreductase, skipping complex I, which is why each is worth about 1.5 ATP, like the FADH₂ of succinate dehydrogenase.

### Chains that skip the shuttle

The carnitine gate is a long-chain checkpoint. Short- and medium-chain fatty acids mostly cross into the matrix as free acids without the carnitine shuttle, most clearly in liver, and are activated to acyl-CoA inside.

### CPT1A in Arctic populations

The CPT1A P479L variant, common in Inuit and other Arctic populations, lowers CPT-I activity and makes it less sensitive to malonyl-CoA, which shows how much the import step matters. It carries genetic signs of past selection, though what it was selected for is still debated, and it has been linked to fasting hypoglycemia and higher infant death rates, without proof that it causes those deaths.
