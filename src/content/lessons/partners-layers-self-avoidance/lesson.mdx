    blurb: >-
      A marker such as tau-lacZ is placed into a gene in mouse so that every neuron using that
      gene's promoter also makes the marker, which fills the axon. Staining then shows where those
      axons end. Replacing the gene's coding sequence at the same time shows what the protein

  - name: "Misexpression and knockdown in developing retina"
    blurb: >-
      An adhesion protein is made in cells that normally lack it, or reduced in cells that
      normally have it, while the cells are still growing. Comparing where their processes end
      in the inner plexiform layer shows whether the protein biases sublaminar choice.
  - name: "Single-neuron labelling"
    blurb: >-
      Only one or a few neurons in a field are made to express a fluorescent protein, so that one
      cell's whole arbor can be traced. This is how self-avoidance is scored: you count how often
      a cell's own branches cross or bundle, separately from its overlap with neighbours.
  - name: "Homophilic binding assays"
    blurb: >-
      Purified outer domains or whole cells carrying one isoform are tested for binding to the
      same or a different isoform, by aggregation or by binding of soluble protein. A grid of such
      tests shows how strictly each form binds only its own kind.

**A single fly gene, *Dscam1*, can be spliced into 38,016 different proteins. A neuron uses them to recognise its own branches.**

That number answers a question you have not asked yet. The previous lesson ended with axons arriving at roughly the right place in a target, sorted by gradients. Arriving is not the end of the trip. A retinal axon reaching the tectum, or a bipolar cell process reaching the inner retina, still has to stop in the right layer, find the right partner, and avoid tangling with itself. This lesson is about the labels that do that work.

## Where in the target?

Targeting can be broken into four stages: reach the target region, slow down and branch, find the right place, and choose the right partner. Growth cones that enter a target slow down, pause and branch, and branches tend to form where the growth cone paused.

"How does a neuron know its position and know where to go?" There are three answers. Position can be "determined by individual labels", "by gradients", or "by neighbors". The [topographic maps](/lessons/topographic-maps/) lesson was about gradients. A gradient is good at continuous order: neighbouring points in the eye map to neighbouring points in the tectum. It is poor at discrete choices. A thin layer is either the right one or not, and a glomerulus either belongs to one receptor channel or does not. Discrete choices call for discrete labels.

Think of a conference with name tags. A gradient is like telling people to sit in rows by age. A label is like seating people at the table whose sign matches their tag.

## The idea: layers are chosen by like binding like

The inner plexiform layer (IPL) of the retina is a thin band where bipolar cells, amacrine cells and retinal ganglion cells make synapses. It is divided into thinner sublaminae, and each cell type sends its processes into one or a few of them. A cell that ends in the wrong sublamina meets the wrong partners.

In chick retina, Masahito Yamagata, Joshua Weiner and Joshua Sanes found in 2002 that two related proteins, Sidekick-1 and Sidekick-2, are made by largely separate groups of retinal neurons, and that the processes of each group end in different sublaminae. Both are members of the immunoglobulin (Ig) superfamily, cell-surface proteins built from repeated Ig-like domains. Each binds its own kind, which is called homophilic binding. Cells carrying Sidekick-1 stick to other cells carrying Sidekick-1.

The key test was misexpression: making a protein in a cell that normally lacks it. When retinal neurons were made to produce a Sidekick they did not normally make, their processes shifted toward the sublamina where that Sidekick was already concentrated. In 2008 Yamagata and Sanes extended the set to four proteins, adding Dscam and DscamL, each marking a different group of cells and sublaminae. Reducing their levels disturbed the order. The short version: misexpressing one of these adhesion molecules shifts the sublamina an arbor occupies.

Keep the scope in view. The four-protein code was described in chick. Later work in mouse retina from the same laboratory tied Sidekick-2 to one particular circuit between an amacrine cell type and a ganglion cell type, but mouse sublaminae are not simply assigned by these four proteins. These molecules bias a choice; they are one part of how layers form.

## Protein isoforms for neuronal individuality

The problem this heading names is the opposite of the layer problem. Instead of joining like with like, a neuron must keep like away from like, where "like" means itself.

Many neurons spread their dendrites so that they cover their territory evenly, without their own branches crossing or bundling. This is self-avoidance. It requires each branch to recognise sister branches from the same cell and move away from them, while still overlapping the branches of other neurons. To do that, each neuron needs a surface identity that differs from its neighbours'.

The fly found one in a single gene. In 2000 Dietmar Schmucker, James Clemens, Huidy Shu, Lawrence Zipursky and colleagues described *Dscam* in *Drosophila* (now called *Dscam1*). It has four clusters of alternative exons, with 12, 48, 33 and 2 members, 95 alternative exons in all. Each messenger RNA keeps one exon from each cluster:

> **12 × 48 × 33 = 19,008 outer domains; × 2 membrane-spanning choices = 38,016 isoforms**

In 2004 Woj Wojtowicz, Clemens, Zipursky and colleagues showed that Dscam1 isoforms bind mainly to identical isoforms. Binding is homophilic, as with Sidekick, but here the outcome is repulsion: when two branches carrying the same isoforms touch, they pull apart. In 2007 three groups (Matthews, Grueber and colleagues; Hughes, Schmucker and colleagues; Soba, the Jans and colleagues) showed that fly sensory neurons lacking Dscam1 have dendrites that cross and bundle with their own branches.

Then came the population test. Daisuke Hattori, Ebru Demir and colleagues in 2007 made flies whose *Dscam1* could produce only one isoform. Circuits in these flies were severely disorganised. The experiment also showed that neighbouring neurons must carry different isoforms, while which isoform any one neuron carries matters little: neighbours sharing one isoform cannot tell themselves from each other. In 2009 Hattori and colleagues found that thousands of isoforms are needed for normal wiring. Each neuron appears to express a small, partly random subset, so a large menu keeps accidental matches rare.

Watch a trap here. Vertebrate DSCAM and DSCAML1 are not spliced this way and cannot supply this diversity. In mouse, the job falls largely to the clustered protocadherins, about 58 genes in three clusters (alpha, beta and gamma), expressed in different combinations in different neurons. In 2012 Julie Lefebvre, Sanes and colleagues showed that starburst amacrine cells in mouse retina need the gamma protocadherins for their dendrites to avoid each other. When every starburst cell was made to express the same single isoform, each cell's branches separated, but neighbouring cells also began to avoid each other. Same problem, different molecules.

## Map of spatial organization, map of qualities: smell

Put two kinds of map side by side: a "Map of spatial organization" and a "Map of qualities". The retina-to-tectum map preserves where things are. The olfactory map sorts what things are.

In 1991 Linda Buck and Richard Axel identified a large family of genes in rat that encode odorant receptors. They are [G protein-coupled receptors](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/), and in rodents the family runs to about a thousand genes. How binding an odour leads to an electrical signal is covered in [the senses lesson](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/). Here the question is wiring.

Each olfactory sensory neuron in the nose expresses one odorant receptor gene, with rare exceptions. Neurons expressing a given receptor are scattered within a zone of the epithelium, yet in situ hybridisation by Vassar, Axel and colleagues and by Ressler, Sullivan and Buck in 1994 showed that their axons gather in a few glomeruli in the olfactory bulb. A glomerulus is a ball of neuropil where many sensory axons meet the dendrites of bulb neurons. In 1996 Peter Mombaerts and colleagues in Axel's laboratory engineered mice in which neurons using the P2 receptor promoter also made a marker that fills the axon. The P2 axons converged on a pair of glomeruli in each bulb, one on its medial side and one on its lateral side. The rule is one receptor, one neuron, and one receptor channel per glomerulus.

Something surprising sits underneath. The receptor protein is found on the axon terminals as well as the dendrites, and it helps choose the target. Receptor-made cAMP is also thought to set how much of some guidance receptors the axon makes (see [the cAMP lesson](/lessons/camp-and-pka/)), though that link has been worked out in only a few receptor lines.

### Design problem

You replace the coding sequence of the P2 odorant receptor in mouse with a marker, so neurons that use the P2 promoter make the marker but no P2 receptor. Predict where their axons go. Then predict what happens if, instead, you put a different receptor's coding sequence in place of P2's.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start from what each experiment keeps and what it removes. Both keep the P2 promoter, so the same neurons are labelled, in the same zone of the nose. Only the receptor protein changes.

If the promoter or the neuron's position decided the target, the receptor-less axons would still converge on the P2 glomerulus. They do not. In the deletion experiments reported by Feng Wang, Axel and colleagues in 1998, the labelled axons failed to form a P2 glomerulus; they wandered instead of converging on a specific glomerulus. So the receptor is needed.

If the receptor were the whole address, a swapped receptor would send the axons to the donor receptor's own glomerulus. That is not quite what happened. In the 1996 swap, with M12 in place of P2, the axons converged near the P2 glomerulus and far from M12's own. In a 1998 swap, with P3 in place of P2, they converged on a glomerulus touching the P3 glomerulus. So the receptor moved the axons, but not to a fixed donor address. So the receptor helps choose, and other features of the neuron also count. The receptor is part of the label, not all of it.

## Labels and gradients together

Put the cases side by side. Gradients give continuous position across a sheet. Labels give discrete membership: a layer, a glomerulus, or self versus other. The same chemistry can serve opposite ends. Homophilic binding of Sidekick draws matching processes together, while homophilic binding of Dscam1 drives matching branches apart. What the cell does after the bond forms decides which.

None of this is final. Target selection ends with "Refinement later in life": maps and connections set up by these molecules are adjusted by activity, a subject for the lessons on spontaneous activity and synapses.
