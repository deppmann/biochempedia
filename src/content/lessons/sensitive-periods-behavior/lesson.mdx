      species' song far more readily than another species' song. The work split song learning
      into a phase of listening and a later phase of practice, and gave later researchers a
      behavior with a measurable window that could be traced to particular brain circuits.

      right. The map turned a learned behavior into a set of named nuclei, so later work could ask

techniques:
  - name: "Tutor-controlled rearing of songbirds"
    blurb: >-
      Young birds are raised in sound-isolated chambers and exposed to recorded or live song at
      chosen ages. The adult song is recorded and compared with the tutor song syllable by
      syllable on a sound spectrogram, a plot of frequency against time. The age at which tutoring
      still yields a good copy marks the window.
  - name: "Local drug infusion into a brain nucleus"
    blurb: >-
      A fine cannula delivers a drug, such as the NMDA receptor blocker AP5, into one nucleus at a
      chosen time. Comparing infusions given on tutoring days, on other days and into a control
      region shows whether receptors in that place are needed for that step of learning.
  - name: "Grammaticality judgment testing"
    blurb: >-
      Listeners hear sentences, some grammatical and some with a single error, and say which are
      correct. Scores across many sentences and structure types give a measure of grammar that
      can be compared across groups defined by age of first exposure.
  - name: "Forced-choice preferential looking"
    blurb: >-
      An infant is shown a striped grating on one side and a plain gray field of equal mean
      brightness on the other. An observer who cannot see which side holds the stripes judges
      where they are from where the infant looks. The finest stripes the infant reliably looks at
      give an estimate of grating acuity.

**A male white-crowned sparrow raised without hearing adult song still sings as an adult, but its song is simple and abnormal. Playing it the right song later in life does little to fix it.**

The bird is not deaf and its vocal organ works. What it missed was an experience at the right age. Many behaviors, from song to grammar to fine vision, are built this way: the nervous system expects certain input during a window of development, and what arrives in that window shapes the circuit. This lesson asks how sharp those windows are, what sets their edges, and how much of the evidence from animals carries over to people.

## Song: listen first, practice later

Songbirds give the clearest case of a window for learning. White-crowned sparrows from different places sing recognizably different dialects, a hint that the song is learned. In the 1960s and 1970s Peter Marler set out to find when. He raised young white-crowned sparrows in sound-isolated chambers, played them recorded song at chosen ages, and recorded what they sang as adults.

**Predict.** Some birds hear the tape early in life, some when they are older, and some hear no adult song at all. Which birds will sing a good copy as adults?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Birds that heard song early in life later sang good copies, even though months passed before they first sang. Recordings played to older birds had little effect. Birds that heard no adult song developed simple, abnormal songs. The young birds also learned their own species' song far more readily than another species' song.

The same tape, played at different ages, left very different marks.

</details>

Marler's result splits song learning into two phases. In the **sensory phase** the bird listens and memorizes. In the **sensorimotor phase** it practices, producing rambling sounds that it gradually shapes until they match the memory.

So what does practice need? Masakazu Konishi deafened young sparrows at different stages.

**Predict.** A bird hears adult song, so it has the memory. It is then deafened before it starts to practice. Will it sing a good copy?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Its song is extremely abnormal, no better than that of a bird deafened without ever hearing a tutor. Hearing the tutor was not enough: the bird must hear itself to correct its voice.

</details>

Konishi's result led to the idea of a **template**: an auditory memory, laid down in the sensory phase, against which the bird compares and corrects its own practice. Memory first, then matching.

## How sharp is the window?

Marler's design defines a window. Plot a lasting outcome, such as how much of the tutor's song the adult copies, against the age at which the input was given (or, in studies of late onset, the age at which it first arrived). That plot can take two broad shapes, and this course names each.

| Name | Picture | How the lasting effect falls with later input |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Critical period** | a door that shuts | abruptly: high, then a sudden drop |
| **Sensitive period** | a tide going out | gradually: high early, then a steady decline |

These names are this course's convention for the shape of an outcome curve; researchers also use the terms in other ways (see [Going deeper](#other-uses-of-the-terms-critical-and-sensitive-period)). Both kinds are windows of opportunity and of vulnerability. Telling them apart takes the shape of the whole curve, not a single summary number. The one case here with a measured outcome curve, second-language grammar, is graded; for song and cataracts the lesson gives only a few ages, so their sharpness is not settled by these data.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Same input, different ages: measure what lasts. A developmental window is a range of ages when an experience has an unusually strong lasting effect, compared with the same experience at other ages under comparable conditions. In zebra finches the expected input itself appears to help end that range.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which behaviors.** Learned behaviors that need a specific input: song in songbirds, grammar in people, particular visual functions. It does not cover behaviors that develop without input, such as the spontaneous movements of chick embryos (see [Going deeper](#early-movements-that-do-not-need-experience)).
- **Which experiments.** The core design is that age plot, with outcomes such as adult song, grammar years after arrival, or vision years after surgery. Matched-age rearing asks what experience does to the window, deafening, lesions and drugs ask what each step of learning needs, and the human studies compare histories people happened to have, not ones assigned to them.

## Who closes the window?

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The zebra finch, now the standard laboratory songbird, runs a faster schedule than the sparrow. Only males sing. A young male memorizes a tutor, usually his father, between about 25 and 65 days after hatching, overlaps the two phases, and settles on a fixed, or crystallized, song by about three months.

Is the end of that window set by age alone, like a clock, or does the experience it is waiting for help close it? To ask, you need birds of the same age with different histories. Raise one group normally and one without adult males, then give each a new tutor at the same late age, after socially reared males have stopped taking up new song.

| Group | Early life | New tutor at the same late age | Copies the new tutor? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Socially reared | hears his father sing | yes | ? |
| Reared without adult males | hears no adult male song | yes | ? |

**Predict.** Fill in the last column. What would a clock set by age alone predict? What would you see if hearing a tutor helps close the window?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A clock predicts that neither group copies, because both are the same age. Lucy Eales reported in 1985 that males reared without adult males can still copy a new tutor after the age at which socially reared males stop.

Same age, different history, different outcome. This suggests that age alone does not set the window and that hearing a tutor helps close it, although later studies gave mixed results and the groups differ in more than tutor exposure, such as social and visual contact with adults.

</details>

The result has a limit. Missing input delays the closing but does not abolish it: birds kept without a tutor eventually crystallize a song of their own. And a late copy can be less complete than the copy of a bird tutored at the normal age.

This is where the old argument of **nature vs. nurture** becomes an experiment. Genes build the circuit that can learn and set roughly when it can learn. Experience supplies what is learned, and in zebra finches it also appears to help decide when the window closes; in people that has not been shown causally. Neither can be removed without changing the outcome.

## Which circuit matters while song is learned?

Is the part of the brain that learns the song the same part that sings it?

The song circuit was mapped in adult canaries by Fernando Nottebohm, Tegner Stokes and Christiana Leonard in 1976. Lesions of the forebrain nucleus HVC, or of the nucleus RA to which it projects, disrupted song, and RA sends fibers to the brainstem motor neurons that control the syrinx, the bird's vocal organ. The letters abbreviate anatomical names (see [Going deeper](#names-of-the-song-nuclei)); here they are used simply as names. Two routes leave HVC.

- **The motor pathway:** HVC to RA to the brainstem motor neurons of the syrinx.
- **The anterior forebrain pathway:** HVC to area X, then the thalamus, then the nucleus **LMAN**, and from LMAN back to RA. It is a loop that leaves the motor pathway and rejoins it.

If LMAN belongs to the learning machinery rather than the singing machinery, damaging it should matter more in a young bird than in an adult. In 1984 Sarah Bottjer, Elizabeth Miesner and Arthur Arnold tested this in male zebra finches: LMAN lesions in juveniles disrupted song development but did not affect stable song in adults. The anterior forebrain pathway matters while song is learned, though the lesions alone cannot say where the tutor memory is kept.

A motor route runs from HVC to RA to brainstem motor neurons to the syrinx. A second route leaves HVC for area X, the thalamus and LMAN, and rejoins the motor route at RA. An inset notes that LMAN lesions disrupt song development in juveniles but leave stable adult song intact." caption="Two routes leave HVC: a motor pathway that produces song, and an anterior forebrain loop through area X, the thalamus and LMAN that rejoins it at RA. LMAN lesions disrupted song development in juveniles but not stable song in adults, so this loop matters while song is learned." />

What do the loop's synapses need while the bird memorizes? A natural suspect is the **NMDA receptor**, a glutamate receptor (see the lesson on [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/)). It works as a coincidence detector:

1. Glutamate and a co-agonist, glycine or D-serine (a second molecule that must bind alongside glutamate), activate the receptor.
2. At normal magnesium levels, a magnesium ion plugs the pore while the receiving cell is near rest.
3. Depolarization of the receiving cell drives the magnesium out, and current flows.

The glutamate comes from the cell sending the signal, and the depolarization reflects activity in the receiving cell. So the receptor reports that two cells were active at about the same time. In many brain regions that current triggers strengthening of the active synapse, a leading model of how memories are written.

In 1996 Mark Basham, Ernest Nordeen and Kathy Nordeen infused the NMDA receptor blocker AP5 into the brains of juvenile zebra finches, either into the anterior forebrain near LMAN or into a control region, and later scored how much of the tutor song each bird copied as an adult.

**Predict.** Which of these groups copied the tutor poorly?

1. AP5 into the anterior forebrain before each of ten tutoring sessions.
2. AP5 into the anterior forebrain, but only on non-tutoring days.
3. AP5 into the cerebellum.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only the first group copied significantly less of the tutor song than controls. The other groups learned normally, and each argues against an alternative explanation.

| Group | Copied normally? | What it argues against |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AP5 in the anterior forebrain before tutoring | no, significantly less | — |
| AP5 in the anterior forebrain on non-tutoring days only | yes | a deficit caused by the drug disrupting singing or practice rather than memorizing (weakens it without fully excluding it, since birds could make up practice on drug-free days) |
| AP5 into the cerebellum | yes | an effect of the drug wherever it is infused |

Treated birds could also still tell zebra finch song from canary song, so they were not simply deaf to it.

</details>

The result places a requirement for NMDA receptors in that region, during tutoring, in the step of memorizing, without showing where the memory is stored. Hearing a tutor also stabilizes dendritic spines on HVC neurons in young zebra finches (Roberts, Tschida, Klein and Mooney, 2010), a possible structural correlate of the memory forming. The cortical brakes that close windows in mammals are taken up in [critical period control](/lessons/critical-period-control/).

## Language: what the age effects do and do not show

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

In 1967 Eric Lenneberg proposed a **critical period hypothesis** for language: a first language can be acquired only between early childhood and puberty. It is hard to test, because nearly every child is exposed to language from birth.

The case of a girl known as Genie, found at age 13 after years of severe isolation and abuse, is often cited. But a child deprived so completely was deprived of much more than language, and such cases cannot isolate a language window.

The main natural test is deaf children of hearing parents who first meet a signed language late. They are deprived mainly, though not only, of an accessible language, rather than of the whole range of care and social contact, and delayed access to sign does affect later proficiency.

- In 1990 Elissa Newport compared learners of American Sign Language first exposed from birth, at ages 4 to 6, or after 12. On the grammar of word structure (morphology), those first exposed later fell progressively further below native signers.
- In 1993 Rachel Mayberry compared adults who learned American Sign Language late as a *first* language with adults who learned it at the same age as a *second* language. The second-language group had learned English before losing their hearing; the first-language group had no accessible language before sign. The first-language group did worse.

The groups also differed in when they lost their hearing, so this is not a perfect control. Still, it is some of the cleanest human evidence that missing early language, not only age, leaves a lasting mark.

### What a second language can and cannot show

Second languages are easier to study than late first languages, because there are many learners and they arrive at every age. They answer a narrower question, though: these learners already speak a first language, so a decline with age could come from a brain that has become less plastic or from a first language that gets in the way.

In 1989 Jacqueline Johnson and Elissa Newport tested 46 adults whose first language was Korean or Chinese and who had moved to the United States between the ages of 3 and 39. Each listened to English sentences and judged whether each one was grammatical.

**Predict.** Sketch adult score against age of arrival. What would the outcome curve look like if it were a door at puberty? What if it were a tide?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A door in the outcome would be a flat, native-level line until puberty and then a drop. A tide would start falling earlier and decline gradually.

The data look like the second. People who arrived at ages 3 to 7 scored like native speakers. Scores then fell steadily with age of arrival up to about puberty. Among later arrivals, scores were lower and varied widely, and no longer tracked age of arrival. The authors reported that years of residence did not explain the age effect.

</details>

The age effect is strong, and the evidence that it is graded lies in the steady fall among the early arrivals; a single correlation across all arrivals could come out much the same for a sudden step as for a steady slope. But this is a curve of what lasts, and by itself it cannot say when, or how sharply, the ability to learn changes.

Nearly thirty years later a much larger study seemed to say something different. In 2018 Joshua Hartshorne, Joshua Tenenbaum and Steven Pinker gave an online grammar quiz to about 670,000 people and estimated how fast people learn grammar at each age. Their answer: grammar-learning ability stays high until about age 17, then declines.

So how can a 12-year-old arrive with undiminished learning ability and still end up below native level?

Grammar takes years to learn. Take a toy model, for illustration only: suppose learning runs at full speed until 17 and much more slowly after that. Suppose too that native-like grammar takes about 7 years at full speed. That number is not a measurement: it is picked so the toy matches the model's conclusion below, and in the fitted model the time needed is far longer, on the order of decades of experience.

| Starts at | Full-speed years before 17 | Against the 7 needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 12 | 5 to spare |
| 10 | 7 | exactly enough |
| 12 | 5 | 2 short |
| 14 | 3 | 4 short |
| 25 | 0 | all 7 still to learn, at the slower rate |

The 12-year-old starts with the same ability as the 5-year-old but runs out of full-speed time. If later learning is slow enough, and the learner does not keep at it long enough, the gap remains. So the curve of what lasts can start to fall in late childhood even if the ability to learn falls only in late adolescence. On Hartshorne and colleagues' model, reaching native-like grammar needs a start by about age 10. The toy gives a curve that is flat to about 10 and then bends; it does not reproduce the decline Johnson and Newport already found among arrivals aged 8 to 10, or the wide variation among later arrivals, which would need people to differ in the years they need.

Taken together, the graded outcome curves and the late-signer studies do not fit a simple boundary at puberty, though they do not show how learning ability itself changes.

Two questions remain open. Where the slowdown begins depends on the model's assumptions (see [Going deeper](#where-the-slowdown-in-grammar-learning-begins)). And neither study says why learning slows: whether from a change in the brain's plasticity, competition from an entrenched first language, or differences in the kind and amount of input is still debated.

## Sight: separate windows for separate abilities

A newborn sees, but coarsely. Acuity, the finest detail the eye can resolve, and contrast sensitivity, the ability to detect faint differences in brightness, both improve over the first years of life, steeply in the first year.

How do you ask a baby what it can see? Watch where it looks, since infants prefer a pattern to a plain field. In forced-choice preferential looking, a striped grating appears on one side and a gray field of equal brightness on the other; an observer who cannot see which side holds the stripes judges from the infant's gaze. The finest grating the infant reliably looks at gives its acuity in cycles per degree, one cycle being one dark and one light stripe.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/sensitive-periods-behavior/preferential-looking.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: an infant on a parent&#39;s lap faces a screen carrying a striped patch on one side and a plain grey patch of equal brightness on the other. The infant looks toward the stripes, and an observer behind the screen judges the gaze through a peephole. An inset shows gratings of finer and finer stripes." caption="In forced-choice preferential looking, an observer who cannot see which side holds the stripes judges from the infant&#39;s gaze, and the finest grating the infant reliably looks at gives its acuity." />

Children born with dense cataracts in one or both eyes show what visual input does. In 1999 Daphne Maurer, Terri Lewis, Henry Brent and Alex Levin reported 28 infants treated between 1 week and 9 months of age. Right after treatment their acuity was no better than a newborn's, whatever their age, and some improvement appeared after as little as an hour of visual input: improvement needs patterned input, not age alone.

Yet the same group found lasting deficits in some functions, among them fine acuity and processing of the spacing between facial features, even when the cataracts were treated within the first months. Different abilities were affected by deprivation at different ages, so different visual abilities have different sensitive periods. Some are affected by deprivation before the ability itself has appeared, which Maurer and colleagues call a **sleeper effect**. Processing of facial spacing is an example: it keeps developing for years after infancy, yet deprivation only in the first months left it impaired. The cortical mechanisms behind such windows are taken up in [ocular dominance](/lessons/ocular-dominance/) and [critical period control](/lessons/critical-period-control/).

Two reminders from the cases:

- **A curve of what lasts is not a curve of learning ability.** If learning takes years, the outcome can fall first.
- **Separate curves for different functions, species and exposure conditions.** Different visual abilities have different curves in the same child, timings differ between species, and rearing history or tutor format can shift the curve within one species. Compare ages within one rearing and tutoring protocol.

### Design problem

You want to know whether a zebra finch has a sensitive period for learning its tutor's song, and whether experience itself helps to close that window. Design the experiment, and say what result would tell you that hearing a tutor closes the window rather than age alone.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Raise young males without an adult male, then introduce a tutor at different ages in separate groups (for example from day 20, day 40, day 65 and day 90). Record each bird's crystallized adult song and score how much of the tutor's song it copied, syllable by syllable, from spectrograms (plots of frequency against time). Plotting copy score against age at first tutoring defines the window, and a gradual fall would mark it as sensitive rather than critical.

For the second question, use the Eales comparison above: at an age past the usual end of copying, give a new tutor to normally reared birds and to birds kept from adult males. If only the deprived birds copy, the experience is part of what closes the window; if both fail, a clock tied to age alone is the simpler explanation. Eales's 1985 results were of the first kind; some later studies supported them and others did not.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Other uses of the terms critical and sensitive period

A classic definition says a critical period is a time during which a behavior is especially susceptible to, and requires, particular experience in order to develop normally. Many researchers draw the line another way: in a critical period the experience is essential and its effects are permanent, while in a sensitive period experience only has an unusually strong effect. Some use the two terms interchangeably.

### Early movements that do not need experience

The earliest behaviors come from simple circuits that run on their own, without experience. In chick embryos, leg movements continue after the sensory input to the lumbar spinal cord is removed, so they are generated within the cord. Early movements do different jobs. Some, such as yawning and smiling in the fetus, come before patterns used for life. Others serve only the current stage, such as fetal breathing movements that help the lungs develop, or the movements a chick uses to hatch. Spontaneous activity, sensory feedback and learning then work together as behavior develops.

### Live tutors versus tapes

Marler's late tapes had little effect, but a tape is not the only kind of input: in white-crowned sparrows, live tutors have been reported to extend learning past the age at which tapes stop working (Baptista and Petrinovich, 1984). The kind of input, not only its timing, can shape the window.

### Where the slowdown in grammar learning begins

Hartshorne and colleagues read their own data as a sharply defined critical period that ends late. In their model, "sharply defined" describes the age, about 17, at which learning begins to slow, not a sudden collapse in ability; whether the decline after it is a sharp break or a gradual slope is still argued. A later reanalysis of the same data by van der Slik and colleagues (2022) argued that they fit a steady decline from earlier ages instead.

### Do NMDA receptor changes set the song window?

Developmental changes in NMDA receptor subunit composition in the anterior forebrain have been proposed as one mechanism that closes the song window. Experiments in songbirds have challenged the idea that NMDA receptor changes alone set it.

### Names of the song nuclei

HVC is now used as a proper name rather than an abbreviation. RA is the robust nucleus of the arcopallium, and LMAN is the lateral magnocellular nucleus of the anterior nidopallium. In Bottjer's 1984 paper LMAN is called by its older name, the magnocellular nucleus of the anterior neostriatum.

### Converting a grating to a Snellen fraction

> **Snellen denominator ≈ 20 × (30 ÷ finest grating in cycles per degree)**

By this method a newborn's acuity is often near 1 to 2 cycles per degree, roughly 20/600 to 20/300. Numbers depend on the method: recordings of visual evoked potentials from the scalp give better values at the same age than looking tests do.
