  - name: "William Bateson"
    contribution: >-
      With Edith Saunders and Reginald Punnett, reported in 1905 that flower color and pollen shape in
      sweet peas did not sort independently in the second generation of a cross, and named the
      effect coupling.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Bateson, Saunders & Punnett, Reports to the Evolution Committee of the Royal Society, Report II (1905)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      William Bateson was among the first in Britain to test Mendel's rules on many kinds of
      plants and animals, and he did much of that work with Edith Saunders and Reginald Punnett.
      Their second report to the Evolution Committee of the Royal Society, in 1905, described
      a cross between two white-flowered sweet peas, one with long pollen grains and one with
      round. The first generation was purple. The second generation had purple, red and white
      plants, and flower color and pollen shape did not sort independently: purple plants had
      long pollen, and red plants round pollen, far more often than independent assortment
      predicts. The group called the effect coupling. They did not
      explain it with chromosomes; in later papers Bateson and Punnett treated it as a matter of
      some kinds of gamete being formed more often than others. The explanation did not last, but
      the data did. Their sweet peas were an early clear case of two traits that fail to assort
      independently, and a result that any chromosome account of heredity would have to explain.
  - name: "Thomas Hunt Morgan"
    contribution: >-
      Proposed in 1911 that coupled factors lie near one another on a chromosome and are separated
      by exchange between partner chromosomes, more often the farther apart they lie.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Morgan, Science 34:384 (1911), 'Random segregation versus coupling in Mendelian inheritance'; Nobel Lecture, 4 June 1934."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Morgan's laboratory at Columbia University bred the fruit fly Drosophila and, from 1910,
      collected mutant flies whose traits followed the X chromosome. Some of these traits did not
      assort independently of one another. In a short paper in Science in 1911, Morgan offered a
      physical reason. Factors that travel together lie near each other on one chromosome. The
      two partner chromosomes of a pair can exchange parts, as F. A. Janssens had proposed in
      1909 from the crossed threads he saw in cells entering meiosis, and the farther apart two
      factors lie, the more often an exchange falls between them. That turned coupling from a
      puzzle about gametes into a statement about position. Work in Morgan's group also showed
      that Drosophila melanogaster males normally make no crossover gametes, a fact every fly geneticist still plans
      crosses around. Morgan received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for 1933 for his
      work on the role of chromosomes in heredity, and the unit of genetic distance, the
      centimorgan, is named after him.
  - name: "Alfred Sturtevant"
    contribution: >-
      Built the first linear genetic map in 1913, from the frequency of crossover progeny between
      six sex-linked factors in Drosophila, and showed that the distances could be added along a
      single line.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Sturtevant, J. Exp. Zool. 14:43–59 (1913); E. B. Lewis, Alfred Henry Sturtevant, National Academy of Sciences Biographical Memoirs (1998)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Alfred Sturtevant entered Columbia in 1908 and was still an undergraduate when he began
      working in Morgan's fly laboratory. Morgan had suggested that the strength of coupling
      reflects distance. Sturtevant made that idea a measurement. He took six sex-linked factors,
      gathered crossover sons from crosses between pairs of them, using results from Morgan's laboratory, from J. S. Dexter and from his own crosses, and asked whether the percentages
      could be laid on one line so that the distances added up. In the main, they could. Two
      factors, C and O, never separated in his data, so he placed them at one point. He built the
      map from short intervals where possible, pointed out that a double crossover returns the
      outside markers to their parental combination, and saw signs that one crossover made a
      second one nearby less likely. He also warned that his distances need not match physical
      spacing on the chromosome. The paper appeared in 1913 in the Journal of Experimental
      Zoology under his name alone, and thanked Morgan for material. Its method, ordering genes by
      the frequency of recombinants, is the one later maps of flies, maize, mice and human families
      have used.
  - name: "Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock"
    contribution: >-
      Showed in maize in 1931 that when two genes on chromosome 9 recombine, the chromosome
      carrying them has visibly exchanged parts with its partner.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Creighton & McClintock, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 17:492–497 (1931); McClintock, Nobel Lecture, 8 December 1983."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In 1931 Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock, both of the Botany Department at Cornell
      University, published a direct test of the chromosome account of crossing over. The
      question was easy to state. When two genes recombine, do the chromosomes that carry them
      swap pieces? To see a swap you need two partner chromosomes that look different under the
      microscope. They used maize in which one copy of chromosome 9 had a visible knob at one end
      and, at the other, an extra segment from another chromosome left by an interchange. The
      partner copy had neither landmark. Genes for coloured aleurone (C) and waxy starch (wx) lay
      on this chromosome, so the offspring could be classified both by their genes and by the
      look of their chromosomes. Offspring with new combinations of the genes carried the new
      combinations of knob and interchange that a physical exchange predicts. The authors noted
      that their critical ear gave few kernels. Curt Stern reported the same correlation in
      Drosophila that year. McClintock later received the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physiology or
      Medicine for her discovery of mobile genetic elements.

techniques:
  - name: "Two-point test cross"
    blurb: >-
      An organism heterozygous at two loci is crossed to one homozygous recessive at both. Each
      offspring's phenotype then reads directly which combination of alleles came in the
      heterozygote's gamete, so counting the classes counts parental and recombinant gametes.
  - name: "Cytological chromosome markers"
    blurb: >-
      Partner chromosomes are made to look different, with a knob, a translocated piece or a
      broken arm, and offspring are classified both genetically and under the microscope. If gene
      recombination always comes with a visible exchange of landmarks, crossing over is physical.
  - name: "Chi-square test against 1:1:1:1"
    blurb: >-
      Observed test-cross counts are compared with the counts expected if the loci assort
      independently. A large chi-square says the departure is unlikely to be chance; a separate
      test of each locus for 1:1 checks whether one allele survives less well.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "For short intervals, recombination frequency estimates map distance. What does a map distance of 1 centimorgan between two loci mean?"
    choices:
      - "About 1 recombinant offspring in every 1,000 scored"
      - "About 1 recombinant offspring in every 100 scored"
      - "Exactly 1 million base pairs of DNA between the loci"
      - "About 1 crossover per pair of homologues in each meiosis"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A centimorgan is defined from recombinant progeny: over a short interval, 1 cM corresponds
      to 1% recombinant offspring, that is about 1 in 100. One in 1,000 is a tenth of a
      centimorgan. A centimorgan is a unit of genetic map distance, not a physical length, so it does not
      equal a fixed number of base pairs; the ratio of base pairs to centimorgans varies along
      chromosomes, between species and between the sexes. One crossover per chromatid on average (two per pair of homologues in each meiosis)
      defines a whole morgan (100 cM), not a centimorgan.
  - stem: "A Drosophila female received A b from her mother and a B from her father. She is test-crossed to an a b / a b male. Which progeny classes are the recombinants?"
    choices:
      - "A b and a B, since these will be the rarest classes"
      - "A b and a B, since these are the alleles she carries"
      - "A B and A b, since both classes carry the allele A"
      - "A B and a b, since she received A b and a B instead"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Recombinant means a combination that neither of her parents gave her. She received A b and
      a B, so A B and a b are new, and if the loci are linked these will be the smaller classes.
      Calling A b and a B the rarest assumes the coupling phase without checking the parents; in
      this repulsion female they are the parental classes and the most common. All four alleles
      are carried by her, so carrying them says nothing about which combinations are new. A B
      and A b share the allele A but differ at B, so this pairs one recombinant with one
      parental class.
  - stem: "Why can the recombination frequency measured between two loci not exceed 50%, however far apart they are?"
    choices:
      - "Each crossover involves only two of the four chromatids"
      - "No chromosome arm can have more than one crossover"
      - "Recombinant gametes survive less well than parental ones"
      - "Loci more than 50 cM apart move to different chromosomes"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      When a crossover forms between two loci, it joins two of the four chromatids of the paired
      chromosomes, so half the products of that meiosis are recombinant and half parental. With
      several crossovers, each recombines the loci or restores them, and on average the
      recombinant share still tops out at one half. Chromosome arms can have more than one
      crossover; Sturtevant saw double crossovers in 1913. Reduced survival of recombinants is a
      possible bias in a particular cross, not the reason for the ceiling. Loci do not move
      between chromosomes when they are far apart; they stay put and simply behave as if
      unlinked.
  - stem: "Loci A and B are 12 cM apart. A female A b / a B is test-crossed to an a b / a b male. Assuming equal survival, what fraction of the progeny have the double recessive a b phenotype?"
    choices:
      - "44%"
      - "12%"
      - "6%"
      - "3%"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Over a short interval, twelve centimorgans means about 12% recombinant gametes. The female is in repulsion, so a b is a
      recombinant gamete, and the two recombinant classes, A B and a b, share the 12% equally:
      6% each. 44% is the share of each parental class (88% divided by two), which is what you get
      if you assume coupling. 12% is both recombinant classes together, not one. 3% halves the
      recombinant share twice, as if each class were split again.
  - stem: "Over long intervals, hidden crossovers make recombination frequency underestimate distance. In two-point crosses, loci X and Y give 50% recombinants. Separately, X and Z give 30% recombinants and Z and Y give 35%, with Z between them on chromosome 3. What is the best conclusion about X and Y?"
    choices:
      - "They lie on different chromosomes, since the frequency is 50%"
      - "They lie at least 65 cM apart on chromosome 3, beyond the ceiling"
      - "They lie 50 cM apart on chromosome 3, since frequency is distance"
      - "They lie 5 cM apart on chromosome 3, the gap between the intervals"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Map distances add over adjacent intervals, so X and Y are at least 30 + 35 = 65 cM
      apart on chromosome 3. That is a lower bound, because each of those frequencies already
      misses some double crossovers; corrected with Haldane's function the two intervals come to
      about 106 cM, which predicts about 44% recombinants, close to the 50% ceiling. So a
      two-point count near 50% fits. A 50% result cannot by itself tell distant loci on one
      chromosome from loci on different chromosomes, so concluding different chromosomes ignores
      the linkage through Z. Reading 50% as 50 cM treats a frequency that has hit its ceiling as
      a distance. Taking the difference, 5 cM, would be right only if Z lay outside the X to Y
      interval, but Z lies between them.

    label: "Like I'm five"
    text: >-
      Genes sit along a chromosome like beads on a string. When a parent makes an egg or a sperm,
      its two strings can swap pieces. Beads that sit close together almost never get split up by
      a swap, and beads far apart often do. So by counting how often two traits get split up,
      you can tell how far apart their genes sit.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      Mendel's law of independent assortment works for genes on different chromosomes. Genes on
      the same chromosome tend to be inherited together, which is called linkage. They can still
      be separated, because during meiosis the two copies of a chromosome swap matching pieces, a
      process called crossing over. The farther apart two genes are, the more often a swap falls
      between them. Geneticists count the offspring with new combinations of traits, and 1% new
      combinations counts as 1 centimorgan of distance. The count can never pass 50%, because at
      that point the genes behave as if they were on different chromosomes.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      In a test cross of a double heterozygote to a double recessive, unlinked loci give four
      gamete classes in a 1:1:1:1 ratio. Linked loci give an excess of the parental classes, the
      allele combinations the heterozygote received, whether in coupling (A B / a b) or repulsion
      (A b / a B). Recombinant gametes come from crossing over between non-sister chromatids in
      prophase I; Creighton and McClintock in maize and Stern in Drosophila showed in 1931 that
      genetic recombination comes with visible exchange of chromosome parts. Recombination
      frequency, RF = recombinants / total, gives map distance for short intervals (1 cM = 1%
      recombinant progeny), and distances add along a chromosome. Because each crossover involves
      two of four chromatids, RF cannot exceed 50%, so long intervals are underestimated and
      maps are built from short ones.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      Map distance is the expected number of crossovers per chromatid in an interval, in morgans;
      recombination frequency is the probability that an odd number of crossovers falls there.
      The two agree only when multiple crossovers are rare. Haldane's 1919 function assumes
      crossovers form independently (a Poisson process along the chromatid) and gives r = (1 -
      e^(-2d)) / 2, so 50 cM corresponds to r of about 0.316 and r = 0.5 to an unbounded d. In most
      organisms studied, crossovers interfere: one makes another nearby less likely, which Sturtevant suspected in
      1913 and which single-sperm sequencing detected in every one of 20 human donors (Bell and
      colleagues, 2020). Functions such as Kosambi's add interference, but none converts distance
      to frequency for every organism. Rates also differ between the sexes and among individuals,
      so a map is an estimate for a stated sex and population.


      An open question is how a cell spaces its crossovers. In most organisms studied, each pair
      of homologues gets at least one crossover, and crossovers are spread out more evenly than
      chance would give, yet many more double-strand breaks are made than become crossovers.
      Family sequencing in Iceland (Palsson and colleagues, 2025) estimated that noncrossovers are the more common outcome of recombination
      in human meiosis, and these usually leave no trace on a classical linkage map.

**Test-cross fruit flies that carry two mutations and score 1,000 offspring. Mendel's law of independent assortment predicts that 500 will show one grandparent's combination of traits or the other's. You count 830.**

Independent assortment ([Mendel's peas](/lessons/mendels-peas/), [probability and crosses](/lessons/probability-and-crosses/)) holds for loci on different chromosomes. For loci close together on one chromosome it fails, and the failure becomes a ruler.

> **The one idea.** For short intervals, recombination frequency estimates map distance; over long intervals, hidden crossovers make it underestimate distance.
>
> *Scope:* this is two-locus mapping when progeny accurately report gametes. “Short” means double crossovers in the interval are rare. Phase identifies recombinants first, and distance is genetic rather than physical.

## Read the four classes before counting them

A **locus** is a gene's place on a chromosome; its versions are **alleles**. Take two loci, A and B, with recessive alleles a and b. The first film showed how a **test cross**, a double heterozygote mated to an a b / a b tester, makes each offspring's phenotype name the heterozygote's gamete. Unlinked loci give the four classes 1:1:1:1; when the inherited combinations come back more often, the loci are **linked**, near each other on one chromosome.

The combinations inherited by the heterozygote are the **parental** classes. The other two are **recombinant**. Which is which depends on **phase**, the arrangement of alleles in that heterozygote:

- **Coupling** (cis), A B / a b: the dominant alleles came in on one chromosome. Parental classes are A B and a b.
- **Repulsion** (trans), A b / a B: each chromosome carries one dominant allele. Parental classes are A b and a B.

**Predict.** A female received A b from her mother and a B from her father. Before seeing any offspring, which two test-cross classes are parental?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A b and a B are parental because those are the combinations she received; A B and a b are recombinant. Parental classes are usually larger, but inheritance defines them, not their counts.

</details>
## Crossing over makes recombinants

In prophase I, each chromosome has already copied itself into two **sister chromatids**, and the partner chromosomes, or **homologues**, pair, placing four chromatids side by side (see [chromosomes and meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/)). **Crossing over** is a reciprocal exchange of matching segments between **non-sister chromatids**, one from each homologue. The cross-shaped contact that remains visible is a **chiasma**.

In the first film's single crossover, the two chromatids that took part became A b and a B and the other two stayed parental. Because the exchange is reciprocal, the two recombinant classes should be about equal in a test cross, as should the two parental classes. A large difference within a pair suggests that one genotype survives less well.

**Predict.** In one reciprocal pair you count 85 A b offspring but only 20 a B offspring. What should you question before treating the total as an unbiased crossover count?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Question equal survival and scoring. A reciprocal exchange makes the two recombinant combinations about equally, so a strong deficit in one class can mean that genotype survives poorly or is hard to score.

</details>

Was crossing over a physical swap? In 1931 Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock used maize whose two copies of chromosome 9 looked different. One copy had a visible knob at one end and an extra segment from another chromosome at the other; its partner had neither, so offspring could be classified by both genes and chromosome structure.

**Predict.** If a new gene combination comes from physical exchange, what should happen to the visible knob and extra segment?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The landmarks should appear in new combinations too. That is what Creighton and McClintock found: recombinant genes travelled with the chromosome structures predicted by an exchange. Curt Stern reported the same correlation in Drosophila that year, using X chromosomes with visible structural changes.

</details>

## Sturtevant's map: frequency as distance

The first film turned the recombinant share into a ruler; here is the same step as an equation. **Recombination frequency** (RF) is the number of recombinant progeny divided by the total number scored.

<EquationCard
  purpose="Turn a count of recombinant offspring into a distance on the genetic map."
  eq="cM = 100 × RF, where RF = recombinant progeny / total progeny"
  say="The map distance in centimorgans is a hundred times the recombination frequency, and the recombination frequency is the recombinant offspring divided by all the offspring."
  symbols={[
    ["cM", "centimorgans", "map distance between the two loci. Use this conversion for short intervals only."],
    ["RF", "R F, recombination frequency", "the fraction of offspring in the recombinant classes, between 0 and 0.5"],
  ]}
  toolkit={["ratios"]}
  check="If no offspring are recombinant, RF = 0 and the loci map to the same spot. RF cannot rise past 0.5, so a test cross never reads more than 50 cM directly; the section on the 50% ceiling explains why."
>
  <Fragment slot="example">
    <p>Round numbers to keep the arithmetic easy (not from a real cross): 50 recombinant flies out of 500.</p>
    <p>RF = 50 / 500 = 0.10, or 10%. Map distance = 100 × 0.10 = 10 cM.</p>
  </Fragment>
</EquationCard>

One map unit, or **centimorgan** (cM), named for Morgan, corresponds to 1% recombinant progeny over a short interval. It is not a length of DNA: the ratio of DNA to centimorgans varies along chromosomes, among species and between sexes, and crossover rates also vary among individuals, so a map is an estimate for a stated population and sex. Drosophila melanogaster males normally make no crossover gametes; in humans and many other species the two sexes also give different maps.

**Worked problem.** Assume complete penetrance, equal survival of all classes, and accurate scoring. A Drosophila female heterozygous for recessive mutations a and b on one autosome received A B from one parent and a b from the other, so she is in coupling. She is test-crossed to an a b / a b male. Of 1,000 progeny:

| Class | Count |
|---|---:|
| A B | 420 |
| a b | 410 |
| A b | 85 |
| a B | 85 |

1. *Name the classes from the phase.* She received A B and a b, so those are parental: 420 + 410 = 830. A b and a B are recombinant: 85 + 85 = 170. Here they are also the smallest classes, as expected for linked loci.
2. *Calculate RF.* RF = 170 / 1,000 = 0.17, or 17%. Read directly, the map distance is about 17 cM.
3. *Test for linkage* with the chi-square score from [probability and crosses](/lessons/probability-and-crosses/). Independent assortment predicts 250 in each class. Chi-square = (420 - 250)^2/250 + (410 - 250)^2/250 + 2 × (85 - 250)^2/250 = 115.6 + 102.4 + 108.9 + 108.9 = 435.8, with 3 degrees of freedom. The 5% critical value from a chi-square table is 7.81. The excess of parentals is far beyond chance.
4. *Predict the other phase.* A female carrying the same loci in repulsion, A b / a B, would still give 17% recombinants, but now A B and a b are the recombinant classes: 8.5% each (half of 17%), with 41.5% each of A b and a B (half of the remaining 83%).

Step 4 is where students slip: “recombinants are the two smallest classes” is a habit, not a rule. It fails when you must predict the classes before any flies hatch, and it fails when a class survives poorly enough to shrink below a recombinant class.

An RF of 17% gives an uncorrected estimate of 17 cM. Whether that interval is short enough for RF to approximate distance depends on how often multiple crossovers occur; over long intervals, hidden crossovers make recombination frequency underestimate distance. The second film shows how.

## The 50% ceiling, and what a mapping function corrects

The second film showed that a two-point count reads only the final outside-marker combination on each scored chromatid, so an even number of exchanges affecting that chromatid hides. (An even number of crossovers in the whole bivalent need not hide, because different crossovers can involve different chromatids.) Why the average stops at exactly one half, the film did not show; the reason is the four chromatids. A crossover between the loci involves two of them, so a meiosis with one crossover in the interval gives half recombinant products. A second crossover between the same loci either recombines them again or puts them back, depending on which chromatids it joins; on average the recombinant share stays at one half. Loci far apart on one chromosome therefore give about 50%, the same as loci on different chromosomes. A two-point cross cannot tell those apart; linkage of both loci to a third marker can.

**Map distance** is the expected number of crossovers per chromatid in an interval, measured in morgans (1 morgan = 100 cM); **recombination frequency** is the fraction of gametes whose two marker alleles finish in a recombinant combination. In 1919 J. B. S. Haldane gave a mapping function that connects the two, assuming crossovers form independently along the chromosome. The second film plotted its curve; here is the equation.

<EquationCard
  purpose="What recombination frequency a given map distance should produce if crossovers fall independently (Haldane's mapping function)."
  eq="RF = (1 − e^(−2d)) / 2, with d in morgans"
  say="The recombination frequency is one half of one minus e to the minus two d."
  symbols={[
    ["RF", "R F", "the recombination frequency you would observe in a test cross"],
    ["e^(−2d)", "e to the minus two d", "under Haldane's assumption, the chance that a meiosis has no crossover at all between the two loci. e is about 2.718."],
    ["d", "d", "the true map distance in morgans (1 morgan = 100 cM)"],
  ]}
  toolkit={["e-and-ln", "powers"]}
  check="At d = 0, e^0 = 1 and RF = 0: loci in the same place never recombine. As d grows, e^(−2d) shrinks toward 0 and RF climbs toward 1/2, the 50% ceiling. For small d, RF is close to d, which is why short intervals can be read directly."
>
  <Fragment slot="example">
    <p>A 50 cM interval is d = 0.5 morgans, so 2d = 1.</p>
    <p>e^(−1) is about 0.368 (see e and ln). 1 − 0.368 = 0.632, and half of that is 0.316.</p>
    <p>So a 50 cM interval gives only about 31.6% recombinants: read directly, it would look like 31.6 cM.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="derivation">
    <p>Optional: where it comes from. A distance of d morgans means d crossovers per chromatid on average, so 2d per meiosis, because each crossover involves two of the four chromatids. If crossovers fall at random, the chance that a meiosis has none in the interval is e^(−2d). A meiosis with at least one crossover gives, on average, half recombinant products, as the four-chromatid argument above showed. So RF = 1/2 × (1 − e^(−2d)).</p>
  </Fragment>
</EquationCard>

To get a distance from an observed RF under this model, rearrange:

1. Multiply both sides by 2: 2 RF = 1 − e^(−2d).
2. Swap terms: e^(−2d) = 1 − 2 RF.
3. Take the natural log of both sides (ln undoes e): −2d = ln(1 − 2 RF).
4. Divide by −2: **d = −1/2 × ln(1 − 2 RF)**.

For the worked cross, Haldane's model asks what distance would produce an RF of 0.17 if crossovers occurred independently:

> d = −1/2 × ln(1 − 0.34) = −1/2 × ln(0.66) = 0.208 morgans (ln(0.66) is about −0.416, from a calculator's ln key), or about 20.8 cM.

The gap grows with distance. Two adjacent intervals of 20 and 40 cM add to 60 cM, yet under the same model their own frequencies, 16.5% and 27.5%, would add to 44%, while the whole 60 cM span gives 34.9%.

It can overcorrect. Fewer double crossovers are hidden than the no-interference model expects, so less extra distance is needed. Kosambi's function builds interference in and gives about 17.7 cM for the worked RF, closer to the direct 17 cM than Haldane's 20.8 cM. Drosophila crossovers interfere strongly over short intervals, so for this cross the direct 17 cM is likely nearer the truth.

In most organisms studied, crossovers interfere: one makes another nearby less likely. Sturtevant suspected this, and single-sperm sequencing of 20 human donors detected it in every donor in 2020. No mapping function fits every organism, so the second film's closing practice holds: map with short intervals and add them, and use a third marker to catch double crossovers, as in [three-point mapping](/lessons/three-point-mapping/).

### Design problem

You isolate a new recessive mutation in Drosophila, dumpy-like (dl), and suspect it lies on chromosome 2 near a known recessive marker, m. Design crosses to test whether dl and m are linked and to estimate the distance between them. Say what result would leave you unable to decide.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Build a heterozygote in known phase.** Cross dl/dl flies to m/m flies. Every F1 fly is dl + / + m (+ is the wild-type allele), in repulsion, and you know the phase because you made it.

**Use females.** Because Drosophila melanogaster males normally make no crossover gametes, an F1 male gives only parental classes if dl and m share a chromosome, whatever the distance, and all four classes if they do not. A male test cross can show linkage but cannot measure distance. Test-cross F1 females to dl m / dl m males. If no double-mutant stock exists yet, you first recover a dl m recombinant chromosome from the progeny of F1 females.

**Score enough flies.** Score all four classes in several hundred progeny at least, more if the loci may be close, since a short interval yields few recombinants.

**Test linkage without being fooled by survival.** Check each locus alone for 1:1 (dl versus +, m versus +); a deficit of dumpy-like flies, for instance, would distort the four classes even if the loci were unlinked. Then test the association in a 2 x 2 table (dl versus + against m versus +) with a one-degree-of-freedom chi-square, which unequal survival at one locus does not inflate.

**Calculate.** Here the recombinants are wild type and dl m. RF = (wild type + dl m) / total, and the distance in cM is 100 x RF, corrected with a mapping function if RF is large.

**Know the limit.** An RF near 50% cannot tell a distant locus on chromosome 2 from one on another chromosome. To place dl, add a third marker on chromosome 2 and do a three-point cross, which also orders the three genes.

</details>

## Going deeper

### From coupling to a chromosome map

In 1905 William Bateson, Edith Saunders and Reginald Punnett reported that in the second generation of a sweet-pea cross, purple flowers went with long pollen, and red flowers with round pollen, far too often for independent assortment. They called this coupling and later explained it by gamete formation, not shared chromosomes.

In 1911 Thomas Hunt Morgan, whose laboratory found X-linked Drosophila mutants (see [sex linkage](/lessons/sex-linkage-and-beyond/)), proposed the chromosome explanation. He drew on F. A. Janssens's 1909 interpretation of crossed meiotic figures as chromosome exchanges.

As an undergraduate in Morgan's laboratory, Alfred Sturtevant used crossover progeny as a distance index. His 1913 map placed six X-linked factors on one line and added short intervals. He warned that genetic and physical spacing need not match.

### The mismatch that exposed hidden crossovers

Sturtevant's short intervals placed B and M 57.6 units apart; a direct cross gave 37.6% recombinants. He proposed, as probable rather than proved, that double crossovers restored the parental outside markers.
