techniques:
  - name: "Kinome cataloguing by sequence search"
    blurb: >-
      Start from the conserved motifs of the kinase catalytic domain, build a statistical profile
      of them, and scan a whole genome for every gene that fits. Sorting the hits by similarity
      gives families, and comparing families across species shows which are old and which grew.
  - name: "In vitro phosphotransfer with labelled ATP"
    blurb: >-
      Mix purified proteins with ATP whose terminal phosphate is radioactive, stop the reaction at
      intervals, separate the proteins on a gel and see which one carries the label. Adding the
      proteins in different orders shows the direction in which the phosphate travels.
  - name: "Gene trees: orthologs and paralogs"
    blurb: >-
      Align related genes from several species and build a tree from the differences. Genes that
      split when two species split are orthologs, and genes that split by duplication inside one
      lineage are paralogs. The tree dates each duplication against the branching of species.
  - name: "Domain swapping and chimeric proteins"
    blurb: >-
      Join a domain from one protein onto another and ask whether the hybrid carries the borrowed
      function. If a binding domain or the outside of a receptor still works in its new setting,
      it is a true module, and the swap shows what that module contributes.

  - stem: "A receptor tyrosine kinase gene starts to be made in a tissue where it was never made before, and the protein it encodes has not changed. Which kind of change in the genome best explains this?"
    choices:
      - "A new docking site added to the tail of the receptor"
      - "A change in the short stretch of regulatory DNA that decides which cells make the gene"
      - "A rearrangement that joins the coding segment for an SH2 domain to another gene"
      - "A new sensor kinase that passes a phosphate to an aspartate"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "Short stretches of regulatory DNA near a gene decide which cells make it and when, so a change there can put an unchanged receptor into new cells, where it meets different partners and target genes. A new docking site changes the tail of the protein, and the protein here has not changed. Joining an SH2 domain to another gene makes a protein with a new partner, which is also a change to the protein. A sensor kinase passing a phosphate to an aspartate describes a bacterial two-component system, not the reuse of an animal receptor."
  - stem: "After a gene duplication, each copy loses a different part of the original gene's job, so the organism now needs both copies. What is this outcome called?"
    choices:
      - "Neo-functionalization"
      - "Co-option by a new tissue"
      - "Pseudogene formation"
      - "Sub-functionalization"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Sub-functionalization is the division of the ancestral job between two copies, which is why
      both are kept. Neo-functionalization means one copy gained a job the ancestor never had, and
      the stem describes only losses. Pseudogene formation is the loss of the protein-coding function in one copy, after
      which the organism would not need it. Co-option by a new tissue is the reuse of an existing
      gene for a new purpose and does not require a duplication.
  - stem: "A receptor tyrosine kinase that has never signalled to PI 3-kinase begins to do so in one lineage. Which change is the smallest that could account for it?"
    choices:
      - "A whole-genome duplication gives the lineage a spare PI 3-kinase"
      - "A second kinase domain is joined to the receptor by domain shuffling"
      - "A few amino acids change near one tyrosine in the receptor tail"
      - "A new second messenger is invented for the receptor to produce"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      PI 3-kinase is recruited through its SH2 domains to a phosphotyrosine with the right
      neighbours, so changing a few residues near one tyrosine in the tail can create the docking
      site. A second kinase domain adds catalytic activity but no site for PI 3-kinase to bind. A
      new second messenger would need new enzymes to make it and to read it, far more than the
      problem requires. A spare PI 3-kinase from a whole-genome duplication still has nowhere to
      dock on this receptor.
  - stem: "The genome of the choanoflagellate Monosiga brevicollis encodes tyrosine kinases, tyrosine phosphatases and SH2 domains, but no recognisable Wnt or TGF-beta genes. Choanoflagellates are the closest living relatives of animals. Which reading of these facts is best supported?"
    choices:
      - "Choanoflagellates descend from animals that lost their tissues"
      - "Tyrosine kinases passed from animals to choanoflagellates in food"
      - "Phosphotyrosine signalling arose before animals and before Wnt"
      - "Wnt and TGF-beta signalling arose before phosphotyrosine signalling"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Parts found in both animals and their closest relatives were most likely present in the
      single-celled ancestor they share, so the kinase, the phosphatase and the SH2 domain predate
      animals, while Wnt, which is found only in animals, came later. Descent from animals that
      lost their tissues is ruled out by gene trees, which place choanoflagellates beside the
      animals and not among them. Transfer of genes from animals in food does not explain a full
      and diversified set of kinases, phosphatases and SH2 domains. Wnt and TGF-beta arising first
      is the reverse of the data, since the organism has the tyrosine parts and lacks those two.
  - stem: "A tumour depends on receptor tyrosine kinase A. A drug that blocks A stops its growth for months. Growth then resumes with A still blocked, and the tumour cells now carry many extra copies of the gene for receptor B. Which feature of animal signalling best explains the relapse?"
    choices:
      - "The drug is pumped out of the cell faster once B is abundant"
      - "Receptors A and B feed the same downstream core of kinases"
      - "Receptor A has mutated so that the drug no longer fits it"
      - "Receptor B has a private cascade that shares no parts with A's"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      If A and B feed the same core, extra B can restart that core while A stays blocked, which is

      A, which again contradicts the stem.

      atypical, and 90 are tyrosine kinases (58 receptor, 32 cytoplasmic). ## A few old pathways, expanded at the receptor end

Animal genome comparisons keep returning the same short list of core pathways. Pires-daSilva and Sommer (2003) named seven developmental pathways, and Amit, Wides and Yarden (2007) added G-protein-coupled receptors to make eight:

- G-protein-coupled receptors ([lesson](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/))
- nuclear receptors ([lesson](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/))
- TGF-beta ([lesson](/lessons/tgf-beta-and-smads/))
- Notch ([lesson](/lessons/notch-signaling/))
- JAK and STAT ([lesson](/lessons/jak-stat/))
- Hedgehog ([lesson](/lessons/hedgehog-and-cilium/))
- Wnt, called Wingless in flies ([lesson](/lessons/wnt-signaling/))
- receptor tyrosine kinases ([lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/))

A fruit fly has all eight, and so do you. What differs is the copy count at each layer: humans have four EGF receptors where the fly and nematode worm have one each. After adjusting for total gene number, Amit, Wides and Yarden (2007) compared humans with four invertebrates (see Going deeper for the species and unadjusted ratios):

## Copy, then change: three fates for a spare gene

DNA copying errors occasionally duplicate a single gene or an entire genome. Susumu Ohno argued in *Evolution by Gene Duplication* (1970) that duplication is the main source of new genes, because a spare copy can change while the original keeps working. Jawed vertebrates carry traces of two early whole-genome doublings, the likely route from one ancestral EGF receptor to four.

**Predict.** Suppose an ancestral family of 10 receptor genes undergoes two rounds of whole-genome duplication, with no single-gene gains, and the descendant keeps 24 genes. What fraction of copies after the second doubling was lost?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

40 percent. Two doublings turn 10 genes into 40 (10 → 20 → 40). Keeping 24 leaves 16 lost, and 16 ÷ 40 = 0.40—showing loss still clears many spares even in expanding receptor families.

</details>

A spare copy faces three main fates:

1. **Loss.** A mutation disables the spare, selection ignores it while the original works, and it decays into a **pseudogene** (a gene-like sequence that no longer makes a working protein). This is the common outcome.
2. **Sub-functionalization** (dividing the old job). Each copy loses a different part of the ancestral role, so both must be kept. If the old receptor worked in gut and in nerve, one copy may lose the regulatory DNA for gut and the other the regulatory DNA for nerve, so the animal needs both. (For a partnership that mimics a split without sister copies, see Going deeper on ErbB2 and ErbB3.)
3. **Neo-functionalization** (taking a new job). One copy gains a role the ancestor lacked, such as binding a new ligand.

A related process is **co-option**: reusing an existing gene or pathway for a new purpose, which need not involve copying it. In flies, Toll patterns the embryo and also fights infection ([lesson](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb/)).

**Predict.** Because one *Toll* gene handles both embryonic patterning and immunity in flies, is this sub-functionalization, neo-functionalization, or co-option?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Co-option. Sub- and neo-functionalization act on two duplicates, whereas co-option reuses a single existing gene without copying it.

</details>

Carry duplication across a whole pathway, from one ancestral cascade to four receptors. If downstream kinases duplicated and stayed insulated alongside each receptor, you would get four vertical wires. In the other outcome, the duplicated receptors keep sharing downstream parts and form a network funnelling many inputs into heavily connected **hubs**—nodes receiving many inputs and driving several outputs, such as Ras.

The genome table fits shared wiring better, because receptors multiplied faster than the core they feed. Two cautions apply: the core expanded too—humans have three Ras genes and, after adjusting for gene number, ~1.8× as many MAP kinases as the four invertebrates (Going deeper)—and gene counts alone cannot prove how parts are wired inside a cell.

Duplication explains where extra receptors came from, but not how a spare receptor connects to a new pathway—or why animal cascades rewire so cheaply compared with the simplest bacterial relay.

## The simplest relay vs. an animal pathway

The second film contrasted a bacterial relay with an animal RTK; here are the biochemical details. Note upfront that this is a design contrast, not an ancestor–descendant line: a bacterial sensor kinase has a different catalytic fold from animal serine, threonine and tyrosine kinases, and animals have no two-component systems, though plants and fungi do.

Many bacteria sense their surroundings with **two-component systems**, though single proteins carrying both sensing and output domains are even more common. Component one is a **sensor kinase**, usually in the membrane, which moves the terminal phosphate of ATP onto one of its own histidines. Component two is the **responder** (**response regulator**), which transfers that phosphate onto one of its own aspartates, changing shape to switch on. Most responders are transcription factors, linking sensor and gene switch through one phosphate:

> **ATP → histidine on the sensor → aspartate on the responder → output**

*E. coli* has about thirty pairs (for osmotic strength, nitrogen supply, and more). Its swimming uses a variant: CheA sits beneath the receptors as a separate kinase, and CheY binds the flagellar motor rather than DNA ([signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/)). Sequence comparisons showed CheY belongs to one responder family serving unrelated behaviours such as sporulation and membrane protein synthesis, and purified proteins showed direct phosphotransfer from CheA to CheY (Going deeper gives the 1985 and 1988 experiments).

Beside this relay, an animal kinase pathway adds three features:

- **A mark that stays.** Aspartate phosphate is labile, lasting from seconds for CheY—well under a minute unaided, faster with helper CheZ—to minutes or more in other responders. Serine, threonine or tyrosine phosphate stays until a phosphatase removes it ([the phosphorylation switch](/lessons/phosphorylation-switch/)), so phosphatases set signal duration rather than mark chemistry.
- **A reader.** Because the mark stays, another protein can bind it: an SH2 domain reads a phosphotyrosine without removing it. No separate domain reads the bacterial aspartate phosphate.
- **Branches and layers.** Most sensor kinases signal to one partner responder and largely ignore the rest. An animal RTK recruits several pathways at once, several running through kinase tiers.

## Modularity: new wiring from old parts

If every new link needed a new enzyme, expansion would crawl. Instead, signaling proteins are built from **domains**—segments that fold and work on their own ([interaction modules](/lessons/protein-interaction-modules/)). Building on the second film's overview, here are the motifs, adaptors and genome counts behind cheap rewiring.

**Move a domain.** Joining an SH2 coding segment to another gene gives that protein a new partner in one step. Adaptors such as Grb2 are nothing but binding domains ([adaptors and scaffolds](/lessons/adaptors-and-scaffolds/)). Try swapping domains yourself:

**Add a docking site.** A **docking site** is a short stretch of amino acids around a phosphorylated residue. Grb2's SH2 domain reads a phosphotyrosine followed two positions later by asparagine (pY-X-N); PI 3-kinase's SH2 domains read a phosphotyrosine followed three positions later by methionine (pY-X-X-M). One or two point mutations in a receptor's tail can create or erase a site, adding or removing a line to Ras or PI 3-kinase.

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Mutating leucine to asparagine. A single amino-acid change builds the pY-X-N docking site Grb2's SH2 domain binds, plugging into the existing Ras hub without moving a whole enzyme.

</details>

**Change where and when a gene is on.** Short stretches of **regulatory DNA** near—or sometimes far from—a gene decide which cells make it and when. An unchanged receptor expressed in a new tissue meets different partners and target genes. The logic matches at both levels: a short DNA motif shifts expression, and a short peptide motif shifts protein binding.

Interaction-domain counts track this expansion from yeast to worm and fly to mouse and human. Budding yeast has one SH2-domain protein; humans have more than a hundred, alongside 90 tyrosine kinases.

Where did that tyrosine kit originate? **Choanoflagellates** are animals' closest living single-celled relatives. Nicole King and Sean Carroll reported an RTK gene in *Monosiga brevicollis* in 2001, and its 2008 genome encodes tyrosine kinases (128, more than humans' 90), tyrosine phosphatases and SH2 domains, yet lacks recognisable Wnt and TGF-beta genes. Thus the writer (kinase), reader (SH2) and eraser (phosphatase) kit predates animals, while embryo-patterning pathways like Wnt and TGF-beta arrived later. What it does in a single cell is still largely unknown.

## Why a network and not a set of wires

Copying old pathways at the receptor end and rewiring them with modular parts leaves a shared network rather than isolated wires. Giving up insulation buys **robustness**: output holds when a part fails or an input is noisy ([signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/)). Sister receptors feeding the same core can cover part of a loss if made in the same cells, and negative feedback loops in the shared core steady output for every receptor feeding it.

The same design carries three clinical costs:

- **A hub is a single point of failure.** Lock Ras on ([GTPase switches](/lessons/gtpase-switches/)) and upstream receptors lose control of everything Ras drives.

**Predict.** In a cell expressing four RTKs, a mutation locks Ras permanently active. Will blocking all four receptors at the membrane stop Ras-driven growth?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Because all four receptors funnel into Ras, a locked-on Ras hub keeps signalling downstream regardless of upstream receptor blockade.

</details>

- **A shared core allows bypass.** A lung tumour controlled by an EGF receptor inhibitor can escape by amplifying MET, another receptor feeding the same core.
- **Relatives resemble each other.** Nearly all human kinases descend from one ancestral catalytic domain and keep a similar ATP pocket, so a drug for one often fits relatives. Imatinib, targeting BCR-ABL in chronic myeloid leukaemia, also inhibits KIT and PDGF receptors; KIT and one PDGF receptor are sister genes side by side on chromosome 4. That cross-reaction explains why imatinib treats a KIT-driven gut tumour and why new kinase drugs are profiled against hundreds of kinases—where the drugs lesson in this unit begins.

### Design problem

The gene for a receptor tyrosine kinase is copied by accident, so the animal now has two. Describe three different fates for the extra copy. For the fate in which the copy comes to carry a new message, say what must change in the copy, and what must change so that the new message does not leak into the old pathway.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Lost.** The original still does the work, so a damaged spare is not removed by selection and decays into a pseudogene.

**Shared.** Suppose the old receptor worked in gut and in nerve. If one copy loses the regulatory DNA for gut and the other loses it for nerve, the animal needs both. No new message exists. One job is now held by two genes.

**New message.** The outside of the copy must come to bind a different ligand, often a drifting duplicate of the old one, or the copy must come to be made in cells where the old receptor was absent. Its tail may change too: a different set of tyrosines recruits a different mix of SH2 proteins.

**Stopping the leak.** A receptor tyrosine kinase signals as a pair ([lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)), so the first leak is an old receptor pairing with a new one. The surfaces that hold the pair together must diverge, or the two receptors must be kept in different cells. The old ligand must also stop fitting the new receptor. Downstream, both receptors still reach Ras, so the new one must differ in its docking sites, in how long it signals, or in the scaffold that holds its kinases. Evolution rarely finishes this job, and the leaks that remain are the cross-connections of a network.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The 2007 genome comparison

Amit, Wides and Yarden (*Molecular Systems Biology* 3:151, 2007) compared humans against a sea squirt, a sea urchin, the fruit fly and a nematode worm. Beyond the gene-adjusted ratios tabulated above, even the unadjusted counts—about 3.6-fold for RTKs and 2.2-fold for MAP kinases—sit below the fourfold ceiling of two whole-genome doublings with full retention, reflecting selective receptor retention alongside widespread duplicate loss.

### Who showed that the relay is shared

In 1985 Ann Stock, Daniel Koshland and Jeffry Stock reported that CheY's sequence resembled regulators of sporulation and membrane protein synthesis, revealing one shared responder family. In 1988 Melvin Simon's laboratory and Jeffry Stock's group at Princeton independently traced labelled phosphate from CheA to CheY.

### ErbB2 and ErbB3: a partnership, not a split

ErbB2 has no known ligand, ErbB3 has a feeble kinase, and the two signal strongly as a pair, resembling sub-functionalization. Because ErbB2's closest relative is the EGF receptor and ErbB3's is ErbB4, however, they are a partnership between relatives rather than a textbook split of one gene's sister copies.
