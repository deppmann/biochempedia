techniques:
  - name: "Herbarium range maps and pollinator observations"
    blurb: >-
      Museum and herbarium records show where each species grows, and the overlap estimates how
      often the two could meet at all. Watching pollinators in a mixed stand and recording whether
      each foraging bout stays on one species gives an estimate of pollinator isolation, since visits are not always the same as pollen moved.
  - name: "Controlled crosses and hand pollinations"
    blurb: >-
      Heterospecific pollen or mates are given alongside conspecific controls, and seed set,
      hybrid survival and hybrid fertility are scored. The comparison with controls turns each
      postmating stage into a measured barrier strength.
  - name: "Laboratory mating trials"
    blurb: >-
      Pairs or groups from chosen populations are given the chance to mate, and the fraction that
      spawn or copulate is compared across combinations. Crossing same and different ecological
      forms from separate sites tests whether isolation follows ecology or ancestry.
  - name: "Genome-wide markers and demographic model comparison"
    blurb: >-
      Thousands of markers, for example from RADseq, are typed in the species and in candidate
      source populations. Explicit histories such as divergence with gene flow from the start, or
      isolation followed by secondary contact, are simulated and compared to see which fits the
      data best.

**Two monkeyflowers of western North America can be crossed by hand to give hybrid seed. One is pink and visited mostly by bumblebees; the other is red and visited mostly by hummingbirds. Yet in the wild almost no genes pass between them. They are two species because barriers that reduce gene flow have evolved between them, and those barriers grew mostly out of adapting to different places and different pollinators.**

That is the main idea of this lesson. In sexually reproducing organisms, **speciation** is the evolution of reproductive barriers that reduce gene flow between populations that once interbred. A population cut off this way becomes a line of descent that evolves separately from others, which, on one influential view covered at the end of this lesson, is what a species is. The barriers need not be complete. Often they are a side effect of **divergent selection**: two populations adapt to different habitats or pollinators, and mating between them falls as a result. The lesson follows that line. First, what the barriers are and how they add up. Then how adaptation builds them, where speciation happens, a faster route through polyploidy, and how biologists recognize the result.

## Speciation is the evolution of barriers to gene flow

A **reproductive barrier** is anything that reduces gene flow between populations. Barriers are sorted by when they act relative to the **zygote**, the fertilized egg.

**Prezygotic** barriers act before a zygote forms:
- *Ecogeographic*: the populations live in different places or habitats and rarely meet.
- *Temporal*: they breed at different times of day or year.
- *Behavioral*: courtship, song, scent or mate preference differ. For plants, pollinator preference plays this role.
- *Mechanical*: reproductive structures do not fit.
- *Gametic*: egg and sperm, or pollen and stigma, are incompatible, so fertilization fails.

**Postzygotic** barriers act after a hybrid zygote forms. In **hybrid inviability** hybrids fail to develop or survive. In **hybrid sterility** they survive but have reduced fertility or none. Sterility often traces to genes that changed separately in each lineage and work badly together in a hybrid. It can also trace to chromosomes that pair poorly at meiosis, covered in [chromosomes and meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/) and [chromosome changes](/lessons/chromosome-changes/).

Each barrier is measured with its own method, against its own control. Ecogeographic isolation comes from range maps and the overlap between them. Pollinator isolation comes from watching pollinators in a mixed stand. Gametic and postzygotic barriers come from controlled crosses, scored against crosses within a species. A barrier strength (RI) is therefore a fraction with a definite denominator, not a general impression.

In the monkeyflowers, Ramsey, Bradshaw and Schemske measured barriers at several stages in 2003: where each species grows, which pollinators visit it, how well the other species' pollen sets seed, and how fertile the hybrids are. Each barrier was partial. Together they were estimated to block nearly all gene flow, and most of the isolation arose before any hybrid formed. One pair of species can be held apart by several barriers at once.

Barriers act in sequence. A pollinator barrier can only act on plants that grow close enough to share pollinators, and hybrid fertility only matters for hybrid seed that was formed. So each barrier works on the gene flow left over by the ones before it, and the fractions that get through multiply. If each barrier's strength (RI) is the fraction of the remaining gene flow it blocks, and the barriers act in sequence and independently:

> **total isolation = 1 - (1 - RI1) x (1 - RI2) x (1 - RI3)**

Do not add the raw strengths. Their sum can be greater than 1, which is meaningless as a fraction of gene flow. A worked example, and an interactive ledger that shows how the order of barriers changes the credit each one gets, are in Going deeper.

A species does not need sterile hybrids. In a 2019 study, Heliconius elevatus and H. pardalinus butleri, butterflies that live together in the Amazon, rarely mated in captivity but produced fertile hybrids. They are held apart by mate choice and ecology instead. Sterile hybrids are one kind of barrier, not a requirement for being a species.

## How barriers evolve: divergent selection

Barriers are not built for a purpose. Selection acts on individuals, and isolation often appears as a side effect of other changes. A well-supported way this happens is **ecological speciation**: isolation evolves as a by-product of adaptation to different environments by divergent selection, selection favoring different traits in different places (see [selection in ecological context](/lessons/selection-in-ecological-context/)). In plants, adaptation to different pollinators and habitats commonly drives the evolution of prezygotic barriers.

The monkeyflowers show how. Mimulus cardinalis grows mainly at lower elevations and M. lewisii mainly higher up, and transplant experiments showed that each is adapted to the elevations where it normally grows. Their flowers suit different pollinators. M. lewisii has wide pink flowers and a small nectar reward, visited mainly by bumblebees. M. cardinalis has red flowers with a narrow tube and a large nectar reward, visited mainly by hummingbirds. Those adaptations are themselves the barriers. Plants adapted to different elevations rarely meet, and a pollinator that favors one flower carries little pollen to the other. Ramsey and colleagues concluded that ecological factors, the products of adaptive divergence, were the main barriers between this pair.

The clearest test of the idea uses evolution that repeated itself. Several small lakes in British Columbia each hold two forms of threespine stickleback, a bottom-feeding benthic form and an open-water limnetic form, and each lake's pair is thought to have arisen independently. If isolation is a by-product of adapting to a habitat, fish of the same form from different lakes should mate readily, and fish of different forms should not, whatever lake they came from. In more than 750 laboratory mating trials with fish from three lakes, Rundle, Schluter and colleagues found that pattern: benthic-limnetic pairs were strongly isolated, while same-form pairs from different lakes were not. Isolation followed ecology, not ancestry. When isolation repeats with the environment, lake after lake, divergent selection, not chance, is the likely cause.

The main case where selection favors isolation itself is **reinforcement**: where two forms meet and their hybrids do poorly, individuals that avoid mating with the other form leave more offspring, so prezygotic isolation grows stronger. Rosser and colleagues read the strong mate choice of the Amazon Heliconius pair as consistent with reinforcement.

Other mechanisms include **mutation-order speciation**, in which separate populations under similar selection fix different advantageous mutations, and drift in small populations ([drift and gene flow](/lessons/drift-gene-flow-explanations/)), which has weaker evidence behind it. Polyploidy, a faster route, has its own section below.

## Geography and mechanism are separate questions

Two questions are easy to merge and should be kept apart. The first is **geography**: where were the populations while they diverged? The second is **mechanism**: what process built the barriers?

On geography, **allopatric** speciation happens in populations that are physically separated. Separation can come by **vicariance**, when a barrier such as a new sea or mountain range divides one population, or by **dispersal**, when a few individuals found a new population somewhere else. **Sympatric** speciation happens without a geographic barrier, within one area. Between the two lie **parapatric** cases, in which neighboring populations diverge along a boundary while still exchanging some genes.

The Isthmus of Panama is a classic vicariance event. As it closed, it divided marine animals into Caribbean and Pacific halves. Even so, one event did not split every pair at one moment. Nancy Knowlton and colleagues studied Alpheus snapping-shrimp pairs split by the isthmus and found that the pairs were probably isolated at different times, some well before the final closure, although ancestral variation explains much of the spread in their genetic distances.

Ecological speciation is a mechanism, not a third geographic mode. It can act in allopatry or in sympatry. The stickleback trials show that isolation followed ecology, but they do not tell you where the forms first diverged.

Present-day coexistence does not tell you either. Two species living together today could have diverged there, or diverged elsewhere and met later, called **secondary contact**. Telling the two histories apart takes genetic data from possible source populations and a comparison of explicit models. For crater-lake cichlids in Nicaragua, Kautt, Machado-Schiaffino and Meyer (2016) did this with genome-wide data. Their analyses supported divergence beginning in sympatry, with admixture from source populations into both lakes shortly before diversification.

## Polyploidy: isolation in one step

Polyploidy is a gain of extra whole chromosome sets, covered in the chromosome changes lesson linked above. It is a different route to a new species, and an important one. Polyploid speciation, in which the entire genome is duplicated, is particularly frequent in plants. It most often happens together with hybridization: a hybrid between two species duplicates its genome, a route called **allopolyploidy**. The duplication gives the new lineage partial reproductive isolation from its parent species at once, in a single generation, with no long period of divergent selection. Wheat, one of the world's major crops, is a polyploid that arose through polyploid speciation. The route is not limited to plants: the clawed frogs (Xenopus and Silurana) include species with different numbers of chromosome sets.

Hybridization without genome duplication can also give rise to a new species, called homoploid hybrid speciation, but how often that happens remains unclear.

## What counts as a species: criteria as evidence

Barriers explain how lineages separate. Recognizing that two lineages have separated is a different job, and the right tool depends on the organisms and the evidence you have.

A **lineage** is a line of ancestors and descendants traced through time. A **metapopulation** is a set of local populations linked by occasional migration. On one influential view, the main species concepts agree that a species is a metapopulation lineage that evolves separately from others. The disagreements come when you try to recognize one.

The **biological species criterion**, associated with Ernst Mayr, asks whether populations are reproductively isolated: do they interbreed in nature and leave fertile offspring? It suits living, sexual organisms that meet. It fails for asexual organisms, which do not interbreed at all, and for fossils, which cannot be tested.

The **ecological criterion** separates species by their **niche**, the set of conditions and resources a population uses. The **phylogenetic criterion** comes in two versions. One asks that a species be **diagnosable** by fixed character differences. The other asks that it be **monophyletic**: a group made up of an ancestor and all its descendants, read from a tree as in [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/). **Morphological** criteria use differences in form, which is what paleontologists and museum taxonomists work with.

Think of the criteria as different instruments pointed at the same object. A thermometer, an infrared camera and a hand on the forehead each detect a fever, and none of them is the fever. Kevin de Queiroz argued in 2007 that the species criteria work this way. Each detects a property, such as intrinsic isolation, a distinct niche, fixed differences or monophyly, that a lineage acquires as it separates. The properties arise at different times, so for young species the criteria disagree. In his unified concept they are lines of evidence, and the more of them agree, the stronger the case that two lineages are evolving separately.

### Design problem

Two cichlid species live together in one small crater lake. Design a study to decide whether they diverged in the lake (sympatric speciation) or diverged elsewhere and arrived separately (secondary contact).

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

1. *Sample widely.* Collect both species in the crater lake and every plausible source population in nearby large lakes and rivers.
2. *Genotype genome-wide.* Use thousands of markers so that ancestry can be traced across the genome, not from one gene.
3. *Ask who is related to whom.* If the two lake species are each other's closest relatives, a single colonization with divergence in the lake is plausible. If each groups with a different outside population, separate arrivals are more likely.
4. *Compare explicit models.* Simulate divergence with gene flow from the start against isolation followed by contact, and compare how well each fits the data.
5. *Test for admixture.* Alleles can enter from outside even when divergence happens in the lake, as Kautt and colleagues found, and a test that finds no admixture does not prove there was none.
6. *Use geology.* The age of the crater sets an upper bound on how long divergence in the lake could have taken.

State the conclusion as support for one model over another, not as proof, and name the histories you could not test.

## Going deeper

### The monkeyflower numbers

In the real monkeyflower study, ecogeographic isolation was estimated at 58.7% from herbarium records, 97.6% of pollinator foraging bouts in a mixed population stayed on one species, and greenhouse crosses gave nearly 50% fewer seeds than conspecific controls. Combined, the measured barriers were estimated to prevent an average of 99.87% of gene flow for that pair. Those numbers belong to those two species; they are not a ranking of barriers across all plants.

### A worked ledger: why order moves the credit

Ramsey, Bradshaw and Schemske combined their monkeyflower barriers this way, using the formula in the first section.

**Worked problem.** These numbers are illustrative, in the style of that study. Three barriers act in this order between two plant species. Each RI is the fraction of the *remaining* gene flow that barrier blocks. Assume the barriers act in sequence and independently.

- Ecogeographic, RI1 = 0.60
- Pollinator, RI2 = 0.90
- Hybrid fertility, RI3 = 0.50

1. *First barrier.* Its absolute contribution is AC1 = 0.60. Gene flow left: 1 - 0.60 = 0.40.
2. *Second barrier.* It acts on the 0.40 left: AC2 = 0.90 x 0.40 = 0.36. Total so far: 0.60 + 0.36 = 0.96.
3. *Third barrier.* It acts on the 0.04 left: AC3 = 0.50 x 0.04 = 0.02. Total isolation: 0.96 + 0.02 = **0.98**.
4. *Check.* Remaining gene flow is the product of what each barrier lets through: 0.40 x 0.10 x 0.50 = 0.02, which equals 1 - 0.98.
5. *Order matters for credit.* Swap the first two barriers and the pollinator barrier gets AC = 0.90, the ecogeographic barrier 0.60 x 0.10 = 0.06, and hybrid fertility again 0.02. The total is still 0.98, but the credit moves. Here the early, weaker barrier gets more credit than the later, stronger one, because the later one has less gene flow left to block, so credit depends on position as well as strength.

Here the raw strengths sum to 2.00, which is why they cannot simply be added. The multiplication also rests on the assumptions above, and Sobel and Chen (2014) proposed a unified linear index of barrier strength because earlier indices used different null expectations and denominators.

### Panama and the snapping shrimps

The Isthmus of Panama finished closing about 2.8 million years ago by the usual estimate, although some studies argue for a much earlier closure. Biologists used the many species pairs split by it to calibrate molecular clocks, on the assumption that every pair split when the land closed. Knowlton and colleagues tested that assumption in seven pairs of Alpheus snapping shrimps, comparing biochemical divergence with how readily shrimps from opposite oceans would pair in the laboratory. The comparison indicated that isolation was staggered rather than simultaneous. A 2009 follow-up by Hurt, Anker and Knowlton used several nuclear genes and found that ancestral variation, present before the split, explained much of the spread in mitochondrial distances, and that two pairs had probably diverged well before the final closure.

### Methods behind the cichlid answer

Genome-wide markers for studies like the cichlid one are often typed by RADseq, which reads thousands of sites spread across the genome. Admixture statistics need care, because drift in one population can hide an admixture signal, so a test that finds no admixture does not prove there was none.

### Why polyploidy is common in plants

Rieseberg and Willis suggested that polyploid speciation may be frequent in plants because polyploid plants often show ecological differentiation, local dispersal, high fecundity, a perennial life history, and self-fertilization or asexual reproduction.
