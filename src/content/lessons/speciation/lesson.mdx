      By the early 2000s biologists had more than a dozen named species concepts, and arguments
      about which one was right could run for years without settling a single case. De Queiroz, a
      zoologist at the Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History, took a different route in
      a 2007 paper in Systematic Biology. He pointed out that the concepts mostly agree on what a

      When the Isthmus of Panama closed, it split marine populations into a Caribbean half and a
      Pacific half. That looks like a perfect experiment in vicariance: many pairs of species, one

techniques:
  - name: "Herbarium range maps and pollinator observations"
    blurb: >-
      Museum and herbarium records show where each species grows, and the overlap estimates how
      often the two could meet at all. Watching pollinators in a mixed stand and recording whether
      each foraging bout stays on one species gives an estimate of pollinator isolation, since visits are not always the same as pollen moved.
  - name: "Controlled crosses and hand pollinations"
    blurb: >-
      Heterospecific pollen or mates are given alongside conspecific controls, and seed set,
      hybrid survival and hybrid fertility are scored. The comparison with controls turns each
      postmating stage into a measured barrier strength.
  - name: "Laboratory mating trials"
    blurb: >-
      Pairs or groups from chosen populations are given the chance to mate, and the fraction that
      spawn or copulate is compared across combinations. Crossing same and different ecological
      forms from separate sites tests whether isolation follows ecology or ancestry.
  - name: "Genome-wide markers and demographic model comparison"
    blurb: >-
      Thousands of markers, for example from RADseq, are typed in the species and in candidate
      source populations. Explicit histories such as divergence with gene flow from the start, or
      isolation followed by secondary contact, are simulated and compared to see which fits the
      data best.

**Two monkeyflowers of western North America, one pink and visited mostly by bumblebees, the other red and visited mostly by hummingbirds, can be crossed by hand to give hybrid seed. Yet in the wild almost no genes pass between them. They count as two species because barriers that reduce gene flow have evolved between them, and those barriers grew mostly out of adapting to different places and different pollinators.**

> **The one idea.** **Gene flow**, the movement of genes between populations through mating, is whatever leaks through a series of barriers, so the fractions that get through multiply. Several partial barriers can therefore block far more gene flow than any one of them, and they often grow as side effects of adapting to different places and partners.
>
> *Scope:* sexually reproducing organisms, and barriers that act one after another and independently. The rule measures how much gene flow is blocked. It does not say where the populations were while they diverged, and it does not define a species: lineages can stay distinct despite some gene flow (see the criteria section), and polyploidy can raise a strong barrier in one step.

In sexually reproducing organisms, **speciation** is the evolution of reproductive barriers that reduce gene flow between populations that once interbred.

## Measuring barriers, and why their leaks multiply

The first film showed leaks multiplying. Left out: where each gate's number comes from, and which gate gets the credit.

A **reproductive barrier** is anything that reduces gene flow between populations. Its strength, **RI** (reproductive isolation), is the share of the gene flow reaching that barrier that it stops, measured against a control. For example, if range maps show two species growing together only 40% as often as they would if spread at random, the habitat barrier's RI is 1 − 0.40 = 0.60. Each barrier has its own control: range maps for habitat, pollinator watches in a mixed stand for pollinators, and, for later barriers, crosses between species scored against crosses within a species.

The film's 99.87% is the estimate of Ramsey, Bradshaw and Schemske (2003), combining barriers across the life cycle: range overlap, pollinator isolation, **pollen precedence** (a plant's own pollen fertilizing ahead of the other species') and several parts of hybrid fitness, from germination to fertility. It is the mean of their estimates for the two directions of gene flow, not a sign that the genomes never mix (Going deeper).

The ledger uses illustrative numbers in the style of that study. Three barriers act in this order, each independently: habitat RI = 0.60, pollinators RI = 0.90, hybrid fertility RI = 0.50.

| Barrier, in order | RI | Gene flow reaching it | Blocked here (its credit) | Gene flow left |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Habitat | 0.60 | 1.00 | 0.60 | 0.40 |
| Pollinators | 0.90 | 0.40 | 0.36 | 0.04 |
| Hybrid fertility | 0.50 | 0.04 | 0.02 | 0.02 |

The credits add up to the total isolation: 0.60 + 0.36 + 0.02 = **0.98**. As a formula, the one idea reads:

> **total isolation = 1 − (1 − RI1) × (1 − RI2) × (1 − RI3)**, here 1 − 0.40 × 0.10 × 0.50 = 0.98

Credits can be added because each is already weighted by the gene flow left for it; raw strengths cannot, since they sum to 2.00, more than all the gene flow there is. Habitat gets more credit (0.60) than pollinators (0.36), although pollinators have the larger RI (0.90), because habitat acts first and leaves pollinators only 0.40 to block.

**Predict.** Swap the first two barriers, so pollinators act before habitat. Does total isolation change? Which barrier now gets the most credit?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The total stays 0.98, because multiplication does not care about order. The credit moves: pollinators now stop 0.90, habitat only 0.60 × 0.10 = 0.06, and hybrid fertility still 0.02. A barrier's credit depends on its position as well as its strength, so "which barrier matters most" has an answer only for a stated order. In real pairs the life cycle sets that order.

## Kinds of barrier: before and after the zygote

The first film drew a line at the **zygote**, the fertilized egg: **prezygotic** barriers act before it, **postzygotic** barriers after a hybrid zygote forms. Common textbook categories include these seven:

| Barrier | Acts | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Ecogeographic (habitat) | Prezygotic | The populations live in different places or habitats and rarely meet |
| Temporal | Prezygotic | They breed at different times of day or year |
| Behavioral | Prezygotic | Courtship, song, scent or mate preference differ; in plants, pollinator preference plays this role |
| Mechanical | Prezygotic | Reproductive structures do not fit |
| Gametic | Prezygotic | Egg and sperm, or pollen and stigma, are incompatible, so fertilization fails |
| Hybrid inviability | Postzygotic | Hybrids fail to develop or survive |
| Hybrid sterility | Postzygotic | Hybrids survive but have reduced fertility or none |

Sterility often traces to genes that changed separately in each lineage and work badly together in a hybrid. It can also trace to chromosomes that pair poorly at meiosis, covered in [chromosomes and meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/) and [chromosome changes](/lessons/chromosome-changes/).

In the monkeyflowers the strongest early gates were habitat and pollinators. Were those gates built to keep the species apart, or did they come free with adaptation? The second film tests the idea with fish.

## How barriers evolve: divergent selection

The second film showed benthic-limnetic stickleback isolation following ecology lake after lake, the mark of **divergent selection**, selection that favours different traits in different places.

For the monkeyflowers, transplant experiments showed that each species is adapted to the elevations where it normally grows, and Ramsey and colleagues concluded that ecological factors, the products of adaptive divergence (adaptation pulling the two apart), were the main barriers between this pair. More broadly, in plants adaptation to different pollinators and habitats commonly drives the evolution of prezygotic barriers ([selection in ecological context](/lessons/selection-in-ecological-context/)).

The stickleback trials did not identify which traits drove the mate preferences, and they point to divergent selection as the likely cause of the isolation but not to where the forms first diverged, the next section's question.

Selection can also favour isolation itself. In **reinforcement**, two forms meet and their hybrids do poorly, so individuals that avoid mating with the other form leave more offspring, and prezygotic isolation grows stronger.

Two Heliconius butterflies that fly together in the Amazon are held apart by mate choice and ecology, not hybrid sterility (figure), and Rosser and colleagues (2019) read that strong mate choice as consistent with reinforcement.

Two other mechanisms build barriers without divergent selection between habitats. In **mutation-order speciation**, separate populations under similar selection fix different advantageous mutations, and those mutations work badly together when the populations meet, so hybrids suffer. **Drift**, chance change in gene frequencies in small populations ([drift and gene flow](/lessons/drift-gene-flow-explanations/)), has weaker evidence behind it as a cause of speciation.

## Geography and mechanism are separate questions

**Geography** asks where the populations were while they diverged; **mechanism** asks what process built the barriers.

**Allopatric** speciation happens in populations that are physically separated. Separation can come by **vicariance**, when a barrier such as a new sea or mountain range divides one population, or by **dispersal**, when a few individuals found a new population somewhere else. **Sympatric** speciation happens without a geographic barrier, within one area. (Cases in between are in Going deeper.)

The Isthmus of Panama is a classic vicariance event, yet it did not split every pair at one moment: Nancy Knowlton and colleagues found that Alpheus snapping-shrimp pairs divided by it were probably isolated at different times, some well before the final closure (Going deeper).

**Ecological speciation**, isolation as a by-product of adaptation, is a mechanism, not a third geographic mode: it can act in allopatry or in sympatry.

## Polyploidy: isolation in one step

**Polyploidy** is a gain of extra whole chromosome sets ([chromosome changes](/lessons/chromosome-changes/)). Polyploid speciation, in which the entire genome is duplicated, is an important route to a new species, particularly frequent in plants. It most often happens together with hybridization: a hybrid between two species duplicates its genome, a route called **allopolyploidy**. The duplication gives the new lineage partial reproductive isolation from its parent species in a single generation, with no long period of divergent selection. The reason is chromosome number: a cross back to a parent gives offspring with unmatched chromosome sets, which pair poorly at meiosis, so those offspring are often sterile.

Wheat, a major crop, is a polyploid that arose through polyploid speciation. The route is not limited to plants: the clawed frogs (Xenopus and Silurana) include species with different numbers of chromosome sets. Hybridization without genome duplication can also give rise to a new species, called homoploid hybrid speciation, but how often that happens remains unclear.

## What counts as a species: criteria as evidence

Barriers explain how lineages separate. Recognizing that they have separated is a different job, and the tool depends on the organisms and evidence.

A **lineage** is a line of ancestors and descendants traced through time. A **metapopulation** is a set of local populations linked by occasional migration. A metapopulation lineage, then, is a series of connected populations, ancestors to descendants, that evolves as one unit through time. On one influential view, the main species concepts agree that a species is such a lineage, evolving separately from others; they disagree about how to recognize one.

The **biological species criterion**, associated with Ernst Mayr, treats species as groups of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups. Occasional fertile hybrids do not by themselves erase that isolation if the populations stay distinct, as in the Heliconius pair. The **ecological criterion** separates species by their **niche**, the set of conditions and resources a population uses. The **phylogenetic criterion** comes in two versions. One asks that a species be **diagnosable** by fixed character differences. The other asks that it be **monophyletic**: a group made up of an ancestor and all its descendants, read from a tree as in [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/). **Morphological** criteria use differences in form.

**Predict.** You have only fossils of two kinds of animal, or only an asexual microbe. Which criterion cannot be used in either case?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The biological criterion. It suits living, sexual organisms that meet. It fails for asexual organisms, which do not interbreed at all, and for fossils, which cannot be tested. Differences in form are what paleontologists and museum taxonomists work with.

</details>

Think of the criteria as instruments pointed at one object. A thermometer, an infrared camera and a hand on the forehead each detect a fever, and none of them is the fever. Kevin de Queiroz argued in 2007 that species criteria work this way: each detects a property, such as intrinsic isolation, a distinct niche, fixed differences or monophyly, that a lineage acquires as it separates. The properties arise at different times, so for young species the criteria disagree. In his unified concept they are lines of evidence, and the more agree, the stronger the case for separate evolution.

### Design problem

Design a study to decide whether two cichlid species in one small crater lake diverged there (sympatric speciation) or elsewhere, arriving separately (secondary contact).

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

1. *Sample widely.* Collect both species in the crater lake and every plausible source population in nearby large lakes and rivers.
2. *Genotype genome-wide.* Use thousands of markers so that ancestry can be traced across the genome, not from one gene.
3. *Ask who is related to whom.* If the two lake species are each other's closest relatives, a single colonization with divergence in the lake is plausible. If each groups with a different outside population, separate arrivals are more likely.
4. *Compare explicit models.* Simulate divergence with gene flow from the start against isolation followed by contact, and compare how well each fits the data.
5. *Test for admixture.* Alleles can enter from outside even when divergence happens in the lake, as Kautt and colleagues found.
6. *Use geology.* The age of the crater sets an upper bound on how long divergence in the lake could have taken.

State the conclusion as support for one model over another, not as proof, and name the histories you could not test.

## Going deeper

### The monkeyflower numbers

M. lewisii has wide pink flowers with a small nectar reward and is visited mainly by bumblebees; M. cardinalis has red, narrow-tubed flowers with a large nectar reward and is visited mainly by hummingbirds. In the study, ecogeographic isolation was estimated at 58.7% from herbarium records, 97.6% of pollinator foraging bouts in a mixed population stayed on one species, and greenhouse crosses gave nearly 50% fewer seeds than crosses with pollen from the same species. These numbers, and the 99.87% total, belong to those two species; they are not a ranking of barriers across all plants. The pollinator figure counts foraging bouts, and visits are not always the same as pollen moved.

### An isolation index is not genome-wide separation

The 99.87% leaves about 0.13% of potential gene flow unblocked. Treated, only as a toy model, as a steady migration rate each generation, gene flow that size could make neutral regions of the genome nearly alike in large populations. Species with strong isolation can stay distinct because selection keeps the barrier genes and adapted traits apart; Heliconius elevatus, for example, has persisted as a hybrid lineage despite ongoing gene flow with one parent species. An isolation index is not itself a migration rate, and populations need not be at equilibrium, so this is an illustration, not a claim about the monkeyflowers.

### Comparing barrier strengths across studies

Ramsey, Bradshaw and Schemske used the sequential method of the ledger above. Sobel and Chen (2014) proposed a unified linear index of barrier strength because earlier indices used different null expectations and denominators, and barrier strengths can be compared only when indices share both.

### Between allopatry and sympatry

Between the two geographic modes lie **parapatric** cases, in which neighbouring populations diverge along a boundary while still exchanging some genes.

### Panama and the snapping shrimps

The Isthmus of Panama finished closing about 2.8 million years ago by the usual estimate, although some studies argue for a much earlier closure. Biologists used species pairs split by it to calibrate molecular clocks, assuming every pair split when the land closed. Knowlton and colleagues tested that in seven pairs of Alpheus snapping shrimps, comparing biochemical divergence with how readily shrimps from opposite oceans would pair in the laboratory; isolation was staggered rather than simultaneous. A 2009 follow-up by Hurt, Anker and Knowlton used several nuclear genes and found that ancestral variation, present before the split, explained much of the spread in mitochondrial distances, and that two pairs had probably diverged well before the final closure.

### Methods behind the cichlid answer

Genome-wide markers for studies like the cichlid one are often typed by RADseq, which reads thousands of sites spread across the genome. Admixture statistics need care, because drift in one population can hide an admixture signal, so a test that finds no admixture does not prove there was none.

### Why polyploidy is common in plants

Rieseberg and Willis suggested that polyploid speciation may be frequent in plants because polyploid plants often show ecological differentiation, local dispersal, high fecundity, a perennial life history, and self-fertilization or asexual reproduction.
