    contribution: >-
      Wrote down the patch-occupancy model in which a region's occupied fraction of habitat patches
      rises by colonization and falls by local extinction, so that a species can persist regionally
      while every local population is temporary.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Levins, Some demographic and genetic consequences of environmental heterogeneity for biological control, Bulletin of the Entomological Society of America 15:237-240 (1969)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Levins published the model in 1969 in a short paper in the Bulletin of the Entomological
      Society of America. The
      paper's title frames the question in those practical terms: what does a patchy, variable
      environment mean for the control of a pest? His move was to stop following the number of
      individuals and follow the number of occupied patches instead. Each patch is either occupied
      or empty. Occupied patches send out colonists that can found populations in empty ones, and
      each occupied patch has some chance of losing its population. Written that way, the whole
      regional population obeys one equation with two rates, and its long-run state depends only on
      their ratio. The model is deliberately simple: identical patches, all equally reachable, with
      no attention to how many animals each holds. Levins is usually credited with the word
      metapopulation. What the model made possible was a way to ask whether a species persists in a
      region without requiring that any one local population lasts.

    story: >-
      Pulliam's 1988 paper in the American Naturalist, titled Sources, sinks, and population
      regulation, asked what happens when a species lives in habitats of unequal quality that are
      joined by movement. In his model, some habitats are sources: there, local births exceed local
      deaths, and the surplus emigrates. Other habitats are sinks: there, local
      reproduction does not make up for local mortality, and without arrivals the population would
      dwindle away. Immigrants from sources can keep sinks occupied indefinitely. The idea sounds
      modest, but it breaks a habit of field ecology. Seeing a species in a place, even in large
      numbers, does not show that the place can sustain it. To label a habitat a source or a sink
      you need its birth and death rates and some measure of movement in and out, which are much
      harder to collect than counts. What the paper made possible was a way to explain occupied
      habitat that cannot replace itself, and a warning about inferring habitat quality from
      abundance alone.

    story: >-
      Field surveys produce two kinds of zero. A site can be empty, or the species can be there and
      simply not be found on the day. A single visit cannot tell these apart, so a survey that
      counts every non-detection as an absence underestimates how many sites are occupied.
      MacKenzie and colleagues, writing in Ecology in 2002, built a model in which each site has a
      probability of being occupied and, if occupied, a probability of being detected on each visit.
      Visiting the same sites several times while their occupancy stays fixed gives histories such
      as 010 or 000, and the pattern of those histories lets both probabilities be estimated
      together. They illustrated the method with American toads and spring peepers at 32 wetlands in
      Maryland surveyed in 2000, and estimated toad occupancy at 0.49. Later work by the same group
      extended the approach across seasons, to estimate colonization and local extinction without
      mistaking missed detections for losses. What the method made possible was patch-occupancy
      data that could be trusted enough to test metapopulation models.

techniques:
  - name: "Mark-recapture"
    blurb: >-
      Animals are caught, marked or photographed so that each can be recognised, and released; a
      later sample records how many are already marked. Under closure, equal catchability and
      correct matching, the marked fraction of the second sample estimates the marked fraction of
      the whole population.
  - name: "Repeat-visit occupancy surveys"
    blurb: >-
      Each site is visited several times within a period when its occupancy is assumed not to
      change, and detection or non-detection is recorded each time. The detection histories let a
      model estimate the probability of detection and the fraction of sites occupied separately, provided the species is never recorded where it is absent and detection is independent among sites, with any variation in it modelled.
  - name: "Corridor experiments with area controls"
    blurb: >-
      Habitat fragments are connected or left isolated, and some fragments receive about the same added
      habitat area in a form that does not connect them. Comparing the treatments separates the
      effect of connection from the effect of extra area.
  - name: "Genetic assignment of dispersers"
    blurb: >-
      Individuals are genotyped and assigned, with some uncertainty, to the patch they most likely came from, so that departure,
      straight-line dispersal distance and settlement can be estimated for successful dispersers without following each animal.

**Visit a pond three times, hear no toads, and you still cannot say the pond is empty.** Walk into a meadow full of butterflies and you still cannot say that population replaces itself. Both problems come from the same fact: many species live in patches, move between them, and are hard to see. This lesson asks how a species persists across patches that keep losing their populations, and how you count animals you mostly do not observe.

## The idea: populations in patches

Picture a string of ponds across farmland. Each pond holds a **local population**, the individuals that live and breed there, and the fields between are hostile ground. A **metapopulation** is a set of such local populations in separate patches, linked by dispersal.

**Dispersal** is movement from where an individual was born, or last bred, to where it breeds next. It has three stages that can be measured separately: **departure**, **transfer** across the landscape, and **settlement**. They need not share causes. DiLeo and colleagues (2022) used genetic assignment to reconstruct dispersal of the Glanville fritillary butterfly across five years and 232 habitat patches in the Åland Islands of Finland. The factors that explained departure explained little of the variation in how far butterflies went, and they found no effect of distance on survival after settling. A single "dispersal rate" would have hidden both results.

Dispersal is not **migration**, the seasonal round trip that many birds and whales make between the same areas; in some insects, such as painted lady butterflies, the cycle is completed over several generations. A migrating warbler returns to the same areas each year; a dispersing one changes where it breeds, and it may move again later. Dispersal also carries genes between patches, the gene flow treated in [drift, gene flow and competing explanations](/lessons/drift-gene-flow-explanations/), with the arithmetic in [population genetics](/lessons/population-genetics/).

This lesson builds on [demography](/lessons/demography/), where survival estimates appear, and on [population growth](/lessons/population-growth/), where a growth rate lambda above or below 1 decides whether a population grows. Here the question moves up a level: can a region keep a species when no single pond does?

## Levins: persistence as colonization balancing extinction

Richard Levins (1969) answered by ignoring how many animals each patch holds. Each patch is simply occupied or empty. Let p be the fraction of patches occupied.

> **dp/dt = c p (1 - p) - e p**

The first term is colonization. Colonists come from occupied patches (p) and succeed only where a patch is empty (1 - p), at a rate c per year. The second term is local extinction: each occupied patch loses its population at rate e per year. Set dp/dt to zero and divide by p:

> **p* = 1 - e/c, which is positive only when c is greater than e**

The analogy is a row of lamps that fail at random and get relit from lamps still burning. Lamps are always going out, yet the row stays partly lit as long as relighting outpaces failure. The model's assumptions are strong: many identical patches, all equally reachable from every other, and constant rates. Real networks have big and small patches, near and far ones.

**Worked problem 1 (illustrative numbers).** Suppose a network has c = 0.4 per year and e = 0.1 per year.

1. *Equilibrium.* p* = 1 - 0.1/0.4 = 1 - 0.25 = **0.75**. Three patches in four are occupied at any time, though which ones keeps changing.
2. *Raise extinction.* Now let e rise to 0.4 per year, equal to c. Then p* = 1 - 0.4/0.4 = **0**, and the network is lost.
3. *It is lost slowly.* With c = e = 0.4, the equation becomes dp/dt = -0.4 p^2. Starting from 0.75, occupancy falls at 0.4 x 0.75^2 = 0.225 per year at first, and the solution 1/p = 1/0.75 + 0.4t gives p = 0.19 after 10 years.

That third step is the practical warning. A network at or just past its threshold can keep occupied patches for years, because decline is slowest near the threshold. Seeing animals in some patches does not show the region is persisting.

## Sources, sinks and corridors

Levins treats every patch as equal. H. Ronald Pulliam (1988), in a different kind of model that follows births and deaths rather than occupied patches, let habitats differ. In a **source**, local births exceed local deaths, and the surplus emigrates. In a **sink**, local reproduction does not make up for local mortality, and the population persists only because immigrants keep arriving. So a sink can be full of animals. Abundance does not label a source; birth and death rates do. This is also why a species can be found outside the conditions it can sustain, a point taken up in [niches and distributions](/lessons/niches-and-distributions/).

<LessonFigure src="/figures/spatial-populations/source-sink-flow.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a modest source patch sends a gold arrow of immigrants to a more crowded sink patch; two small balances show births outweighing deaths in the source and deaths outweighing births in the sink." caption="A source exports its surplus; a sink persists only because immigrants keep arriving, so it can be full of animals. Abundance does not label a source; birth and death rates do." />

Two ways that movement keeps a patch occupied are often confused. The **rescue effect** (a term from Brown and Kodric-Brown, 1977) is immigration into a population that is still present, which lowers its chance of dying out. **Recolonization** refounds a patch after its population has gone. In model terms, rescue lowers e (the basic Levins model leaves rescue out), and recolonization is what the colonization term, c p(1 - p), describes.

Movement also brings a measurement trap. Survival estimated by traditional mark-recapture is **apparent survival**, the chance an animal is alive *and still in the study area*. An animal that emigrated permanently looks exactly like one that died.

If connection rescues populations, linking patches with **corridors**, strips of habitat between them, should reduce losses. Experiments support that in particular systems, as the design problem shows. Connectivity is not good in every model, though: in simulations of 21 competing bird-like species, Schippers and colleagues (2015) found that greater connectivity sped up diversity loss. That is a model result, a caution about extrapolation, not a field finding.

## Counting what you cannot see: mark-recapture and occupancy

Every quantity above (c, e, births, deaths) has to be measured on animals that hide. Births and deaths need data on individuals; c and e need reliable records of which patches are occupied.

**Worked problem 2 (invented data).** You catch and mark 50 animals on the first occasion (n1 = 50), release them, and later catch 90 (n2 = 90), of which 20 are marked (m2 = 20). Assume the population is **closed** (no births, deaths or movement between occasions), every animal is **equally catchable**, and marks are neither lost nor misread.

1. *The logic.* The marked fraction in the second sample, 20/90, estimates the marked fraction of the population, 50/N.
2. *Lincoln-Petersen.* N = n1 x n2 / m2 = 50 x 90 / 20 = 4,500 / 20 = **225 animals**.
3. *Chapman.* The plain estimator runs high when samples are small, so Chapman's version adds one to each count:

> **N = (n1 + 1)(n2 + 1) / (m2 + 1) - 1**

N = (51 x 91) / 21 - 1 = 4,641 / 21 - 1 = 221 - 1 = **220 animals**.

The overlap is 20, not 90. Using the whole second catch in the denominator is an easy slip to make. And 220 is an estimate, not a head count. Each assumption can fail. Trap-happy animals inflate m2 and shrink N; trap-shy ones do the reverse. Photo identification, as in a 2018 survey of free-roaming dogs in Quito, Ecuador, avoids handling but can mismatch individuals.

For many species you cannot even mark individuals, only record whether a site has the species. **Occupancy** is the fraction of sites occupied. The trouble is that a non-detection is not an absence. MacKenzie and colleagues (2002) visited sites repeatedly while occupancy was assumed fixed, and recorded histories such as 010 (found on the second visit only) or 000.

**Worked problem 3 (illustrative numbers).** A pond is occupied, and each visit detects the species with probability 0.5, independently.

1. *Three misses.* P(000) = 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.5^3 = **0.125**. One occupied pond in eight looks empty.
2. *Harder to detect.* At 0.3 per visit, each miss has probability 0.7, so P(000) = 0.7^3 = **0.343**. One in three.
3. *The meaning.* A 000 history is consistent with an empty pond or an occupied one missed three times. The pattern of histories across many sites lets the model estimate both probabilities. In their Maryland data from 2000, MacKenzie and colleagues estimated that American toads occupied 0.49 of the 29 wetlands analysed, out of 32 surveyed. Across seasons, treating 000 as absence also distorts turnover: a missed population is scored as a local extinction, and finding it again later is scored as a colonization, so both e and c are biased. Dynamic occupancy models correct for this.

### Design problem

Gonzalez and colleagues (1998) worked with moss growing on limestone rocks in Derbyshire, England, home to tiny mites, springtails and other microarthropods. They cut the moss into patch systems of three kinds: patches joined by moss corridors; isolated patches; and patches with **pseudocorridors**, corridors of similar moss area broken by a small strip of bare rock, so with no continuous connection. After six months, species losses were 14.5% in connected landscapes and 41% in the isolated and pseudocorridor treatments. What can you conclude, what can you not, and how would you improve the study?

<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**What the pseudocorridor buys.** A corridor adds habitat as well as connection. The pseudocorridor adds similar moss area without the connection, and it lost species like the isolated patches. So the difference reflects connection, not area.

**What it is consistent with.** Fewer losses where patches connect fits a rescue effect: immigrants arriving through corridors propped up dwindling populations. But no animal's movement was tracked, so recolonization after loss, or different conditions in connected moss, are not ruled out.

**Improvements.**
1. Mark animals, or use genetic assignment, to measure movement through corridors.
2. Record each species in each patch at several times, to separate fewer extinctions from more recolonizations.
3. Run it longer than six months, and replicate across rocks and seasons.
4. Keep the scale. This is moss on rocks, centimetres to metres, with animals of short generation time. It says nothing directly about forests or mammals.

**A larger, longer contrast.** Damschen and colleagues (2019) arranged restored longleaf pine savanna fragments at the Savannah River Site, South Carolina, in blocks, one fragment per block connected by a corridor and the others not, followed the plants for 18 years and analysed 239 species. They reported annual colonization 5% higher and annual extinction 2% lower in connected fragments, and nearly 14% more species after almost two decades. Yearly censuses of every species let that design estimate colonization and extinction separately, which the moss study could not, and its length shows that small yearly differences accumulate. It also stays in its system: one site, plants, one corridor geometry.
