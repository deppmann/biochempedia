scientists:
  - name: "Richard Levins"
    contribution: >-
      Wrote down the patch-occupancy model in which a region's occupied fraction of habitat patches
      rises by colonization and falls by local extinction, so that a species can persist regionally
      while every local population is temporary.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Levins, Some demographic and genetic consequences of environmental heterogeneity for biological control, Bulletin of the Entomological Society of America 15:237-240 (1969)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Levins published the model in 1969 in a short paper in the Bulletin of the Entomological
      Society of America. The
      paper's title frames the question in those practical terms: what does a patchy, variable
      environment mean for the control of a pest? His move was to stop following the number of
      individuals and follow the number of occupied patches instead. Each patch is either occupied
      or empty. Occupied patches send out colonists that can found populations in empty ones, and
      each occupied patch has some chance of losing its population. Written that way, the whole
      regional population obeys one equation with two rates, and its long-run state depends only on
      their ratio. The model is deliberately simple: identical patches, all equally reachable, with
      no attention to how many animals each holds. Levins is usually credited with the word
      metapopulation. What the model made possible was a way to ask whether a species persists in a
      region without requiring that any one local population lasts.
  - name: "H. Ronald Pulliam"
    contribution: >-
      Described in 1988 how source habitats, where births exceed deaths, can export individuals that
      maintain populations in sink habitats, where local reproduction cannot balance local mortality.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Pulliam, Sources, sinks, and population regulation, American Naturalist 132:652-661 (1988)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Pulliam's 1988 paper in the American Naturalist, titled Sources, sinks, and population
      regulation, asked what happens when a species lives in habitats of unequal quality that are
      joined by movement. In his model, some habitats are sources: there, local births exceed local
      deaths, and the surplus emigrates. Other habitats are sinks: there, local
      reproduction does not make up for local mortality, and without arrivals the population would
      dwindle away. Immigrants from sources can keep sinks occupied indefinitely. The idea sounds
      modest, but it breaks a habit of field ecology. Seeing a species in a place, even in large
      numbers, does not show that the place can sustain it. To label a habitat a source or a sink
      you need its birth and death rates and some measure of movement in and out, which are much
      harder to collect than counts. What the paper made possible was a way to explain occupied
      habitat that cannot replace itself, and a warning about inferring habitat quality from
      abundance alone.
  - name: "C. B. Huffaker"
    contribution: >-
      Showed in laboratory arrays of oranges that a predatory mite and its herbivorous prey mite
      kept interacting through three successive oscillations when food was spread out and movement
      was partly obstructed.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Huffaker, Experimental studies on predation: dispersion factors and predator-prey oscillations, Hilgardia 27:343-383 (1958)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Huffaker was an entomologist in biological control at the University of California's
      experiment station in Berkeley, according to the author record of his 1958 paper in
      Hilgardia. The question was whether a predator and its prey could keep going together in a
      confined laboratory universe. His prey was the six-spotted mite, Eotetranychus sexmaculatus,
      which fed on oranges, and his predator was another mite, Typhlodromus occidentalis. He
      changed the spatial arrangement of the food and added partial barriers to movement, so that
      the prey and the predator could not reach every orange equally easily. In the most complex
      arrangement, the two species went through three successive oscillations of rising and falling
      numbers. The run did end. The result is easy to overstate: it is a finite run in one preparation, not proof that
      the species could coexist forever. What it made possible was an experimental case that
      spatial structure can prolong a predator-prey interaction.
  - name: "Darryl MacKenzie"
    contribution: >-
      With colleagues, published in 2002 a method that uses repeated visits to estimate the fraction
      of sites a species occupies while separately estimating the chance of detecting it.
    profileSourceAnchor: "MacKenzie, Nichols, Lachman, Droege, Royle and Langtimm, Estimating site occupancy rates when detection probabilities are less than one, Ecology 83:2248-2255 (2002)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Field surveys produce two kinds of zero. A site can be empty, or the species can be there and
      simply not be found on the day. A single visit cannot tell these apart, so a survey that
      counts every non-detection as an absence underestimates how many sites are occupied.
      MacKenzie and colleagues, writing in Ecology in 2002, built a model in which each site has a
      probability of being occupied and, if occupied, a probability of being detected on each visit.
      Visiting the same sites several times while their occupancy stays fixed gives histories such
      as 010 or 000, and the pattern of those histories lets both probabilities be estimated
      together. They illustrated the method with American toads and spring peepers at 32 wetlands in
      Maryland surveyed in 2000, and estimated toad occupancy at 0.49. Later work by the same group
      extended the approach across seasons, to estimate colonization and local extinction without
      mistaking missed detections for losses. What the method made possible was patch-occupancy
      data that could be trusted enough to test metapopulation models.

techniques:
  - name: "Mark-recapture"
    blurb: >-
      Animals are caught, marked or photographed so that each can be recognised, and released; a
      later sample records how many are already marked. Under closure, equal catchability and
      correct matching, the marked fraction of the second sample estimates the marked fraction of
      the whole population.
  - name: "Repeat-visit occupancy surveys"
    blurb: >-
      Each site is visited several times within a period when its occupancy is assumed not to
      change, and detection or non-detection is recorded each time. The detection histories let a
      model estimate the probability of detection and the fraction of sites occupied separately, provided the species is never recorded where it is absent and detection is independent among sites and among visits, with any variation in it modelled.
  - name: "Corridor experiments with area controls"
    blurb: >-
      Habitat fragments are connected or left isolated, and some fragments receive about the same added
      habitat area in a form that does not connect them. Comparing the treatments separates the
      effect of connection from the effect of extra area.
  - name: "Genetic assignment of dispersers"
    blurb: >-
      Individuals are genotyped and assigned, with some uncertainty, to the patch they most likely came from, so that departure,
      straight-line dispersal distance and settlement can be estimated for successful dispersers without following each animal.

    choices:
      - "Outbound migration, stopover and return"
      - "Birth, growth and first reproduction"
      - "Departure, transfer and settlement"
      - "Colonization, local extinction and rescue"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Dispersal is leaving a place, moving across the landscape and settling in a new place, and
      the factors that predict one stage need not predict another. Birth, growth and first
      reproduction are life-history stages of an individual, not stages of movement. Outbound
      migration, stopover and return describe a seasonal round trip, which is migration rather than
      dispersal. Colonization, local extinction and rescue are patch-level outcomes that dispersal
      can cause, not the stages of an individual's movement.
  - stem: "In the Levins model, a patch network has a colonization rate c = 0.5 per year and an extinction rate e = 0.2 per year. What fraction of patches is occupied at equilibrium?"
    choices:
      - "0.30"
      - "0.40"
      - "0.71"
      - "0.60"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The equilibrium is p* = 1 - e/c = 1 - 0.2/0.5 = 1 - 0.4 = 0.60. The value 0.40 is e/c itself,
      the fraction left empty rather than the fraction occupied. The value 0.30 subtracts the rates
      (c - e), which has units of per year and is not a fraction of patches. The value 0.71 is
      c/(c + e), which treats the model as if each patch simply switched states at fixed rates
      without colonists having to come from occupied patches.

    choices:

      - "A sink kept occupied by immigrants"
      - "A stable population at its capacity"
      - "An empty patch refounded every year"

      Births below deaths means the marsh cannot replace itself; immigrants hatched elsewhere keep

      are defined by vital rates. A self-sustaining population at capacity can show births below deaths when immigrants push it above capacity, but its births would catch up with deaths when numbers are low; here they fall short even then. An empty patch refounded every year does not fit a marsh that held breeders and produced young in every one of the eight years.

    rationale: >-
      Each visit misses the frog with probability 1 - 0.4 = 0.6, so four misses in a row have
      probability 0.6^4 = 0.1296, about 0.13. The value 0.03 is 0.4^4, the chance of detecting the

  - stem: "In a two-sample mark-recapture study, marked mice learn that traps hold food and enter them more readily than unmarked mice. How will the Lincoln-Petersen estimate be biased?"
    choices:
      - "Too high, as marked mice are caught more often"
      - "Too high, as some of the marked mice have died"
      - "Too low, as marked mice are caught more often"
      - "Unbiased, as the marks themselves are not lost"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Trap-happy marked mice make up too large a share of the second sample, so the recaptures m2
      are inflated, and since N = n1 x n2 / m2, a larger m2 gives too small an estimate. Saying the
      estimate is too high gets the direction wrong: more recaptures shrink N. Calling it unbiased
      checks only one assumption (no mark loss) and ignores equal catchability. Death of marked
      mice is a real bias toward a high estimate, but it is a different violation (closure) from
      the one described.

The first film showed how a region outlasts its ponds. It left out three things: how fast a network is lost once the balance tips, what sets the two rates, and how to measure them on animals that hide. **Visit a pond three times, hear no toads, and you still cannot say it is empty.** [Population growth](/lessons/population-growth/) asked whether one population grows (its growth factor per time step, lambda, above or below 1) and [demography](/lessons/demography/) estimated its birth and survival rates; here the question moves up to a region of patches.

> **The one idea.** A region can keep a species even though no patch keeps its population, as long as colonization beats extinction: the **colonization rate c is greater than the extinction rate e**. Then the fraction of patches occupied, p, settles at **p\* = 1 − e/c**, the level where patches refounded balance patches lost.
>
> *Scope:* this is the deterministic Levins model: many identical, equally reachable patches, constant rates, no attention to how many animals each patch holds. There p\* is the long-run occupied fraction, not a fixed set of ponds. A real, finite network only wanders near p\* for a while: once every patch is empty it stays empty, so a small network can eventually lose every patch by chance even when c is greater than e.

## The ledger: colonization against extinction

For the first film's **metapopulation**, local populations linked by dispersal across hostile ground, Richard Levins (1969) wrote the occupied-or-empty bookkeeping as a ledger:

Occupancy stops changing, at its **equilibrium**, when the two terms balance:

    <p>1. Set dp/dt to zero: c p (1 − p) = e p.</p>
    <p>2. Divide both sides by p (for any network that has occupied patches): c (1 − p) = e.</p>

**Predict.** A network sits at p\* = 0.75, with c = 0.4 and e = 0.1 per year. If e rises to 0.4, equal to c, the first film showed that no equilibrium above zero is left. Will the network be gone within two or three years?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. The ledger becomes dp/dt = −0.4 p², so occupancy falls at 0.4 × 0.75² = 0.225 per year at first, and the slide slows as fewer patches are left: about 0.47 is still occupied after 2 years, 0.39 after 3, and about one patch in five (0.19) after 10. Falling from 0.75 to 0.05 takes about 47 years here, against about 5.5 years if e were 0.8. Once e is at or above c, the closer e is to c, the slower the slide (working in Going deeper).

</details>

## What sets the rates: dispersal, sources and rescue

**Dispersal** is movement from where an individual was born, or last bred, to where it breeds next. It is what c is made of, in three stages that can be measured separately: **departure**, **transfer** across the landscape, and **settlement**. They need not share causes: in the Glanville fritillary butterfly in the Åland Islands of Finland, the factors that explained departure explained little of the variation in how far butterflies went (DiLeo and colleagues, 2022). Dispersal is not **migration**, a seasonal round trip: a migrating warbler returns to the same areas each year; a dispersing one changes where it breeds, and it may move again later. Dispersal also carries genes, the gene flow of [drift, gene flow and competing explanations](/lessons/drift-gene-flow-explanations/), with the arithmetic in [population genetics](/lessons/population-genetics/).

<LessonFigure src="/figures/spatial-populations/dispersal-three-stages.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a butterfly leaves one of a string of ponds, crosses fields along a dashed path, and settles at another pond, numbered departure, transfer and settlement; two small insets contrast one-way dispersal with a two-way seasonal migration." caption="Dispersal has three stages that can be measured separately: departure, transfer and settlement. Migration is a seasonal round trip; dispersal changes where an animal breeds." />

Levins treats every patch as equal. H. Ronald Pulliam (1988), in a different kind of model that follows births and deaths rather than occupied patches, let habitats differ. Each habitat keeps its own ledger: births and immigrants (arrivals) in, deaths and emigrants (departures) out. In a **source**, local births exceed local deaths, and the surplus emigrates. In a **sink**, local reproduction does not make up for local mortality, and the population persists only because immigrants keep arriving.

**Predict.** A meadow holds the most butterflies of any patch in the region, year after year. Is it a source?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

You cannot tell from the count. A sink fed by a strong source can be full of animals. Birth and death rates, and how many animals arrive and leave, label a source; abundance does not. That is also why a species can be found outside the conditions it can sustain, a point taken up in [niches and distributions](/lessons/niches-and-distributions/).

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/spatial-populations/source-sink-flow.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a modest source patch sends a gold arrow of immigrants to a more crowded sink patch; two small balances show births outweighing deaths in the source and deaths outweighing births in the sink." caption="A source exports its surplus; a sink persists only because immigrants keep arriving, so it can be full of animals. Abundance does not label a source; birth and death rates do." />

Two ways movement keeps patches occupied, often confused:

| | What movement does | Where it enters the ledger |
|---|---|---|
| **Recolonization** | refounds a patch after its population has gone | the colonization term, c p (1 − p) |
| **Rescue effect** (Brown and Kodric-Brown, 1977) | immigrants join a population that is still present and lower its chance of dying out | lowers e for patches that receive immigrants; the basic Levins model leaves it out |

Linking patches with **corridors**, strips of habitat between them, can raise c; where immigrants rescue populations that are still present, it can also lower e. But a corridor adds habitat area as well as connection, so an experiment must pull the two apart (design problem below). Connectivity does not help in every model (Going deeper).

A sink, a rescued population, a corridor that raises c: each claim rests on knowing which patches are occupied, year by year, and that starts with whether the species was there at all.

## Two kinds of zero: occupancy

The second film's repeat visits estimate occupancy within one season: the same kind of quantity as p in the Levins model, now measured in the field. It left out time. Across years, colonization and extinction are read from which sites switch between occupied and empty, and there the same zeros do more damage.

**Predict.** A survey team scores every 000 history (no detection on any of three visits) as an absence, year after year. Will the network look more or less occupied, and more or less changeable, than it is?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Less occupied and more changeable. A missed population is scored as a local extinction, and finding it again as a colonization, so both transitions look more frequent while occupancy looks lower than it is. An illustration in yearly probabilities: empty ponds are colonized with probability 0.3 and occupied ones lost with probability 0.1, keeping three ponds in four occupied. If detection is 0.3 on each of three visits a year, independently, an occupied pond is missed on all three with probability 0.7³ = 0.343. Apparent extinction is the true 0.1 plus surviving populations missed, 0.9 × 0.343: about 0.41, four times the truth. Apparent colonization comes out near 0.40, and only 0.75 × (1 − 0.343), about 0.49, of ponds show a detection. This is error in the record, not a measure of how near the network is to collapse. **Dynamic occupancy models**, which estimate detection alongside colonization and extinction, correct for this; their yearly probabilities are field counterparts of c and e, not the same numbers (Going deeper).

</details>

## Counting animals: mark-recapture

Occupancy surveys repeated across years show how often sites are colonized and lost; source-sink claims need births, deaths and movement, which need data on individuals. The simplest count of hidden animals is a proportion: the marked share of a second catch should match the marked share of the whole population.

<EquationCard
  purpose="Estimate the size of a population you cannot count, from one marking and one recapture."

  <Fragment slot="example">

    <p>N = 10 × 10 / 2 = 50. Check: 2 of 10 is one fifth, and 10 marked animals are one fifth of 50.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="derivation">
    <p>Set the two marked fractions equal: m2 / n2 = n1 / N. Multiply both sides by N and by n2: m2 × N = n1 × n2. Divide by m2: N = n1 × n2 / m2.</p>

**Worked problem (invented data).** You catch and mark 50 animals on the first occasion (n1 = 50), release them, and later catch 90 (n2 = 90), of which 20 are marked (m2 = 20). Assume the population is **closed** (no births, deaths or movement between occasions), every animal is **equally catchable**, and marks are neither lost nor misread.

1. *Lincoln-Petersen.* The marked fraction 20/90 estimates 50/N, so N = n1 x n2 / m2 = 50 x 90 / 20 = 4,500 / 20 = **225 animals**.
2. *Chapman.* The plain estimator runs high when samples are small; Chapman's version adds one to each count, which reduces that bias and avoids dividing by zero if no marked animals are recaught:

> **N = (n1 + 1)(n2 + 1) / (m2 + 1) - 1**

Add one to each count: 50 + 1 = 51, 90 + 1 = 91 and 20 + 1 = 21. Then N = (51 x 91) / 21 - 1 = 4,641 / 21 - 1 = 221 - 1 = **220 animals**.

The overlap is 20, not 90. And 220 is an estimate resting on every assumption, not a head count.

**Predict.** Suppose marked animals learn to avoid traps, so they are less likely than unmarked ones to be caught the second time. Will the estimate come out too high or too low?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Too high: trap-shy marked animals are under-represented in the second catch, so m2 is too small and N too large. Trap-happy animals do the reverse, inflating m2 and shrinking N. Photo identification, as in a 2018 survey of free-roaming dogs in Quito, Ecuador, avoids handling but can mismatch individuals.

</details>

Movement also breaks closure. Traditional live-encounter mark-recapture estimates **apparent survival**, the chance an animal is alive *and still in the study area*: one that emigrated permanently looks exactly like one that died, so the dispersal that keeps a metapopulation going blurs the death rates a source-sink claim needs.

### Design problem

Gonzalez and colleagues (1998) worked with moss on limestone rocks in Derbyshire, England, home to tiny mites, springtails and other microarthropods. They cut the moss into patch systems of three kinds: patches joined by moss corridors; isolated patches; and patches with **pseudocorridors**, corridors of similar moss area broken by a small strip of bare rock, so with no continuous connection. After six months, species losses were 14.5% in connected landscapes and 41% in the isolated and pseudocorridor treatments. What can you conclude, what can you not, and how would you improve the study?

<LessonFigure src="/figures/spatial-populations/moss-corridor-design.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: three top-down panels of moss patches on grey limestone; in the first the patches are joined by continuous moss strips, in the second they are separate, and in the third broken strips of similar moss area lie between them with gaps; an inset shows tiny mites and springtails in moss." caption="A pseudocorridor adds about as much moss as a corridor but no continuous connection, which is how the moss experiment separated connection from added area. Schematic, not to scale." />

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**What the pseudocorridor buys.** It adds similar moss area without the connection, and it lost species like the isolated patches, so the difference points to connection rather than added area.

**What it is consistent with.** Fewer losses where patches connect fits a rescue effect, immigrants arriving through corridors propping up dwindling populations. But no movement was tracked, so recolonization after loss, or different conditions in connected moss, are not ruled out.

**Improvements.**
1. Mark animals, or use genetic assignment, to measure movement through corridors.
2. Record each species in each patch at several times, to separate fewer extinctions from more recolonizations.
3. Run it longer than six months, and replicate across rocks and seasons.
4. Keep the scale. This is moss on rocks, centimetres to metres, with animals of short generation time. It says nothing directly about forests or mammals.

**A larger, longer contrast.** Damschen and colleagues (2019) censused 239 plant species yearly for 18 years in connected and unconnected longleaf pine savanna fragments in South Carolina, estimating colonization and extinction separately, which the moss study could not: colonization 5% higher and extinction 2% lower each year in connected fragments, and nearly 14% more species after almost two decades. It too stays in its system: one site, plants, one corridor geometry.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Dispersal stages and migration

DiLeo and colleagues (2022) reconstructed Glanville fritillary dispersal by genetic assignment across five years and 232 habitat patches, and also found no effect of distance on survival after settling. Migration varies too: in some insects, such as painted lady butterflies, the cycle is completed over several generations.

### Small networks can be lost by chance

In an illustrative simulation with c = 0.15 and e = 0.1 per year (p\* = 0.33), started half occupied, most 10-patch networks lost every patch within 100 years, while 100-patch networks kept the species. In a small network, c greater than e is necessary but not sufficient.

### The exact path when c equals e

With c = e = 0.4 from a start of 0.75, dp/dt = −0.4 p² has the solution 1/p = 1/0.75 + 0.4t. After 10 years 1/p = 1.333 + 4 = 5.333, so p is about 0.19; reaching p = 0.05 (1/p = 20) takes (20 − 1.333)/0.4, about 47 years. With e = 0.8 the ledger is dp/dt = −0.4 p (1 + p), and solving it the same way gives about 5.5 years.

### Rescue makes e depend on p

In the Levins model the extinction rate does not depend on regional occupancy. Adding rescue makes e fall as p rises and, in some versions, can produce alternative stable states.

### When connectivity hurts

In simulations of 21 competing bird-like species, Schippers and colleagues (2015) found that greater connectivity sped up diversity loss. That is a model result, a caution about extrapolation, not a field finding.

### Spatial structure and predator-prey cycles

C. B. Huffaker (1958) kept a predatory mite with its prey mite on arrays of oranges. With the food spread out and movement partly blocked, the most complex arrangement sustained three successive oscillations before the run ended: a finite run in one preparation, not proof of permanent coexistence, but a case that spatial structure can prolong a predator-prey interaction. Predator-prey cycles return in [predation](/lessons/predation/).

### Dynamic occupancy and the symbol ψ

Survey papers write occupancy as ψ (psi); this lesson keeps p, to match the Levins model. Later work by MacKenzie's group extended the method across seasons, estimating colonization and local extinction without mistaking missed detections for losses. Those yearly probabilities, written γ and ε, are not the Levins c and e: Levins refounds an empty patch at rate c p, which grows with occupancy, and his c and e are continuous rates. Linking the two takes an explicit model of how colonists spread.
