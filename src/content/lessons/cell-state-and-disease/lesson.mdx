techniques:
  - name: "Single-cell RNA sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      Cells are separated, each cell's RNA is tagged with a cell barcode, and the pooled
      molecules are sequenced and counted. The result is a matrix of counts, genes by cells,
      which is clustered and annotated. It measures RNA captured from cells that survived
      dissociation, not protein and not the cells left behind.
  - name: "CITE-seq"
    blurb: >-
      Antibodies against surface proteins are tagged with DNA oligonucleotides that are read out
      by sequencing alongside the cell's RNA. One experiment then gives protein and RNA for the
      same cell, so an annotation does not have to infer surface protein from its RNA.
  - name: "Inducible Cre lineage tracing"
    blurb: >-
      A drug switches on Cre recombinase in a few cells, which both deletes a floxed gene (one flanked by loxP sites) such as
      Notch1 and turns on a reporter such as YFP. The two sites can recombine independently, so loss of the gene is checked directly, for example by staining for its protein. Every descendant stays labelled, so the size and
      makeup of each clone can be followed over weeks.
  - name: "EdU pulse and pulse-chase"
    blurb: >-
      EdU, a thymidine analogue, is built into DNA by cells in S phase and detected later by
      click chemistry. A short pulse gives the fraction of cells in S phase; a pulse followed by
      a chase shows where the labelled daughters end up, such as still basal or moved upward.

    choices:
      - "No molecule of that RNA was captured and counted in that cell"
      - "The gene is not expressed by any cell of that type in the tissue"
      - "The gene is silenced in that cell by chromatin compaction"
      - "The cell is a doublet and the gene was diluted below detection"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Single-cell methods capture only a fraction of each cell's RNA, so sampling alone can give a
      zero; the zero records a failure to count a molecule, not a measured absence. Silencing by
      chromatin is a biological claim the count cannot make on its own. A doublet would add RNA
      from two cells, not dilute one gene to zero. Concluding that no cell of the type expresses
      the gene extends one cell's zero to a whole population, which is the error twice over.

      The question is about surface protein, and RNA does not measure protein; CITE-seq reads

  - stem: "In mouse oesophagus, clones that lack Notch1 grow steadily larger than wild-type clones. A student concludes that the mutant cells must divide faster. Which observation would most directly refute that conclusion?"
    choices:
      - "Mutant clones also contain more differentiated suprabasal cells"
      - "A short EdU pulse labels equal fractions of mutant and normal basal cells"
      - "Normal cells next to mutant clones divide faster than distant cells"
      - "Mutant clones keep expanding after the mouse is several months old"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A short EdU pulse measures the fraction of basal cells in S phase; equal labelling in
      mutant and wild-type clones argues against faster cycling (though S-phase length could also differ) and points to other causes, such as altered

**In a 2018 study of organ donors, NOTCH1 mutations were more common in healthy oesophagus than in oesophageal cancers: in middle-aged and elderly donors, mutant cells made up an estimated 12 to 80% of the lining.**

The first film showed how, in mouse, one such clone fills a lining. It left two questions: how anyone could tell fate from speed, and why a lining full of these clones is not a tumour (answered under *Back to the puzzle*).

> **The idea to carry through this lesson.** A tissue can change because *which cells are there* changes, not only because its cells behave differently. How the mix changes: in a simple model with fixed odds of PP, PD and DD, and differentiation the only way out of the basal layer, each round of division multiplies a clone's expected progenitors by m = 2 × p(PP) + p(PD). Daughter fate sets m, so it decides whether the clone grows; division rate sets only how often the multiplying happens. And a bulk measurement expressed per cell is a weighted average over cell types: if the types differ in their per-cell level, changing their proportions shifts it even when expression within each type stays fixed (RNA-normalized measurements also depend on each type's RNA content).

## Fate, not speed: the bookkeeping

Here is the first film's tally of progenitors left behind, as arithmetic. A PP division leaves two progenitors, a PD division one and a DD division none; weight each by how often it happens, and add.

<EquationCard

  eq="m = 2 × p(PP) + p(PD)"
  say="m is twice the chance of a two-progenitor division, plus the chance of a one-of-each division."
  symbols={[
    ["m", "m", "average number of progenitors left behind per division", "progenitors per division"],
    ["p(PP)", "p of P P", "fraction of divisions that give two progenitors", "none"],
    ["p(PD)", "p of P D", "fraction of divisions that give one progenitor and one differentiating daughter", "none"],
  ]}
  toolkit={["powers"]}
  check="The three fractions add to 1. If p(PP) equals p(DD), then p(PD) = 1 − 2 × p(PP), so m is exactly 1."
/>

At m = 1, as in wild-type (normal, unmutated) tissue, the layer holds its size; above 1, each round multiplies the pool by m. Take made-up numbers. Balanced outcomes of 0.25, 0.50 and 0.25 (PP, PD, DD) give m = 0.50 + 0.50 = 1.0. A mutant whose divisions land 0.35, 0.50 and 0.15 has m = 0.70 + 0.50 = 1.2. Start with 100 mutant progenitors and no death: after three rounds there are about 100 × 1.2 × 1.2 × 1.2 ≈ 173, with no change in cycle time.

**Predict.** Another mutant keeps the balanced outcomes, 0.25, 0.50 and 0.25, but divides twice as often as its neighbours. Over the same stretch of time, does its pool of progenitors grow, shrink or stay the same, on average?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In this model it stays the same, on average. With m = 1 every round multiplies the pool by 1, and doing that twice as often still gives 1. With m = 1, dividing twice as often leaves the expected pool unchanged. It means more turnover and, over a fixed time, more clone extinctions and larger surviving clones. When m is above 1, speed also raises the expected growth: at m = 1.2, six rounds instead of three give about 300 expected progenitors instead of about 173.

</details>

The first film used the simplest case, a fixed tilt and a clone that spreads smoothly; here is the fuller picture. The rule averages over many clones and leaves out crowding. A single clone is a run of chance: one whose first divisions are DD vanishes; one that rolls PP early grows large. So surviving wild-type clones grow too, and mutant clone growth shows an advantage only against wild-type clones labelled the same way. Nor is m fixed: in mouse, once mutant cells occupied the epithelium their behaviour returned toward wild type.

## Rate or fate? The mouse experiment

First, how to tell fate from speed. Notch signalling is linked to differentiation of squamous (flattened) epithelial cells (see [Notch signalling](/lessons/notch-signaling/)), so a clone lacking Notch1 might win by fate, or by dividing faster (see [cell-cycle control](/lessons/cell-cycle-control/)).

### Design problem

Notch1-mutant clones take over mouse oesophageal epithelium. Did the mutant cells divide faster, or did their daughters differentiate less? Design the experiment, and say what each answer predicts.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use inducible Cre lineage tracing to make sparse Notch1-deleted YFP clones, with wild-type YFP clones in control mice as the comparison. Estimate division rate with a one-hour EdU pulse, and fate with a pulse-chase. Follow clone sizes over time. Faster division predicts more EdU-labelled mutant basal cells after the pulse. A fate tilt predicts similar labelling after the pulse but more labelled mutant daughters still basal after the chase. The two tools are described next.

</details>

Two tools tell these apart.

**Inducible Cre lineage tracing** marks a few cells and all their descendants. A drug switches on the enzyme Cre recombinase, which cuts DNA at short loxP sequences. It deletes a *floxed* gene (one flanked by loxP sites) such as *Notch1* and turns on a **reporter**, a gene whose product is easy to see: here yellow fluorescent protein (YFP), the first film's gold. Staining for NOTCH1 protein confirms the loss; wild-type YFP clones in control mice are the comparison, and clone sizes are followed over weeks.

**EdU**, a mimic of the DNA building block thymidine, is built into DNA only during S phase, while a cell copies its DNA:

- A one-hour **pulse** is a snapshot of the cells copying DNA at that moment, a stand-in for how many are cycling.
- A **pulse-chase** labels them, then waits to see where their daughters end up: still basal, or moved up.

**Predict.** If Notch1-mutant cells take over by dividing faster, what should the one-hour pulse show inside mutant clones compared with wild-type clones? If they take over by tilting fate, what should the chase show?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Faster division predicts more EdU-labelled basal cells in mutant clones after the pulse. A fate tilt predicts similar labelling after the pulse but, after the chase, more labelled mutant daughters still basal. The pulse is only a proxy for rate: the fraction in S phase also depends on how long S phase lasts and on how many basal cells are still cycling progenitors.

</details>

In 2023 Abby and colleagues found that mouse clones lacking one or both copies of *Notch1* expanded without a higher division rate, and EdU tracking was consistent with more progenitor and fewer differentiating daughters per division: fate, not speed. At the edges of clones lacking both copies, wild-type neighbours differentiated and left the basal layer faster, as in the first film.

## Neighbours keep the count

In the first film, the clone's room came from neighbours that differentiated and left. Space is a second balance alongside m: a basal layer has no spare room, so an extra cell needs a neighbour to leave. Other epithelia show a second exit. Eisenhoffer, Rosenblatt and colleagues counted cells being squeezed out of the sheet (**extrusion**) in human colon sections, developing zebrafish epidermis and crowded sheets of dog kidney (MDCK) cells. Most lacked active caspase-3, an enzyme switched on in apoptosis, a programmed cell death (see [epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/) and [cell death](/lessons/cell-death/)). Crowding pushed out living cells, which then died, probably because they lost the survival signals that attachment to the sheet provides. Knocking down Piezo1, a channel opened by membrane stretch, blocked this extrusion in zebrafish and produced masses of epidermal cells.

**Predict.** In a mouse model of oesophageal carcinogenesis, should more early lesions (small, early tumours) persist, or fewer, if the normal-looking clones around them are made more competitive?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Fewer. Colom and colleagues found that early lesions were removed more often when the surrounding clones were highly competitive, and less often when competition was blocked with a drug. Competition for space can protect the tissue.

</details>

### Back to the puzzle

Why can a healthy oesophagus carry so many NOTCH1-mutant cells and not be a tumour? In mouse, losing Notch1 does two things. In normal lining it tilts fate, so clones win space. In a mouse model where oesophageal tumours are induced, deleting *Notch1* reduced tumour growth, and a NOTCH1-blocking antibody reduced the size of established lesions. Winning space in normal lining is not the same as growing as a tumour. These are mouse results about mechanism, not human prevention data; which genes do push a clone toward cancer is the subject of [cancer genetics](/lessons/cancer-genetics/) and [oncogenes](/lessons/oncogenes/).

Winning space changes which cells the lining holds, and that is the idea's second half: an average over a tissue shifts when its mix of cells shifts. The second film picks up here: what a change in the mix does to a measurement, and how single-cell RNA sequencing sees the mix.

## Seeing the mixture

In a bulk assay, RNA from all sampled cells is mixed, so an amount expressed per cell is a weighted average over the cell groups. Now try the second film's arithmetic on a new case.

**Predict.** Made-up numbers: cell type T expresses gene X at 50 units per cell, other cells at 5. After a treatment, T rises from 20% to 40% of cells, and no cell changes its expression. Does bulk X double?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. bulk signal = (fraction T × level in T) + (fraction other × level in other).

1. Before: 0.20 × 50 + 0.80 × 5 = 10 + 4 = 14 units per average cell.
2. After: 0.40 × 50 + 0.60 × 5 = 20 + 3 = 23 units per average cell.
3. Fold change: 23 / 14 = 1.64. A bulk experiment would report gene X up 1.6-fold, although no cell regulated it.

Single-cell data would show the same expression inside each cluster and a changed proportion of clusters.

</details>

The rule is exact for amounts per cell; for data normalized per unit of RNA, see Going deeper.

<BulkVersusComposition />

Every cell in a tissue carries essentially the same genome, apart from small somatic changes like the mutations above. What makes a keratinocyte differ from a macrophage is which genes it transcribes, a pattern held in place by transcription factors and chromatin (see [eukaryotic gene regulation](/lessons/eukaryotic-regulation/)). Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is one way to read that pattern in sampled cells, as the second film sketched.

The tissue is dissociated into single cells, usually with enzymes. Each captured RNA is copied into DNA tagged with a cell barcode, a short sequence shared by the DNA copies from that cell. The pooled DNA is sequenced (see [sequencing and genomes](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/)), and reads are sorted by barcode and counted into a **count matrix**: one row per gene, one column per sampled cell, each entry the number of molecules (with molecular identifiers) or reads counted. Software groups cells with similar expression profiles. Each group is a **cluster**; comparing its genes with known profiles to propose an identity is **annotation**.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/cell-state-and-disease/scrna-seq-pipeline.webp" width={1200} height={394} alt="AI-generated illustration: three steps of single-cell RNA sequencing. Tissue is separated into single cells, each cell's RNA molecules receive a shared cell barcode and individual molecular identifiers, and counts are arranged in a genes-by-cells grid whose cells group into named clusters." caption="Single-cell RNA-seq in three steps: separate the cells, tag every molecule with its cell's barcode, then count molecules into a genes-by-cells matrix that is clustered and annotated." />

Single-cell data have their own composition trap: if dissociation loses fragile cells preferentially, cluster proportions are biased before you count, and a shift in cluster sizes between conditions can come from how well each sample survived the enzymes.

## Type, state or artifact?

In single-cell data, a change in the sampled mix can appear as a change in cluster sizes, so first ask what a cluster represents. The second film's line between a **cell type** (a relatively stable identity) and a **cell state** (variation within it: cycling, stressed, activated, early or late in differentiation) is a working definition; where the line falls depends on the question and on how finely you look. One cell type can vary a great deal in its global gene expression, and cells of one type in different states can land in separate clusters.

A cluster is an observation to explain, not a type by definition. The second film showed three ways the method can make one; the full list has six, each able to create or erase a cluster, or make one look better supported than it is.

| Artifact | What it does to your clusters | How you would check |
|---|---|---|
| **Sampling zeros**: only some RNA molecules are captured | A marker looks "missing" from some cells | Do not read one zero as "off" |
| **Doublets**: one barcode labels two cells | A fake hybrid type with two types' markers | See the Predict below |
| **Batch effects**: technical differences between samples processed separately | Cells of one kind split by batch, not biology | Where a population is expected in several samples, check that its cells mix across batches; a real type can be missing from one |
| **Dissociation stress**: breaking up tissue perturbs state before capture | A stress state made in the dish | Compare with fixed tissue or staining in situ |
| **Resolution**: a clustering setting for how finely cells are split | Turned up, a type splits into states or noise; turned down, types merge | Check the cluster across resolutions |
| **Circular marker testing**: clustering on the data, then testing the same data for genes that differ between clusters | Markers look better supported than they are: P values too small, because the clusters were drawn to differ | Confirm with independent evidence, such as protein |

**Predict.** A small cluster expresses both T-cell genes and epithelial keratin genes. A new hybrid cell type? What would you check first?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The likeliest reading is a doublet, a T cell and an epithelial cell under one barcode; RNA floating free from broken cells can also contaminate a barcode. Check whether the same barcodes carry both full programmes, whether doublet-detection scores and RNA counts per barcode are high, and whether the cluster's size fits the rate expected for T-cell–epithelial pairs given how common each type is (not the overall doublet rate). A new type needs independent evidence too.

</details>

The 2016 melanoma study of Tirosh and colleagues, 4,645 cells from 19 patients' tumours, shows the payoff when this is done with care: malignant cells within one tumour differed in programmes associated with the cell cycle, a state rather than a separate cancer type.

## From description to cause

The first film followed its clone by a mark every descendant inherited. Single-cell data lack such a mark, so software infers an order: it arranges cells along a path and gives each a **pseudotime**, its distance along that inferred path from the start. The path is not a movie of one cell maturing. Sequencing destroys each cell, so pseudotime orders different cells caught at different points. Its direction is an assumption, usually set by choosing which end is the start.

Three kinds of evidence test such a picture. Lineage shows ancestry, perturbation tests cause, and protein checks the annotation.

**Lineage.** Mark a cell and every descendant, as the inducible Cre clones of the mouse experiment did (see [stem cells and tissue renewal](/lessons/stem-cells/)). Weinreb, Klein, Camargo and colleagues gave mouse blood (haematopoietic) progenitors inherited DNA barcodes that are expressed as RNA, sequenced some cells early and followed their sisters' progeny. Sister cells shared fate biases that the RNA profiles had not detected.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/cell-state-and-disease/barcoded-sisters.webp" width={1200} height={723} alt="AI-generated illustration: a mouse blood progenitor with a gold barcode divides into two sisters. One sister is sequenced at once; the other is left to make progeny that all carry the same barcode, so the early profile can be matched to the clone's later fate." caption="Sisters share an inherited barcode. One is sequenced at once, the other is left to make progeny, and the barcode matches the early RNA profile to the clone's later fate." />

**Perturbation.** Remove or block the candidate and see whether the outcome changes, then restore it (see [experiments that distinguish mechanisms](/lessons/experiments-that-distinguish/)).

**Protein.** RNA does not measure protein. CITE-seq, described in 2017, reads DNA-tagged antibodies alongside RNA, so a surface marker can be measured in the same cell rather than inferred.

## Going deeper

### From counts to clusters

In many methods each molecule also gets a unique molecular identifier, so copies made later can be collapsed back into one original molecule. Analysis then runs through quality control, normalization, choosing informative genes and reducing dimensions. Cells are linked to their most similar neighbours in a graph, and groups of tightly linked cells become clusters. Markers also depend on what else is in the data set. Software such as Monocle builds pseudotime paths.

### Weighting by RNA content

If bulk data are normalized per unit of RNA, each type also counts in proportion to its RNA content. Were type T in the worked example to hold twice the total RNA of other cells, the same shift would read about 1.41-fold, not 1.64-fold.

### Chance in single clones

In the made-up balanced model, about 15 in 100 labelled clones survive 20 rounds and average about 6.5 progenitors, while the average over all clones stays at 1. Even a tilted clone (m = 1.2) is lost by chance about 43% of the time.
