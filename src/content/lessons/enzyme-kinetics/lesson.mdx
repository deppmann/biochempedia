techniques:
  - name: "The enzyme assay"
    blurb: >-
      Velocity is a slope: product formed per unit time. If the product absorbs
      light, you watch a spectrophotometer's reading climb and read the rate
      straight off the line — how many classic assays work.
  - name: "Measuring initial velocity (V₀)"
    blurb: >-
      Measure the slope in the first instants, before substrate is depleted or
      product builds up and starts to inhibit the enzyme or run the reaction
      backward. V₀ is the clean, reproducible number
      the steady-state parameters in this lesson are built from.
  - name: "Extracting Km and Vmax"
    blurb: >-
      Measure V₀ at many substrate concentrations and fit the hyperbola (today, by
      non-linear regression). The Lineweaver–Burk double-reciprocal linearizes the
      same data: y-intercept = 1/Vmax, x-intercept = −1/Km, slope = Km/Vmax.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "An enzyme obeys Michaelis–Menten kinetics. By definition, the Michaelis constant Km is the substrate concentration at which:"
    choices:
      - "the reaction velocity is half of Vmax"
      - "the reaction reaches its maximum velocity, Vmax"
      - "every active site is permanently and irreversibly occupied"
      - "the enzyme begins to denature"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Km is defined as the [S] at which v = ½Vmax. Vmax itself is only approached
      as [S] → ∞ (it's an asymptote you never quite reach), which is why Km — a
      point you can actually locate on the curve — is the useful handle.

  - stem: "For a Michaelis–Menten enzyme, what substrate concentration (as a multiple of Km) gives 80% of Vmax?"
    choices:
      - "0.8 × Km"
      - "1.25 × Km"
      - "4 × Km"
      - "8 × Km"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Set v = 0.8 Vmax in v = Vmax[S]/(Km+[S]): 0.8(Km+[S]) = [S] → 0.8 Km = 0.2[S]
      → [S] = 4 Km. In general, to reach a fraction f of Vmax you need [S] =
      (f / (1−f)) · Km, so 0.8/0.2 = 4.

  - stem: "On a Lineweaver–Burk (double-reciprocal) plot of 1/v versus 1/[S], the y-intercept equals:"
    choices:
      - "Vmax"
      - "−1/Km"
      - "Km/Vmax"
      - "1/Vmax"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Taking reciprocals of the Michaelis–Menten equation gives 1/v =
      (Km/Vmax)(1/[S]) + 1/Vmax — a straight line. The y-intercept (where 1/[S] = 0)
      is 1/Vmax; the x-intercept is −1/Km; the slope is Km/Vmax. A classic exam trap
      is forgetting the intercept is the reciprocal of Vmax, not Vmax itself.

  - stem: "A competitive inhibitor binds the free enzyme's active site. Compared with the uninhibited reaction, it:"
    choices:
      - "lowers Vmax and raises the apparent Km"
      - "lowers both Vmax and the apparent Km"
      - "leaves Km unchanged but lowers Vmax"
      - "leaves Vmax unchanged but raises the apparent Km"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Substrate and inhibitor compete for the same site, so flooding the system
      with substrate still out-competes the inhibitor — Vmax is unchanged. But you
      now need more substrate to reach ½Vmax, so the apparent Km rises. On a
      Lineweaver–Burk plot this is a shared y-intercept (1/Vmax) with a steeper slope.

  - stem: "Vmax measured in a test tube depends on how much enzyme you added. To get a property of the enzyme molecule itself you compute kcat = Vmax/[E]ₜ. kcat tells you:"
    choices:
      - "the substrate concentration at half-maximal velocity"
      - "the total amount of product the reaction will ever form"
      - "how many substrate molecules one enzyme molecule converts per second when saturated"
      - "the activation energy of the reaction"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      kcat (the turnover number, units s⁻¹) is the maximum number of reactions one
      enzyme molecule catalyzes per second when fully saturated — carbonic
      anhydrase's is ~600,000 s⁻¹. The half-max [S] is Km; the ratio kcat/Km
      (the specificity constant, often called catalytic efficiency) governs
      velocity when substrate is well below Km.

mcatQuestions:
  - stem: "An enzyme has Vmax = 80 µmol·min⁻¹ and Km = 5 mM. At a substrate concentration of 15 mM, the initial velocity is closest to:"
    choices:
      - "20 µmol·min⁻¹"
      - "40 µmol·min⁻¹"
      - "60 µmol·min⁻¹"
      - "80 µmol·min⁻¹"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      v = Vmax[S]/(Km+[S]) = 80 × 15 / (5 + 15) = 1200/20 = 60 µmol·min⁻¹. Shortcut:
      [S] = 15 mM is 3 × Km, and at [S] = 3 Km three-quarters of the enzyme is busy,
      so v = ¾ Vmax = 60. Choosing 40 treats [S] as if it equalled Km (half of Vmax);
      choosing 80 assumes saturation, which the curve only approaches.

  - stem: "On a Lineweaver–Burk plot, the lines measured with and without an inhibitor intersect on the y-axis (same 1/Vmax), but the inhibited line has a steeper slope. The inhibitor is:"
    choices:
      - "competitive"
      - "uncompetitive"
      - "purely noncompetitive"
      - "irreversible"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      A shared y-intercept means Vmax is unchanged; a steeper slope (Km/Vmax) means
      the apparent Km rose — the competitive signature. Uncompetitive inhibition
      lowers Vmax and Km together, giving parallel lines; pure noncompetitive
      inhibition lowers Vmax with Km unchanged, so those lines share the x-intercept
      (−1/Km). You can reproduce these signatures with the inhibition explorer in
      the lesson.

  - stem: "Enzymes accelerate reactions by binding the transition state more tightly than they bind the substrate. This principle predicts that especially potent reversible inhibitors are molecules that:"
    choices:
      - "closely resemble the substrate in its stable, ground state"
      - "closely resemble the high-energy transition state"
      - "closely resemble the final product"
      - "are far larger than the active site can accommodate"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      The active site is complementary to the transition state, not the substrate.
      A transition-state analog therefore exploits the enzyme's full binding energy
      and clings far more tightly than the substrate does — which is exactly why
      transition-state analogs make such powerful inhibitors (Pauling's insight,
      central to rational drug design).

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm 5"
    text: |
      Imagine a toll booth on a road with no waiting lane. When only a few cars come,
      the worker waves each one through as fast as it shows up — but they can only
      handle one car at a time, and a car that finds the booth busy drives around
      and tries again later. When lots of cars come, the worker is already going as
      fast as they possibly can, and adding more cars doesn't speed them up at all. Enzymes
      are tiny workers in your body, and substrates are the cars. Once every worker
      is busy, they all run at top speed and no faster.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: |
      An enzyme has to grab a molecule — the substrate — and hold it before it can
      change it. When substrate is scarce, adding more speeds things up, because
      empty enzymes find new substrate quickly. But there's a fixed number of
      enzymes. As substrate climbs they all get busy, until every enzyme is
      occupied. Now the enzyme is saturated and works at its top speed, called Vmax
      — pouring in more substrate can't push past it.

      Plot speed against substrate concentration and you get a curve that rises,
      then flattens at Vmax. Km is the substrate concentration needed to run at half
      of Vmax. A small Km means the enzyme gets going on very little substrate — think
      of it as how much substrate it takes to reach cruising speed.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: |
      Michaelis and Menten captured the whole saturation curve in one equation:

      v = Vmax·[S] / (Km + [S])

      At low [S], velocity rises nearly linearly; at high [S] it plateaus at Vmax.
      Setting [S] = Km gives v = ½Vmax, so Km is the substrate concentration at
      half-maximal velocity — read it as "how much substrate the enzyme needs," not
      automatically as affinity.

      Vmax depends on how much enzyme you added, so divide it out: kcat = Vmax/[E]ᴛ,
      the turnover number — reactions per enzyme per second when saturated (carbonic
      anhydrase ≈ 600,000 s⁻¹). The ratio kcat/Km, often called catalytic
      efficiency, is the specificity constant: it governs the rate at low [S] and is
      safest for comparing substrates of one enzyme.

      Because Vmax is an asymptote you never reach, the Lineweaver–Burk plot
      linearizes the data: 1/v = (Km/Vmax)(1/[S]) + 1/Vmax, with y-intercept 1/Vmax,
      x-intercept −1/Km, and slope Km/Vmax.

      Three reversible inhibitors give distinct signatures: competitive (binds the
      free active site) leaves Vmax unchanged but raises apparent Km, so the lines
      share a y-intercept; uncompetitive (binds only ES) lowers both Vmax and Km,
      giving parallel lines; noncompetitive (binds E and ES equally well) lowers
      Vmax with Km unchanged, so the lines share an x-intercept.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: |
      The mechanism is E + S ⇌ ES → E + P, with rate constants k₁, k₋₁ (binding)
      and k₂ (catalysis). Briggs and Haldane replaced Michaelis and Menten's
      rapid-equilibrium assumption with the steady-state assumption: shortly after
      initiation, d[ES]/dt ≈ 0. Solving for [ES] and substituting into v = k₂[ES]
      yields the Michaelis–Menten form with Km = (k₋₁ + k₂)/k₁.

      So Km equals the dissociation constant Kd = k₋₁/k₁ only when k₂ ≪ k₋₁
      (substrate falls off far faster than it turns over); otherwise, in this minimal
      scheme, Km exceeds Kd and "low Km = high affinity" is unsafe. For this minimal scheme kcat = k₂.

      The ratio kcat/Km is the specificity constant: it governs velocity at low [S]
      (v ≈ (kcat/Km)[E][S]) and sets an enzyme's preference among competing
      substrates. Its ceiling is the diffusion-controlled limit (~10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹s⁻¹),
      where nearly every encounter is productive — "kinetically perfect" enzymes.

      Cooperative enzymes, usually allosteric multi-subunit ones, depart from
      Michaelis–Menten: shifts between low- and high-activity states (the T⇌R model
      is one description) give sigmoidal v–[S] curves, so we report K₀.₅ rather than a
      mechanistic Km. Underlying all of it, catalysis comes from transition-state
      stabilization — the active site is complementary to the high-energy transition
      state, not the ground-state substrate, and the binding energy released pays
      down ΔG‡. That's why transition-state analogs are such potent inhibitors.

**Give an enzyme 12 mM substrate and it makes 45 µmol of product a minute. Double the substrate to 24 mM. Does it now make 90?**

It does not; by mid-lesson you can work out what it does make. At any instant, each enzyme molecule is either **empty**, waiting for substrate, or **busy**, holding one substrate molecule. Only busy enzymes make product, and each works at its own fixed pace, its **turnover number, kcat**: how many substrate molecules one busy enzyme converts per second.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** An enzyme's velocity is how fast one busy enzyme works times how much of the enzyme is busy: in the simplest scheme, v = kcat × [ES]. The busy fraction works out to [S]/(Km + [S]). Saturation is that fraction climbing toward 1; Vmax is every enzyme busy; Km is the substrate concentration at which half the enzyme is busy. An inhibitor pulls enzyme out of the working pool, and *which* pool it pulls from (free enzyme, busy enzyme, or both) decides how the curve changes.

## Measuring the reaction: the assay and V₀

Thermodynamics tells you the *destination*: whether a reaction can happen. Kinetics tells you the *journey*: how fast. An enzyme first has to **grab its substrate and hold it**, forming a temporary enzyme–substrate (ES) complex, before converting it to product:

> E + S ⇌ ES → E + P

Velocity is a slope: product formed per unit time. If the product (or substrate) absorbs light, a spectrophotometer reads the change continuously. Plot product against time and the line starts straight, then bends as substrate is depleted, product accumulates and interferes, and the enzyme loses activity. The one discipline that matters most: take the slope in the first moments, the **initial velocity, V₀**.

Repeat at several substrate concentrations and plot each V₀ against [S]. The points rise steeply, then level off: the record this lesson explains. Fitting that curve gives Km and Vmax, and from them kcat and kcat/Km.

**Predict.** Partway up that curve, where it has started to bend, you double the substrate. Does V₀ double?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. In the opening example, 12 to 24 mM raises V₀ by only about 14%. The next section works it out.

</details>

## Building the curve: how much of the enzyme is busy

<MiniPlot view="mm" vmax0={60} km0={4} s0={12} showBusy />

The velocity you measured is v = kcat × [ES], so the shape of the curve comes down to one question: how many enzymes are busy? An enzyme becomes busy in one way and stops being busy in two:

- **In:** substrate collides with an empty enzyme and sticks, at k₁[E][S]. More substrate means more collisions.
- **Out, no product:** the substrate falls back off, at k₋₁[ES].
- **Out, with product:** the enzyme converts it and lets the product go, at k₂[ES], and is empty again. In this simple scheme k₂ is kcat.

Adding substrate speeds up the way in but does nothing to the ways out. That is why velocity levels off: once nearly every enzyme is busy (**saturated**), no empty ones are left to fill.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/enzyme-kinetics/enzyme-busy-cycle.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: an empty enzyme binds a substrate to become busy; the busy enzyme either lets the substrate fall back off or converts it and releases product, becoming empty again. An inset shows rows of ten enzymes: one busy with little substrate, five busy at a substrate concentration equal to Km, and nine busy at nine times Km." caption="Every enzyme is empty or busy, and only busy ones make product. Substrate opens the one way in; falling off and turnover are the two ways out. Velocity is kcat times the number busy, so it saturates as the busy fraction, [S]/(Km + [S]), approaches 1: half busy at Km, 90% busy only at 9 Km." />

Soon after mixing, usually within milliseconds, enzymes become busy as fast as they stop being busy, and the number of busy enzymes holds steady. This **steady state** is the model's central assumption. Measuring an initial velocity keeps the others true: [S] is still what you added, and there is not yet enough product to run the reaction backward or inhibit the enzyme. Five lines give the equation:

1. Steady state, in = out: k₁[E][S] = (k₋₁ + k₂)[ES]. Both ways out depend only on [ES], so their rate constants add.
2. Collect the rate constants into one number: [E][S] / [ES] = (k₋₁ + k₂) / k₁. Call it **Km**.
3. Every enzyme is empty or busy: [E] = [E]ₜ − [ES], where [E]ₜ is the total enzyme.
4. Substitute: ([E]ₜ − [ES])[S] = Km[ES]. Move the [ES] terms together: [E]ₜ[S] = [ES](Km + [S]). So the busy fraction is **[ES] / [E]ₜ = [S] / (Km + [S])**.
5. Multiply both sides by kcat[E]ₜ. The left side becomes v; kcat[E]ₜ, the speed with every enzyme busy, is Vmax. So **v = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S])**.

That is the Michaelis–Menten equation (Michaelis and Menten, 1913, who assumed rapid equilibrium between E, S and ES; the steady-state derivation above is Briggs and Haldane's, 1925, and gives the same form), a **hyperbola** rising toward Vmax but never reaching it. It has the shape of single-site binding in [protein binding](/lessons/protein-binding-allostery/), Y = L/(Kd + L), with Km in place of Kd. How the two constants differ comes next.

Its scope: one substrate, far more substrate than enzyme (so free [S] is about the [S] you added), measured at initial velocity. With extra steps in the mechanism, such as a covalent intermediate, "busy" means every form holding substrate or an intermediate, and the busy fraction is still [S]/(Km + [S]). Cooperative and multi-substrate enzymes and tight-binding inhibitors need other models (Going deeper, "Where the model stops").

A booth with a queue would rise in a straight line to capacity and stop at a sharp corner. Without one, the curve bends gradually toward the booth's ceiling, **Vmax**.

### Km is the half-busy point, not the affinity

Split Km into two parts:

> Km = (k₋₁ + k₂) / k₁ = k₋₁/k₁ + k₂/k₁ = **Kd + k₂/k₁**

Kd = k₋₁/k₁ is the dissociation constant from protein binding: the half-full point if substrate only ever fell back off. The second part is turnover, a second way to empty a site, so a fast enzyme needs more substrate to keep half its sites full.

**Predict.** An enzyme's Kd is 10 µM, and it turns substrate over nine times faster than substrate falls off. Is its Km bigger or smaller than Kd, and by how much?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Ten times bigger: with k₂ = 9 k₋₁, Km = 10 + 90 = 100 µM. At 100 µM substrate, binding equilibrium alone would fill 91% of the sites; turnover keeps it at 50%.

</details>

Km equals Kd only when turnover is much slower than falling off (k₂ ≪ k₋₁), so Km is *not* binding affinity: a **small Km** means the enzyme reaches half speed on very little substrate, whatever the reason. (With extra steps, Km can even be below Kd: Going deeper.) Under fixed conditions (pH, temperature, ionic strength), Km is a constant for a given enzyme–substrate pair; for a two-substrate enzyme it also depends on the other substrate.

## Speed and efficiency: kcat and kcat/Km

Vmax depends on how much enzyme is in the tube: double the enzyme, double Vmax. Divide it out for a property of the molecule: **kcat = Vmax / [E]ₜ**, in s⁻¹ (strictly, per active site). Carbonic anhydrase is one of the fastest known: each molecule converts about 600,000 CO₂ molecules to bicarbonate every second.

kcat is only top speed. When substrate is scarce, almost every enzyme is empty, the busy fraction is about [S]/Km, and

> v ≈ (kcat/Km) × [E]ₜ × [S]

So **kcat/Km** is the rate constant for an encounter that ends in product, in M⁻¹s⁻¹. It cannot exceed how often enzyme and substrate collide in water, the diffusion limit, typically about 10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹s⁻¹; enzymes that come close, where nearly every encounter is productive, are called "kinetically perfect". The film calls kcat/Km "catalytic efficiency"; more precisely it is the specificity constant, which sets an enzyme's preference among competing substrates. It is safest for comparing substrates of one enzyme: ranking different enzymes by it can mislead, because it describes only the low-substrate regime.

The film notes that in *E. coli*, most measured substrate levels exceed the Km of the enzyme that uses them. Above Km means more than half busy, not flat out: at 3 Km an enzyme runs at three-quarters of Vmax and still tracks changes in substrate. Regulation, covered in other lessons, also sets its pace.

## Reading the curve a second way: Lineweaver–Burk

This **double-reciprocal** or **Lineweaver–Burk** plot has **y-intercept 1/Vmax**, **x-intercept −1/Km** and **slope Km/Vmax**. (Watch the reciprocals: the intercept is 1/Vmax, *not* Vmax — a reliable source of exam mistakes.) The mini-plot draws this line live as you slide Km and Vmax.

One caution: taking reciprocals magnifies the error in the low-[S] points. So today Km and Vmax are estimated by fitting the curve directly (non-linear regression), and the double-reciprocal plot is used to see patterns, such as the type of inhibitor.

## When something gets in the way: inhibitors

<InhibitionExplorer />

Back to the rule: whether an inhibitor ties up free enzyme (E), busy enzyme (ES), or both sets its pattern. The **apparent** Km and Vmax are the values you would measure with the inhibitor present; the enzyme's own Km has not changed. **Ki** is the dissociation constant of the complex the inhibitor forms (EI for a competitive inhibitor, ESI for an uncompetitive one). For worked numbers, take an inhibitor dose that makes its factor, 1 + [I]/Ki, equal to 3 (that is, [I] = 2 Ki).

**Competitive: ties up free enzyme.** The inhibitor and substrate cannot bind at the same time, usually because both need the active site. It takes more substrate to make half the enzyme busy, so the **apparent Km rises** (here, threefold). But flood the system with substrate and almost no free enzyme is left for the inhibitor, so **Vmax is unchanged** and the Lineweaver–Burk lines **share a y-intercept**.

**Predict.** Can you rescue a competitively inhibited reaction by adding more substrate?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes. With the factor at 3, the apparent Km is 3 Km, so at [S] = Km, v = Vmax × Km/(3 Km + Km) = ¼ Vmax instead of ½ Vmax: halved. The same arithmetic gives about 15% lost at 10 Km and only about 2% at 100 Km.

</details>

**Uncompetitive: ties up busy enzyme.** The inhibitor binds only the ES complex, forming the enzyme–substrate–inhibitor complex **ESI** and locking the enzyme mid-reaction.

**Predict.** Does an uncompetitive inhibitor matter more or less as [S] rises?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

More, because more substrate makes more ES for it to grab. With the factor at 3, divide both Km and Vmax by 3: v = Vmax[S]/(Km + 3[S]). At [S] = Km that is ¼ Vmax instead of ½: half lost. The same arithmetic gives about 15% lost at 0.1 Km and about two-thirds at high substrate.

</details>

Why do **Vmax and Km both fall**? Pulling ES into ESI draws more E + S into ES to replace it (Le Chatelier's principle). With the factor at 3, half the enzyme holds substrate, as ES or ESI, at [S] = Km/3: a lower apparent Km. But two-thirds of that substrate-holding enzyme is stuck as ESI, so v is lower at every [S]. Both fall by the same factor, leaving the slope, Km/Vmax, unchanged: **parallel lines**.

**Noncompetitive: ties up both equally.** The inhibitor binds a site away from the active site, equally well with or without substrate. Taking the same share out of E and ES is like having less enzyme: **Vmax falls, Km unchanged**, so the lines **share the x-intercept** (−1/Km). An irreversible inactivator that has finished reacting gives the same plot, because it simply leaves less enzyme. Pure noncompetitive is the special case of **mixed** inhibition; if the inhibitor binds E and ES with different strengths, Vmax still falls but the apparent Km can rise or fall.

| Type | Ties up | Apparent Vmax | Apparent Km | Lineweaver–Burk | Share of v lost as [S] rises |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Competitive | E | unchanged | rises | shared y-intercept | shrinks |
| Uncompetitive | ES | falls | falls, same factor | parallel lines | grows |
| Pure noncompetitive | E and ES equally | falls | unchanged | shared x-intercept | stays the same |

Because the active site is shaped to grip the fleeting **transition state** more tightly than the substrate, stable mimics of that state, **transition-state analogs**, make some of the most potent inhibitors known. They usually occupy the active site, and many bind so tightly that they need the tight-binding treatment (Going deeper, "Where the model stops").

## Going deeper

### Where the model stops

The derivation treats free [S] as the [S] you added. With enzyme concentration comparable to Km, much of the substrate sits on the enzyme, and the busy fraction at an added [S] = Km falls below one half (about 0.38 when [E]ₜ = Km); tight-binding inhibitors raise the same problem. Cooperative enzymes, usually allosteric multi-subunit ones, give sigmoidal v–[S] curves (shifts between low- and high-activity states; the T⇌R model is one description), so we report K₀.₅ rather than a mechanistic Km.

### When Km is below Kd

Km = Kd + k₂/k₁, which makes Km at least Kd, holds only for the minimal scheme. With a slow step after ES, such as an acyl-enzyme intermediate, enzyme piles up in that busy form, so Km can be smaller than Kd. It is still the [S] at half of Vmax.

### From lock and key to the transition state

That tighter grip on the transition state is where catalysis comes from: the binding energy released pays down ΔG‡ (Pauling's insight). The same binding energy also underlies specificity: kcat/Km for each substrate reflects how tightly the enzyme binds that substrate's transition state, so a substrate whose transition state fits the site poorly is turned over slowly even if it binds. The scientists on the rail trace the picture: Fischer's rigid "lock and key" (complementary to the substrate, a view the transition-state idea later corrected), Koshland's "induced fit", and Phillips modeling how lysozyme bends its substrate out of shape.
