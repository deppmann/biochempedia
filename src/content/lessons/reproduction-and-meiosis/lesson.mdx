## Explain: one parent or two

<WordBank terms={['asexual reproduction', 'sexual reproduction', 'offspring', 'sex cells', 'body cells', 'meiosis', 'fertilization', 'chromosome pair', 'allele']} />

A species lasts only if its members reproduce. Every living thing alive today came from living things before it. Reproduction passes genetic information from parents to their **offspring**, the new living things made by reproduction.

**Asexual reproduction** is reproduction with one parent, where the offspring are genetically the same as the parent. Examples:

- a strawberry plant growing new plants from its runners
- a potato growing new plants from its eyes
- a spider plant making small plantlets
- yeast forming a bud that breaks off
- a bacterium splitting into two

The copies are not always perfect. Rare changes in DNA can still happen.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/reproduction-and-meiosis/one-parent-copies.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a strawberry plant sends a runner along the ground to a small new plant that has rooted in the soil. Small insets show a potato sprouting from its eyes and a yeast cell with a bud growing from one end." caption="Three ways to reproduce with one parent. Each new plant or yeast cell carries the same genetic information as its parent." />

**Sexual reproduction** is reproduction where two sex cells join, usually one from each of two parents. **Sex cells** are the cells that carry genetic information to the next generation: sperm and egg cells in animals, and cells in pollen and egg cells in plants. **Body cells** are all the other cells, such as skin, leaf or muscle cells. Offspring from sexual reproduction resemble their parents but are not copies of them.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Asexual reproduction makes offspring that are genetically the same as one parent; sexual reproduction joins two sex cells and makes offspring that vary.</Support>

## Explain: meiosis makes sex cells with half

Your body cells have their chromosomes in pairs. A **chromosome pair** is two matching chromosomes, one from your mother and one from your father. A typical human body cell has 46 chromosomes, in 23 pairs.

**Meiosis** is the kind of cell division that makes sex cells with half of the parent’s chromosomes, one from each pair. Before it starts, each chromosome is copied, as in mitosis. A copied chromosome is still one chromosome, made of two joined copies. You will learn the steps in high school. Here the result is what matters: a human egg or sperm normally has 23 chromosomes.

**Fertilization** is when a sperm cell and an egg cell join into one new cell. In humans:

> 23 chromosomes (egg) + 23 chromosomes (sperm) → 46 chromosomes (fertilized egg)

The fertilized egg has full pairs again, one chromosome of each pair from each parent. In plants there is an extra stage before the sex cells form, but they still end up with half.

## Explain: sorting pairs makes variety

Each gene can come in different versions. An **allele** is one version of a gene. You carry two alleles of most genes, one on each chromosome of a pair.

During meiosis, each pair splits up on its own, without regard to the other pairs. So a sex cell gets one chromosome from each pair, chosen by chance. If a parent has two different alleles, B and b, each sex cell has a 50 percent chance of carrying B and a 50 percent chance of carrying b.

Count the mixes. One pair gives 2 kinds of sex cells. Two pairs give 2 × 2 = 4. With 23 pairs, one person can make 2 multiplied by itself 23 times, which is 8,388,608 different mixes of whole chromosomes (when the two chromosomes in every pair differ). That is one reason each child gets a different mix. (Linking these mixes to traits comes in [Predicting Traits](/lessons/predicting-traits/).)

The alleles stay separate inside the child. They do not blend like paint, so a hidden allele can show up again in a later generation.

| | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Makes | Body cells | Sex cells |
| Used for | Growth, repair, asexual reproduction | Sexual reproduction |
| Chromosomes in new cells | Same as parent cell | Half, one from each pair |
| New cells | Match the parent cell | Each a different mix |

You met mitosis in [How Cells Divide](/lessons/cells-divide/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Chromosome pairs sort by chance in meiosis, so each sex cell carries a different mix of alleles, and offspring vary.</Support>

### Design problem

Do plants grown from runners vary less than plants grown from seeds? Plan an investigation with strawberry plants.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do strawberry plants grown from runners of one parent vary less in leaf length than plants grown from seeds of the same kind of strawberry?
- **Independent variable:** how the plant was made, from a runner (asexual) or from a seed (sexual).
- **Dependent variable:** the length of the largest leaf in millimetres after 8 weeks. Find the range, the longest minus the shortest, for each group.
- **Constants:** the same soil, pot size, water, light and temperature.
- **Control:** measure the parent plant too, to compare with the runner plants.
- **Repeated trials:** at least 10 plants in each group.
- **Safety:** wear gloves for soil, wash your hands, and do not eat any part of the plants.

A smaller range in the runner group would fit the idea that asexual offspring share the parent’s genetic information. Some variation will still appear in both groups, because light, water and space also affect growth. Runner plants may also start with food from the parent, which is another difference between the groups.

</details>
