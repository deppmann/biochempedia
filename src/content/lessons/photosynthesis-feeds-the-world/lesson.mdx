## Explain: how a plant makes its own food

<WordBank terms={['producer', 'photosynthesis', 'radiant energy', 'chemical energy', 'chloroplast', 'chlorophyll', 'sugar (glucose)', 'rate']} />

A deer cannot make its own food. It has to eat. A plant does not eat, yet it grows. A **producer** is a living thing that makes its own food. Green plants, algae and phytoplankton are producers. Phytoplankton are tiny producers that drift in water, such as algae and some bacteria.

Plants, algae and phytoplankton make food by **photosynthesis**, the process that uses the energy of sunlight to turn carbon dioxide and water into sugar and oxygen. The energy in sunlight is **radiant energy**, energy that travels as light. In words:

> carbon dioxide + water + radiant energy → sugar (glucose) + oxygen

In this summary the sugar is glucose. **Sugar (glucose)** is a small sugar molecule that stores energy.

The energy does not disappear and is not made. It changes form. Radiant energy becomes **chemical energy**, energy stored in the way atoms are joined in a molecule. Sugar holds more chemical energy than the carbon dioxide and water it came from.

## Explain: where it happens and where the sugar goes

In plants and algae, photosynthesis happens inside a **chloroplast**, a green organelle found in some plant cells, mostly in leaves. (Bacteria that photosynthesize have no chloroplasts.) Root cells, which sit in the dark, usually have none. Each chloroplast holds **chlorophyll**, a green pigment that captures radiant energy. [Plant cells and animal cells](/lessons/plant-and-animal-cells/) shows where chloroplasts sit in a cell.

The chloroplasts work as part of a larger system:
- Roots take in water from the soil.
- Leaves take in carbon dioxide from the air through tiny openings.
- Leaves catch the light, and chloroplasts make the sugar.

Carbon dioxide is only a tiny part of air. Even so, the carbon dioxide from the air becomes most of a plant’s solid material. Soil gives water and small amounts of minerals.

Once a plant has sugar (glucose), it can do three things with it:
- **Use it now.** The plant’s cells break sugar down to release energy for living. [Releasing energy from food](/lessons/releasing-energy-from-food/) shows how.
- **Store it.** The plant links sugars into starch and keeps it in roots, seeds and leaves for later.
- **Rearrange it.** The plant rebuilds sugar into other compounds, such as the wood of its trunk and the parts of new leaves.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/photosynthesis-feeds-the-world/where-the-sugar-goes.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a young tree with gold arrows carrying sugar from one large leaf to a new leaf at a branch tip, into the wood of the trunk, down to the roots where starch is stored, and to a cluster of seeds. An inset zooms into a leaf cell full of green chloroplasts." caption="Chloroplasts in the leaves make sugar, and the plant stores it as starch or rebuilds it into wood and new leaves. Some sugar is also used right away for energy." />

Photosynthesis feeds almost every food web. A rabbit eats clover. A fox eats the rabbit. Both live on energy the clover captured from sunlight. Some communities, such as those around deep-sea vents and in a few caves, run on chemicals instead of light, so "almost every" is the honest phrase. You will build full food webs in [Food webs and energy](/lessons/food-webs-and-energy/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Chloroplasts make sugar (glucose), and the plant uses it now, stores it, or rebuilds it into wood and leaves, which feeds almost all other life.</Support>

## Explain: what changes the rate

The **rate** of a process is how fast it happens, such as the amount made per hour. Three conditions change the rate of photosynthesis:
- **Light intensity.** More light usually speeds it up, up to a point.
- **Carbon dioxide.** More carbon dioxide can speed it up, up to a point.
- **Temperature.** Warmth can speed it up, but too much heat slows it down.

Think of a sandwich shop with one worker making bread and one adding fillings. Speeding up the bread maker helps only until the fillings worker falls behind. In a plant, the factors also affect each other, so the comparison is simpler than real life.

Photosynthesis is not the end of the story. Cells release the stored energy in respiration, and the two processes work as partners. [Partners in energy](/lessons/photosynthesis-and-respiration-partners/) puts them together.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Light, carbon dioxide and temperature change the rate of photosynthesis, and the rate stops rising when a different factor holds it back.</Support>

### Design problem

Plan an experiment to test how light affects the rate of photosynthesis in a water plant such as Elodea from a store. Name your variables, your constants, a control and how many trials you would run.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does the plant give off oxygen bubbles faster when a lamp is closer?
- **Independent variable:** distance from the lamp: 10, 20, 30 and 40 cm.
- **Dependent variable:** oxygen bubbles counted per minute.
- **Constants:** same length of plant, same amount of water and baking soda, same water temperature, same lamp. An LED lamp heats the water less than an old-style bulb, but check the water temperature with a thermometer at each distance.
- **Control:** one sprig in a tube wrapped in foil, so no light reaches it.
- **Trials:** three sprigs at each distance; find the mean bubble count.
- **Safety:** keep cords and lamps away from water. Wash your hands. Never pour the plant into a pond or stream, because it can spread.

If bubbles come faster as the lamp gets closer, then level off, light was the limit at first.

## Going deeper

### Sugar made inside the leaf, and the chemical equation

Inside the leaf, the plant first makes a smaller sugar, then builds glucose and other sugars from it. In chemistry this is written 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2, but you only need the words.

### How much of air is carbon dioxide

Carbon dioxide is about 0.04 percent of air.

### Where the blueberry curve comes from

The blueberry data are from leaves of four-year-old blueberry plants in an orchard in Chile. The study compared three pruning levels, and the curve used here is the conventional-pruning curve, with means of 12 leaves. The blueberry data come from one study.
