## Explain: how a plant makes its own food

<WordBank terms={['producer', 'photosynthesis', 'radiant energy', 'chemical energy', 'chloroplast', 'chlorophyll', 'sugar (glucose)', 'rate']} />

The first film sorted matter from energy; here are the words for it. A **producer** is a living thing that makes its own food: green plants, algae and phytoplankton. **Phytoplankton** are tiny producers that drift in water, including algae and some bacteria. **Photosynthesis** uses **radiant energy**, the energy that travels as light, to build sugar and oxygen from carbon dioxide and water. In words:

> carbon dioxide + water + radiant energy → sugar (glucose) + oxygen

**Sugar (glucose)** is a small sugar molecule that stores energy. Light supplies the energy; carbon dioxide and water supply the matter. Energy is not made or destroyed here. The plant transfers some radiant energy into **chemical energy**, energy stored in the way atoms are joined. Most of the rest leaves as heat. Sugar holds more chemical energy than the carbon dioxide and water used to build it.

## Explain: where it happens and where the sugar goes

In plants and algae, photosynthesis happens inside a **chloroplast**, a green organelle found in some cells, mostly in leaves. Each chloroplast holds **chlorophyll**, a green pigment that captures radiant energy. Root cells, which sit in the dark, usually have none. Bacteria that photosynthesize have none at all. [Plant cells and animal cells](/lessons/plant-and-animal-cells/) shows where chloroplasts sit.

The first film left out a surprise. Carbon dioxide enters leaves through tiny openings, and it is only a tiny part of air. Even so, it supplies most of a plant's solid material.

Stored sugar is linked into starch and kept in roots, seeds and leaves. [Releasing energy from food](/lessons/releasing-energy-from-food/) follows the use-it-now route, where cells break sugar down to release energy.

The first film's clover, rabbit and fox show why photosynthesis feeds almost every food web. The exceptions are some communities around deep-sea vents and in a few caves. They use chemical energy instead of light, so “almost every” matters. Build the longer paths in [Food webs and energy](/lessons/food-webs-and-energy/).

## Evidence: the willow rules out soil

### Design problem

Plan an experiment to test how light affects the rate of photosynthesis in a water plant such as Elodea from a store. Name your variables, your constants, a control and how many trials you would run.

**Predict.** Moving a lamp closer can also warm the water. If bubbles increase, can you tell whether light caused the change?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not yet. Temperature may be a second changed variable. Measure and hold it steady, or the test cannot separate the effects of light and temperature.

</details>

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does the plant give off oxygen bubbles faster when a lamp is closer?
- **Independent variable:** distance from the lamp: 10, 20, 30 and 40 cm.
- **Dependent variable:** oxygen bubbles counted per minute.
- **Constants:** same length of plant, same amount of water and baking soda, same water temperature, same lamp. An LED lamp heats the water less than an old-style bulb, but check the water temperature with a thermometer at each distance.
- **Control:** one sprig in a tube wrapped in foil, so no light reaches it.
- **Trials:** three sprigs at each distance; find the mean bubble count.
- **Safety:** keep cords and lamps away from water. Wash your hands. Never pour the plant into a pond or stream, because it can spread.

If bubbles come faster as the lamp gets closer, then level off, light was the limit at first.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Sugar made inside the leaf, and the chemical equation

Inside the leaf, the plant first makes a smaller sugar, then builds glucose and other sugars from it. In chemistry this is written 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2, but you only need the words.

### How much of air is carbon dioxide

Carbon dioxide is about 0.04 percent of air.

### Where the blueberry curve comes from

The blueberry data are from leaves of four-year-old blueberry plants in an orchard in Chile. The study compared three pruning levels, and the curve used here is the conventional-pruning curve, with means of 12 leaves.
