scientists:
  - name: "Michael Reth and Arthur Weiss"
    contribution: >-
      Reth noticed, by lining up published sequences, the paired-tyrosine motif now called the
      ITAM in the tails of antigen receptor partner chains. Weiss's laboratory showed that the
      zeta tail alone can signal, that the kinase Lck is required, and cloned ZAP-70.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Reth, Nature 338:383 (1989); Irving & Weiss, Cell 64:891 (1991)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the late 1980s the T-cell receptor had been cloned, and the part of it inside the cell
      contained no enzyme. In 1989 Michael Reth published a short letter in Nature with no
      experiment in it. He had set published sequences of partner-chain tails side by side and
      seen one arrangement in all of them: two tyrosines a fixed short distance apart, each
      followed, after two more residues, by a leucine or an isoleucine. He proposed that, once receptors were cross-linked, these residues
      would come together to form a binding site for the proteins that start the signal. In 1991 Irving and Weiss tested whether the zeta tail alone could signal, by joining the outer part of
      CD8 to the zeta tail; clustering this hybrid activated a T cell that could see no antigen
      through it. The next year Weiss's laboratory restored the kinase Lck to a mutant T-cell
      line that could not signal, and the calcium response came back. It also cloned ZAP-70, the
      kinase that reads the doubly phosphorylated motif. Knowing that one short tail carries the
      whole signal is what later let engineers attach that tail to an antibody fragment.
  - name: "Matthew Krummel and James Allison"
    contribution: >-
      They reported in 1995 that blocking CTLA-4 increases T-cell responses, so it opposes CD28.
      Allison's laboratory then showed that an antibody against CTLA-4 makes mice reject tumours.
    profileSourceAnchor: "James P. Allison, Nobel Lecture, 7 December 2018; Krummel & Allison, J. Exp. Med. 182:459 (1995)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      CTLA-4 looks like CD28 and binds the same B7 proteins, so in the early 1990s it was
      reasonable to think it was a second accelerator. Matthew Krummel, in James Allison's
      laboratory at Berkeley, tested this with antibodies on cultured T cells. Blocking CTLA-4
      raised proliferation, which is what releasing a brake does. Jeffrey Bluestone's laboratory
      had reported the same conclusion a year earlier, in 1994. Allison's laboratory then asked a
      different question. Dana Leach injected mice with tumour cells and gave some of them the
      blocking antibody, and the treated mice rejected their tumours. Allison had the experiment
      repeated blind, and by his own account he measured the tumours himself without knowing
      which mice were treated. The paper appeared in 1996. He has written that convincing a drug
      company to develop it was hard. The antibody that finally reached patients, ipilimumab, was
      approved in 2011, and Allison shared the 2018 Nobel Prize. The work showed that a cancer
      drug can act on the patient's T cells and never touch the tumour.
  - name: "Tasuku Honjo"
    dates: "born 1942"
    contribution: >-
      His laboratory cloned PD-1, showed that mice lacking it develop autoimmune disease, and
      with collaborators identified its partner PD-L1 and showed in mice that blocking the pair
      holds tumours back.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Tasuku Honjo, Nobel Lecture, 7 December 2018; Ishida et al., EMBO J. 11:3887 (1992)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In 1991 Yasumasa Ishida, in Tasuku Honjo's laboratory at Kyoto University, was looking for
      genes switched on in dying cells. He compared the messenger RNAs of two cell lines pushed
      toward death with those of the same lines left alone, and one new gene came out. The
      laboratory named it programmed death-1, on a guess about cell death that turned out to be
      wrong. Mice made without the gene at first looked normal. Bred onto an inbred background
      and watched for longer, they developed an autoimmune disease with arthritis and inflamed
      kidneys, reported in 1999. That marked PD-1 as a brake on the immune system. In 2000 Gordon
      Freeman's group and Honjo's showed that a B7-family protein, PD-L1, is its partner; Lieping Chen's group had described the same protein a year earlier as B7-H1. In 2002 Honjo's and Nagahiro
      Minato's groups reported that mice held tumours back better when the pair could not meet.
      Honjo has described how hard it was to find a company willing to develop an antibody.
      Antibodies against PD-1, nivolumab and pembrolizumab, are now used against many cancers,
      and Honjo shared the 2018 Nobel Prize with Allison.
  - name: "Zelig Eshhar and Carl June"
    contribution: >-
      Eshhar's laboratory joined antibody variable regions to T-cell receptor chains and got a
      working receptor with an antibody's specificity. June's laboratory took a later design,
      with a co-stimulatory domain added, into patients with leukaemia.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Gross, Waks & Eshhar, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 86:10024 (1989); Porter et al., N. Engl. J. Med. 365:725 (2011)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      A T cell normally recognises a peptide held by an MHC protein, while an antibody grips
      whole folded surfaces. In 1989 Gideon Gross, Tova Waks and Zelig Eshhar joined an
      antibody's variable regions to the constant regions of T-cell receptor chains. A T cell
      carrying the hybrid was activated by the antibody's target. Yoshihisa Kuwana and colleagues
      in Japan had published the same kind of construct in 1987. Later designs put the antibody
      fragment directly on the zeta tail. In early cancer trials, T cells carrying these often failed to persist in
      patients; they gave signal one without signal two. Adding a co-stimulatory tail from CD28
      or 4-1BB greatly improved this, and several groups worked it out, Michel Sadelain's among them. Carl
      June's laboratory at the University of Pennsylvania put such a receptor, aimed at the
      B-cell protein CD19, into patients' own T cells. In 2011 it reported an adult with
      leukaemia in whom the cells multiplied more than a thousandfold. In 2012 Stephan Grupp's
      team treated a girl with leukaemia, Emily Whitehead, the same way. Engineered T cells are now an
      approved treatment for several blood cancers.

techniques:
  - name: "Chimeric receptor (domain swap)"
    blurb: >-
      Join the outside of one membrane protein to the cytoplasmic tail of another and express
      the hybrid in a cell. If clustering the hybrid gives the response, that tail is enough to
      signal. This is how the zeta tail was shown to carry the signal, and it is the principle
      behind a CAR.
  - name: "Mutant cell line and rescue"
    blurb: >-
      Mutagenise a T-cell line, pick out cells that no longer respond when the receptor is
      stimulated, and find the damaged gene. Then put an intact copy back. If the response
      returns, the gene is required, which is how Lck was shown to be needed for the calcium
      response.
  - name: "Blocking antibody"
    blurb: >-
      An antibody that binds a surface receptor can keep its ligand off without triggering it.
      If a response rises when a receptor is blocked, the receptor was a brake. The same
      reagent, given to an animal, tests whether releasing that brake changes the course of a
      tumour.
  - name: "Knockout mouse"
    blurb: >-
      Delete one gene in the germ line and see what the animal can no longer do. Mice without
      CTLA-4 and mice without PD-1 showed what each brake holds back. The result can depend on
      age and genetic background, as it did for PD-1.

  - stem: "Which part of the T-cell receptor complex carries the ITAMs that Lck phosphorylates?"
    choices:
      - "The membrane-crossing helices of the alpha chain"
      - "The outer variable tips of the alpha and beta chains"
      - "The cytoplasmic tails of the CD3 and zeta chains"
      - "The outer domains of the co-receptor CD4 or CD8"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      ITAMs sit on the cytoplasmic tails of the partner chains, CD3 gamma, delta and epsilon and
      zeta, where a kinase inside the cell can reach them. The variable tips of alpha and beta
      are outside the cell and do the recognising. A membrane-crossing helix holds the complex
      together but carries no tyrosine motif for Lck. The outer domains of CD4 or CD8 bind the
      MHC protein; what the co-receptor brings inside the cell is a share of Lck, and it is not
      the substrate.
  - stem: "ZAP-70 has two SH2 domains side by side. What does that arrangement require of an ITAM before ZAP-70 binds it tightly?"
    choices:
      - "A phosphate on either one of its two tyrosines"
      - "A phosphate on both of its two tyrosines"
      - "A calcium ion bound between its two tyrosines"
      - "A ubiquitin chain attached beside its tyrosines"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Each SH2 domain grips one phosphotyrosine, and the two domains are spaced to match the two
      tyrosines of one ITAM, so tight binding needs both phosphates. A phosphate on only one
      tyrosine gives weak binding, which protects the cell from firing on a stray
      phosphorylation. Ubiquitin is a different tag read by different domains, and SH2 domains
      do not bind it. Calcium rises later, downstream of PLC-gamma, and plays no part in docking
      ZAP-70.
  - stem: "In the classic experiments with cultured T cells, the antigen receptor was stimulated while CD28 was left unengaged. What did the cells do?"
    choices:
      - "They switched to secreting their antigen receptor"
      - "They divided and made interleukin-2 just as usual"
      - "They died at once from an overload of calcium"
      - "They became unresponsive to later stimulation"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Signal one without signal two left the cells anergic: alive, but unable to respond
      properly when stimulated again, which protects tissues that display a peptide without B7.
      Normal division and interleukin-2 production need CD28 as well as the antigen receptor.
      T cells do not secrete their receptor; releasing the antigen receptor as antibody is what
      a B cell does. A calcium rise is part of normal activation and is not what switched these
      cells off.
  - stem: "CTLA-4 and CD28 bind the same B7 proteins on the presenting cell. Why does a T cell slow down once CTLA-4 reaches its surface?"
    choices:
      - "CTLA-4 carries a kinase domain that phosphorylates Lck"
      - "CTLA-4 covers the peptide groove of the MHC protein"
      - "CTLA-4 cuts the zeta tails off the receptor complex"
      - "CTLA-4 binds B7 more tightly and so starves CD28"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      CTLA-4 competes for the ligand of the second signal. It binds B7 with higher affinity than
      CD28 does and can strip B7 from the presenting cell, so CD28 gets less. CTLA-4 has no
      kinase domain; its short tail mainly controls its own traffic to and from the surface. It
      binds B7 and not the MHC protein, so the receptor can still see its peptide. It is not a
      protease and does not cut zeta: signal one is left intact while signal two is withdrawn.

      - "Add a second antibody fragment against the same target"
      - "Remove the hinge so the fragment lies against the membrane"
      - "Replace the zeta tail with the tail of the brake PD-1"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      A zeta-only receptor delivers signal one without signal two, the condition under which
      T cells respond weakly and do not persist. Putting a co-stimulatory domain from CD28 or

    rationale: >-
      The concentration that half-saturates a receptor is its Kd, so Kd is 4 µM for CD28 and
      0.4 µM for CTLA-4. For CD28, 0.4 / (4 + 0.4) is about 0.09, or 9%. For CTLA-4,

      occupancy rises in proportion to affinity, but a receptor is only half full at its Kd.
      The answer of 50% and 91% is what you would find at 4 µM B7-1, not at 0.4 µM.

explainLevels:
  - level: "eli5"
    label: "Like I'm five"
    text: >-
      Your body has guard cells called T cells. A guard needs two things before it attacks: it
      has to spot a bad sign on another cell, and a helper has to tell it to go ahead. Guards
      also have brakes so they do not hurt the healthy parts of you. Some cancer medicines take
      the foot off a brake, and others give the guard a new pair of eyes.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: >-
      T cells are white blood cells that check other cells for signs of infection or cancer.
      Each one has a receptor on its surface that recognises one particular fragment of protein
      displayed by another cell. The receptor cannot send a message into the cell by itself, so
      partner proteins beside it do that once an enzyme tags them with phosphate groups. To
      avoid mistakes, the T cell needs a second go-ahead signal from the other cell before it
      acts, and it carries brakes, among them CTLA-4 and PD-1, that slow it down afterwards.
      Tumours can press the PD-1 brake to protect themselves. Two cancer treatments come from
      this: antibody drugs that block a brake, and T cells given a designed receptor that points
      them at cancer cells.
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      An antigen receptor separates recognition from signalling. The alpha and beta chains of
      the T-cell receptor bind a peptide held by an MHC protein but have almost no cytoplasmic
      tail; the associated CD3 and zeta chains carry ITAMs, pairs of tyrosines at a fixed
      spacing. The Src-family kinase Lck, anchored in the membrane and partly carried on the co-receptor CD4 or CD8, phosphorylates
      both tyrosines. ZAP-70 binds the pair through its tandem SH2 domains, is activated, and
      phosphorylates the scaffold LAT. From LAT, PLC-gamma produces IP3 (calcium, calcineurin,
      NFAT) and DAG (protein kinase C, NF-κB), and Ras drives ERK. Full activation also needs
      CD28 bound to B7 on the presenting cell. Two inhibitory receptors limit the response:
      CTLA-4 competes for B7, and PD-1 recruits the phosphatase SHP-2. Antibodies that block
      either one release T cells against tumours, and a chimeric antigen receptor fuses an
      antibody fragment to co-stimulatory and zeta tails.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      The receptor complex has ten ITAMs (one each on CD3 gamma, CD3 delta and the two CD3
      epsilon chains, three on each zeta), with the consensus YxxL/I-x(6-8)-YxxL/I. Selectivity
      comes from coincidence detection: the tandem SH2 domains of ZAP-70 bind a doubly
      phosphorylated ITAM far more tightly than a singly phosphorylated one, and in the crystal
      structure the pocket for one phosphotyrosine lies at the interface of the two domains.
      Binding releases ZAP-70 from autoinhibition and Lck phosphorylates it. Children without
      functional ZAP-70 have a severe immunodeficiency in which CD8 T cells are nearly absent
      and CD4 T cells are present but do not signal. CTLA-4 acts largely from outside the cell, much of it from regulatory T cells,
      by higher-affinity, bivalent binding to B7 and by removing B7 from the presenting cell.
      PD-1 acts from inside, mainly through its phosphorylated ITSM motif, which SHP-2 grips with both of its SH2 domains.

      An open question is what PD-1 dephosphorylates in a living T cell. In a membrane system
      rebuilt from purified proteins, CD28 was dephosphorylated at much lower PD-1 densities
      than the zeta chain. Proteomic work in primary mouse T cells found the antigen receptor
      and CD28 pathways damped to a similar degree, and T cells lacking SHP-2 still respond to
      PD-1 blockade, perhaps because the related phosphatase SHP-1 substitutes.

**The receptor that tells a T cell it has found an infected cell has a tail inside the cell only a few amino acids long, with no enzyme anywhere in it.** Unlike the cytokine receptors in [JAK-STAT signalling](/lessons/jak-stat), its own chains do not even hold a kinase. The first film animated how partner chains carry that signal inside; here are the experiments that proved it, the speed of the branches it launches, and how two brakes and two cancer therapies follow from the wiring.

<Support>**The one idea.** Two phosphates on partner ITAMs switch on the antigen receptor; full activation needs a second signal, and checkpoints brake outside or inside. **Scope:** This rule describes alpha-beta T-cell activation and the cancer therapies built from it (B-cell receptors follow the same partner-ITAM plan). Signal two is required for a T cell meeting its antigen for the first time to avoid anergy, and for engineered CAR T cells to multiply and persist; CTLA-4 and PD-1 are two of several brakes, and CAR signalling depends less on LAT.</Support>

## One set of chains recognises, another set signals

A **T cell** is a white blood cell that inspects other cells' surfaces. An **antigen** is whatever its receptor recognises: for most T cells, a short peptide held in the groove of an **MHC protein**, a display molecule on another cell. The T-cell receptor is a pair of chains, alpha and beta, whose outer tips vary from one T cell to the next, with no catalytic domain inside.

The receptor travels with partner chains—**CD3** gamma, delta and epsilon, plus **zeta**—which it needs to reach the surface. In 1989 Michael Reth published a short *Nature* letter with no experiment in it. Lining up published partner-chain tails—zeta, CD3 gamma and delta, MB-1 (now Ig-alpha) of the B-cell receptor, and two chains of the mast-cell IgE receptor—he found one shared pattern, written here in modern form:

> **Y-x-x-(L or I), then 6 to 8 residues, then Y-x-x-(L or I)**

Here x is any amino acid. Reth proposed that once receptors were cross-linked, these conserved residues would form a binding site for the proteins starting the signal. The pattern is now called an **ITAM** (immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif). One T-cell receptor complex carries ten ITAMs: three on each of its two zeta tails and one on each of its four CD3 tails.

**Predict.** In 1991 Irving and Weiss fused the outer and membrane-crossing parts of CD8 to the cytoplasmic zeta tail alone, creating a hybrid that recognised no antigen. If they clustered it on a T cell with an anti-CD8 antibody, should the T cell activate?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Yes. Clustering the CD8–zeta hybrid set off activation signals (other laboratories reported similar hybrids that year). The zeta tail alone was enough to signal.

</details>

## The first minute: Lck, ZAP-70 and three branches from LAT

The first film introduced the Src-family tyrosine kinase **Lck** ([protein interaction modules](/lessons/protein-interaction-modules)), held to the inner membrane by fatty acid chains and already partly active at rest, with a share riding on the co-receptor **CD4** or **CD8** gripping an unvarying part of the same MHC protein. At rest, the phosphatase **CD45** removes phosphates Lck adds. In one leading model, pressing both cells close pushes bulky CD45 out of the contact so phosphates accumulate on engaged receptors; how triggering works is still debated.

**Predict.** Finding Lck attached to CD4 and CD8 in broken-open T cells shows association, not necessity. In 1992 Straus and Weiss studied a mutant T-cell line with intact receptors, a faulty *Lck* gene and no calcium response. What step proved Lck was required?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Restoring an intact *Lck* gene to the mutant line and seeing the calcium response return, which ruled out an unrelated defect elsewhere in the line.

</details>

Once Lck phosphorylates both ITAM tyrosines, the tyrosine kinase **ZAP-70** docks via two side-by-side **SH2 domains** (phosphotyrosine-binding modules) spaced to fit both marks. Tight binding needs both phosphates, preventing a stray phosphate from firing the cell. Docking opens ZAP-70, Lck phosphorylates it, and ZAP-70 phosphorylates **LAT**, a membrane scaffold ([adaptors and scaffolds](/lessons/adaptors-and-scaffolds)) whose phosphotyrosines become SH2 docking sites.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

NFAT, NF-κB and AP-1 meet on the control regions of genes such as **interleukin-2**, a cytokine driving T-cell division through a JAK-STAT receptor. Triggering the receptor with a light-activated peptide phosphorylated LAT within **4 seconds** and raised calcium within **6 to 7 seconds** (Huse and colleagues, 2007).

Firing the receptor in seconds is only half the story: because receptors vary almost at random and some fit self peptides, a T cell needs a second check before committing—and brakes to hold it back.

## One signal is not enough: the second signal and two brakes

The second film traced how signal two licenses activation and how two brakes act outside or inside the membrane; here are the binding numbers, knockout mice and clinical outcomes behind them.

**Signal two (CD28 and B7).** For a T cell meeting its antigen for the first time, **CD28** must bind a **B7** protein (B7-1 or B7-2) on the presenting cell, which displays far more B7 after detecting signs of infection. CD28 has no enzyme either: once phosphorylated, a tyrosine motif in its short tail recruits **PI 3-kinase** ([PI 3-kinase, Akt and insulin](/lessons/pi3k-akt-insulin)) and Grb2. In the classic experiments on cultured T cells, signal one alone did worse than nothing: the cells entered a lasting unresponsive state called **anergy**.

On the left, the T-cell receptor binds a peptide held on an MHC protein while CD28 has no partner, and the outcome box reads Anergy. **Brake one: CTLA-4 (outside).** Activated T cells put up **CTLA-4**, a close relative of CD28 binding the same B7 proteins, while **regulatory T cells** (a subset that restrains other T cells) carry CTLA-4 all the time. On isolated proteins, CTLA-4 binds B7 roughly ten times more tightly than CD28—for soluble B7-1, the dissociation constant Kd (the free ligand concentration at which half the receptors are occupied) is near **0.4 µM** for CTLA-4 versus **4 µM** for CD28.

**Predict.** Using simple one-site binding, where fraction bound = [L] / (Kd + [L]), suppose free B7-1 is **1.0 µM**. Roughly what percentage of CD28 and of CTLA-4 is occupied?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

CD28: 1.0 / (4.0 + 1.0) = 0.20, or **20%**. CTLA-4: 1.0 / (0.4 + 1.0) = 0.71, or about **71%**—over three times the occupancy, even in solution.

</details>

On a cell the gap is wider: a CTLA-4 dimer grips two B7 molecules at once and physically pulls B7 off the presenting cell into the T cell to be destroyed (David Sansom's laboratory, 2011). Blocking CTLA-4 increased cultured T-cell responses (Jeffrey Bluestone's laboratory, 1994; Matthew Krummel and James Allison, 1995). Knockout mice lacking CTLA-4 (Arlene Sharpe's and Tak Mak's laboratories) died within weeks of birth with unchecked lymphocyte proliferation, and deleting CTLA-4 from regulatory T cells alone caused fatal **autoimmunity** (an immune attack on the body's own tissues) in mice (Shimon Sakaguchi's laboratory, 2008).

**Brake two: PD-1 (inside).** Tasuku Honjo's laboratory in Kyoto cloned **PD-1** (programmed death-1) in 1992. Displayed on activated T cells, PD-1 binds **PD-L1** on many healthy cells and many tumours. Two tyrosines on PD-1's tail are phosphorylated when PD-L1 binds; the main one, in the **ITSM** (immunoreceptor tyrosine-based switch motif—a phosphatase-docking site, not an ITAM), recruits the tyrosine phosphatase **SHP-2**, an enzyme that strips phosphates off other proteins. In a membrane system rebuilt from purified proteins, the target SHP-2 reached most readily was CD28, and work in T cells suggests the antigen-receptor pathway is damped as well (see **Going deeper**). Mice lacking PD-1 looked normal at first; aged on an inbred background, they developed milder, later lupus-like autoimmunity (1999).

## Releasing a brake to treat cancer

Many tumours carry altered proteins T cells can recognise and display PD-L1. Antibodies covering a brake act on the patient's own T cells, never touching the tumour, so a stalled response runs on. In mice, blocking CTLA-4 caused tumour rejection (Dana Leach, Krummel and Allison, 1996), and preventing PD-1 from meeting PD-L1 held tumours back (Honjo's and Nagahiro Minato's groups, 2002). Allison and Honjo shared the 2018 Nobel Prize.

**Predict.** Given the knockout mice above (fatal early proliferation without CTLA-4; milder, later autoimmunity without PD-1), what clinical side effects follow checkpoint blockade, and which target should trigger them more often?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Autoimmune inflammation of the gut, skin, liver and hormone glands—and, as the knockouts predicted, generally more common with CTLA-4 blockade than with PD-1 blockade.

</details>

| Brake | Location | Mechanism | Blocking antibodies (approval) & clinical limits |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **CTLA-4** | Outside | Outbids CD28 for B7 and strips B7; heavily used by regulatory T cells | **Ipilimumab** (2011, advanced melanoma, a skin cancer); autoimmune inflammation generally more common |
| **PD-1** | Inside | Phosphorylated ITSM recruits **SHP-2** to erase activating phosphates | **Nivolumab**, **pembrolizumab** (2014, many cancers); autoimmune inflammation, generally milder |

Across both classes, many tumours still do not respond at all.

## Building a receptor from parts

Blocking a brake helps only when the patient already has T cells recognising the tumour. Otherwise, engineers build a new receptor from modular parts. In 1989 Gideon Gross, Tova Waks and Zelig Eshhar joined antibody variable regions to T-cell receptor constant chains so T cells responded to an antibody's target (Yoshihisa Kuwana and colleagues published a similar construct in 1987). Because an antibody grips a folded surface protein directly, the hybrid needs no peptide or MHC. Later single-chain designs—a **chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)**—link an outer single-chain antibody fragment via a hinge and transmembrane segment to the cytoplasmic zeta tail (three ITAMs).

Remember the guiding rule: full activation needs a second signal as well as partner ITAMs. Early zeta-only CAR T cells killed targets in a dish yet often failed to multiply and persist in patients (signal one without signal two). Several groups, including Michel Sadelain's, fixed this by adding the cytoplasmic domain of **CD28** or **4-1BB** to the same chain so one binding event delivers both signals. Carl June's laboratory put a CD19-directed CAR into patients' own T cells and in 2011 reported an adult with leukaemia (a blood cancer) whose cells multiplied more than a thousandfold.

Two hazards follow directly from this wiring:
- **On-target attack on healthy cells:** The antibody fragment cannot tell cancer cells from healthy cells carrying the same protein. Because normal B cells also display CD19, the treatment removes them and patients may need supplied antibodies.
- **Whole-body cytokine inflammation:** When many CAR T cells fire at once, their cytokines—multiplied by the patient's own macrophages—can severely inflame the whole body.

### Design problem

You want to engineer T cells against a tumour displaying surface protein Marker-X, which also appears at low levels on cells lining the lung. Using parts from an antibody, the T-cell receptor complex and a co-stimulatory receptor, assign recognition, signal one and signal two—and name two harms your receptor could cause, explaining why Marker-X on lung cells is more dangerous than CD19 on B cells.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Parts.** Join anti-Marker-X antibody variable regions with a short linker, hinge and transmembrane segment (recognition); attach the zeta tail's three ITAMs (signal one); splice in a CD28 or 4-1BB cytoplasmic domain (signal two) for persistence.

**Harms.** First, on-target killing of healthy Marker-X lung lining cells—far more life-threatening than losing CD19 B cells, whose antibodies can be replaced. Second, macrophage-amplified whole-body cytokine inflammation when many CAR T cells fire together.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Cloning the T-cell receptor and the B-cell parallel

Because T cells keep their receptor in the membrane and release none of it, its sequence took years to find. In March 1984 Tak Mak's laboratory (Toronto) and Mark Davis's (NIH and Stanford) reported a receptor chain gene in the same issue of *Nature*, predicting an antibody-like protein; two months later Yueh-hsiu Chien, with Davis, showed the gene is cut and rejoined in T cells, as antibody genes are in B cells (the white blood cells that make antibodies).

The **B-cell receptor** follows the same partner-ITAM plan: a membrane-bound antibody with a three-amino-acid tail (in its IgM form), flanked by **Ig-alpha** and **Ig-beta** carrying one ITAM each. A Src-family kinase, mainly **Lyn**, phosphorylates the ITAMs, and **Syk** (a relative of ZAP-70) reads them.

### Lck association, PD-1 targets, checkpoint nuances and CAR signalling

In 1988 Rudd and colleagues, then Veillette and colleagues, reported that Lck comes out of broken-open T cells attached to CD4 and CD8, setting up Straus and Weiss's 1992 genetic rescue.

Which target SHP-2 dephosphorylates most strongly in vivo remains active research: in a membrane system rebuilt from purified proteins in 2017, SHP-2 reached CD28 most readily, while work in T cells suggests the antigen-receptor pathway is damped as well. In mice, part of a CTLA-4 antibody's effect also comes from removing regulatory T cells inside the tumour. Finally, a CAR's intracellular signals are not identical to the native T-cell receptor's and depend less on LAT.
