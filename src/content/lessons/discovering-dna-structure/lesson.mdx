    profileSourceAnchor: "Franklin and Gosling, Molecular configuration in sodium thymonucleate, Nature 171:740-741 (1953), doi:10.1038/171740a0."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling worked with thin fibers pulled from DNA that had been
      purified from calf thymus. They shone X-rays through the fibers and recorded the patterns of
      spots the rays made on film. They found that the same DNA gave two different patterns as the
      air around it got wetter or drier, so the way a sample is prepared matters. From the wetter
      form, called the B form, they measured a repeat of about 3.4 nanometers along the fiber and a
      width of about 2 nanometers. They wrote that a helix was highly probable, a careful word for
      strong but indirect evidence. Their paper appeared in Nature in April 1953, in the same issue as the double helix model. Measurements like theirs let any model of DNA be checked against real
      numbers.
  - name: "Frederick Sanger"
    contribution: >-
      With Nicklen and Coulson, published in 1977 a way to read the order of bases in
      DNA by stopping the copying of a strand at each kind of base.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Sanger, Nicklen and Coulson, DNA sequencing with chain-terminating inhibitors, PNAS 74:5463-5467 (1977), doi:10.1073/pnas.74.12.5463."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the 1970s, biologists knew DNA was a double helix, but reading the order of its bases was
      slow and hard. Frederick Sanger and his colleagues Nicklen and Coulson found a
      faster way. They let an enzyme copy a DNA strand in a test tube. They also added a small
      amount of altered building blocks that stop the copy as soon as one is added. The result is
      a mix of copies that end at different places, and their lengths reveal where each base
      sits. They first used the method on DNA from a virus that infects bacteria, not on human
      DNA. They published it in 1977. The Human Genome Project later used improved versions of this chain-stopping idea to read human chromosomes.

  - name: "Base-composition analysis"

    blurb: >-

## What each model can answer

<WordBank terms={['model', 'nucleotide', 'base pairing', 'double helix', 'X-ray diffraction', 'DNA sequencing', 'genome', 'Human Genome Project', 'chromosome', 'reference sequence']} />

A **model** is a simplified representation that helps explain or predict something real. A city map shows streets but not the people on them.

A **nucleotide** is one building block of DNA, made of a sugar, a phosphate group and a base. DNA has four bases, written A, T, G and C. The order of the bases carries the instructions, as [the DNA structure lesson](/lessons/dna-structure-and-copying/) shows.

| Model | Good for showing | Leaves out |
|---|---|---|
| Flat ladder with letters | Which bases pair, and the two backbones | The twist, and all the atoms |
| Double helix ribbon | How two strands coil around each other | Individual atoms, and often the actual base order |
| Base-letter string (ATTGC...) | The order of bases, for comparing DNA | Shape, size and the partner strand |
| Space-filling model | How much space the atoms fill | Base pairs hidden inside the molecule |

{/* DIAGRAM: the same short stretch of DNA drawn four ways side by side: 1 flat ladder, 2 helix ribbon, 3 letter string, 4 space-filling balls, with a bracket marking the same base pair in each */}

The map analogy has a limit: a map is drawn from a place you can visit. The B-form double helix is about 2 nanometers wide, far too narrow to see with your eyes or a light microscope. Its models had to be built from measurements.

## Three kinds of evidence, one model

DNA was identified in 1869, long before anyone knew its shape. By the early 1950s, the three kinds of evidence in the first film could rule models out.

| Evidence | What a model had to explain |
|---|---|
| Base amounts | Erwin Chargaff's group found A close to T and G close to C in a sample. |
| X-ray diffraction | X-rays scattered from lined-up DNA fibers into spots. Their spacing pointed to a helix about 2 nanometers wide and gave repeat distances, but it was not a photograph of the bases. |
| Chemistry and model building | The charged phosphate backbones had to face the surrounding water, and the known sizes of the parts had to fit together. |

Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling measured diffraction patterns, including one often called Photo 51. Their work on how DNA fibers changed as the air got wetter or drier also supported the idea that the phosphates sit on the outside, facing water.

**Predict.** A species has more G and C than A and T. Does that rule out base pairing in the double helix?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Pairing predicts only that G is close to C and A is close to T. Chargaff's work showed that the four bases do not come in equal amounts, and that the base mix differs among species.

</details>

James Watson and Francis Crick's paper said they were stimulated by knowing the general nature of unpublished results and ideas from Maurice Wilkins, Franklin and their coworkers at King's College London.

Their model, published on 25 April 1953, was the **double helix**: two strands of DNA coiled around each other, running in opposite directions, with the backbones outside and bases inside. **Base pairing**, the rule that A pairs with T and G pairs with C across the two strands, explained the equal amounts of A and T. It also suggested how DNA could be copied, because each strand fixes the order of its partner.

## Reading the letters since 1977

**DNA sequencing** is any method that reads the order of bases in a piece of DNA. Frederick Sanger and his colleagues Nicklen and Coulson published the method in the second film in 1977. Its core trick: a small amount of stop blocks, mixed in with ordinary ones, stops copies at many different matching positions. The 1977 method ran four separate reactions, each with a stop block for only one kind of base. So the reaction a copy came from told which base ended it. Sorted by length, shortest first, the copies reveal the base order of the new, complementary strand.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/discovering-dna-structure/sequencing-copies-by-length.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a top row of eight lettered, coloured blocks labelled template strand (T A C G G A T C). Below it a staircase of eight copies, from one block long (labelled shortest copy) to eight blocks long (labelled longest copy), each ending in a coloured stop block that pairs with the template. An arrow reads the copies from short to long, and the stop letters, read in that order, spell A T G C C T A G, labelled as the new complementary strand." caption="The idea behind Sanger's 1977 method: copies of a strand stop at each base, and their lengths reveal the order of the bases." />

**Predict.** One strand reads GATTC. What does its partner read when each partner base is written directly underneath?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

CTAAG. G pairs with C, A with T, T with A and C with G. Biologists often write the partner strand in the opposite direction, which would reverse these letters, but here each letter sits under its match.

</details>

A **genome** is all the DNA in one set of an organism’s chromosomes, and a **chromosome** is one long DNA molecule wound with proteins. A human genome has about 3 billion base pairs.

The **Human Genome Project** was an international project, from October 1990 to April 2003, that read the order of bases in human DNA. It mostly used improved Sanger sequencing and produced a **reference sequence**, a standard human DNA sequence used for comparison.

The second film drew the reference as a ruler for lining up other sequences, but this ruler has limits. It was pieced together from several donors, with about 70 percent from one donor, and it still had gaps. Human chromosome 22, published in 1999, was the first human chromosome sequenced. Its sequence covered the gene-rich part and still had gaps.

In 2022 a team reported a human sequence with no gaps in the 22 numbered chromosomes and the X, and a finished Y followed in 2023. In 2023 another team released a draft “pangenome” built from 47 people, to show how human DNA varies. The main features of the double helix model still hold, though later measurements refined details such as how many base pairs make one turn. Sequencing added the letters, how many there are, and how they differ between people.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Sequencing reads the order of DNA bases, and since 1990 it has grown to read whole human genomes.</Support>

### Design problem

Sequencing machines read short pieces of DNA, and a computer joins overlapping pieces back together. Repeated stretches make this hard. Plan an investigation with paper strips to test whether longer pieces make it easier to rebuild a sequence that contains a repeat.

**Predict.** Will 6-letter, 12-letter or 20-letter strips be easiest to rebuild correctly around an 8-letter repeat?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Write a 60-letter made-up DNA sequence that contains the same 8-letter stretch twice. Make several copies and cut them into overlapping strips.

- Independent variable: strip length, such as 6, 12 and 20 letters.
- Dependent variable: whether a classmate who has not seen the original rebuilds it correctly, and how long it takes.
- Constants: the same original sequence, the same number of copies, and a cut at random places each time.
- Control: a version with no repeated stretch, cut into 6-letter strips, to show the puzzle is solvable without a repeat.
- Trials and sample size: at least five classmates for each strip length, each working alone.

If short strips often give the wrong order around the repeat while 20-letter strips do not, longer reads help. This is one reason long-read methods helped fill gaps in 2022. A limit: paper strips have no reading errors, and real sequencers make some.

</details>
