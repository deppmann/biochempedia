    story: >-

    story: >-
      By the 1970s, biologists knew DNA was a double helix, but reading the order of its bases was
      slow and hard. Frederick Sanger and his colleagues Nicklen and Coulson found a
      faster way. They let an enzyme copy a DNA strand in a test tube. They also added a small
      amount of altered building blocks that stop the copy as soon as one is added. The result is

      DNA. They published it in 1977. The Human Genome Project later used improved versions of this chain-stopping idea to read human chromosomes.

## Explain: every DNA model leaves something out

<WordBank terms={['model', 'nucleotide', 'base pairing', 'double helix', 'X-ray diffraction', 'DNA sequencing', 'genome', 'Human Genome Project', 'chromosome', 'reference sequence']} />

A **model** is a simplified representation that helps explain or predict something real. A city map is a model: it shows streets but not the people on them.

DNA is built from units called nucleotides. A **nucleotide** is one building block of DNA, made of a sugar, a phosphate group and a base. DNA has four bases, written A, T, G and C. The order of the bases carries the instructions, as [the DNA structure lesson](/lessons/dna-structure-and-copying/) shows.

Each common model of DNA is good at one job:

| Model | Good for showing | Leaves out |
|---|---|---|
| Flat ladder with letters | Which bases pair, and the two backbones | The twist, and all the atoms |
| Double helix ribbon | How two strands coil around each other | Individual atoms, and often the actual base order |
| Base-letter string (ATTGC...) | The order of bases, for comparing DNA | Shape, size and the partner strand |
| Space-filling model | How much space the atoms fill | Base pairs hidden inside the molecule |

{/* DIAGRAM: the same short stretch of DNA drawn four ways side by side: 1 flat ladder, 2 helix ribbon, 3 letter string, 4 space-filling balls, with a bracket marking the same base pair in each */}

These are four pictures of one substance, not four kinds of DNA. The map analogy breaks down in one way: a map is drawn from a place you can visit and see. A DNA molecule is only about 2 nanometers wide, far too narrow to see with your eyes or a light microscope, so DNA models are built from measurements.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Each DNA model shows some features and hides others, so scientists pick the model that fits the question.</Support>

## Explain: three kinds of evidence, one model

DNA was identified in 1869, long before anyone knew its shape. By the early 1950s, scientists had three kinds of evidence to work with.

- Base amounts. Chemists broke DNA into its bases and measured each one. Erwin Chargaff’s group found that in a DNA sample, the amount of A is close to the amount of T, and G is close to C. Other teams showed that the four bases do not simply come in equal amounts in every species.
- X-ray patterns. In **X-ray diffraction**, X-rays shone through lined-up DNA fibers scatter into a pattern of spots on film. Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling measured such patterns, including one often called Photo 51. The spacing of the spots pointed to a helix about 2 nanometers wide, with phosphates on the outside. The pattern gave shape clues, not a photograph of the bases.
- Model building. James Watson and Francis Crick fitted these facts together with the known chemistry of the parts. Their paper said they had been stimulated by knowing the general nature of unpublished results and ideas from Maurice Wilkins, Franklin and their coworkers at King’s College London.

The result, published on 25 April 1953, was the **double helix**: two strands of DNA coiled around each other, running in opposite directions, with the backbones outside and bases inside. **Base pairing** is the rule that A pairs with T and G pairs with C across the two strands. That rule explained the equal amounts of A and T. It also suggested how DNA could be copied, because each strand fixes the order of its partner.

The authors were careful. Watson and Crick called their model unproved until it could be checked against more exact results. Franklin and Gosling called a helix highly probable. Later experiments tested the model and supported it.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** The double helix model was built to fit base-amount data, X-ray measurements and chemistry together, and later tests supported it.</Support>

## Explain: reading the letters since 1977

Knowing the shape did not tell anyone the order of the bases. **DNA sequencing** is any method that reads the order of bases in a piece of DNA. In 1977 Frederick Sanger and his colleagues published a method that stops copies of a strand at each kind of base. The lengths of the copies reveal the base order.

A **genome** is all the DNA in one set of an organism’s chromosomes. A **chromosome** is one long DNA molecule wound with proteins. A human genome has about 3 billion base pairs.

The **Human Genome Project** was an international project, from October 1990 to April 2003, that read the order of bases in human DNA. It mostly used improved Sanger sequencing. Human chromosome 22 was the first finished human chromosome, published in 1999. The project produced a **reference sequence**, a standard human DNA sequence that other sequences are compared against. It was pieced together from several donors, and it still had gaps.

Newer methods kept going. In 2022 a team reported a human sequence with no gaps in the 22 numbered chromosomes and the X, and a finished Y followed in 2023. In 2023 another team released a draft “pangenome” built from 47 people, to show how human DNA varies. The double helix model still holds. Sequencing added the letters, how many there are, and how they differ between people.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Sequencing reads the order of DNA bases, and since 1990 it has grown from single chromosomes to whole human genomes.</Support>

### Design problem

Sequencing machines read short pieces of DNA, and a computer joins overlapping pieces back together. Repeated stretches make this hard. Plan an investigation with paper strips to test whether longer pieces make it easier to rebuild a sequence that contains a repeat.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Write a 60-letter made-up DNA sequence that contains the same 8-letter stretch twice. Make several copies and cut them into overlapping strips.

- Independent variable: strip length, such as 6, 12 and 20 letters.
- Dependent variable: whether a classmate who has not seen the original rebuilds it correctly, and how long it takes.
- Constants: the same original sequence, the same number of copies, and a cut at random places each time.
- Control: a version with no repeated stretch, cut into 6-letter strips, to show the puzzle is solvable without a repeat.
- Trials and sample size: at least five classmates for each strip length, each working alone.

If short strips often give the wrong order around the repeat while 20-letter strips do not, longer reads help. This is one reason long-read methods helped fill gaps in 2022. A limit: paper strips have no reading errors, and real sequencers make some.

</details>
