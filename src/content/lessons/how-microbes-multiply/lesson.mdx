**Under the best laboratory conditions, one E. coli cell can split into two about every 20 minutes. A cold sore can heal, then come back later in about the same spot.** The herpes virus behind cold sores cannot copy itself, yet it can stay in your body for years. How can something that never divides keep coming back, and what stops a bacterium from doubling forever?

> **The one idea.** A bacterium copies itself; a virus is copied by a host cell. A bacterium grows and splits in two, so with food its numbers double every generation, until food runs low or wastes build up. A virus never divides: a **host cell**, the living cell a virus infects, does the copying. In the lytic cycle it builds new viruses right away. In the lysogenic cycle it copies only the viral DNA, along with its own, each time it divides; new viruses are built only if that DNA switches to the lytic cycle, which may never happen.
>
> *Scope:* bacteria and the viruses that infect them, with phage lambda as the model for the two paths. Some other phages have different cycles; a few release new phages without bursting the cell. Budding still makes a new cell; conjugation adds genes, not cells. Herpes is a comparison, not the same mechanism.

## Explain: how bacteria make more bacteria

<WordBank terms={['asexual reproduction', 'binary fission', 'budding', 'conjugation', 'plasmid', 'bacteriophage', 'host cell', 'lytic cycle', 'lysogenic cycle', 'prophage', 'doubling time']} />

**Asexual reproduction** is reproduction with one parent, where the offspring get copies of that parent’s DNA. Most bacteria reproduce this way, mostly by **binary fission**, the copy, grow and split of the first film. It looks like mitosis from [the cell cycle lesson](/lessons/cell-cycle-and-mitosis/), but it is simpler: a bacterium has no nucleus, and each new cell just gets a complete copy of the **chromosome**, the cell’s main DNA.

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/how-microbes-multiply/binary-fission-steps.webp" width={1200} height={676} keyItems={["chromosome", "copy of the chromosome", "grows longer", "daughter cells"]} given={[0, 3]} alt="AI-generated illustration: four frames of a rod-shaped bacterium with no nucleus: one loop of DNA, then two loops, then a longer cell with one loop at each end, then two separate matching cells each with one loop. Four numbered circles mark parts and steps for the student to name." caption="Binary fission in four steps: a bacterium copies its DNA, grows and splits into two cells. The copy is coloured only so you can follow it; both new cells carry a complete chromosome." />

Some bacteria use **budding**: a small new cell grows out of the parent and then breaks off, as in the ocean bacterium below.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/how-microbes-multiply/budding-hyphomonas.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: three frames of an oval bacterium with a thin stalk, a small bud growing at the stalk's tip, getting larger, then breaking off and swimming away while the parent stays." caption="Budding in the ocean bacterium Hyphomonas neptunium: a bud forms at the tip of a thin stalk, then breaks off and swims away as a new cell." />

The daughter cells are usually genetically identical. Copying DNA sometimes makes mistakes: sequencing studies of lab E. coli, which read the cells’ DNA, found mutations (changes in the DNA) building up over many generations.

Bacteria have one more way to change their genes. In **conjugation**, one bacterial cell passes DNA directly to another through contact. The DNA is often a **plasmid**, a small extra circle of DNA separate from the chromosome. Virginia’s science standards (the SOL) call it a form of sexual reproduction, because genes from two cells combine. Many biologists call it gene transfer, because no new cell is made.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/how-microbes-multiply/conjugation-before-after.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two rod-shaped bacteria touching, one passing a small ring of DNA called a plasmid to the other along a short bridge, and afterwards the same two cells apart, both holding the small ring." caption="In conjugation, one bacterium passes DNA, often a plasmid, to another through contact. No new cell is made: there are still two cells, but one has new genes." />

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Bacteria multiply by splitting in two (or budding), and they can gain new genes by conjugation, which does not add a cell.</Support>

## Explain: doubling, and why it stops

**Doubling time** is the time a population takes to double. If it stays the same, you can predict the count:

> number of cells = starting number × 2 × 2 × 2 ... (one × 2 for each doubling)

Worked example: a model population starts with 100 cells and doubles every 20 minutes. How many cells after 2 hours?

- 2 hours is 120 minutes, and 120 ÷ 20 = 6 doublings.
- 100 → 200 → 400 → 800 → 1,600 → 3,200 → 6,400 cells.

The first film’s two million is the same sum: one cell after 21 doublings (7 hours) gives 2,097,152. These are calculated numbers, not counts. The model assumes no cells die, no waiting before growth starts and endless food.

The flat start of the film’s curve is a lag, where cells are active but the count barely rises. If the culture is left long enough, the number of living cells can start to fall. Every population meets limits; which ones matter depends on the organism and where it lives, as the carrying capacity lesson shows.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Constant doubling makes numbers grow very fast, but a real population levels off when food runs out and wastes build up.</Support>

### Design problem

Baker’s yeast is a single-celled fungus, not a bacterium, but it also reproduces by budding and is safe for a classroom. Plan an investigation to test whether the amount of sugar changes how high a yeast population grows before it levels off.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Independent variable:** sugar added to the same nutrient broth in every tube, for example 0, 1, 2 and 4 grams per 100 mL. The broth must also supply nitrogen and minerals (for example a yeast nutrient from your teacher); in sugar water alone, yeast soon runs short of them and stops growing.
- **Dependent variable:** cloudiness every hour, using a turbidity probe or a printed card behind the tube, until the readings stop rising. Mix each tube gently first, because yeast settles and gas bubbles add cloudiness. To check that cloudiness tracks cell number, count cells in a drop under a microscope a few times.
- **Constants:** the same yeast packet and amount, the same water volume and temperature, the same tubes.
- **Control:** the tube with no sugar.
- **Trials and sample size:** three tubes per sugar level, then average them.
- **Safety:** use only yeast from the store, as your teacher directs. Never grow microbes from skin, soil or surfaces, because you cannot know what will grow.

If the high-sugar tubes level off higher, sugar was limiting how far the population grew. If all tubes level off at the same height, something else, such as another nutrient running out or wastes building up, may set the limit first.

</details>

## Explain: how viruses make more viruses

The cells in that well used food to multiply until the reading stopped rising. A free phage, a virus that infects bacteria, takes in no food and does nothing on its own. Inside a bacterium, though, the host’s food, energy and machinery build new phages, and one infected cell can release many. The second film follows one phage into one cell.

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

What the second film left out: in a real infection, nobody picks the path. In phage lambda the outcome depends on the state of the host cell and on how many phages infect it at once. It is also partly a matter of chance. Why a virus needs a host at all is the subject of [the living-or-not lesson](/lessons/living-or-not/).

In the **lytic cycle**, the build-now-then-burst path, the host’s own machinery does the copying. Some proteins made from phage genes help by steering the cell to copy phage DNA.

In the **lysogenic cycle**, the carried-along path, the stored viral DNA is called the **prophage**. Lambda’s prophage joins the chromosome, but some other phages keep theirs as a separate piece inside the cell. Either way, both daughter cells get a copy at each division. A bacterium carrying a prophage is a **lysogen**.

**Try this.** In the simulation’s second panel, take the lysogenic path and let the cells divide for three rounds. Then switch one cell to lytic and step it through. How many cells still carry the prophage?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Seven: three rounds of division make 1 × 2<sup>3</sup> = 8 carriers, and after the switched cell bursts, 8 − 1 = 7 remain.

</details>

**Predict.** A culture of lysogens grows in a closed well until its cloudiness levels off. What happens to the number of cells carrying the prophage?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Roughly, it levels off too. The prophage is copied only along with its host’s DNA, so while cells keep it and do not switch, it meets its host’s limits. New phages come only after a switch to lytic, for example after damage to a cell’s DNA.

</details>

| Host cell afterward | No host needed for most bacteria | Dies, for a phage (the cell bursts) | Alive, unless it later switches to lytic |

Now the cold sore. Herpes simplex virus infects people, not bacteria, but it hides in a similar way. It can stay in sensory nerve cells, the cells that carry feeling from the skin. Studies of infected human and mouse tissue suggest its DNA stays as a separate circle in the nucleus. It does not join the chromosome the way lambda does. From time to time the virus can become active again and travel back to the skin. Sometimes a new sore appears, but sometimes it does not.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A virus never divides; a host cell builds new viruses in the lytic cycle. In the lysogenic cycle, viral DNA is copied with the host’s DNA and may later switch to the lytic cycle.</Support>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

A bacterium copies itself; a virus is copied by a host cell. In lambda’s two paths, the host builds new phages only on the lytic path; on the lysogenic path it copies just the prophage DNA. Doubling cannot go on forever: in the well above, the rise roughly doubled every 2 hours, then levelled off. A cold sore comes back because herpes can hide in nerve cells and become active again, like, but not the same as, a lysogenic phage.
