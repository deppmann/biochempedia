## Explain: how bacteria make more bacteria

<WordBank terms={['asexual reproduction', 'binary fission', 'budding', 'conjugation', 'plasmid', 'bacteriophage', 'host cell', 'lytic cycle', 'lysogenic cycle', 'prophage', 'doubling time']} />

**Asexual reproduction** is reproduction with one parent, where the offspring get copies of that parent’s DNA. Most bacteria reproduce this way.

The most common method is **binary fission**, when one bacterial cell grows, copies its DNA and splits into two cells. Each new cell gets a complete copy of the chromosome. The result looks like mitosis, which you met in [the cell cycle lesson](/lessons/cell-cycle-and-mitosis/): one cell becomes two matching cells. The process is simpler, though, because a bacterium has no nucleus.

{/* DIAGRAM: binary fission in four steps: 1 one cell with a circular chromosome, 2 the chromosome copied, 3 the cell grows longer and the copies move apart, 4 the cell pinches into two daughter cells */}

Some bacteria use **budding**, when a small new cell grows out of the parent and then breaks off. In the ocean bacterium *Hyphomonas neptunium*, buds form at the tip of a thin stalk. When the bud breaks off, it swims away as a new cell.

The daughter cells are usually genetically identical. "Usually" matters. Copying DNA sometimes makes mistakes, and sequencing studies of lab E. coli found mutations building up over many generations.

Bacteria have one more way to change their genes. **Conjugation** is when one bacterial cell passes DNA directly to another through contact. The DNA is often a **plasmid**, a small extra circle of DNA separate from the chromosome. Conjugation does not make a new cell. Afterward there are still two cells, but one has new genes. The Virginia SOL calls conjugation a form of sexual reproduction, because genes from two cells are combined. Many biologists call it gene transfer, because no new cell is made. Conjugation is far rarer than binary fission.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Bacteria multiply by splitting in two (or budding), and they can gain new genes by conjugation, which does not add a cell.</Support>

## Explain: how viruses make more viruses

A virus cannot split, grow or make anything on its own. It needs a **host cell**, the living cell a virus infects and uses to make new viruses. [The living-or-not lesson](/lessons/living-or-not/) explains why. A **bacteriophage**, or phage, is a virus that infects bacteria.

The **lytic cycle** is the path where a virus takes over the host, the host builds new viruses, and the cell bursts open to release them. It runs like this:

1. The phage attaches to the bacterium and injects its DNA.
2. The host’s machinery copies the viral DNA and builds viral parts.
3. New phages are assembled from those parts.
4. The cell bursts and the new phages escape. The host cell dies.

The **lysogenic cycle** is the path where the viral DNA joins the host’s own DNA and is copied along with it. The best-known example is phage lambda, which infects E. coli. Its DNA becomes part of the bacterial chromosome. That stored viral DNA is called a **prophage**. Each time the bacterium divides by binary fission, both daughter cells get a copy. The cell stays alive, and the virus spreads quietly through a growing family of cells. Later, something such as damage to the cell’s DNA can switch the prophage into the lytic cycle.

{/* DIAGRAM: lytic and lysogenic cycles side by side: 1 phage injects DNA, 2 lytic path: viral parts made and assembled, 3 cell bursts and releases phages, 4 lysogenic path: viral DNA joins the chromosome as a prophage, 5 host divides and both daughters carry the prophage, 6 switch arrow from prophage back to lytic */}

| | Binary fission (bacteria) | Lytic cycle (virus) | Lysogenic cycle (virus) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who does the copying | The bacterium itself | The host cell | The host cell, each time it divides |
| What is made | Two new cells | Many new virus particles | Host cells that carry viral DNA |
| Host cell afterward | No host involved | Usually dies | Alive, until a switch to lytic |

Now the cold sore. Herpes simplex virus infects people, not bacteria, but it uses a similar hiding strategy. It can stay in sensory nerve cells, the cells that carry feeling from the skin. Studies of infected human and mouse tissue suggest its DNA stays as a separate circle in the nucleus rather than joining the chromosome the way lambda does. From time to time the virus can become active again and travel back to the skin, and a new sore appears.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** A virus never divides; a host cell builds new viruses in the lytic cycle. In the lysogenic cycle, viral DNA is copied with the host’s DNA and may later switch to the lytic cycle.</Support>

## Explain: doubling, and why it stops

**Doubling time** is the time it takes a population to double in number. If the doubling time stays the same, you can predict the count:

> number of cells = starting number × 2 × 2 × 2 ... (one × 2 for each doubling)

Here is a worked example. A model population starts with 100 cells and doubles every 20 minutes. How many cells after 2 hours?

- 2 hours is 120 minutes.
- 120 minutes ÷ 20 minutes = 6 doublings.
- 100 → 200 → 400 → 800 → 1,600 → 3,200 → 6,400 cells.

Starting from one E. coli cell with 21 doublings (7 hours), the model gives 2,097,152 cells. That is the "millions in an afternoon" from the opening. These are calculated numbers, not counts. The model assumes no cells die, no waiting before growth starts and endless food.

Real cultures follow a curve with stages. First there is a lag, where cells are active but the count barely rises. Then comes fast growth, close to steady doubling. Last, food runs low and wastes build up, so growth levels off. The same limits apply to every population, as [the carrying capacity lesson](/lessons/carrying-capacity/) shows.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Constant doubling makes numbers grow very fast, but a real population levels off when food runs out and wastes build up.</Support>

### Design problem

Baker’s yeast is a single-celled fungus, not a bacterium, but it also reproduces by budding and is safe for a classroom. Plan an investigation to test whether the amount of sugar changes how high a yeast population grows before it levels off.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Independent variable:** sugar in warm water, for example 0, 1, 2 and 4 grams per 100 mL.
- **Dependent variable:** cloudiness every hour, using a turbidity probe or by comparing against a printed card behind the tube, for about 8 hours.
- **Constants:** the same yeast packet and amount, the same water volume and temperature, the same tubes.
- **Control:** the tube with no sugar.
- **Trials and sample size:** three tubes per sugar level, then average them.
- **Safety:** use only yeast from the store, as your teacher directs. Never grow microbes from skin, soil or surfaces, because you cannot know what will grow.

If the high-sugar tubes level off higher, food limits the population. If all tubes level off at the same height, something else, such as wastes, may set the limit first.

</details>
