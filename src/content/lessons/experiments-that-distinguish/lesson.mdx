    contribution: >-
      With Berton Pressman, Robert Gianetto, Robert Wattiaux and Françoise Appelmans, measured how
      enzymes spread across centrifugal fractions of rat liver, and used those patterns, with
      latency, to recognize the lysosome as a particle group distinct from mitochondria.
    profileSourceAnchor: "de Duve, Pressman, Gianetto, Wattiaux & Appelmans, Biochem. J. 60:604 (1955); de Duve, Nobel Lecture, 12 December 1974."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By his own account in his Nobel lecture, de Duve came to cell fractionation to answer a
      question about insulin and the liver: where in the liver cell is glucose-6-phosphatase? To
      answer it his group at the University of Louvain broke rat liver gently, spun the homogenate
      into five fractions, and measured each enzyme in every fraction, not only in the one it was
      expected to favour. Acid phosphatase behaved oddly: in fresh
      preparations much of its activity could not be measured until the particles holding it were
      broken. In the 1955 paper with Pressman, Gianetto, Wattiaux and Appelmans, acid phosphatase
      and other hydrolases peaked in a fraction lighter than the one richest in cytochrome
      oxidase, the mitochondrial marker. The group proposed a new particle, the lysosome. They did
      not claim to have seen it: a 1956 electron-microscope study with Alex Novikoff withheld a
      firm identification until purer preparations existed. De Duve shared the 1974 Nobel Prize
      with Albert Claude and George Palade. The method he left is the habit of reading a whole
      distribution.
  - name: "George Palade"
    contribution: >-
      With James Jamieson, followed a three-minute pulse of radioactive amino acid through guinea
      pig pancreatic slices, by fractionation and by autoradiography, and so put the compartments
      of the secretory route in temporal order.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Jamieson & Palade, J. Cell Biol. 34:577 and 34:597 (1967); Jamieson & Palade, J. Cell Biol. 39:580 (1968)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The pancreas suited this work because its acinar cells spend most of their effort making
      and exporting digestive enzymes. Earlier studies had injected labelled amino acid into
      whole guinea pigs, where label could not be removed at a chosen moment. Slices of pancreas
      in a dish solved that. In the 1967 papers, Jamieson and Palade gave slices a three-minute
      pulse of carbon-14 leucine and then a chase without label. At the end of the pulse the label
      was in rough microsomes. Smooth microsomes, the peripheral elements of the Golgi complex, peaked seven minutes into the chase, and label
      then reached the zymogen-granule fraction. The second paper checked that pellet by
      autoradiography and found that its early label sat largely in condensing vacuoles, not in
      mature granules; in intact cells, labelled mature granules appeared at the earliest 57
      minutes after the pulse. In 1968 they added cycloheximide during the chase and found that
      transport went on. The papers made the order of the secretory route measurable and showed
      how to check what a fraction contains.
  - name: "Stephen Royle"
    contribution: >-
      With Nicholas Bright and Leon Lagnado, depleted clathrin from cultured human cells, found

      that separate clathrin's mitotic role from its role in endocytosis, to test the cause.

    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Clathrin was known as the coat protein of endocytosis, the process by which a cell takes in
      material from its surface. In 2005 Royle, Bright and Lagnado reported that clathrin also
      sits on the mitotic spindle, the array of microtubules that separates chromosomes, and that
      depleting clathrin heavy chain with shRNA in HEK293 cells prolonged mitosis. That raised the
      obvious objection: a cell that cannot take in nutrients or receptors might divide badly for
      indirect reasons. The rescue experiments answered in steps. Constructs were made resistant
      to the shRNA by silent mutations. Full-length clathrin restored normal mitosis; the
      N-terminal fragment did not. In 2006, with Lagnado, Royle reported that constructs unable to
      form three-legged trimers failed to rescue mitosis, and that a shortened construct partly
      rescued mitosis while failing to support transferrin uptake, a measure of endocytosis. The
      2005 paper said plainly that the composition of the bridges seen between spindle
      microtubules was not yet known. The work became a model for separating two jobs of one
      protein.

techniques:
  - name: "Differential and density-gradient centrifugation with marker enzymes"
    blurb: >-
      A gently broken tissue is spun at rising force to collect pellets that differ in size and
      density, and a fraction can be refined on a gradient where particles settle at their own
      buoyant density. Measuring protein and several marker enzymes in every fraction shows which
      fractions are enriched in which organelle, and how impure each one is.
  - name: "Pulse-chase labelling with autoradiography"
    blurb: >-
      Cells get a short pulse of radioactive amino acid, then an excess of unlabelled amino acid,
      so only proteins made during the pulse carry label. Samples taken at later times, fractionated
      or imaged by autoradiography, show where that cohort of protein is at each moment.
  - name: "Knockdown or knockout with rescue"
    blurb: >-
      A protein is depleted with RNAi or its gene is disrupted with CRISPR, and a phenotype is
      scored. Putting back a version the knockdown cannot touch, such as a construct with silent
      mutations, tests whether the phenotype came from losing that protein; mutant versions test
      which of its activities is needed.
  - name: "Genotype-by-drug comparison"
    blurb: >-
      The same drug is given, at a range of doses, to cells with and without its supposed target.
      If cells lacking the target respond just as well, the drug can act through something else.

      - "Acid phosphatase, measured in the light mitochondrial fraction"

    rationale: >-
      Glucose-6-phosphatase is a membrane enzyme of the endoplasmic reticulum, and in rat liver its
      activity concentrates in the microsomal fraction, which is made largely of ER fragments.
      Cytochrome oxidase marks mitochondria, not ER. Acid phosphatase marks lysosomes. Lactate
      dehydrogenase is a cytosolic enzyme that stays in the soluble supernatant, so it marks the
      cytosol rather than any membrane compartment.
  - stem: "A fraction holds 30% of the cell's acid phosphatase and 3% of its protein. What does a relative specific activity of 10 tell you?"
    choices:
      - "The fraction lost ninety percent of its acid phosphatase activity during the spin"
      - "The fraction is enriched tenfold for acid phosphatase compared with the homogenate"
      - "The fraction is ninety percent lysosomes, since enrichment measures purity directly"
      - "The fraction holds ten percent of all the lysosomes that were present in the cell"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Relative specific activity is percent of activity divided by percent of protein, so 30/3 is
      a tenfold enrichment over the starting homogenate. It does not give purity: the same fraction
      can carry other organelles, which only other markers reveal. The 30% recovery estimates the share
      of lysosomes in the fraction, if every lysosome carries the enzyme equally; the ratio does not. Nothing in the ratio measures loss of
      activity; loss shows up when the fractions fail to add up to the starting total.
  - stem: "Particles in a fraction show only 10% of their acid phosphatase activity until a detergent is added, which raises it to 100%. What is the best explanation?"
    choices:
      - "The detergent activates the enzyme by changing its fold and its catalytic site"
      - "The substrate is broken down by a second enzyme until the detergent inhibits it"
      - "The enzyme is a soluble protein that stuck to the outer surface of the particles"
      - "A membrane around the enzyme keeps the substrate out until the detergent opens it"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      This is latency: the enzyme is enclosed by a membrane that its substrate cannot cross, so
      only broken particles show activity, and detergent breaks them all. An enzyme stuck to the
      outer surface would meet its substrate at once and show no latency. A detergent that
      refolded the enzyme would be a strange and testable claim, and latency that tracks membrane
      damage by other means, such as osmotic shock, rules it out. A competing enzyme would not
      explain why the gain tracks the opening of the particles.
  - stem: "Cells depleted of protein C by an shRNA show a defect. A rescue construct encodes the same amino acid sequence as C, but several codons in the shRNA target site are changed to synonymous codons. Why make it this way?"
    choices:
      - "So the shRNA cannot pair with the construct's mRNA while the protein stays the same"
      - "So the shRNA is diverted to the construct, protecting the cell's own mRNA from it"
      - "So the protein made from the construct carries a tag that tells it from the original"
      - "So the construct makes more protein than the gene, overcoming the shRNA by excess"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Synonymous (silent) changes alter the mRNA sequence the shRNA pairs with but not the
      protein, so the construct's mRNA escapes destruction and the same protein returns. Silent
      changes add no tag; a tag would be a separate added sequence. Overexpressing an unprotected
      construct can fail or mislead, and expression level must be checked, not relied on. A
      construct that drew the shRNA away would need to be a target, which is the opposite of what
      the silent changes do.
  - stem: "Pancreatic slices are pulse-labelled and then chased. Label is found first in rough microsomes, then in smooth microsomes identified with the Golgi complex, and then in the zymogen-granule fraction. What does this time course show?"
    choices:
      - "Newly made protein moves between compartments only by budding and fusing vesicles"
      - "The order of compartments is ER, then Golgi, then granules, but not how the protein moves between them"
      - "The granule fraction makes the protein and sends it back to the endoplasmic reticulum"
      - "The route cannot be known unless protein synthesis is also blocked"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "A pulse-chase gives the order in which one labelled batch visits compartments. The same order fits vesicles that bud and fuse, and it also fits compartments that mature into one another, so the order alone does not choose between them. The protein is made on the ER, not in granules, so the reverse direction is wrong. Blocking synthesis is a separate test of whether newly made protein is pushed along by later protein; it is not needed to read the order."

**In 1955 a group in Louvain proposed a new organelle, the lysosome, from numbers in a table of centrifuge fractions, before anyone could say what it looked like.**

They broke rat liver, spun it into five fractions, and measured enzymes in every one. Acid phosphatase did not quite travel with the mitochondria: it peaked in a lighter fraction. The first film showed the logic; the text adds the arithmetic, the controls and the cases it skips.

> **The first film's rule, with one addition.** The predictions must also differ by more than the result's own noise. So before you read a result, write down what each model predicts. If the predictions are the same, the result cannot choose between the models, however clean the data.

Each method below is best at one kind of question and is easily pushed to answer one it cannot. The rule says whether a test *can* decide, not that your two models are the only ones; the strongest designs make them predict opposite results.

## Where: breaking the cell carefully

First the tissue is broken gently in a sucrose solution, so that the plasma membrane tears but organelles stay mostly whole; this suspension is the **homogenate**. Then comes **differential centrifugation**: spin at low force and the largest, densest particles (nuclei, unbroken cells) form a pellet, a packed sediment at the bottom of the tube. Spin the liquid above at higher force and smaller particles pellet, and so on. In de Duve's rat-liver scheme this gives five fractions: N (nuclear), M (heavy mitochondrial), L (light mitochondrial), P (microsomal: microsomes are small sealed fragments of membrane) and S (soluble, mostly the cytosol). The names come from the spin, not the contents: the "light mitochondrial" fraction is where de Duve found lysosomes, so a fraction's name is itself a model. (Sizes: [a measured eukaryotic cell](/lessons/measured-eukaryotic-cell/); centrifugation: [purification](/lessons/protein-purification-techniques/).)

Differential spins separate by size and density together, and particle populations overlap, so every pellet is a mixture. **Density-gradient centrifugation** refines a fraction: in a tube whose sucrose concentration rises toward the bottom, each particle settles, given long enough, where the solution matches its own buoyant density, whatever its size.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/experiments-that-distinguish/density-gradient-settling.webp" width={1200} height={747} alt="AI-generated illustration: a tube after a differential spin with a mixed pellet beside a tube with a rising sucrose gradient where particles of different sizes sit at levels set by their buoyant density." caption="A differential spin gives a mixed pellet; in a sucrose gradient each particle, given long enough, settles at its own buoyant density whatever its size." />

To tell which particles went where, you measure a **marker enzyme**, one assumed to belong to a single compartment. In rat liver the standard markers are cytochrome oxidase for mitochondria, acid phosphatase for lysosomes, and glucose-6-phosphatase for the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where most secreted and many membrane proteins are made.

### Reading a fractionation table

Three quantities do most of the work. The first compares a fraction's share of an enzyme with its share of the protein: hold 45% of the enzyme in 5% of the protein, and the enzyme is nine times more concentrated there than in the homogenate.

> **relative specific activity = (% of total enzyme activity in the fraction) / (% of total protein in the fraction)**

Both numbers are percentages, so the ratio has no units; it is not the specific activity of biochemistry (units per mg). A value above 1 means the fraction is **enriched**. **Recovery** in a fraction is the percent of the homogenate's activity found there; summed over all fractions, it checks that none was lost or gained. **Latency** is activity that cannot be measured until a membrane is opened, usually with detergent, because the substrate cannot reach an enzyme sealed inside a compartment.

### Worked problem: lysosome or mitochondrion?

Here is a made-up rat-liver data set; the first film's tracks were computed from it. Each row gives the percent of the homogenate's total found in each fraction, and every row adds to 100. Model 1: the unknown enzyme X is lysosomal. Model 2: X is mitochondrial.

| | N | M | L | P | S |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Protein | 15 | 20 | 5 | 20 | 40 |
| Cytochrome oxidase | 10 | 70 | 15 | 5 | 0 |
| Acid phosphatase | 8 | 25 | 45 | 12 | 10 |
| Glucose-6-phosphatase | 10 | 10 | 10 | 65 | 5 |
| Unknown enzyme X | 9 | 24 | 44 | 13 | 10 |

**Step 1: relative specific activity.** Divide each activity by the protein in the same column.
- Cytochrome oxidase: M is 70/20 = 3.5 and L is 15/5 = 3.0. It peaks in M but is enriched in L as well.
- Acid phosphatase: L is 45/5 = 9.0 and M is 25/20 = 1.25. It peaks in L.
- Glucose-6-phosphatase: P is 65/20 = 3.25. It peaks in P.
- Enzyme X: L is 44/5 = 8.8 and M is 24/20 = 1.2. Its whole profile, fraction by fraction, is close to acid phosphatase's and far from cytochrome oxidase's.

**Step 2: enrichment, recovery and purity.** Fraction L holds 45% of the acid phosphatase in 5% of the protein: a 9-fold enrichment with 45% recovery. More than half is elsewhere, so a pure lysosome preparation would be a low-yield one. But L also holds 15% of the cytochrome oxidase, a 3-fold enrichment for mitochondria.

**Predict.** Is L at least mostly lysosomes, 9-fold against 3-fold? What else would you need to know?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Enrichment alone cannot say: you also need each organelle's share of the homogenate's protein, which the table does not give. Suppose lysosomes hold 2% of it and mitochondria 18% (invented values that the table allows), and that each marker is confined to its organelle, fully measured and equally active per mg of that organelle's protein. Then L is about 9 × 2% = 18% lysosomal protein and 3 × 18% = 54% mitochondrial protein: on these assumptions the fraction most enriched in lysosomes is mostly mitochondria. Enrichment says which way a fraction leans, not what it is made of.

</details>

**Step 3: latency.** Latency answers a different question: inside or outside? Suppose that in intact L particles only 10% of X's activity can be measured, and detergent raises it to 100%. X is 90% latent, so it sits behind a membrane, inside a particle, rather than stuck to the outside. It cannot say which particle: a mitochondrial matrix enzyme can also look latent when its substrate cannot cross intact membranes.

**Conclusion.** X is provisionally lysosomal. A whole-profile match is stronger evidence than a shared peak, but two organelles can happen to sediment together, so to confirm it you would want a second lysosomal marker that tracks X on a density gradient, and imaging in intact cells ([microscopy](/lessons/microscopy-and-images/)).

## When: pulse and chase

A fractionation table is a snapshot; to learn the order of compartments, label one batch of protein and follow it. This is a **pulse-chase**: give cells radioactive amino acid for a short time (the pulse), then flood them with the unlabelled amino acid (the chase), so only proteins made during the pulse are labelled.

The first film's order came from this experiment. In 1967 James Jamieson and George Palade did it with slices of guinea pig pancreas, whose acinar cells make large amounts of digestive enzymes for export. After a three-minute pulse of carbon-14 leucine, label was in the rough microsomes, the ribosome-studded ER fragments. Smooth microsomes, which these experiments identified as peripheral elements of the Golgi complex, held it next, and later it reached the zymogen-granule fraction, the storage granules that release enzyme from the cell (times in Going deeper).

As the first film showed, the order alone cannot choose between carriers and Golgi compartments that themselves mature, cis to trans; the *shape* of the time course can, in principle (Going deeper). Other models do disagree.

**Predict.** Is a batch of new protein pushed along by the protein made after it? Model 1: yes, like a queue. Model 2: no, it moves on its own. If you block protein synthesis during the chase, what does each model predict?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Model 1: transport stalls. Model 2: transport goes on. In 1968 Jamieson and Palade added cycloheximide, a drug that blocks protein synthesis, during the chase. Synthesis fell sharply, yet transport continued at a large fraction of the control efficiency. Newly made protein is not pushed along by protein made after it, at least over that chase and in that tissue: a pulse-chase answering a question about cause.

</details>

**A pellet's name is a model too.** The granule fraction picked up label early, but electron-microscope autoradiography, which shows where radioactive label sits in a thin section, put that early label largely in condensing vacuoles of the Golgi complex, the large forerunners of granules, which sediment with granules. Labelled mature granules appeared much later in intact cells. A labelled pellet dates the label's arrival in the pellet, not in the organelle it is named for (more in the [secretory pathway](/lessons/secretory-pathway/)).

## Cause: remove it, then put it back

Being in the right place at the right time fits a protein that does the job and one that only happens to be there. To choose, take it away and see whether the job fails, then put it back.

A direct small-molecule inhibitor can block a protein within minutes, often faster than RNAi or gene editing, though phenotype timing varies. RNA interference uses a short RNA, delivered as an siRNA or made inside the cell as an shRNA, to guide destruction of a matching mRNA over a few days. CRISPR uses the nuclease Cas9 and a guide RNA to cut the gene; the cell's error-prone repair of the cut usually breaks it, but some edits keep the reading frame or leave partial protein, so loss of the protein must be checked. (Tools: [CRISPR and functional genomics](/lessons/crispr-and-functional-genomics/), [recombinant DNA](/lessons/recombinant-dna-and-pcr/), [drugs against a part](/lessons/drugs-against-a-part/).) Each can mislead, and each has a control that makes the models disagree again.

**A drug can hit more than its target.** The first film's drug is OTS167, developed as an inhibitor of the kinase MELK; Ann Lin, Jason Sheltzer and colleagues ran its test in 2017. In their MELK-null breast cancer clones, made by CRISPR, in Cal51 populations the mean GI50, the concentration that halves growth, was 10 nM with control guides and 11 nM with MELK guides. Whether these cells need MELK at all depends on the assay and is still debated (see the anecdote "A drug that did not need its target"). This **genotype-by-drug** comparison is a chemical-genetic test of target dependence, not a rescue: if the drug effect persists without the target, the target is not required for it; resistance supports the target if the cells are viable and controls hold. A functional drug-resistant target allele is the rescue-like test.

**Predict.** Suppose you give cells a version of MELK that OTS167 cannot bind. What does each model predict?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If the drug works through MELK, cells with an active, normally expressed drug-proof MELK should keep growing in it; if through something else, growth still falls. Growth would support MELK as a target that matters, not the only one; a fall means little until the drug-proof MELK is shown to be active.

</details>

**RNAi can silence the wrong mRNA.** A short stretch of the guide strand can pair with, and silence, mRNAs it was never meant for (Going deeper). One control is a second shRNA against another part of the same mRNA, since a shared off-target is unlikely. The stronger one is **rescue**: put back a copy whose mRNA the shRNA cannot touch. If the phenotype came from losing the protein, rescue should cure it; if from an off-target, it should stay. A rescue that fails means little until the construct is shown to be made at about the normal level, folded and correctly placed.

**Predict.** How can a rescue construct make the same protein and still escape an shRNA that pairs with that protein's mRNA?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Change the codons in the shRNA's target site to synonymous ones, codons for the same amino acid. These silent mutations alter the mRNA, so the shRNA no longer pairs with it, but leave the amino-acid sequence the same. Check by immunoblot (an antibody test for the protein) that the rescue protein is made at about the normal level, since far too much can cause effects of its own.

</details>

**A knockout can be quietly compensated.** In selected zebrafish and mouse models, decay of a mutant mRNA switched on related genes (Going deeper), so a mild knockout phenotype does not prove the gene unimportant.

The second film picks up here: a rescue that works is strong evidence that the defect came from losing the protein, but not which of its jobs mattered.

### Two jobs, one protein: separation of function

The chapter's experiment, in detail: in 2005 Stephen Royle, Nicholas Bright and Leon Lagnado depleted clathrin heavy chain in HEK293 cells, and the front part that failed to rescue was the N-terminal fragment. Since clathrin also coats endocytic vesicles, it might act in mitosis itself (Model 1) or through [endocytosis](/lessons/endocytosis/) (Model 2).

**Predict.** What does each model predict for a resistant clathrin construct that cannot support endocytosis?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Model 1: it can still rescue mitosis. Model 2: it cannot, because the route that matters is still broken. In 2006 Royle and Lagnado reported that a shortened construct partly rescued mitosis while failing to support transferrin uptake, the standard readout of clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Keeping part of one job while losing the other argues that the jobs are separate.

</details>

A **separation-of-function mutant** like this is the sharpest test of whether two jobs are separate.

### Design problem

Depleting protein C by shRNA prolongs mitosis in cultured human cells. Model 1: C acts directly on the spindle. Model 2: the defect is indirect, through C's role in endocytosis. Design experiments that tell the two models apart.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**1. Make the phenotype trustworthy.** Use a non-targeting control shRNA and a second shRNA against another region of C's mRNA; if both prolong mitosis, an off-target effect is unlikely. Confirm loss of C by immunoblot.

**2. Rescue.** Express full-length C, at about the normal level, from a construct with silent mutations in the shRNA target site, alongside an empty-vector control. Mitosis should return to normal.

**3. Make the models disagree.** Score every condition for two things: time in mitosis (by live imaging) and endocytosis (transferrin uptake). Then express shRNA-resistant mutants of C, each keeping one activity and losing the other, such as one that cannot bind the endocytic adaptors (proteins linking the coat to the membrane) and one that cannot reach the spindle. Model 1 predicts that a mutant failing at endocytosis can still rescue mitosis. Model 2 predicts rescue only by versions that restore endocytosis, and that blocking endocytosis another way, without touching C, also prolongs mitosis. RNAi phenotypes are usually scored after a day or more, so indirect effects have time to build. To ask whether C is needed acutely in mitosis, rapid removal at mitotic entry is a sharper complementary test; earlier direct roles need separate tests.

Do not decide on localization: seeing C on the spindle fits Model 1 but does not exclude Model 2, and a fragment can reach the spindle and still fail to rescue.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Fractionation fine print

De Duve's modified scheme added the light mitochondrial fraction, L, to the older four-fraction one. Summed over all fractions, his group's recoveries usually came to between 90 and 110%.

### The pulse-chase numbers

Times are minutes of chase, from the end of the three-minute pulse. In the Jamieson and Palade pancreas experiments, smooth microsomes peaked at 7 minutes. The isolated zymogen-granule fraction was half-maximally labelled at 17 minutes, mostly in condensing vacuoles; in intact cells, labelled mature granules appeared at the earliest at 57 minutes. In the 1968 experiment, cycloheximide was added at 0.5 mM during a 37-minute chase: synthesis fell by up to 98%, yet transport continued with about 80% of the control efficiency.

### Order versus shape

Take a toy model with an instantaneous, synchronous pulse: every labelled molecule enters the ER at time zero and spends exactly 10 minutes there and exactly 15 in the Golgi. No label reaches the granules before 25 minutes, and then all of it arrives together; a finite pulse would broaden that step. If each molecule instead leaves each compartment with a steady chance per minute, with the same average stays, some label arrives within minutes and the rise is smooth: same order, different shapes, so measured kinetics can test such models.

### RNAi off-targets and compensation in detail

In HeLa-cell profiling, unintended targets were enriched for 3′ untranslated regions matching positions 2 to 7 of the guide strand. In the compensation studies, alleles that made no mutant mRNA at all lacked the response (El-Brolosy and colleagues, 2019).
