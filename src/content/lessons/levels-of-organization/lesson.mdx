## Explain: why not one giant cell?

Here are the numbers behind the first film's growing cell. Pretend the cell is a cube built from small cubes. A cube has 6 faces, so its surface is 6 × width × width squares, and its inside is width × width × width small cubes.

| Width of the cube | Surface (squares on the outside) | Inside (small cubes) | Surface for each small cube of inside |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | 1 | 6 |
| 2 | 24 | 8 | 3 |
| 4 | 96 | 64 | 1.5 |

For these same-shaped cubes, doubling the width makes the surface 4 times bigger but the inside 8 times bigger, so the surface for each small cube of inside is **halved**. The middle also ends up farther from the surface.

**Predict.** Grow the cube once more, to a width of 8. How much surface does each small cube of inside get now?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

0.75: 6 × 8 × 8 = 384 squares of surface for 8 × 8 × 8 = 512 small cubes of inside, less than one square each.

</details>

The cube grows in every direction. Without special features, a big enough cell like that may not move materials to its middle fast enough. A cell can grow long instead: some nerve cells reach about a metre but stay thin, so no part of the inside is far from the surface. A few unusual single cells, such as certain algae, grow large by using special shapes and ways of moving materials inside.

## Elaborate: more cells, more kinds of cells?

Among mammals, a bigger body mostly has more cells, not bigger ones. Red blood cells and liver cells, for example, are not much bigger in an elephant than in a mouse. A few kinds, such as nerve cells and fat cells, do grow larger: a nerve cell reaching from the spine to the foot must be longer in a longer animal.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Bigger mammals mostly have more cells, not much bigger ones.</Support>

So what happens to the number of **kinds of cells**? A kind of cell (scientists say cell type) is a group of cells that share a shape, parts and job, such as red blood cells.

### Design problem

Does adding a little salt to a Paramecium's water change how often its contractile vacuole empties? Plan an investigation with a Paramecium culture from a science supply company.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does the amount of salt in the water change how often the contractile vacuole empties?
- **Independent variable:** salt added, such as 0 g, 0.1 g and 0.2 g per 100 mL of spring water.
- **Dependent variable:** number of times the vacuole empties in 60 seconds.
- **Constants:** same culture, same temperature, same microscope, and the same wait (10 minutes) before counting.
- **Control:** spring water with no salt added.
- **Repeated trials:** count 5 paramecia in each kind of water, and find the mean (the average).
- **Safety:** wash your hands, handle glass slides with care, never taste anything, and give the culture back to your teacher.
- **What it means:** if the mean counts differ clearly and the difference shows up again in repeated trials, salt likely changes how often the vacuole empties.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Volvox and the history of many-celled life

Volvox, a green alga, is a ball of many cells with only a few kinds of cells. It is not a step between a Paramecium and you: many-celled life arose separately in several groups, including algae like Volvox and animals.

### How many cells in a person?

The 2016 estimate gives about 30 trillion cells for a reference man; the 2023 study behind the 400 kinds estimated about 36 trillion. So you will see 30 to 36 trillion, depending on the estimate.

### Sources for the cell-count table

Bell and Mooers, 1997, a table built from older studies, which turned body size into a rough cell number that can be off by up to about ten times; Matt and Umen, 2018 (Volvox carteri, lab strain); Sulston and Horvitz, 1977, Sulston and colleagues, 1983, and WormAtlas, 2009 (worm nuclei); Sender and colleagues, 2016, and Hatton and colleagues, 2023 (human).
