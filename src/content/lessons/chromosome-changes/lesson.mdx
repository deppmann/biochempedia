techniques:
  - name: "G-banded karyotyping"
    blurb: >-
      Dividing cells are stopped at metaphase, swollen, fixed and spread on a slide, then treated
      with trypsin and stained with Giemsa so that each chromosome shows its own pattern of light
      and dark bands. Counting and comparing bands reveals extra or missing chromosomes and
      rearrangements large enough to see, about 5 to 10 million base pairs in routine work.
  - name: "Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)"
    blurb: >-
      A fluorescent DNA probe pairs with its matching sequence in fixed cells. Counting spots gives
      the copy number of that one region, even
      in nuclei that are not dividing. On spread metaphase chromosomes, the spots also show which chromosome carries the region. A FISH result says nothing about regions the probe does not
      cover.
  - name: "Chromosomal microarray"
    blurb: >-
      Patient DNA is labelled and compared with reference DNA or reference data at thousands of mapped sites, giving the
      relative dose of each segment. It finds gains and losses far smaller than a karyotype can,
      but it cannot see a balanced rearrangement, and a dose-only array can miss triploidy unless
      it also reads SNP genotypes (which base a person carries at common single-base variant sites).
  - name: "DNA marker typing of parent and child"
    blurb: >-
      Short, variable DNA sequences near a centromere are typed in a child and both parents. Which
      parental alleles the child carries, and in how many copies, shows which parent contributed
      an extra chromosome and, if no crossover separates marker and centromere, whether the error looks like one at meiosis I or meiosis II.

**In 1959 Jérôme Lejeune, Marthe Gautier and Raymond Turpin in Paris counted 47 chromosomes, not the usual 46, in cultured cells from nine children with Down syndrome.**

That one extra chromosome, later numbered 21, explained a condition that had been described for nearly a century. It shows the main point of this lesson: a cell with an extra or missing chromosome has the wrong dose of the genes on it, and that usually matters. Most aneuploid human embryos do not survive pregnancy, and trisomy 21 is the commonest of the few autosomal trisomies seen at birth. In the same year, other laboratories tied an extra X to Klinefelter syndrome and a missing one to Turner syndrome. This lesson asks how a cell ends up with the wrong number of chromosomes, why some wrong numbers are tolerated and others are not, and what happens when a chromosome breaks and rejoins in the wrong way.

## The idea: a missed separation leaves one cell with two copies and another with none

A few words first. **Aneuploidy** is the gain or loss of one or more individual chromosomes, short of a whole set. **Trisomy** means three copies of one chromosome instead of two; **monosomy** means one. **Nondisjunction** is a failure of chromosomes to part when a cell divides.

You met the two meiotic divisions in [the lesson on chromosomes and meiosis](/lessons/chromosomes-and-meiosis/). At meiosis I the two homologues of each pair separate. At meiosis II the two sister chromatids of each chromosome separate. Think of two pairs of gloves, the two gloves in each pair identical: meiosis I hands one pair to each of two people, and meiosis II splits each pair between two more. Fail at the first step and one person gets both pairs. Fail at the second and one person gets both gloves of a single pair.

### Worked problem, part 1: which gametes?

Follow chromosome 21 in one human spermatocyte. Call the father's two homologues 21a and 21b. Assume only one error, otherwise normal divisions, and that all four products are recovered.

1. **Error at meiosis I.** 21a and 21b go to the same secondary spermatocyte. Meiosis II then runs normally. That cell gives two sperm, each with one chromatid of 21a and one of 21b: **two disomic sperm (n + 1)**. The other secondary spermatocyte had no chromosome 21: **two nullisomic sperm (n − 1)**.
2. **Error at meiosis II.** Meiosis I is normal. In one secondary spermatocyte, say the one with 21a, the sisters fail to part: **one sperm with two copies of 21a, one with none**. The other secondary spermatocyte divides normally: **two normal sperm**.
3. **Fertilize a normal egg** (one chromosome 21). A disomic sperm gives a trisomic zygote, a nullisomic sperm a monosomic one, a normal sperm a normal one.

<NondisjunctionGametes />

> **disomic gametes per meiosis = 2 after a meiosis I error, 1 after a meiosis II error**

Now scale up. Take 100 meiocytes: 70 normal, 10 with a meiosis I error, 20 with a meiosis II error. Disomic products are 10 × 2 + 20 × 1 = 40. Nullisomic products are also 40. Normal products are 70 × 4 + 20 × 2 = 320. Check: 320 + 40 + 40 = 400 = 100 × 4. With normal partners, that gives 80 percent euploid, 10 percent trisomic and 10 percent monosomic zygotes. These numbers come from an invented exercise, not from measured human rates.

### Worked problem, part 2: which parent, which division?

The two errors leave different fingerprints near the centromere, because at an informative marker the homologues differ there and sisters usually do not. Take a DNA marker tightly linked to the chromosome 21 centromere. The father carries alleles 1 and 2; the mother carries 3 and 4.

- **Child A types 1, 3, 4.** One paternal allele, two different maternal alleles. The extra chromosome is maternal, and since it carries both of her homologues, the error was at **maternal meiosis I**.
- **Child B types 1, 3, 3.** Two copies of one maternal allele: sisters stayed together, consistent with **maternal meiosis II**.

The caveat is crossing over. A crossover between the marker and the centromere can make sisters differ at the marker, so a meiosis II error would look like a meiosis I error, and the reverse. Markers as close to the centromere as possible reduce that risk. This exercise models the classical two-step errors; human eggs also make others (see Going deeper).

## Too many or too few: trisomy and monosomy in people

Most aneuploid human embryos do not survive pregnancy. Of the autosomal trisomies, only 21, 18 and 13 are seen regularly at birth, and trisomy 21 is by far the commonest of these. Down syndrome occurs in about 1 in 700 births in widely cited estimates for the United States, a figure that varies by population and strongly by maternal age. Most cases are free trisomy 21, a separate extra chromosome. Studies that typed DNA markers in families found that most free trisomy 21 is maternal in origin, and that most maternal errors arise at meiosis I. The risk rises with the mother's age.

The sex chromosomes are different. **Turner syndrome** occurs in females; about half have monosomy X, a 45,X karyotype, and the rest have mosaicism (a mix of cell lines) or a partly lost or rearranged X. Most 45,X conceptions are thought to be lost in pregnancy, and many survivors are mosaic. **Klinefelter syndrome** is most often 47,XXY, a male with an extra X. Do not confuse it with 47,XYY.

Why are these tolerated when an extra 21 is so consequential? As [the lesson on sex linkage](/lessons/sex-linkage-and-beyond/) explained, mammalian cells silence every X beyond one, so extra X chromosomes add little active dose. The Y carries few genes. The features that remain are thought to come largely from genes that escape X inactivation, which are present in one active copy in 45,X and in extra active copies in 47,XXY.

## Whole extra sets: polyploidy

A **polyploid** has complete extra sets of chromosomes. An **autopolyploid** has extra sets from one species, often from a gamete that never reduced its chromosome number. An **allopolyploid** combines sets from two or more species, usually through a hybrid whose chromosome sets then doubled. Bread wheat is a hexaploid allopolyploid: six sets, 42 chromosomes, two sets from each of three ancestral grasses. Each set has its own partner, and genes such as *Ph1* keep chromosomes from pairing with their close relatives in the other ancestral sets, so meiosis still forms ordinary pairs.

Odd numbers of sets cause trouble. Most of the seedless bananas grown on a large scale are triploid, and three sets cannot be split evenly at meiosis, so nearly every gamete is unbalanced and seeds rarely form.

Polyploidy is common in flowering plants and rare in animals. Plants often self-fertilize or spread without sex, so a new polyploid does not need a matching partner, and plants appear to tolerate shifts in dose more readily. Exceptions exist among fish and frogs. In humans, triploidy (69 chromosomes) is almost always lost in pregnancy.

## Broken and rejoined: deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations

- A **deletion** removes a segment. Loss of part of the short arm of chromosome 5 causes cri du chat syndrome; a small deletion at 7q11.23 that includes the elastin gene underlies Williams syndrome.
- A **duplication** adds a copy of a segment. In 1991 Lupski and colleagues associated a duplication on 17p with Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1A.
- An **inversion** reverses a segment. A **pericentric** inversion includes the centromere; a **paracentric** one does not. In a heterozygote the chromosomes pair by forming a loop. A single crossover inside a paracentric loop gives one dicentric chromatid (two centromeres), which breaks at anaphase, and one acentric fragment (no centromere), which is lost. A crossover inside a pericentric loop gives chromatids that each carry a duplication and a deletion. Either way, recombinant gametes are unbalanced, so few recombinants appear among the offspring.
- A **reciprocal translocation** swaps segments between two chromosomes. A carrier's four chromosomes pair as a cross-shaped group of four. Alternate segregation gives normal or balanced gametes; adjacent segregation gives unbalanced ones.
- A **Robertsonian translocation** fuses the long arms of two acrocentric chromosomes (in humans 13, 14, 15, 21 and 22), losing most of their short arms. The lost parts carry repeated ribosomal RNA genes and other repetitive DNA, and the other acrocentrics keep many copies of those rRNA genes, so a carrier with 45 chromosomes is usually unaffected. If the fused chromosome includes 21 and a carrier passes it on together with a free 21, the child has translocation Down syndrome with 46 chromosomes.

The **Philadelphia chromosome** is the small chromosome 22 left by a reciprocal 9;22 translocation in blood cells, acquired rather than inherited. The reciprocal swap exchanges end segments between 9 and 22 and joins two genes, *BCR* and *ABL*; the fusion protein is a tyrosine kinase that stays switched on (see [the lesson on oncogenes](/lessons/oncogenes/)).

## Seeing it: from karyotype to microarray

A **karyotype** is a picture of a cell's chromosomes. Banded with trypsin and Giemsa, it shows number and large changes, about 5 to 10 million base pairs in routine work, and it is the standard routine genome-wide test that sees balanced rearrangements directly, though some genome sequencing and optical genome mapping methods can now find them too. **FISH** uses a fluorescent probe for one region, about 100 thousand base pairs, and works in nuclei that are not dividing, though it reports only on the probe's target. A **chromosomal microarray** measures the dose of segments across the genome, down to tens of thousands of base pairs where probes are dense, depending on the array and the region, but it is blind to balanced changes, and a dose-only array can miss triploidy. In one large prenatal study, microarray found an extra clinically relevant deletion or duplication in 6.0 percent of karyotypically normal samples from pregnancies with a structural anomaly. **Sequencing** can place breakpoints to the base, and it has shown that many rearrangements that look balanced under the microscope break genes or hide small losses.

### Design problem

A man with typical features has 45 chromosomes, including a Robertsonian translocation that joins 14 and 21. His child has Down syndrome. Explain why he is unaffected, list the gametes he can make, and say which zygotes you expect to survive with a normal egg. How would you confirm the explanation?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

He is unaffected because he has two doses of the long arms of 14 and 21, one free and one fused; the lost short arms hold repeated ribosomal RNA genes present on other chromosomes.

At meiosis, the free 14, the free 21 and the fused t(14;21) pair as a group of three. In the usual 2:1 split each gamete gets one or two of them, giving six types (a rare 3:0 split, with all three in one gamete and none in another, is left out):

1. 14 + 21: normal.
2. t(14;21): balanced carrier.
3. t(14;21) + 21: translocation trisomy 21 after fertilization.
4. 14 alone: monosomy 21.
5. t(14;21) + 14: trisomy 14.
6. 21 alone: monosomy 14.

Types 4, 5 and 6 are expected to be lost early in pregnancy. If all six were equally frequent and only types 1, 2 and 3 survived, one in three liveborn children would have Down syndrome. Observed risks are well below that, and published series find them lower when the carrier is the father than when it is the mother.

To confirm, karyotype the child, expecting 46 chromosomes including the t(14;21) and two free 21s, and karyotype both parents. FISH with a chromosome 21 probe can confirm the extra dose. This explains a mechanism; it is not reproductive advice.

## Going deeper

### Human egg errors and age

Human eggs add errors the two-step cartoon leaves out. Sister chromatids can separate early, at meiosis I, and in reverse segregation both pairs of sisters split at meiosis I. In donated human eggs, Gruhn and colleagues found that aneuploidy follows a U-shaped curve with donor age, higher in the youngest and the oldest donors. This describes aneuploid eggs from donors, not the risk of a live-born child with trisomy 21, which the main text says rises with the mother's age.

### How the Philadelphia chromosome was worked out

Nowell and Hungerford saw the small chromosome in 1960. Rowley found in 1973 that extra material on chromosome 9 matched what the 22 had lost, suggesting the missing piece had moved there. In the 1980s, other laboratories found that the swap joins two genes, *BCR* and *ABL*, and the fusion protein is a tyrosine kinase that stays switched on.

### Sex determination and extra chromosome sets

Some animals, such as the fruit fly, set sex by counting X chromosomes in a short window of early development, and an extra set of chromosomes can upset that count.
