**A macrophage pulls in about 3 percent of its surface membrane each minute—its whole surface roughly every half hour—without shrinking.** Membrane returns just as fast, and embedded receptors make the trip too, some often and some rarely. The first film followed that loop; here we add the sorting signals, experimental proof, and what the trip does to a receptor's signal.

## What's endocytosis used for?

**Endocytosis** pinches a patch of plasma membrane inward to form a **vesicle**, a small closed membrane bag carrying whatever bound that patch. It serves three jobs:

- **Food.** Blood cholesterol rides in **low-density lipoprotein (LDL)** particles, far too large for channels or carriers. Michael Brown and Joseph Goldstein showed that surface receptors bind LDL and carry it inside (**receptor-mediated endocytosis**); iron enters the same way bound to **transferrin**.
- **Membrane upkeep.** Secretion adds membrane to the surface and endocytosis takes it back, pulling worn membrane proteins in for disposal.
- **Signaling.** Endocytosis can **end** a signal by removing and destroying the receptor, or **move** it to an intracellular platform where the active receptor keeps signaling or signals differently—decided mostly after uptake.

<Support>**The one idea.** Early endosomes sort internalized receptors, returning untagged ones to the surface; active receptors can signal from endosomes until sealed inside internal vesicles. **Scope:** This describes typical transmembrane receptors passing through the early endosome. Receptors can also go silent earlier if acid releases their ligand, some cargo sorts to the Golgi or uses active recycling motifs, and a few receptors (such as TLR3, TLR7 and TLR9) signal only after reaching endosomes.</Support>

## Getting inside: a coat, a reader, and a cut

The first film animated pit formation and fission; here are the links that choose which receptors enter.

In a **clathrin-coated pit**, three-legged pinwheel **clathrin** units assemble into a curved cage on the inner face of the plasma membrane. Clathrin touches neither membrane nor cargo; adaptor proteins bridge them. The main plasma-membrane adaptor, **AP2**, binds the lipid **PIP2** (from the lesson on lipid anchors), short tail sequences, and clathrin. A receptor enters when its cytoplasmic tail carries a sequence an adaptor can read, or binds a linker such as **arrestin**.

Brown and Goldstein met the proof in patient J.D., whose receptors could bind LDL yet the cells could not take it in—a fault later traced to one tyrosine in the LDL receptor's cytoplasmic tail replaced by cysteine.

When the pit hangs by a thin neck, **dynamin**—a large GTPase—forms a collar around it and hydrolyzes GTP to cut the vesicle free; the coat then strips off so the bare vesicle can fuse. Other doors exist: **caveolae** (flask-shaped pits built around **caveolin**), **macropinocytosis** (gulping fluid), and less-mapped clathrin-independent routes. How hard a receptor signals can also alter its route and fate (see EGF receptor under Going deeper).

## The early endosome is a sorting station

Almost all endocytosed cargo first enters the **early endosome** beneath the plasma membrane, which sorts cargo into separate domains headed back to the surface, back to the Golgi, or toward the lysosome. Folding membrane inward leaves the ligand-binding end facing the interior fluid (**lumen**) and the cytoplasmic tail still pointing into the cytosol.

Two physical features drive default recycling:

- **Mild acid.** A proton pump holds the lumen near **pH 6 to 6.5** (three- to tenfold more protons than pH 7), releasing many ligands: LDL falls off its receptor, and iron falls off transferrin.
- **Tubular shape.** A sphere of radius *R* has surface-to-volume ratio 3/*R*; a thin cylinder of radius *r* has 2/*r*. A tubule with one-tenth the radius of the round body holds nearly seven times more membrane per unit volume—so equal membrane encloses only ~15% of the sphere's fluid. Untagged membrane proteins diffuse into the tubules while dissolved cargo stays mostly in the round body.

**Predict.** At pH 6 inside an early endosome, Ligand A detaches from its receptor while Ligand B stays tightly bound. Neither receptor carries ubiquitin, and both drift into thin tubules. Where does Ligand B end up?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Ligand B rides back to the surface attached to its receptor in the tubule membrane, because it is held in membrane rather than dissolved; freed Ligand A stays mostly in the fluid-filled round body.

</details>

As the first film showed, pinched-off tubules recycle receptors rapidly via **Rab4** (a few minutes) or more slowly through a second compartment via **Rab4** and **Rab11** (tens of minutes). An LDL receptor makes that round trip roughly every ten minutes.

**Rab GTPases label compartments and drive maturation.** Rabs are small GTPase switches (on with GTP, off with GDP, flipped by GEFs and GAPs, from the lesson on GTPase switches). Human cells have more than sixty, and most mark a compartment, a domain within one, or a traffic step. **Rab5** marks the early endosome; in its GTP form it sits on the membrane and recruits **effectors**, proteins that bind only the on state, which tether incoming vesicles and help them fuse. (Going deeper explains how GDI, a displacement factor, and GEFs target lipid-tailed Rabs to the right membrane.)

## Headed to the lysosome: the receptor is sealed inside

The second film showed the topological flip that silences a receptor; here are the enzymes, competition, and live-cell evidence behind it.

A receptor leaves the default route when tagged with **ubiquitin**—added to active **EGF receptors** by the ligase **Cbl** (from the lesson on receptor tyrosine kinases). Rather than targeting the proteasome, endosomal ubiquitin is bound by **ESCRT**, which gathers tagged receptors and pushes the patch *inward* (away from the cytosol) into internal vesicles, forming a **multivesicular endosome (MVE)** or **multivesicular body**. Because endosomal deubiquitinating enzymes can clip ubiquitin back off, sorting is a reversible competition until sealing completes.

**Predict.** If a cell overproduces a deubiquitinating enzyme that strips ubiquitin from active EGF receptors on early endosomes, how do their destination and signaling duration change?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Most likely, without ubiquitin ESCRT does not sequester them into internal vesicles; they stay on the outer membrane, recycle to the surface, and signal longer instead of entering lysosomes.

</details>

Sealing the receptor inside an internal vesicle cuts its cytoplasmic tail off from cytosolic partners—ending the signal *before* digestion—and places the whole receptor inside for lysosomal enzymes. Meanwhile, Marino Zerial's laboratory filmed single endosomes in living cells and watched **Rab5** leave as **Rab7** arrived (**Rab conversion**). With Rab7 come new effectors, stronger acid (**~pH 5.5** in **late endosomes**), and more internal vesicles, before fusion with a **lysosome** (digestive enzymes at **pH 4.5 to 5**) breaks the receptor down.

## A receptor that keeps talking, and one that escapes

Recall the second half of our rule: **active receptors can signal from endosomes until sealed inside internal vesicles.**

In 1996 Sandra Schmid's laboratory tested this in HeLa cells by blocking endocytosis with a dominant-negative **dynamin** mutant defective in GTP binding and hydrolysis, leaving active EGF receptors on the surface.

**Predict.** If endocytosis *only* ended signals, what should blocking dynamin do to every EGF output—both cell division and MAP kinase ERK activation?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Every output should rise or persist. Instead, the result split: cells divided more, yet full ERK activation fell.

</details>

That split argued that part of the EGF signal required uptake—though cautiously, because dynamin has other cellular effects and later studies blocking uptake by other methods did not all see the same fall in ERK.

Other receptors clearly change output upon uptake. **Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)** signals via **MyD88** at the plasma membrane and via **TRIF** on endosomes to switch on interferon genes. Nucleic-acid sensors **TLR3, TLR7, and TLR9** work only in endosomes, where microbial remains arrive. Some **G-protein-coupled receptors**, internalized with **arrestin** (from the lesson on turning signals off), also keep activating G proteins from endosomes.

Break degradative sorting and signaling persists. In roughly 3 percent of non-small-cell lung cancers, a splicing error deletes **exon 14** from mRNA for the receptor tyrosine kinase **MET**, removing **tyrosine 1003** (the Cbl docking site). Poorly ubiquitinated, mutant MET escapes the lysosome and keeps signaling for longer than it should—not an overactive kinase, just a receptor missing its turn. Two MET kinase inhibitors, **capmatinib** and **tepotinib**, are approved for these tumors.

**Predict.** Suppose a normal receptor recycles 50% and degrades 50% on each 10-minute endosomal pass, while a sorting mutant recycles 90% and degrades 10%. Starting with equal pools and no new synthesis, what fraction remains after two passes (20 minutes)?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Normal: 0.5 × 0.5 = 25%. Mutant: 0.9 × 0.9 = 81%—more than three times as many surviving receptors after just two passes.

</details>

A neuron faces the hardest version of this problem, carrying an active signaling endosome the length of an axon—our next lesson.

### Design problem

Two receptors on one cell bind ligand simultaneously. One must signal for ~10 minutes and then stay silent for hours; the other must signal for hours. Using only early-endosome sorting decisions, specify what tags or sequences each needs.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Give the brief receptor an activity-dependent ubiquitin-ligase docking site (such as a self-phosphorylated tail tyrosine), lysines for tagging, and a ligand that stays bound at pH 6 so it remains active and tagged. ESCRT seals it into internal vesicles, stopping the signal for hours until new receptors are synthesized.

Give the lasting receptor no ligase site and a ligand that dissociates at pH 6. Empty receptors recycle via tubules in minutes to bind again; adding a recycling-machinery tail motif—like the PDZ-binding sequence at the end of the β2-adrenergic receptor—makes return even surer.

Cells use this logic: on the EGF receptor, EGF tends to stay bound in endosomes and drives lysosomal destruction, whereas transforming growth factor alpha lets go in mild acid and the receptor recycles.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How a Rab finds its membrane

Most Rabs carry one or two 20-carbon **geranylgeranyl** lipids on their tail, which would stick to any membrane. Working on **Rab9**, Suzanne Pfeffer's laboratory showed that soluble **GDI** wraps GDP-bound Rab to hide its tail, a membrane **displacement factor** on endosomes frees it from GDI, and GDP is exchanged for GTP on arrival. Later work points to each Rab's **GEF** as a main part of the address: a Rab is switched on, and so held, on the membrane where its GEF sits. GDI does not pull off the GTP form, so the Rab stays put until a **GAP** switches it off.

### A lipid of late endosomes

Late-endosome internal vesicles are rich in an unusual lipid, **lysobisphosphatidic acid**, which is scarce in early endosomes.

### Dose and route for the EGF receptor

In HeLa cells, Sara Sigismund, Pier Paolo Di Fiore, and colleagues reported that low EGF doses drive EGF receptor uptake almost entirely via coated pits toward recycling, whereas high doses add ubiquitin, shift a share to a clathrin-independent route, and favor destruction. Treat that as one cell line's result, since other labs see clathrin carrying most receptors at high doses too; the safe conclusion is that signaling strength can influence both entry route and fate.
