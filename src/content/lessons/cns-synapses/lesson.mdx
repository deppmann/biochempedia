techniques:
  - name: "Heterologous synapse-formation assay"
    blurb: >-
      A non-neuronal cell line is made to produce one candidate protein and grown with neurons.
      If axons or dendrites build synaptic specializations only on cells carrying the protein,
      the protein is sufficient to induce them under those conditions. It says nothing about
      whether the animal needs it.
  - name: "Immunopanning"
    blurb: >-
      A cell suspension is passed over dishes coated with antibodies that bind unwanted cell
      types, then over a dish that binds the wanted type. The result is a culture of one kind of
      neuron with almost no glia, to which glia or single factors can be added back.
  - name: "Whole-cell recording of miniature currents"
    blurb: >-
      A glass pipette sealed onto a neuron records the small currents caused by single vesicles
      released at its synapses, with action potentials blocked by tetrodotoxin. Their frequency
      reflects the number of active synapses and how often each releases; their size reflects
      the receptors on the receiving side and the amount of transmitter in each vesicle.
  - name: "FM dye uptake"
    blurb: >-
      A fluorescent dye in the bath is taken into vesicles as they are retrieved after release.
      Bright spots after stimulation mark terminals that are cycling vesicles, a functional test
      that a cluster of vesicle proteins is a working terminal.

**A kidney cell line does not form synapses. Yet if it makes one neuronal protein, neuroligin, the axons that touch it in a dish gather synaptic vesicles at the point of contact and start releasing them, as if they had found a partner.**

That result, from Peter Scheiffele and Tito Serafini's laboratory in 2000, sets the questions for this lesson. What tells a growth cone to become a nerve terminal? What builds the receiving side? What do astrocytes add? And why can a protein that builds synapses in a dish turn out not to be needed for synapses in the animal?

## The idea: both partners arrive almost ready

The professor lists three "challenges of synapse formation": making selective connections, differentiating a nerve terminal, and building a postsynaptic apparatus. Partner choice is the subject of [that lesson](/lessons/partners-layers-self-avoidance/). This lesson takes the second and third jobs in the brain and spinal cord, the central nervous system.

Start with his comparison, "NMJ vs. Central synapses." At a mature mammalian neuromuscular junction (see [that lesson](/lessons/neuromuscular-junction/)), a muscle fiber is usually contacted by one motor axon at one site, and the synapse uses one transmitter, acetylcholine, with one main receptor. A neuron in the brain differs on every count. A cortical pyramidal neuron receives thousands of synapses from many neurons. Excitatory synapses that use glutamate and inhibitory synapses that use GABA sit on the same dendrite, each with its own receptors. So a single signal that says "cluster receptors here" cannot be enough. Each contact has to specify what kind of synapse it will be.

A second observation from his lectures: neurons manufacture many synaptic building blocks before contact. In 1983 two groups showed that growth cones of cultured frog and chick neurons release acetylcholine before reaching a muscle, detected with a patch of receptor-rich membrane held near the growth cone. Presynaptic proteins travel down the axon in preassembled transport packets; in cultured hippocampal neurons, Stephen Smith's laboratory watched packets carrying active-zone proteins arrive at new contacts. In culture, dendrites can carry small clusters of receptors, sometimes with scaffold proteins, away from any axon.

His phrase for this: "Lonesome structures indicate that growth cones and dendrites are poised on the brink of differentiating into synaptic specialization." And the rule that follows: "Presynaptic and postsynaptic specializations can form in isolation, but stable synapses require both." Think of two halves of a bridge built out from opposite banks. Each half can stand for a while. Only joined do they carry traffic and last.

He also asks, "The decision to form a synapse: calcium signal?" In cultured frog spinal neurons, contact with a muscle cell raised free calcium in the neurite, but contact with another neuron did not; in a cultured snail motor neuron, a cAMP analogue mimicked the boost in calcium entry that contact with its target produced. In these experiments the trigger came from the partner, from its surface or from the space between the cells.

## Signals that turn a growth cone into a bouton

Start with adhesion. Classical cadherins are membrane proteins with five extracellular repeats that bind the same cadherin on the facing cell (homophilic binding) only when calcium is present. Nectins, held inside the cell by afadin, and SynCAM, a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily, also span the cleft. SynCAM made by a non-neuronal cell induced presynaptic terminals in contacting axons in a 2002 study. The question is which adhesion proteins only hold membranes together and which also instruct.

His example is the mossy fiber synapse of the cerebellum. Neurons in the pons, part of the brainstem, send axons called mossy fibers to cerebellar granule cells. The pontine growth cones carry neurexin and FGFR2, and they respond to Wnt. The granule cells make neuroligin, Wnt7a and FGF22. That gives three pairs:

1. **Neurexin and neuroligin.** Neurexins are axon-side membrane proteins; neuroligins are their dendrite-side partners. Scheiffele's experiment above showed that neuroligin alone, on a cell that is not a neuron, makes pontine axons cluster vesicle proteins that recycle. Soluble beta-neurexin, which caps neuroligin, blocked the effect. The signal also runs the other way: Ann Marie Craig's laboratory reported in 2004 that neurexin on a non-neuronal cell clusters postsynaptic proteins in dendrites that touch it.
2. **Wnt7a and its receptor.** In 2000 Anna Hall, Frances Lucas and Patricia Salinas reported that Wnt7a from granule cells makes mossy fiber ends spread and branch and gather the vesicle protein synapsin I. In mice lacking Wnt7a, maturation of the mossy fiber terminals was delayed. Wnt7a signals through Frizzled receptors in other neurons (see [that lesson](/lessons/wnt-signaling/)), but which receptor the mossy fibers use has not been identified.
3. **FGF22 and FGFR2.** In 2004 Hisashi Umemori, Joshua Sanes and colleagues reported that FGF22, made by granule cells, promotes vesicle clustering in mossy fiber axons through FGFR2, a receptor tyrosine kinase (see [that lesson](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)), and that in mice, neutralizing FGF22 and its relatives FGF7 and FGF10, or inactivating FGFR2, reduced presynaptic differentiation of mossy fibers.

### Design problem

You make a kidney cell line produce neuroligin and grow it under cultured pontine or hippocampal axons. What do you measure to show that an axon has built a real presynaptic terminal at the contact? Which two controls rule out mere adhesion?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Measure two things at each contact. First, stain for synaptic vesicle proteins such as synapsin and synaptophysin, and ask whether they form clusters where the axon crosses a neuroligin-expressing cell more often than where it crosses other cells. Second, test function. Stimulate the axons in the presence of an FM dye, wash, and look for bright spots at the contacts. Dye taken up during stimulation marks vesicles that were released and retrieved, so the cluster is a working terminal and not a pile of parts.

For controls, use cells that are untransfected or that make a different adhesion molecule, which the axons also touch and stick to. If clusters form only on neuroligin cells, stickiness alone does not explain them. Then add soluble neurexin, which binds neuroligin and prevents the axon's own neurexin from reaching it. If the clusters disappear, the effect runs through the neurexin and neuroligin bond. Scheiffele and colleagues used tests of this kind in 2000.

</details>

Now the trap. "Sufficient in a dish" is not "required in the animal." In 2003 Markus Missler, Thomas Südhof and colleagues reported mice lacking all three alpha-neurexins. They died soon after birth, and their excitatory synapses were present at close to normal density, with moderately fewer inhibitory synapses, but released transmitter poorly. In 2006 mice lacking neuroligins 1, 2 and 3 were reported to have synapses in normal number in the brainstem, with impaired transmission, more so at inhibitory synapses, and they died from failed breathing. So alpha-neurexins and neuroligins are required for normal synapse function and maturation, not for forming most synapses. Many organizers overlap, and a dish holding one of them on a bare cell tests what that one can do, not what the brain depends on.

## The receiving side: a scaffold that holds receptors

Under an excitatory terminal sits the postsynaptic density, a dense layer of protein under the membrane that shows as a dark thickening in electron micrographs. One of its most abundant proteins is PSD-95, a scaffold of the MAGUK family with three PDZ domains, short modules that grip the last few amino acids of a partner protein (see [that lesson](/lessons/protein-interaction-modules/)). The first two PDZ domains bind the tail of NMDA receptor GluN2 subunits. The third binds the tail of neuroligin. That gives a chain across the cleft: neurexin in the axon, neuroligin in the dendrite, PSD-95 beneath it, glutamate receptors held by PSD-95. A scaffold works like a pegboard: whatever has the right hook hangs in the right place (see [that lesson](/lessons/adaptors-and-scaffolds/)).

AMPA receptors, the glutamate-gated channels that carry most fast excitatory current (see [that lesson](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/)), do not bind PSD-95 directly. The clue came from a mouse. Stargazer is a spontaneous mutant that tosses its head upward and has seizures. Its gene encodes stargazin, a small membrane protein. In stargazer cerebellar granule cells, mossy fiber synapses give NMDA receptor responses but no AMPA receptor responses. In 2000 the laboratories of David Bredt and Roger Nicoll reported that stargazin binds AMPA receptors and brings them to the cell surface, and that its tail, which binds PSD-95's PDZ domains, holds them at synapses. Stargazin became the first of the transmembrane AMPA receptor regulatory proteins, or TARPs. Other TARPs serve other neurons, which is one reason the stargazer defect shows most clearly in cerebellar granule cells.

Inhibitory synapses use a different scaffold. Gephyrin forms dense clusters under the terminal that hold glycine receptors and many GABA-A receptors.

The signal for the receiving side can come from far away. In mouse sympathetic ganglia, Sharma, Deppmann, Harrington and colleagues reported in 2010 that nerve growth factor from the distant target tissue is needed for postsynaptic assembly in the ganglion neurons, and that postsynaptic assembly is in turn a prerequisite for presynaptic assembly, although preganglionic axons still grow into the ganglion without the growth factor. Signaling endosomes carrying its receptor TrkA travel back from the axon into the dendrites.

## Astrocytes add the missing signal

Ben Barres's laboratory could purify rat retinal ganglion cells by immunopanning, leaving cultures with almost no glia. In 1997 Frank Pfrieger and Barres reported that these neurons survived, grew and fired action potentials, yet barely spoke to one another. A layer of glia across the dish, not touching the neurons, or medium the glia had conditioned, changed that. With action potentials blocked by tetrodotoxin, the frequency of miniature synaptic currents rose roughly twelvefold, and failures of evoked transmission fell from about one stimulus in five to about one in fifty. Neuron survival did not change, so glia were not simply keeping more neurons alive.

In 2001 Erik Ullian and colleagues in the same laboratory reported that astrocytes raised the number of synapses about sevenfold and were needed to keep them. In 2005 Karen Christopherson and colleagues identified thrombospondins 1 and 2 as astrocyte-secreted proteins that induce synapses. Those synapses looked normal and released transmitter but were postsynaptically silent, with no AMPA receptor responses, and mice lacking both thrombospondins had fewer excitatory synapses. Later work identified other astrocyte factors, including glypicans 4 and 6, which recruit AMPA receptors and make such synapses respond, and cholesterol carried in lipoproteins. So astrocytes supply more than one kind of signal: some mainly build synapses, others mainly make them respond.

Keep the scope. Neurons form some synapses without astrocytes, and the size of the glial effect depends on the culture system. Astrocytes add to synapse formation; they are not its sole cause.

## Maturation: GABA changes sign

A new synapse keeps changing after it forms. At the neuromuscular junction the acetylcholine receptor swaps its fetal gamma subunit for the adult epsilon subunit. In the brain, the best-known change is in GABA. In immature rodent hippocampal and cortical neurons, chloride inside the cell is high, because the transporter NKCC1 brings chloride in and the exporter KCC2 is scarce. Opening GABA-A channels then lets chloride flow out, and GABA depolarizes the cell. As KCC2 rises over the first postnatal weeks, internal chloride falls and GABA hyperpolarizes. > **E(Cl) = 61 mV × log10([Cl] inside / [Cl] outside)**

With 30 mM chloride inside and 130 mM outside, E(Cl) is about −39 mV, above a resting potential near −60 mV, so opening the channel depolarizes. With 7 mM inside, E(Cl) is about −77 mV, and the same channel now hyperpolarizes. Depolarizing is not the same as exciting, though. An open GABA-A channel also damps firing by shunting current, and in the cortex of newborn mice GABA depolarizes most neurons yet still holds network activity down.
