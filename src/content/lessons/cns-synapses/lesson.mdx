      pair of binding proteins into a candidate synapse organizer. The same coculture design,

techniques:
  - name: "Heterologous synapse-formation assay"
    blurb: >-
      A non-neuronal cell line is made to produce one candidate protein and grown with neurons.
      If axons or dendrites build synaptic specializations only on cells carrying the protein,
      the protein is sufficient to induce them under those conditions. It says nothing about
      whether the animal needs it.
  - name: "Immunopanning"
    blurb: >-
      A cell suspension is passed over dishes coated with antibodies that bind unwanted cell
      types, then over a dish that binds the wanted type. The result is a culture of one kind of
      neuron with almost no glia, to which glia or single factors can be added back.
  - name: "Whole-cell recording of miniature currents"
    blurb: >-
      A glass pipette sealed onto a neuron records the small currents caused by single vesicles
      released at its synapses, with action potentials blocked by tetrodotoxin. Their frequency
      reflects the number of active synapses and how often each releases; their size reflects
      the receptors on the receiving side and the amount of transmitter in each vesicle.
  - name: "FM dye uptake"
    blurb: >-
      A fluorescent dye in the bath is taken into vesicles as they are retrieved after release.
      Bright spots after stimulation mark terminals that are cycling vesicles, a functional test
      that a cluster of vesicle proteins is a working terminal.

**A kidney cell does not make synapses. Give it one neuronal protein, neuroligin, and the axons that touch it in a dish build nerve terminals on it.**

The result came from Peter Scheiffele and Tito Serafini's laboratory in 2000, and it sets the questions for this lesson. What tells a growth cone to become a nerve terminal? What builds the receiving side? What do astrocytes add? And one question to hold: if neuroligin can start a terminal on a kidney cell, what do you expect in mice that lack neuroligins 1, 2 and 3? Fewer synapses, normal synapses, or synapses that are there but do not work? Keep your answer for [the section on dish and mouse](#a-dish-shows-what-a-protein-can-do-a-mouse-shows-what-the-brain-needs).

## Three things a working synapse does

A finished synapse has three properties, and each needs its own measurement.

1. **It is there.** A nerve terminal sits against a receiving site. You see it by electron microscopy, or by staining for presynaptic and postsynaptic proteins that sit side by side.
2. **It releases.** The terminal releases transmitter from vesicles and retrieves them. You see it with a dye or antibody label that recycling vesicles take up, or by a response on the other side.
3. **It responds.** Receptors on the receiving side open when transmitter arrives. You see it by recording currents from the receiving cell. A synapse can respond through one kind of receptor and not another.

Two more words come from [the lesson on the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/). A factor is **sufficient** if adding it is enough to produce an effect in a system that is otherwise ready, under the conditions tested. It is **necessary** if the effect does not happen without it. A dish with one protein on a bare cell tests sufficiency. A knockout mouse, with a gene disrupted, tests necessity.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson. A synapse that is there is not yet a synapse that works.** A signal can change more than one of the three properties, and each experiment sees only the ones it measures. The rule is about central excitatory and inhibitory synapses as they form and first mature, in the systems this lesson uses: brainstem and cortex in the knockouts, the cerebellar mossy fiber synapse, and purified retinal ganglion cells in culture.

## Why central synapses are harder than the neuromuscular junction

Synapse formation has three jobs: (1) making selective connections, (2) differentiating a nerve terminal, and (3) building a postsynaptic apparatus. Partner choice, the first, is the subject of [the lesson on partners, layers and self-avoidance](/lessons/partners-layers-self-avoidance/). This lesson takes the second and third in the brain and spinal cord, the central nervous system.

At a mature mammalian neuromuscular junction (see [the lesson on the neuromuscular junction](/lessons/neuromuscular-junction/)), a muscle fiber is usually contacted by one motor axon at one site, and the synapse uses one transmitter, acetylcholine, with one main receptor.

**Predict.** A cortical pyramidal neuron receives thousands of synapses from many neurons. Excitatory synapses that use glutamate and inhibitory synapses that use GABA sit on the same dendrite. Why can a single signal that says "cluster receptors here" not be enough?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A neuron in the brain differs from the muscle fiber on every count. Each kind of synapse on that dendrite has its own receptors, so "cluster receptors here" does not say which receptors. Each contact has to specify what kind of synapse it will be.

</details>

Both partners also arrive partly ready. Neurons manufacture many synaptic building blocks before contact. Presynaptic proteins travel down the axon in preassembled transport packets. In culture, a growth cone that never meets a partner can still cluster vesicles and release transmitter, and a dendrite with no axon nearby can carry small clusters of receptors, sometimes with scaffold proteins. Each side can form its specialization alone, but stable synapses require both: contact enlarges and stabilizes what each side began. Think of two halves of a bridge built out from opposite banks: each can stand for a while, but only joined do they carry traffic and last.

## Signals that turn a growth cone into a nerve terminal

Many adhesion proteins span the synaptic cleft, among them cadherins, nectins and SynCAM. The question is which of them only hold membranes together and which also instruct, acting as **organizers**: signals that tell a partner to build its side of a synapse.

A well-studied case is the mossy fiber synapse of the cerebellum. Neurons in the pons, part of the brainstem, send axons called mossy fibers to cerebellar granule cells, and the granule cells make three signals that act on them:

1. **Neurexin and neuroligin.** Neurexins are axon-side membrane proteins; neuroligins, made by granule cells, are their dendrite-side partners. In Scheiffele's experiment, kidney cells made neuroligin and were grown under axons from explants of the pons. Where an axon crossed such a cell, vesicle proteins clustered. When the axons were depolarized, the clusters took up antibodies against the inside face of the vesicle protein synaptotagmin, which they can reach only when a vesicle opens to the outside. So vesicles were being released and retrieved there. Control cells, which the axons also touched, did not do this. Soluble beta-neurexin, which binds neuroligin and keeps the axon's own neurexin from reaching it, blocked the effect. The signal also runs the other way: Ann Marie Craig's laboratory reported in 2004 that neurexin on a non-neuronal cell clusters postsynaptic proteins in dendrites that touch it.
2. **Wnt7a and its receptor.** In 2000 Anna Hall, Frances Lucas and Patricia Salinas reported that Wnt7a from granule cells makes mossy fiber ends spread and branch and gather the vesicle protein synapsin I. In mice lacking Wnt7a, maturation of the mossy fiber terminals was delayed. Wnt7a signals through Frizzled receptors in other neurons (see [the lesson on Wnt signaling](/lessons/wnt-signaling/)), but which receptor the mossy fibers use has not been identified.
3. **FGF22 and FGFR2.** In 2004 Hisashi Umemori, Joshua Sanes and colleagues reported that FGF22, made by granule cells, promotes vesicle clustering in mossy fiber axons through FGFR2, a receptor tyrosine kinase (see [the lesson on receptor tyrosine kinases](/lessons/receptor-tyrosine-kinases/)), and that in mice, neutralizing FGF22 and its relatives FGF7 and FGF10, or inactivating FGFR2, reduced presynaptic differentiation of mossy fibers.

Whether the brain needs neuroligin waits until you know how the receiving side is built and how its responses are read.

## The receiving side: a scaffold that holds receptors

Two kinds of glutamate receptor matter here. AMPA receptors are the glutamate-gated channels that carry most fast excitatory current (see [the lesson on ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/)). NMDA receptors open when glutamate and a co-agonist are bound, but in physiological extracellular magnesium most of their current is blocked near the resting potential; depolarization relieves the block.

Under an excitatory terminal sits the postsynaptic density, a dense layer of protein under the membrane that shows as a dark thickening in electron micrographs. One of its most abundant proteins is PSD-95, a membrane-associated scaffold (a MAGUK) with three PDZ domains, short modules that grip the last few amino acids of a partner protein (see [the lesson on protein interaction modules](/lessons/protein-interaction-modules/)). The first two PDZ domains bind the tail of NMDA receptor GluN2 subunits. The third binds the tail of neuroligin. A scaffold works like a pegboard: whatever has the right hook hangs in the right place (see [the lesson on adaptors and scaffolds](/lessons/adaptors-and-scaffolds/)).

AMPA receptors do not bind PSD-95 directly. The clue to how they get there came from a mouse. Stargazer is a spontaneous mutant that tosses its head upward and has seizures. Its gene encodes stargazin, a small membrane protein.

**Predict.** In stargazer cerebellar granule cells, mossy fiber synapses give NMDA receptor responses but no AMPA receptor responses. Which of the three properties has failed: being there, releasing, or responding?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The synapses are there, and they release: an NMDA response needs glutamate, so transmitter is being released. What fails is one kind of response: the same synapse responds through NMDA receptors but not through AMPA receptors. A count of synapses would miss this defect. A recording shows it, and so does labeling the AMPA receptors.

</details>

In 2000 the laboratories of David Bredt and Roger Nicoll reported that stargazin binds AMPA receptors and brings them to the cell surface, and that its tail, which binds PSD-95's PDZ domains, holds them at synapses. Stargazin became the first of the transmembrane AMPA receptor regulatory proteins, or TARPs. Other TARPs serve other neurons, which is one reason the stargazer defect shows most clearly in cerebellar granule cells.

Inhibitory synapses use a different scaffold. Gephyrin forms dense clusters under the terminal that hold glycine receptors and many GABA-A receptors.

## A dish shows what a protein can do; a mouse shows what the brain needs

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The kidney-cell result makes neuroligin **sufficient**, under these conditions, to start presynaptic differentiation. It does not say whether neuroligin is **necessary**.

**Predict.** Mice are made that lack the alpha-neurexins, or that lack neuroligins 1, 2 and 3. What two measurements would tell "fewer synapses" from "synapses that are there but do not work"? Then check the answer you gave at the start of the lesson.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Count the synapses, and record the currents they produce in the receiving cell.

In 2003 Markus Missler, Thomas Südhof and colleagues reported mice lacking all three alpha-neurexins. They died soon after birth. Their excitatory synapses were present at close to normal density, inhibitory synapses were moderately fewer, and the synapses released transmitter poorly. In 2006 mice lacking neuroligins 1, 2 and 3 were reported to have synapses in normal number in the brainstem, with impaired transmission, more so at inhibitory synapses. They died because breathing failed.

Both results fit best with the answer that these proteins are needed for normal function and maturation more than for forming most synapses.

</details>

With stargazer added:

| Result | Synapses there? | Do they release? | Do they respond? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Neuroligin on a kidney cell (dish) | presynaptic vesicle clusters form on the non-neuronal cell | yes: recycling vesicles | postsynaptic response not tested by this assay |
| No alpha-neurexins (mouse) | excitatory near normal; inhibitory moderately fewer | poorly | — |
| No neuroligins 1 to 3 (mouse brainstem) | normal number | not separated: transmission impaired, inhibitory most | not separated |
| No stargazin (stargazer granule cells) | yes | yes: NMDA receptors respond | NMDA yes, AMPA no |

A dash means the result as given here does not test that property separately.

Read down the columns. In every mouse most synapses are present (in stargazer, shown by their NMDA responses), and recording found something wrong in every one. A synapse can be present without working, so normal counts never prove normal function. The reverse also matters: without the contact there is nothing to work, which is why deletions that lower number do cause deficits.

Why would the brain manage without a protein that works so well in a dish? One likely reason is that several organizers overlap, so removing one family leaves others to start contacts; those usually named are SynCAM, LRRTMs, cadherins and the secreted Wnts and FGFs. Another is that some organizers specify or check contacts rather than start them. Neither reading says these proteins never affect number. "No alpha-neurexins" is not "no neurexins": beta-neurexins were still made in the alpha-neurexin triple knockout, as neuroligin 4 was in the neuroligin 1 to 3 knockout. Later deletions of all neurexins showed that the effect on number depends on the cell type, and in some kinds of interneuron synapse number falls.

## Astrocytes: building and switching on

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Astrocytes add to synapse formation but are not its sole cause: neurons form some synapses without them, and the size of the glial effect depends on the culture system.

Ben Barres's laboratory could purify rat retinal ganglion cells by immunopanning, which left cultures with almost no glia. In 1997 Frank Pfrieger and Barres reported that these neurons survived, grew and fired action potentials, yet barely spoke to one another.

To read their result you need one tool. A **miniature current**, or mini, is the small current from one vesicle released on its own. Tetrodotoxin blocks action potentials, which removes release driven by firing, so what remains depends on the synapses and not on how busy the neurons are. Then:

> **detected mini frequency ≈ (synapses giving detectable minis) × (their average rate of releasing a vesicle on its own)**
>
> **mini size: with recording conditions fixed, set mainly by the receptors facing the vesicle and the transmitter in it**
>
> Detection also depends on recording noise.

A layer of glia across the dish, not touching the neurons, or medium the glia had conditioned, changed the picture. With action potentials blocked, mini frequency rose about twelvefold. Evoked transmission, a separate measurement, failed much less often. Neuron survival did not change, so glia were not simply keeping more neurons alive.

**Predict.** Did the glia make more synapses, or make each synapse release more often? What would you measure to decide?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Mini frequency alone cannot say: more minis can come from more synapses, from each synapse releasing more often, or from synapses that become detectable. So Pfrieger and Barres also counted synapses by electron microscopy, and found about 2.3 times as many with glia.

That is far short of twelvefold. If the same share of synapses gave detectable minis in both conditions, each would release about 5 times as often (12 ÷ 2.3 ≈ 5.2), and the paper read most of the rise as more effective synapses. The minis also got larger, so some extra events may simply have become big enough to detect. In 2001 Erik Ullian and colleagues in the same laboratory reported that astrocytes raised synapse number about sevenfold under their own culture conditions and were needed to keep them. That is much larger than the 2.3-fold count of 1997, so how much of the rise is number and how much is efficacy depends on the culture, and neither paper's count settles it alone.

</details>

In 2005 Karen Christopherson and colleagues identified thrombospondins 1 and 2 as astrocyte-secreted proteins that induce synapses. Those synapses looked normal by electron microscopy and released transmitter but were postsynaptically **silent**: they give no AMPA response near rest. They lack working AMPA receptors, and any NMDA receptors present would be mostly blocked by magnesium at that voltage. Such a synapse adds to the anatomical count but not to the synapses giving detectable AMPA minis near rest. And thrombospondin is one of the cases where number does fall: mice lacking both thrombospondins had fewer excitatory synapses.

Later work identified other astrocyte factors, including glypicans 4 and 6, which recruit AMPA receptors and make such synapses respond, and cholesterol carried in lipoproteins. Thrombospondin mainly builds; the glypicans mainly switch synapses on, and these assays do not show a fixed order.

## Maturation: GABA changes sign

Even after a synapse responds, what the response does to the cell can change. At the neuromuscular junction the acetylcholine receptor swaps its fetal gamma subunit for the adult epsilon subunit. In the brain, the best-known change is in GABA. In immature rodent hippocampal and cortical neurons, chloride inside the cell is high, because the transporter NKCC1 brings chloride in and the exporter KCC2 is scarce.

**Predict.** When GABA-A channels, which pass chloride, open in such a neuron, which way does chloride flow, and what does that do to the membrane voltage? What should change as KCC2 rises?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Chloride flows out when E(Cl) is above the membrane potential, as it is when internal chloride is high, so GABA depolarizes the cell. E(Cl), the voltage that open chloride channels pull the membrane toward, comes from the Nernst equation for chloride at 37 °C:

> **E(Cl) = 61 mV × log10([Cl] inside / [Cl] outside)**

With 30 mM chloride inside and 130 mM outside, E(Cl) is about −39 mV, above a resting potential near −60 mV, so opening the channel depolarizes. As KCC2 rises over the first postnatal weeks, internal chloride falls. With 7 mM inside, E(Cl) is about −77 mV, and the same channel now hyperpolarizes.

</details>

Depolarizing is not the same as exciting, though. An open GABA-A channel also damps firing by shunting current, and in the cortex of newborn mice GABA depolarizes most neurons yet still holds network activity down.

### Design problem

You make a kidney cell line produce neuroligin and grow it under cultured pontine or hippocampal axons. What do you measure to show that an axon has built a real presynaptic terminal at the contact? Which two controls rule out mere adhesion? And once the dish works, what experiment in the animal comes next, and what two things must you measure in it?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Measure two things at each contact. First, stain for synaptic vesicle proteins such as synapsin and synaptophysin, and ask whether they form clusters where the axon crosses a neuroligin-expressing cell more often than where it crosses other cells. Second, test function. Stimulate the axons in the presence of an FM dye, wash, and look for bright spots at the contacts. Dye taken up during stimulation marks vesicles that were released and retrieved, so the cluster is a working terminal and not a pile of parts.

For controls, use cells that are untransfected or that make a different adhesion molecule, which the axons also touch and stick to. If clusters form only on neuroligin cells, stickiness alone does not explain them. Then add soluble neurexin to block the bond in parallel dishes. If clusters fail to form, the effect runs through the neurexin and neuroligin bond. Scheiffele and colleagues used tests of this kind in 2000.

To test necessity, make mice lacking the protein, or, since there are several neuroligins, lacking neuroligins 1, 2 and 3 together. Then both count synapses, by electron microscopy or staining, and record transmission, with evoked and miniature currents. Counting alone would have missed the 2006 result.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Adhesion proteins that span the cleft

Classical cadherins are membrane proteins with five extracellular repeats that bind the same cadherin on the facing cell (homophilic binding) only when calcium is present. Nectins are held inside the cell by afadin. SynCAM is a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily, and SynCAM made by a non-neuronal cell induced presynaptic terminals in contacting axons in a 2002 study, so it passes the same sufficiency test as neuroligin.

### Early growth-cone experiments and the calcium signal

In 1983 two groups showed that growth cones of cultured frog and chick neurons release acetylcholine before reaching a muscle, detected with a patch of receptor-rich membrane held near the growth cone.

In cultured hippocampal neurons, Stephen Smith's laboratory watched the packets carrying active-zone proteins arrive at new contacts.

Is the decision to form a synapse signalled by calcium? In cultured frog spinal neurons, contact with a muscle cell raised free calcium in the neurite, but contact with another neuron did not; in a cultured snail motor neuron, a cAMP analogue mimicked the boost in calcium entry that contact with its target produced. In these experiments the trigger came from the partner, from its surface or from the space between the cells.

### Target-derived NGF and postsynaptic assembly in sympathetic ganglia

The signal for the receiving side can come from far away. In mouse sympathetic ganglia, Sharma, Deppmann, Harrington and colleagues reported in 2010 that nerve growth factor from the distant target tissue is needed for postsynaptic assembly in the ganglion neurons, and that postsynaptic assembly is in turn a prerequisite for presynaptic assembly, although preganglionic axons still grow into the ganglion without the growth factor. Signaling endosomes carrying its receptor TrkA travel back from the axon into the dendrites.

### Three readouts in the glial add-back experiments

In the 1997 cultures, at one stimulus per second, failures of evoked transmission fell from about one stimulus in five to about one in fifty. Under a simple Poisson model, in which a stimulus fails only when no vesicle is released, those failure rates correspond to an average of about 1.6 rising to about 3.9 vesicles per stimulus, about 2.4-fold. That is close to the 2.3-fold rise in synapse number in the same cultures and far from the twelvefold rise in minis. At faster stimulation the paper's binomial analysis, which assumed unchanged release probability, required about four to five times as many release sites (4.0 at 5 Hz, 4.8 at 10 Hz) against 2.3 times observed, which it read as more effective release.

### The reversal potential of the GABA current

GABA-A channels also pass bicarbonate, so the reversal potential of the GABA current sits a little above E(Cl). If bicarbonate is about a fifth to a third as permeant as chloride, with about 15 mM inside and 25 mM outside, the reversal potential is about −65 to −69 mV in the mature example, still below a −60 mV rest, so the conclusion holds: the channel hyperpolarizes. The lesson uses E(Cl) to keep the arithmetic to one ion.
