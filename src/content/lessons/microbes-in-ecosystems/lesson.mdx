## Explain: microbes that keep matter moving

<WordBank terms={['decomposer', 'nitrogen fixation', 'nodule', 'mutualism', 'microbiome', 'fermentation', 'pathogen', 'host', 'bacteriophage', 'biodiversity']} />

### Breaking down the dead

A **decomposer** is an organism that breaks down dead plants, animals and wastes and returns their nutrients to the soil or water. Bacteria and fungi do most of this work. In an Ohio stream study, both fungi and bacteria grew on fallen maple and oak leaves, and the fungi always outweighed the bacteria. These microbes release enzymes that break the large molecules in leaves into smaller ones. Without decomposers, dead matter would pile up and its nutrients would stay locked inside it.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/microbes-in-ecosystems/stream-leaf-decomposers.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: fallen maple and oak leaves on a stream bed, with a magnified view of a leaf surface showing branching fungal threads and clusters of bacteria, and small dots of nutrients leaving the leaf into the water." caption="In streams, fungi and bacteria grow on fallen leaves and break them down, returning nutrients to the water. Fungi usually do more of this work." />

### Feeding plants nitrogen

Living things use nitrogen to build proteins and DNA. The air is mostly nitrogen gas, but plants and animals cannot use it in that form. **Nitrogen fixation** is the change of nitrogen gas from the air into a form living things can use. Only some bacteria and archaea (another group of microbes) can do it.

In soybeans, peanuts and other legumes, these bacteria live inside a **nodule**, a small swelling on a root that holds nitrogen-fixing bacteria. The bacteria turn nitrogen gas into ammonium, which the plant uses. The plant sends the bacteria energy-rich food made by photosynthesis. A partnership in which both species benefit is called **mutualism**.

> nitrogen gas from the air → bacteria in root nodules → ammonium the plant can use → plant proteins and DNA

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/microbes-in-ecosystems/soybean-nodule-exchange.webp" width={1200} height={800} keyItems={["energy-rich food", "nitrogen gas", "nodule", "ammonium", "bacteria"]} given={[2]} alt="AI-generated illustration: a soybean plant with its roots in soil, small round nodules on the roots, air spaces in the soil holding nitrogen gas, an arrow from the soil air into a nodule and from the nodule into the root, a gold arrow running down the stem from the leaves, and a magnified cut nodule, pink inside and packed with tiny rod-shaped bacteria. Five numbered circles mark parts for the student to name." caption="A two-way trade in a soybean root: bacteria in the nodules turn nitrogen gas into a form the plant uses, and the plant sends food from its leaves down to them." />

Most of the fixed nitrogen ends up in the beans, which farmers harvest. When the rest of the plant dies, decomposers break down its roots, stems and leaves, and some fixed nitrogen returns to the soil. You can read more about the whole cycle in Matter Cycles.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** decomposers return nutrients from dead matter, and nitrogen-fixing bacteria turn nitrogen gas into a form that plants can use.</Support>

## Explain: partners in your body, and pathogens

### Your gut microbiome

A **microbiome** is the community of microbes that live in one place, such as soil, a stream or a body. Your gut microbiome lives in your intestines, and most of its bacteria live in the large intestine.

Your body cannot digest some parts of plant food, called fiber. Gut bacteria break fiber down by **fermentation**, a process that breaks down food molecules without oxygen. Fermentation of fiber makes small acids that your body can absorb and use as a source of energy. Diet matters to these partners. A study in mice, described in Going deeper, shows what a low-fiber diet can do.

### Foods and medicines

People have used bacteria for a very long time. Yogurt forms when two kinds of bacteria ferment the sugar in milk and release lactic acid, which thickens the milk and makes it sour. Bacteria can also be used to make human medicines: E. coli carrying lab-made genes for human insulin made parts of human insulin. More on this comes in [Engineering Genes](/lessons/genetic-engineering/).

### Pathogens

A **pathogen** is a microbe or virus that causes disease in its **host**, the living thing it lives in or on. Pathogens attack all kinds of hosts:

| Host | Pathogen | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Grapevine | the bacterium Xylella fastidiosa | causes Pierce’s disease, which blocks the vine’s water-carrying tubes |
| Cattle | the bacterium Mycobacterium bovis | causes bovine tuberculosis |
| Humans | the bacterium Helicobacter pylori | causes inflammation of the stomach lining |
| Birds and humans | West Nile virus, spread by mosquitoes | causes illness, sometimes severe |

How we prevent and treat these diseases is the subject of [Germs, Vaccines and Antibiotics](/lessons/germs-vaccines-antibiotics/).

The same kind of bacterium can be a partner in one place and a problem in another. Most strains of E. coli live quietly in the large intestine, but even these can cause a bladder infection if they reach the urinary tract. Other E. coli strains carry extra genes that make them harmful, and they cause illness when people swallow them in food or water.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** gut bacteria, food bacteria and engineered bacteria help people, while pathogens cause disease in plants, animals and humans.</Support>

## Explain: viruses and diseases reshape ecosystems

A **bacteriophage** (phage for short) is a virus that infects bacteria. Ocean water holds enormous numbers of phages and other viruses. When a virus bursts its host cell, the cell’s contents spill into the water. Researchers estimate that in surface waters viruses kill a large share of the ocean's bacteria and archaea every day, though the estimates are rough and vary from place to place.

What happens to those spilled contents? In a lab model ecosystem, a virus infected an ocean alga called Phaeocystis. Bacteria then broke down the dead algae. The water gained nitrogen and phosphorus that other living things could use.

Viruses can also affect **biodiversity**, the variety of living things in a place. A virus usually infects only certain kinds of bacteria. When one kind becomes very common, its viruses can spread faster and cut it back, which may leave room for other kinds. A 2019 experiment on coastal water found that when viruses switched from lying hidden inside bacteria to bursting them, the bacterial community became more diverse.

On land, disease can reshape whole landscapes. In the Serengeti in East Africa, the virus disease rinderpest was wiped out in the 1960s after the cattle that carried it were vaccinated. A study of decades of records found that wildebeest numbers then rose, they ate more grass, fewer fires burned, and more trees grew.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** viruses and other pathogens change which populations grow, how nutrients recycle and how many kinds of organisms share a place.</Support>

### Design problem

Plan an investigation to test whether adding nodule bacteria helps soybeans grow in nitrogen-poor soil. Name your variables, your control, what you keep the same, how many plants you use, and how you keep it safe.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Question: does adding nodule bacteria increase soybean growth in soil low in nitrogen?
- Independent variable: nodule bacteria added (a garden-store legume inoculant made for soybeans) or not added.
- Dependent variables: dry plant mass after six weeks, the number of nodules per plant, and how many split nodules are pink inside.
- Control: pots of the same sterile, nitrogen-poor potting mix with no inoculant. Sterile mix matters, because ordinary soil may already hold nodule bacteria.
- Constants: the same seed batch, pot size, amount of soil, water, light and temperature. Add no nitrogen fertilizer to either group.
- Sample size and trials: at least 10 pots per group, with pots mixed together on the shelf so neither group gets the better light. Find the mean for each group. Repeat the whole test if you can.
- Safety: wear gloves when handling the inoculant and soil, follow the product label, and wash your hands afterwards.
- What results would mean: if the inoculated plants have more nodules and a higher mean mass, that supports a benefit from the bacteria in these conditions. If both groups match, the bacteria may not have formed nodules, so check the roots.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How many microbes live in a body?

In 2016 Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo estimated that a reference adult man weighing 70 kg carries about 38 trillion bacterial cells and about 30 trillion human cells, the same estimate used in the opening myth check.

### Fiber and the gut lining

In a 2016 study, mice carrying a set of human gut bacteria and fed a low-fiber diet had gut bacteria that began feeding on the protective mucus that lines the gut.

### Numbers from the algae experiment

In the lab model ecosystem of Jakob Haaber and Mathias Middelboe, a virus infected the ocean alga Phaeocystis. After bacteria broke down the dead algae, the water gained about 148 micrograms of nitrogen and 7 micrograms of phosphorus per liter that other living things could use.

### The first insulin from bacteria

In 1979, researchers reported that E. coli carrying lab-made genes for human insulin made the two parts of insulin, showing that bacteria can be used to make human medicines.
