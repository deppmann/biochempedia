**After a receptor lets go, what decides how long an already-loaded G protein keeps signaling?**

The first film built the cycle; the text times it and tests it against experiments.

> **The one idea. Faster GTP cutting shortens an already-loaded GTPase's time on.**
>
> *Scope:* one GTPase molecule after GTP loading, when first-order hydrolysis ends its active state. Receptor shutoff, repeated loading, effector kinetics and messenger removal can also shape a whole-cell response.

## From switch cycle to timer

A **GTPase** cuts the GTP it binds: on with GTP, off with GDP, which is GTP minus its third phosphate.

A **guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF)** promotes release of bound GDP. GTP is far more plentiful than GDP in the cell, so GTP usually takes the empty place. For a heterotrimeric G protein under a receptor, the occupied receptor is the GEF. GTP binding rearranges the complex and can separate alpha from beta-gamma. Alpha regulates a target such as adenylyl cyclase; beta-gamma can also signal, for example by opening potassium channels in the heart. After alpha cuts GTP, it rejoins beta-gamma.

Intrinsic cutting is slow; a **GTPase-activating protein (GAP)** binds the GTPase and accelerates it. The G protein reloads slowly by itself, but one occupied receptor can load many freely moving G proteins in succession, and each can switch on a cyclase of its own.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/gtpase-switches/gtpase-cycle-gef-gap.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a G protein drawn as a slate block holding GDP, loaded by an occupied receptor acting as GEF into a gold block holding GTP that switches on adenylyl cyclase, then slowly cutting GTP back to GDP, with a GAP speeding the return; a small inset compares GDP's two phosphate tiles with GTP's three." caption="A GEF promotes GDP release and GTP loading, while a GAP speeds GTP cutting and shortens the loaded switch's time on." />

For first-order cutting:

> **mean time on = 1 / k(cut)**

The hormone and the receptor start the clock, but they do not appear in the equation; it describes one loaded switch. If k(cut) is 0.05 per second, a round number chosen for illustration, the mean time on is 20 seconds. Speeding the step 40-fold shortens that mean to half a second.

## Two implementations of the cycle

Two main kinds of protein run this switch: heterotrimeric G proteins and small GTPases such as Ras.

| | Trimeric G proteins | Small GTPases such as Ras |
|---|---|---|
| Build | Alpha, beta and gamma subunits; alpha holds the nucleotide | One small protein, about 20 to 25 kilodaltons |
| Loads GTP (GEF) | The occupied receptor itself | For Ras, SOS; each family has its own GEFs |
| Speeds GTP cutting (GAP) | RGS proteins for the Gi and Gq families, but not Gs; some effectors, such as phospholipase C-beta | For Ras, neurofibromin and p120 GAP; each family has its own GAPs |
| Examples | Gs, Gi, Gq | Ras, Rho, Rab, Arf, Ran |

Different alpha subunits point at different targets. Gs switches adenylyl cyclase on, Gi turns it down, and Gq switches on phospholipase C-beta. For Gi- and Gq-family subunits, but not Gs, major GAPs include **RGS proteins**, regulators of G-protein signaling. Some effectors are GAPs too: phospholipase C-beta speeds GTP cutting by the Gq that activated it. RGS4 and its relative GAIP make a Gi-family G protein cut at least 40 times faster in vitro. GAIP binds the GTP-holding form, acting only while the switch is on, so more of these RGS proteins can shorten a Gi-coupled response.

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

</details>

Ras is a single small chain with no receptor attached. SOS, short for son of sevenless, is its exchange factor, and growth-factor receptors recruit it to the membrane. Ras cuts its GTP slowly. In 1987 Frank McCormick and Meg Trahey found the first GAP, a factor that sped normal Ras's GTP cutting more than 200-fold in vitro and did nothing to the oncogenic mutant. That was a crude extract; purified GAP was later found to speed the step about 100,000-fold. The structure of Ras itself, holding a GTP analogue it could not cut, showed a fold that matches EF-Tu, a bacterial protein that delivers tRNAs to the ribosome. Ras and EF-Tu inherited the same ancient fold from a common ancestor.

Ras is one of five small-GTPase families, each with its own GEFs and GAPs:

- **Ras:** cell growth and proliferation.
- **Rho:** the actin cytoskeleton and cell shape.
- **Rab:** vesicle traffic between membranes.
- **Arf:** the budding of vesicles, also membrane traffic.
- **Ran:** movement of proteins into and out of the nucleus.

The shared cycle now lets us classify failures by the step they change.

## Breaking the timer

The second film gave the direction of each break; here are the experiments and molecular detail behind it.

**An uncuttable GTP.** In Gpp(NH)p the oxygen bridging the last two phosphates is replaced by NH, so the G protein can bind it and cannot cut it. In 1975 Schramm and Rodbell treated frog red blood cell membranes with isoproterenol, a drug that activates the adrenaline receptor, together with Gpp(NH)p. Adenylyl cyclase stayed on through extensive washing, sonication and detergent, and propranolol, which blocks the receptor, could no longer reverse it. With isoproterenol alone, diluting the drug dropped activity to nearly nothing.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The blocker should still turn off a receptor-dependent response. It did not, which places the persistent state downstream of the receptor, in the G protein holding the uncuttable nucleotide.

</details>

**Cholera toxin.** In 1976 and 1977 Joel Moss and Martha Vaughan showed that the toxin is an enzyme. It splits NAD and can attach its ADP-ribose group to the amino acid arginine. In 1978 D. Michael Gill and R. Meren, and in a separate study Cassel and Pfeuffer, showed that the target is the cyclase system's GTP-binding protein, now called Gs. The attached group blocks the step that switches Gs off, so cyclic AMP stays high. An intestinal cell responds by opening CFTR chloride channels; salt leaves into the gut, water follows, and the result is the watery diarrhea of cholera.

**Ras G12V and Q61.** A Ras GAP helps form the transition-state arrangement for hydrolysis by placing an arginine in the nucleotide pocket. Glycine 12 sits beside that site. Replacing it with valine puts a bulkier side chain where the GAP's arginine must reach and disrupts the catalytic arrangement, so GAP can bind without giving its normal acceleration. Glutamine 61, changed to lysine in the tumor mutant Q61K, helps stabilize the cutting reaction's transition state. Q61 mutants cut poorly with or without GAP. Either way Ras stays GTP-bound and keeps signaling. A single amino-acid change at codon 12 occurs in many cancers.

**The mirror image: S17N.** Asparagine in place of serine 17 lowers Ras's affinity for GTP much more than for GDP. So Ras is rarely loaded with GTP, and even holding GTP it fails to switch on its targets. It is also thought to hold on to exchange factors such as SOS, soaking up what normal Ras in the same cell needs. A mutant that blocks its normal partner this way is called dominant-negative.

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

</details>

## Going deeper

### How the GTP requirement and the transducer were found

Around 1970, Martin Rodbell's laboratory at the National Institutes of Health studied how glucagon switches on adenylyl cyclase in liver membranes. Adenylyl cyclase makes cyclic AMP, a messenger that carries the hormone signal inside. In plain buffer, glucagon stuck to the membranes and would not wash off; with ATP present it came off within seconds. The cause was GTP contaminating the ATP. Added deliberately, GTP proved necessary: without it, glucagon barely activated the cyclase.

GTP was not the fuel, because the cyclase runs on ATP, and a trace was enough, so it was not used up once per cyclic AMP made. It was acting as a regulator, not a raw material.

For years many laboratories had trouble reproducing the effect, partly because their membranes and nucleotides were not as clean. If ATP is 1% GTP, the “no GTP” tube can already hold enough for a full response, and adding more GTP only scatters the data.

Rodbell had a second problem. Many different hormone receptors switch on the same enzyme, and he thought it impossible that all were built into it. So he proposed a separate component, a transducer, passing the signal between them, before anyone knew what it was.

Here is a UVa moment: the answer came from Alfred Gilman's pharmacology laboratory at the University of Virginia in the 1970s. Gilman used cyc⁻ lymphoma cells, in which hormone cannot raise cyclic AMP because the link between receptor and enzyme is missing. He and Elliott Ross added a soluble extract from normal cells to cyc⁻ membranes, and the response returned even when the extract's own cyclase had been inactivated. The transducer was therefore a separate, movable protein. Using that rescue as an assay, Gilman and colleagues purified Gs from rabbit liver membranes. The protein carried alpha and beta subunits; a small gamma subunit was missed at first. G protein means guanine-nucleotide-binding protein.

### The first Ras structures

In 1988 Sung-Hou Kim's group in Berkeley solved the first structure of Ras, holding GDP. In 1989 a Heidelberg team including Emil Pai, Wolfgang Kabsch, Kenneth Holmes and Alfred Wittinghofer solved Ras holding a GTP analogue it could not cut, and its fold matched EF-Tu, a bacterial protein that delivers tRNAs to the ribosome.

### The S17N affinity figures

In the 1988 study that introduced it, asparagine in place of serine 17 lowered Ras's affinity for GTP 20- to 40-fold without much change for GDP; later work found GDP binding is weakened too, but far less than GTP binding.
