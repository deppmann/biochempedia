      microscope enlarges it. Thin slices show the walls and spaces of separate cells instead of a

  - name: "Staining"
    blurb: >-
      Many cells are almost clear, so a dye is added that sticks to some parts more than others.

      in an unstained sample.
  - name: "Transmission electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      A beam of electrons passes through a very thin, specially prepared sample, and the electrons
      that get through form the image. Because electrons have much shorter wavelengths than visible
      light, this microscope can separate details about a hundred times finer than a light
      microscope can.

## Explain: the six ideas of the cell theory

<WordBank terms={['cell', 'cell theory', 'metabolism', 'DNA', 'magnification', 'resolution', 'stain', 'electron microscope', 'hypothesis', 'theory', 'law']} />

A **cell** is the smallest unit that can carry out all the processes of life. The **cell theory** is the scientific explanation of how cells relate to living things. Virginia lists six ideas:

1. All living organisms are composed of cells.
2. Cells are the basic unit of life.
3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells.
4. Metabolism occurs within cells.
5. Hereditary information (DNA) is passed from cell to cell.
6. All cells have the same basic composition.

**Metabolism** is all the chemical reactions that happen in a living thing, such as breaking down sugar to release energy for the cell to use. Enzymes speed these reactions inside cells ([that lesson](/lessons/enzymes-at-work/)). **DNA** is the molecule that carries hereditary information. When a cell divides, each new cell gets a copy ([that lesson](/lessons/cell-cycle-and-mitosis/)).

"Same basic composition" means that cells are built from the same main kinds of molecules: proteins, lipids, carbohydrates and nucleic acids ([that lesson](/lessons/macromolecules/)). Typical cells have a membrane around them, a watery inside and DNA. The details still differ between kinds of cells.

The same basic plan does not mean the same size or shape. Many bacteria are about 1 to 2 micrometers long. A human cheek cell is about 50 to 80 micrometers across. The yolk of an unfertilized chicken egg is one cell. A micrometer (µm) is one thousandth of a millimeter.

Viruses are not made of cells ([that lesson](/lessons/living-or-not/)).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Every living thing is made of one or more cells, and every new cell comes from a cell that already existed.</Support>

## Explain: seeing smaller things

**Magnification** is how many times larger an image looks than the real object. **Resolution** is the smallest distance between two points at which you can still see them as two separate points. Magnification makes an image bigger. Resolution decides how much detail it holds.

Think of stretching a blurry phone photo until it fills the screen: it gets bigger but stays blurry. The comparison is not perfect. A photo can blur for many reasons, such as poor focus, motion or too few pixels. Even a perfect light microscope blurs the finest details, because light spreads out as it passes through the lens.

Each tool has its own limit:

| Tool | Smallest detail it can separate (about) | Can it show… |
|---|---|---|
| Unaided eye | about 0.1 mm (100 µm) | an African clawed frog egg, about 1 mm across |
| Ordinary light microscope | 0.2 µm | a cheek cell or a bacterium |
| Electron microscope, biological samples | 0.001 to 0.002 µm (1 to 2 nm) | a virus or a ribosome |

The light microscope's limit and the electron microscope's range are typical values for biological samples, and real values vary with the instrument and the sample. An **electron microscope** forms its image with a beam of electrons instead of light. Electrons have much shorter wavelengths than visible light, so they can separate much finer details. Ribosomes, the cell's protein builders, are about 0.02 to 0.03 µm across. That is far below the light microscope's limit, which is why they were seen clearly only with electron microscopes.

Resolution is not the only problem. Many cells are almost clear, so their inside parts are hard to see. A **stain** is a dye that sticks to some cell parts more than others. Stains added contrast, which made parts inside clear animal cells, such as the nucleus, much easier to see.

## Explain: a theory that grew and changed

Scientists use three words that people often mix up:

| Word | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothesis | A testable statement about nature | "If cork is made of cells, thin slices of other plants will show cells too." |
| Theory | A well-tested explanation of some part of nature, supported by many lines of evidence | The cell theory |
| Law | A description of how something behaves under stated conditions | The law of conservation of mass |

A **hypothesis** is a testable statement. A **theory** is a well-tested explanation that ties many observations together. A **law** describes a pattern under stated conditions without explaining why. These are different jobs, so a theory never turns into a law, however much evidence it gets.

The cell theory shows how a theory changes. In 1838 Matthias Schleiden described plant tissues as made of cells. In 1839 Theodor Schwann compared plant and animal tissues, such as tadpole cartilage, and found the same cellular plan. Schleiden and Schwann, though, both thought new cells could form inside a structureless fluid, a little like crystals growing in a solution. Schleiden proposed this for plants, and Schwann extended it to animals.

That part was wrong. In 1852 Robert Remak published evidence that new cells form when existing cells divide. In 1855 Rudolf Virchow spread the idea widely in a short article: every cell comes from a cell. The theory kept its core and dropped the wrong piece. Schwann already treated cells as the places where nutrition and growth happen. The idea that DNA carries hereditary information came much later.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** The cell theory is a well-tested explanation, and scientists corrected it when new evidence showed that new cells come only from cells that already exist.</Support>

### Design problem

Hooke cut his cork slice exceedingly thin. Does the thickness of a slice change how many separate cells you can count in one view of a light microscope? Plan a fair test using an onion or a potato, a light microscope and slides.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Hypothesis: If the slice is thinner, then more separate cells will be countable in one view, because fewer layers of cells overlap.
- Independent variable: slice thickness, for example very thin, medium and thick.
- Dependent variable: the number of cells with walls you can trace all the way around in one field of view.
- Constants: the same onion or potato, microscope, magnification, light setting and stain.
- Comparison: the thickest slice serves as the baseline.
- Repeated trials: make five slides at each thickness, count three views on each, and calculate the mean for each thickness.
- Safety: cutting thin slices needs a sharp blade. Use a guarded blade or a peeler, cut away from your hand, and work only with an adult's permission.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

Hooke saw the walls of dead cork cells, one material from one plant. Building the cell theory took better light microscopes, many comparisons of plants and animals and a corrected idea about how new cells form. Later tools, such as stains, which added contrast, and electron microscopes, which could resolve smaller parts, showed much more of what is inside cells. A theory built that way is a well-tested explanation, not a guess.

**Science practices you used:** making a prediction before testing, reading a data table and comparing values, telling a hypothesis from a theory and a law, using a model of resolution and naming its limits, and planning a fair test with repeated trials and safety steps.

## Going deeper

### Archaeal membranes

Archaea build their membranes from lipids that differ from those of bacteria, plants and animals in both their chains and the chemical links that join them. This is one of the main features that sets archaea apart.

### The Leeuwenhoek microscopes

Two of the few surviving microscopes made by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, both made before 1723, have been studied closely. One, labelled V7017, magnified 118 times, and no resolution was reported for it. It was a single lens, ground and polished. The other, UM-1, magnified 266 times and separated details 1.35 µm apart, and its lens was a single bead shape.

### Where the table's numbers come from

The two Leeuwenhoek microscopes, two of the handful that survive, are in museums in Leiden and Utrecht. A 2021 study (Cocquyt and colleagues, Science Advances) scanned their lenses and reported their magnifications; the 1.35 µm value is an earlier measurement that the study cites. The 1933 magnification and the 1937 diatom image come from Ernst Ruska's 1986 Nobel Lecture. Dates for Schleiden, Schwann, Remak and Virchow come from the OpenStax Microbiology textbook (2016).
