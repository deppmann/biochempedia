## Explain: what makes a population grow or shrink

<WordBank terms={['population', 'limiting factor', 'biotic factor', 'abiotic factor', 'competition', 'overharvesting', 'sustainable', 'biodiversity']} />

A **population** is all the members of one species that live in the same place. The blue crabs in the Bay are one population.

A population changes in a simple way. Births and crabs moving in add to it. Deaths and crabs moving out take away from it. When more are added than lost, the population grows.

A **limiting factor** is anything that keeps a population from growing larger. In [an earlier lesson](/lessons/competition-and-cooperation/) you met food, water, shelter, disease and predators as limiting factors. When one of them changes, the population can change too.

- A **biotic factor** is a living part of the environment, such as predators, food or grass.
- An **abiotic factor** is a nonliving part of the environment, such as temperature or salt in the water.

Here are some factors that can change the number of blue crabs.

| Factor | Living or nonliving | How it can change crab numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Very cold winter | Nonliving | More crabs die before spring. |
| Low salt in cold water | Nonliving | In lab tests, fewer crabs lived through a winter in cold, low-salt water. |
| Big crabs | Living | Adult crabs eat many young crabs. |
| Shallow water and grass | Living and nonliving | Young crabs can hide there from big crabs. |
| Harvest by people | Human action | Crabbers remove crabs every year. |

When there is less of a needed thing, more individuals need the same food or hiding place. They struggle to get enough. That struggle is **competition**. Think of musical chairs. Take away chairs, and more players are left standing. The game breaks down in one way: real crabs do not all lose at once. Some find shelter, and some are eaten or starve.

Changes can also move where a population lives. Lab tests suggest that cold limits how far north blue crabs can survive. So warmer winters could let crabs live farther north, though lab tests alone cannot show that the move has happened.

## Explain: natural causes and human causes

Some changes are natural.

- A very cold winter can kill many crabs buried in the Bay.
- A flood can drop a population below its earlier size.
- A wildfire can kill grown trees. In some pine forests, the heat also opens cones and drops seeds, so new trees start.
- A climate shift over many years can change where a species can live.

People cause changes too.

- **Pollution.** In one study, untreated rain runoff from a busy Seattle road killed all of the adult coho salmon placed in it, but the same runoff after it flowed through soil did not.
- **Habitat destruction.** Stone walls and other hard shorelines can shrink the shallow water where young crabs hide.
- **Overharvesting.** **Overharvesting** means people catch or collect a species faster than it can replace itself. Managers set catch limits on blue crabs and on menhaden, a small fish, to guard against this.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/populations-rise-and-fall/shallow-shore-versus-stone-wall.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two side views of a Bay shore. On the left, land slopes gently into the water, leaving a wide band of shallow water with grass where small crabs hide. On the right, a stone wall drops straight into deep water, leaving only a thin strip of shallow water." caption="Stone walls and other hard shorelines can shrink the shallow water where young crabs hide." />

When a change is large, an ecosystem can lose species. **Biodiversity** is the variety of living things in an area. When one population crashes, the animals that eat it and the things it eats can change too. Larger changes, such as dead zones and climate change, are in [Big Changes](/lessons/eutrophication-and-big-changes/).

## Explain: limits that help a population recover

A population that drops does not always come back. In some places after very hot forest fires, few trees were left nearby to drop seeds, and the forest may not return.

In 2008, Virginia reported that too many blue crabs were being caught each year. Virginia and Maryland set a plan to catch about 34 percent fewer female crabs than in 2004 to 2007. Females carry the eggs, so leaving more of them can mean more young crabs later.

This is **sustainable** harvest: taking only as many as the population can replace, so it lasts for years to come.

Recovery is not a sure thing. How many young crabs survive changes from year to year, and big crabs also eat many young ones. Managers also keep counting, because a limit is not a promise. The 2008 report warned that rebuilding could take several years. In the survey, adult females rose after the plan began, then fell back by winter 2011–2012.

### Design problem

Soapy water can run off driveways into streams. Plan a fair test of whether soapy water changes how a population of duckweed grows. Duckweed is a tiny floating plant made of flat, leaf-like parts called fronds. It makes new fronds by budding, and each one can become a new plant.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** How does the amount of dish soap in water affect the growth of a duckweed population?
- **Independent variable:** soap mixed into the pond water before filling the cups, for example 0, 0.5, 1 and 2 milliliters per liter.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of duckweed fronds, counted every two days for two weeks.
- **Control:** cups with no soap.
- **Constants:** the same pond water, cup size, water volume (200 milliliters), light, temperature and starting number of fronds (10).
- **Repeated trials:** four cups for each soap level.
- **Safety:** wear goggles when you measure soap, wash your hands after handling pond water, and do not dump the plants or soapy water outside.
- **What results would mean:** if cups with more soap end with fewer fronds than the control, soap acted as a limiting factor in this test.

</details>

Recent winter surveys sample about 1,500 sites, and the early surveys sampled fewer, but never just one station.
