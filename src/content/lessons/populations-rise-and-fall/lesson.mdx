## Explain: what makes a population grow or shrink

<WordBank terms={['population', 'limiting factor', 'biotic factor', 'abiotic factor', 'competition', 'overharvesting', 'sustainable', 'biodiversity']} />

The first film built a four-arrow balance for a **population**, all the members of one species living in the same place. “Arrival” and “departure” mean moving into or out of the place being studied.

<Support>**One idea:** Populations rise when additions exceed losses, and fall when losses exceed additions. Here, additions mean births and arrivals; losses mean deaths and departures. **Scope:** the rule tells how the number changes over a chosen time, not which factor caused the change.</Support>

**Predict.** Suppose births and deaths balance, but more crabs arrive than leave. Does the population rise, fall or stay the same?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It rises. Births and deaths cancel, while net arrivals add crabs.

</details>

For the survey, the estimate rose by 485 million crabs, to about 2.32 times its size.

A **limiting factor** is anything that keeps a population from growing larger. A limiting factor changes at least one addition or loss. In [an earlier lesson](/lessons/competition-and-cooperation/) you met food, water, shelter, disease and predators as limiting factors.

- A **biotic factor** is a living part of the environment, such as predators, food or grass.
- An **abiotic factor** is a nonliving part of the environment, such as temperature or salt in the water.

| Factor | Kind | What changes first? |
|---|---|---|
| Very cold winter | Abiotic | More crabs die before spring, increasing losses. |
| Low salt in cold water | Abiotic | In lab tests, fewer crabs survived, increasing losses. |
| Big crabs | Biotic | In field studies, adult crabs ate many young crabs, increasing losses of young crabs. |
| Shallow water and grass | Abiotic habitat and biotic shelter | Hiding places can lower losses by protecting young crabs from big crabs. |
| Harvest by people | Human action | Crabbers remove crabs, increasing losses caused by people. |

**Competition** is the struggle among living things that need the same limited resource, such as food or a hiding place. With a smaller patch of grass, some young crabs find cover, and others are left exposed and may be eaten.

Changes can also move where a population lives. Lab tests suggest that cold limits how far north blue crabs can survive.

**Predict.** If northern winters stay milder, what could happen to the northern edge of the crab's range? What could lab tests alone not show?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Warmer winters could let crabs live farther north. But lab tests alone cannot show that the move has happened; that takes field records of crabs found farther north over many years.

</details>

## Explain: natural causes and human causes

The second film sorted each factor two ways: living or nonliving, and natural or human-caused. Here are cases it left out.

Some changes are natural.

- A flood can drop a population below its earlier size.
- A wildfire can kill grown trees. In some pine forests, the heat also opens cones and drops seeds, so new trees start. After very hot fires elsewhere, few trees were left nearby to drop seeds, and the forest may not return.
- A climate shift over many years can change where a species can live.

**Predict.** A flood pushes crabs out of a cove, but they survive. Which loss changed?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Departures increased. Deaths did not, because the crabs survived.

</details>

People cause changes too.

- **Pollution.** In one study, untreated rain runoff from a busy road killed the adult coho salmon placed in it. After the runoff flowed through soil, it did not.
- **Habitat destruction**, such as the second film's stone wall (see the figure below).
- **Overharvesting** means people catch or collect a species faster than it can replace itself. Managers set catch limits on blue crabs and on menhaden, a small fish, to guard against this.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/populations-rise-and-fall/shallow-shore-versus-stone-wall.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two side views of a Bay shore. On the left, land slopes gently into the water, leaving a wide band of shallow water with grass where small crabs hide. On the right, a stone wall drops straight into deep water, leaving only a thin strip of shallow water." caption="Stone walls and other hard shorelines can shrink the shallow water where young crabs hide." />

When a change is large, an ecosystem can lose species. **Biodiversity** is the variety of living things in an area. When one population crashes, the animals that eat it and the things it eats can change too. Larger changes, such as dead zones and climate change, are in [Big Changes](/lessons/eutrophication-and-big-changes/).

## Explain: limits that help a population recover

In 2008, Virginia reported that too many blue crabs were being caught each year. Virginia and Maryland set a plan to catch about 34 percent fewer female crabs than in 2004 to 2007. Females carry the eggs, so leaving more of them can mean more young crabs later.

This is **sustainable** harvest: taking only as many as the population can replace, so it lasts. A limit is not a promise, so managers keep counting. The 2008 report warned that rebuilding could take several years. In the survey, adult females rose after the plan began, then fell back by winter 2011–2012.

These are Bay-wide estimates from the Winter Dredge Survey of the Maryland Department of Natural Resources and the Virginia Institute of Marine Science, published by Maryland DNR (opened October 2026). Teams sample many places in the Bay, never just one station. The total also counts adult males. Young crabs are ones that joined the population in the summer and fall before the survey. New, tighter limits on female harvest began in spring 2008, after that winter's survey. Maryland reported that about 12 percent of adult females died during the cold winter of 2025–2026.

From 1991–1992 to 1992–1993, most of the jump was young crabs, while adult females barely changed. Young crabs make a round trip. Females carry their eggs toward the mouth of the Bay, where the larvae hatch. The larvae drift out to sea, and young crabs later move back in. For the whole Bay, that path can include a birth, a departure and a later arrival. So the survey's young-crab number is not a direct count of births. It also depends on how many larvae survive at sea and make it back to the Bay.

From 2007–2008 to 2011–2012, the total rose by 472 million, but adult females rose by only 4 million. Most of the later total was young crabs, so the table does not show that the female-harvest limits alone caused the rise.

### Design problem

Soapy water can run off driveways into streams. Plan a fair test of whether soapy water changes how a population of duckweed grows. Duckweed is a tiny floating plant made of flat, leaf-like parts called fronds. It makes new fronds by budding, and each one can become a new plant.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** How does the amount of dish soap in water affect the growth of a duckweed population?
- **Independent variable:** soap mixed into the pond water before filling the cups, for example 0, 0.5, 1 and 2 milliliters per liter.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of duckweed fronds, counted every two days for two weeks.
- **Control:** cups with no soap.
- **Constants:** the same pond water, cup size, water volume (200 milliliters), light, temperature and starting number of fronds (10).
- **Repeated trials:** four cups for each soap level.
- **Safety:** wear goggles when you measure soap, wash your hands after handling pond water, and do not dump the plants or soapy water outside.
- **What results would mean:** if cups with more soap end with fewer fronds than the control, soap acted as a limiting factor in this test.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The coho salmon runoff study

In the study, all of the adult coho salmon placed in untreated rain runoff from a busy Seattle road died within 24 hours. All 20 coho in the same runoff after it flowed through soil survived.

### How many places the winter survey samples

Recent winter surveys sample about 1,500 sites; the early surveys sampled fewer.

### The female counts after 2008

In the survey, adult females rose from 91 million in winter 2007–2008 to 246 million in winter 2009–2010, then fell back to 95 million by winter 2011–2012.
