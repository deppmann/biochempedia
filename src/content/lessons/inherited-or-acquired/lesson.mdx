## Explain: what can be passed on

<WordBank terms={['variation', 'inherited trait', 'acquired trait', 'allele', 'genotype', 'phenotype', 'dominant', 'recessive', 'environment']} />

Scientists describe living things by features like color, shape, body covering and height. Look closely at any group of the same kind, and each one is a little different. **Variation** is the small differences among living things of the same kind.

An **inherited trait** is a trait passed from parents to offspring through genes. Eye color and the shape of a pea seed are inherited traits. An **acquired trait** is a characteristic a living thing gets during its life. A scar, dyed hair, a skill like playing piano, and muscles built by exercise are acquired traits. The language you speak is acquired too.

Why? A new animal grows from sex cells, an egg and a sperm, which carry the parent’s genes. A scar changes skin cells, and lifting weights changes muscle cells. Neither changes the genes in your sex cells, so neither reaches your children. Changes in the DNA of body cells are not passed on either, but changes in sex cells can be, as [that lesson](/lessons/mutations-and-variation/) explains.

An old idea, often linked to the naturalist Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, said giraffes stretched their necks and passed longer necks to their young. Experiments do not support it.

## Explain: dominant does not mean common

You get one **allele**, or version of a gene, from each parent (review it in [that lesson](/lessons/predicting-traits/)). Your **genotype** is the pair of alleles you have. Your **phenotype** is the trait that shows, such as purple or white flowers.

A **dominant** allele shows its trait even when you have only one copy. A **recessive** allele shows its trait only when both of your copies are recessive. When a dominant allele is there, it masks the recessive one. These rules, first worked out with peas by Gregor Mendel, explain how many inherited traits are passed on. Later courses add more patterns.

Dominant describes what happens inside one living thing. It does not tell you how many living things carry that allele.

| Word | What it tells you | What it does not tell you |
|---|---|---|
| Dominant | One copy is enough for the trait to show | How many people have the trait |
| Recessive | Two copies are needed for the trait to show | Whether the trait is rare |

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Dominant tells you how an allele shows its trait, not how common that trait is in a group.</Support>

## Explain: genes and environment work together

The **environment** is everything around a living thing that can affect it, such as soil, light, water, food and temperature.

Cuttings from one plant are clones. They have the same genes as the parent plant. In many bigleaf hydrangeas, acidic soil lets the roots take up more aluminum, and the aluminum helps make the flowers blue. In less acidic soil, the flowers are pink. Gardeners measure this with soil pH, a number where lower means more acidic.

| Soil pH (Virginia extension guide) | Flower color in color-changing hydrangeas |
|---|---|
| About 5.0 to 5.5 | Blue |
| 5.6 to 5.9 | In between |
| Above about 6.0 to 6.5 | Pink |

Some kinds of bigleaf hydrangea stay pink or white in any soil. The genes set which colors are possible, and the soil helps decide which one appears.

### Design problem

Plan an investigation to test whether light changes a trait in plants with the same genes.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Question:** Do cuttings from one plant grow to the same height in bright light and in shade?

- **Plants:** 10 stem cuttings from one coleus plant, so all have the same genes.
- **Independent variable:** light. Put 5 pots by a bright window and 5 pots in a shady corner of the same room.
- **Dependent variables:** height in centimetres and number of leaves, measured each week for 4 weeks.
- **Constants:** the same pot size, the same soil, the same 50 millilitres of water twice a week, and the same room temperature.
- **Control:** the bright-window group, which gets normal growing light.
- **Repeated trials:** 5 cuttings in each group; find the mean height of each group.
- **Safety:** wash your hands after handling soil. Never put plants in your mouth; some houseplants are harmful if eaten.

If the groups differ, the environment changed the phenotype, since the genes were the same.

</details>
