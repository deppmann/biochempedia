> **The one idea.** Scientists sort every species into eight main levels, nested like boxes, from domain, the most general, to species, the most specific. Its two-part scientific name is the genus name plus a second word, and the two words together label its species box. Scientists everywhere use the same name. The more boxes two living things share, the more closely they are grouped. A **dichotomous key** is a list of two-choice questions that leads to a name. It finds the name, but the order of its questions does not show how living things are grouped.
>
> *Scope:* a full classification can add extra levels, such as subspecies, to these eight main ones. The boxes here come from one government database on one day, and scientists can redraw a box when they learn more.

## Explain: one name for each species

<WordBank terms={['species', 'binomial nomenclature', 'genus', 'classification', 'taxonomy', 'domain', 'kingdom', 'phylum', 'dichotomous key']} />

The film's test for a **species**, similar organisms that can mate in nature and have young that can also reproduce, is a useful rule for many organisms that reproduce with sex cells, but it has two limits. It cannot be used for organisms that reproduce without sex, such as many bacteria. And some different species do sometimes mate in nature.

The film's way of naming, one two-part name for each species, is called **binomial nomenclature**. This way of naming spread after the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus used two-part names in a book about plants in 1753. The first word is the **genus**, a small group of closely related species, so one genus can hold several species. The second word, joined to the genus name, names one species in that genus. The black vulture's full name is *Coragyps atratus*.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Each species has a two-part scientific name, the genus name then a second word, that scientists use in every country.</Support>

## Explain: eight boxes, from general to specific

**Classification** is a system for organizing and identifying organisms by the traits they share and how they are related. The science of naming and classifying living things is **taxonomy**. The film showed one **domain**, Eukarya; the other two domains are Archaea and Bacteria, whose cells have no nucleus. A **phylum** is a large group inside a kingdom, such as the arthropods, animals with a hard outer skeleton and jointed legs.

Classification works a bit like a mailing address: country, state, city and street each narrow the search. But the comparison has a limit. Houses on one street are not related to each other, while species in one genus are.

### Design problem

Does the order of questions in a key change how well people use it? Plan a test with picture cards.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does the order of questions change how many organisms people name correctly?
- **Independent variable:** the order of the questions (key 1 or key 2, same questions).
- **Dependent variables:** the number of correct names out of 10 cards, and the time in seconds to finish.
- **Constants:** the same 10 cards, the same time limit, and no help from others.
- **Control:** key 1, the order you started with.
- **Trials:** at least 10 classmates per key. Report the mean score and mean time.
- **What results mean:** similar scores suggest the order matters little for naming in this test, even though the neighbors change; compare the times too.
- **Limit and safety:** one class and one picture set; use pictures, not live or wild-caught animals.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The Costa Rica butterflies

In a 2004 study in Costa Rica, butterflies that looked almost alike were compared by their caterpillars, the plants the caterpillars ate, and short stretches of DNA from museum specimens. The study proposed at least ten species in that region, in a butterfly long treated as one species. Scientists still debate the exact count.

### When databases disagree

Besides the vultures' order, ITIS lists the brook trout's class as Teleostei, while many books use the larger group Actinopterygii, the ray-finned fishes. A count of shared boxes ranks grouping in one classification; it does not tell how long ago the organisms' most recent common ancestor lived.
