## Explain: one name for each species

<WordBank terms={['species', 'binomial nomenclature', 'genus', 'classification', 'taxonomy', 'domain', 'kingdom', 'phylum', 'dichotomous key']} />

A turkey vulture and a black vulture are both large, dark birds with bare heads. Yet they are two different kinds of bird.

A **species** is a group of similar organisms that can mate in nature and have young that can also reproduce. This idea is a useful rule for many organisms that reproduce with sex cells, with limits: it cannot be used for organisms that reproduce without sex, and some different species do sometimes mate in nature.

**Binomial nomenclature** is the system that gives each species a two-part scientific name. The first word is the **genus**, a small group of closely related species. The second word names one species in that genus.

- The turkey vulture is *Cathartes aura*.
- The black vulture is *Coragyps atratus*.
- The buzzard of Britain is *Buteo buteo*.

Write the genus with a capital letter and the second word in lowercase. Print both words in italics, or underline both by hand. Two-part names took hold after the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus used them in a book about plants in 1753.

A scientist in Japan who writes *Cathartes aura* means the same bird you see in Virginia. Names can still change when scientists learn more.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Each species has a two-part scientific name, genus then species, that scientists use in every country.</Support>

## Explain: eight levels, from general to specific

**Classification** is a system for organizing and identifying organisms by the traits they share and how they are related. The science of naming and classifying living things is **taxonomy**.

Scientists sort each species into eight main levels: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species. Each level down is more specific than the one above it.

A **domain** is the largest group. There are three domains: Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya. All plants and animals are in Eukarya. A **kingdom** is the next level down; plants and animals are two. A **phylum** is a large group inside a kingdom, such as the arthropods, animals with a hard outer skeleton and jointed legs. People who study plants often say division instead of phylum.

Classification works a bit like a mailing address. Country, state, city and street each narrow the search. The comparison breaks because houses on one street are not related to each other, and species in one genus are.

To decide the groups, scientists compare body parts, what organisms do, and DNA, which you will explore in [another lesson](/lessons/domains-kingdoms-and-dna/).

## Explain: use a dichotomous key

A **dichotomous key** is a tool that identifies an organism through a series of two-choice steps about traits you can see. Start at step 1 and follow the choice that fits.

This key covers seven animals. Each comes from a different phylum.

1. Has fins: brook trout (chordate). No fins: go to 2.
2. Has jointed legs: blue crab (arthropod). No jointed legs: go to 3.
3. Has two hinged shell halves: eastern oyster (mollusk). No shell: go to 4.
4. Has arms in a five-part pattern around a center: sea star (echinoderm). No five-part arm pattern: go to 5.
5. Has a long body made of ring-like segments: earthworm (segmented worm). No segments: go to 6.
6. Body full of tiny holes and fixed to one spot: sponge. Soft bell with trailing tentacles: sea nettle, a jellyfish (cnidarian).

This key covers four plants, one from each of four main plant groups.

1. Has a woody twig: go to 2. No woody twig: go to 3.
2. Needles in bundles, with cones: loblolly pine. Broad leaves and a flower cluster with four showy bracts: flowering dogwood.
3. Large fronds with spore cases: fern. Tiny leafy plant with a capsule on a thin stalk: moss.

### Design problem

Does the order of questions in a key change how well people use it? Plan a test with picture cards.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does changing the order of questions in a key change how many organisms people identify correctly?
- **Independent variable:** the order of the questions (key 1 or key 2, same questions).
- **Dependent variables:** the number of correct names out of 10 cards, and the time in seconds to finish.
- **Constants:** the same 10 cards, the same time limit, and no help from others.
- **Control:** key 1, the order you started with.
- **Trials:** at least 10 classmates per key. Report the mean score and mean time.
- **What results mean:** similar scores suggest the order does not matter much for naming in this test, even though the neighbors change. Compare the times too.
- **Limit and safety:** one class and one picture set; use pictures, not live or wild-caught animals.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The Costa Rica butterflies

In a 2004 study in Costa Rica, butterflies that looked almost alike were checked with DNA from museum specimens. The study proposed at least ten species in that region, in a butterfly that had been treated as one species. Other scientists still debate the exact count.

### When databases disagree

The American Ornithological Society checklist puts New World vultures in their own order, Cathartiformes, so databases can disagree. ITIS also lists the brook trout's class as Teleostei, while many books use the larger group Actinopterygii, the ray-finned fishes.
