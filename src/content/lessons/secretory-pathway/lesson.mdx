      observation, but put maturation forward as the best candidate for a shared core.

techniques:
  - name: "Pulse-chase with autoradiography"
    blurb: >-
      Cells or tissue get a radioactive amino acid for a few minutes, then a large excess of the
      unlabelled one, so only the protein made during the pulse carries label. Fixing samples at
      chase times and laying photographic emulsion over thin sections shows, by the silver grains,
      which compartment holds the labelled cohort at each time.
  - name: "Subcellular fractionation"
    blurb: >-
      Homogenized tissue is separated by centrifugation into fractions such as rough microsomes,
      smooth microsomes and secretory granules. Measuring label in each fraction over a chase gives
      a precursor-product time course, as long as each fraction is pure; contamination by a
      neighbouring compartment can make label appear early.
  - name: "Endo H digestion and SDS gel electrophoresis"
    blurb: >-
      An immunoprecipitated glycoprotein is treated with endoglycosidase H, which removes
      high-mannose and most hybrid N-glycans but not complex ones, and run on a gel. Sensitive protein runs faster
      after digestion; resistant protein does not. The rise of the resistant band over a chase
      times arrival at the Golgi enzymes that make glycans resistant.
  - name: "Live imaging of a fluorescent cargo"
    blurb: >-
      A cargo such as the temperature-sensitive VSV G protein, fused to a fluorescent protein, is
      held in the ER and then released all at once by a temperature shift (or a chemical switch in
      the RUSH system). Fitting a kinetic model to fluorescence in the ER, Golgi and surface over time
      gives effective rate constants for each stage in living cells.

The film ended on its rule. Here it is with its scope.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Forward is the default. It sets a protein's destination, not its speed, and a few proteins leave by other routes (Going deeper).

## The route, station by station

A secretory protein enters the ER as it is made, guided by its signal sequence and SRP (the signal recognition particle, which brings the ribosome to the ER); [protein sorting](/lessons/protein-sorting/) covers the ribosome and channel, and [translation](/lessons/genetic-code-and-translation/) covers protein synthesis. In the **lumen**, the space inside the ER, the enzyme signal peptidase cuts the signal off. Then come the stations, each with characteristic roles that overlap at the edges.

| Station | Its main roles |
|---|---|
| Rough ER | Makes the protein, adds the core sugar chain, checks folding |
| ERGIC (ER-Golgi intermediate compartment) | The sorting dock between ER and Golgi, marked by the protein ERGIC-53 |
| cis Golgi | The entry face of the stack; Golgi sugar processing begins |
| Medial and trans Golgi | Flattened sacs (cisternae) that continue and elaborate the sugar processing, overlapping into the TGN |
| Trans-Golgi network (TGN) | A tubular exit hall on the far side, where the routes split |
| Door or shelf | Release at once, or storage in a granule until a stimulus |

Folded cargo leaves at ER exit sites in carriers coated with the protein coat **COPII**, long drawn as small vesicles, though some may be tubules. A second coat, **COPI**, makes carriers that travel backward. Both coats, and the SNAREs that fuse carriers, are in [vesicle traffic](/lessons/vesicle-traffic/).

The film's pulse-chase is Lucien Caro and George Palade's 1964 experiment in living guinea pigs, read by electron-microscope autoradiography. James Jamieson and Palade then found the same order by fractionating pancreatic slices. Going deeper covers both methods and how one fraction misled them; [distinguishing experiments](/lessons/experiments-that-distinguish/) sets out pulse-chase in general.

Keep the scope exact. These experiments, in one tissue of one species, showed the order of compartments and roughly when the labelled cohort reached each one; later work placed the ERGIC and the TGN within that coarse order. Jamieson and Palade argued that the kinetics were direct evidence for passage through small Golgi vesicles, but no one watched cargo ride inside a vesicle. Order and timing are what pulse-chase gives you.

## The odometer: what the sugar chain records

Here is the chemistry behind the film's sugar tree.

**In the ER the chain goes on whole.** Very soon after the protein enters the lumen, the enzyme oligosaccharyltransferase attaches a preformed core of 14 sugars (Glc3Man9GlcNAc2: three glucoses, nine mannoses, two GlcNAc) to an asparagine in the sequence Asn-X-Ser/Thr (asparagine, almost any amino acid, then serine or threonine). So N-glycosylation begins in the ER, not the Golgi; the sugar chemistry is in [the carbohydrates lesson](/lessons/carbohydrates/). Glucoses are trimmed while the protein folds, and the folding check in [quality control](/lessons/quality-control-and-autophagy/) holds back protein that has not folded. What leaves carries a **high-mannose** glycan, made mostly of mannose.

**In the Golgi the chain is rebuilt, step by step.** Early enzymes cut mannoses off and add GlcNAc; one of them, **mannosidase II**, trims two more mannoses on the way to the complex type. Trimming by mannosidase II and then GlcNAc branching lead to a **complex** glycan, which many cells go on to decorate with galactose and often sialic acid. A **hybrid** glycan is part way: one branch rebuilt, the other still high-mannose. The enzymes are enriched in different cisternae but overlap (Going deeper).

**Endo H reads the record at one mark.** Endoglycosidase H (Endo H) cuts high-mannose and most hybrid glycans, but not the complex glycans made after mannosidase II has acted. Cut protein is smaller, so it runs faster on an SDS gel, which sorts proteins by size. Resistance is a threshold in a sequence, not a switch between organelles: it shows that a glycan has been processed past mannosidase II. Sensitivity alone does not locate the protein, which could be in the ER, the ERGIC or the first cisterna, or carry a glycan that is never processed. The record branches, and some glycans stay high-mannose even after their protein has crossed the Golgi.

## Fetched back: the KDEL signal

The chaperone that lost its tag in the film was grp78 (also called BiP), in Sean Munro and Hugh Pelham's 1987 work in COS cells, a line of cultured monkey cells. They had noticed that many soluble ER residents end in **KDEL** (lysine, aspartate, glutamate, leucine). The converse test settled it: adding grp78's last six residues to chicken lysozyme, a secreted protein, kept lysozyme in the ER. In that system the tag was both needed and enough. (Yeast uses HDEL in place of KDEL.)

## Crossing the Golgi: residents carried backward

Golgi enzymes face the ER chaperones' problem: cargo flows past them, yet they stay at their level. How cargo crosses the stack has two pictures, and each solves that problem differently. In the **stable-compartment model**, each cisterna keeps its enzymes and small vesicles carry cargo from one to the next; James Rothman's laboratory reconstituted transport between Golgi compartments in a cell-free system from CHO cells (its limits are in Going deeper). In the **cisternal maturation model**, the cisterna itself carries the cargo: it forms at the cis face and ends as trans Golgi and TGN. Its enzymes keep being carried back in COPI vesicles to the younger cisterna behind, so they stay at their level while the sacs flow past. The logic is like KDEL retrieval, recycling against the flow, though Golgi enzymes use their own retention and retrieval machinery.

## Two exits: the door and the shelf

In the film's terms, **constitutive secretion** is the door. **Regulated secretion** is the shelf, with protein stored at high concentration in granules.

Getting onto the shelf takes sorting, and how the selection is made is still argued; clumping of regulated cargo, such as granins, may help (Going deeper). In pancreatic beta cells, entry from the TGN into immature granules is not very selective: regulated proteins condense inside, and lysosomal enzymes that entered with them are removed later. There too, in human and rat, proinsulin is cut to insulin mainly in clathrin-coated immature secretory granules, not in the Golgi stack.

### Design problem

AtT-20 cells, a mouse pituitary tumour line, make the hormone ACTH from a larger precursor. They also carry an endogenous leukaemia virus and make its viral envelope glycoprotein. Design an experiment to show that one cell has two exit routes.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Pulse-label the cells, then chase, collecting the medium at intervals. Split the chase into two arms, with and without 8-bromo-cyclic AMP, a cyclic AMP analogue that stimulates these cells. Immunoprecipitate the viral glycoprotein, precursor ACTH and mature ACTH from medium and cells.

Expected result, as Gumbiner and Kelly reported in 1982: the viral glycoprotein and some precursor ACTH leave steadily, with little change from the stimulus, while mature ACTH is held in the cells and released four to five times more when stimulated. As a further test, Moore, Gumbiner and Kelly found that 200 micromolar chloroquine diverted new ACTH into the steady route: cargo that fails to be stored falls through to the default door.

Controls: equal synthesis in both arms (labelled protein in cells at the end of the pulse), viability under the stimulus, and a cell-associated count to show stored hormone falls when medium hormone rises. Limits: this is a tumour line, and the experiment does not identify the sorting signal.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Autoradiography, microsomes and the granules that filled too early

Caro and Palade injected labelled leucine and, in most animals, followed it with a large excess of unlabelled leucine. In autoradiography, photographic emulsion laid over thin sections forms silver grains where radiation from the label strikes it. The grains moved from the rough ER to Golgi elements to **zymogen granules**, stores of digestive enzymes made as inactive precursors (zymogens). Caro and Palade read their data as suggesting two steps in the Golgi region, small peripheral vesicles first and then large central **condensing vacuoles**, sacs in which secretory protein is concentrated before it becomes a granule.

Jamieson and Palade then moved to slices of pancreas kept alive in a dish, which allowed a precise three-minute pulse and chases of 7, 17 and 57 minutes. They broke the cells open and separated **microsomes**, small vesicles that form from broken membranes, not compartments of the living cell. Label was in rough microsomes (from the ER) at the end of the pulse, peaked in smooth microsomes (from the Golgi periphery) after 7 minutes of chase, and arrived in the zymogen granule fraction later. That is a precursor-product relation: each compartment fills as the one before it empties.

The two methods disagreed at one point. The granule fraction was half-maximally labelled at 17 minutes, yet in intact cells the granules carried label no earlier than 57 minutes. The cause was contamination: labelled condensing vacuoles were sedimenting with the granules. The principle is that a fraction is only as good as its purity. A year later the same slices showed that the step between the ER exit region and the peripheral Golgi vesicles needs energy, and is blocked by inhibitors of respiration but not of glycolysis.

### Inside the Golgi: enzyme order and the cell-free assay

Mannosidase I, GlcNAc transferase I, mannosidase II and GlcNAc transferase II act early; galactose and sialic acid are added later. In Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cell membranes the early enzymes partly separated from the late ones on density gradients, and the authors suggested that they work in some or all of the cis-side cisternae.

Rothman's cell-free assay measured transfer of VSV G between Golgi stacks from donor and acceptor CHO cells, and showed that transport needs ATP and cytosol. It did not show that vesicles carry cargo forward within one stack, and transfer between stacks fits maturation too.

### Export rates of real cargoes

Four secretory proteins in isolated rat hepatocytes left the ER with half-times from 14 to 137 minutes, and in human HepG2 cells five secreted proteins matured at rates differing at least threefold. That spread was first read as evidence against pure bulk flow, and Lodish and colleagues suggested a receptor that selects cargo, though folding and retention in the ER also contribute. For comparison, VSV G protein fused to green fluorescent protein moved from ER to Golgi in living COS cells at about 2.8% per minute (a first-order rate constant), a half-time of 0.693 / 0.028 = 24.8 minutes.

### Leaving the TGN, and other ways out

In a cell-free system from PC12 neuroendocrine cells, a regulated protein and a constitutive one were sorted into different vesicles as they left the TGN. Granins, typical regulated cargo, stay aggregated at pH 6.4 with 1 to 10 mM calcium, conditions then believed to hold in the TGN. Later calcium probes found less free calcium in the trans-Golgi than in the medial Golgi or the ER, so how closely those test conditions match the living TGN is unclear.

Not every secreted protein follows this whole path: some bypass the Golgi after entering the ER, and some proteins without a signal sequence leave without entering the ER at all. Lysosomal enzymes leave the flow at the TGN by a different signal, mannose 6-phosphate.
