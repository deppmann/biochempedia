techniques:
  - name: "Pulse-chase with autoradiography"
    blurb: >-
      Cells or tissue get a radioactive amino acid for a few minutes, then a large excess of the
      unlabelled one, so only the protein made during the pulse carries label. Fixing samples at
      chase times and laying photographic emulsion over thin sections shows, by the silver grains,
      which compartment holds the labelled cohort at each time.
  - name: "Subcellular fractionation"
    blurb: >-
      Homogenized tissue is separated by centrifugation into fractions such as rough microsomes,
      smooth microsomes and secretory granules. Measuring label in each fraction over a chase gives
      a precursor-product time course, as long as each fraction is pure; contamination by a
      neighbouring compartment can make label appear early.
  - name: "Endo H digestion and SDS gel electrophoresis"
    blurb: >-
      An immunoprecipitated glycoprotein is treated with endoglycosidase H, which removes
      high-mannose and hybrid N-glycans but not complex ones, and run on a gel. Sensitive protein runs faster
      after digestion; resistant protein does not. The rise of the resistant band over a chase
      times arrival at the Golgi enzymes that make glycans resistant.
  - name: "Live imaging of a fluorescent cargo"
    blurb: >-
      A cargo such as the temperature-sensitive VSV G protein, fused to a fluorescent protein, is
      held in the ER and then released all at once by a temperature shift (or a chemical switch in
      the RUSH system). Fitting a kinetic model to fluorescence in the ER, Golgi and surface over time
      gives effective rate constants for each stage in living cells.

**Inject a guinea pig with radioactive leucine, and in its pancreas the newly made digestive enzymes sit over the rough endoplasmic reticulum within about five minutes. By about twenty minutes the label lies over the Golgi, and by an hour it is in the granules the cell will later release.**

A secretory protein is made on the rough ER, travels through the Golgi stack gaining and reshaping sugar chains, and leaves the cell at once or after storage in a granule. The order of stops comes from Lucien Caro and George Palade, who published it in 1964. It answers one question, the order of the stops. This lesson asks the rest. What happens to a protein at each stop, how fast does it move, how does the cell keep its own ER proteins from leaving with the cargo, and how does it decide whether to release a protein at once or store it for later?

## Following a cohort: pulse-chase in the pancreas

The pancreatic acinar cell is a good place to start because most of the protein it makes is for export. Palade's method was **pulse-chase**: give radioactive amino acid for a short time (the pulse), then flood the tissue with unlabelled amino acid (the chase), so that only the protein made during the pulse is labelled. That labelled group is a cohort, and you follow it as it moves. The method is set out in [the lesson on distinguishing experiments](/lessons/experiments-that-distinguish/).

Caro and Palade did this in living guinea pigs, injecting labelled leucine and, in most animals, following it with a large excess of unlabelled leucine as a chase, and read the result by electron-microscope autoradiography: photographic emulsion laid over thin sections forms silver grains where radiation from the label strikes it, so the grains show which compartments hold the label. Grains moved from the rough ER to Golgi elements to zymogen granules. They read their data as suggesting two steps in the Golgi region, small peripheral vesicles first and then large central **condensing vacuoles**, sacs in which secretory protein is concentrated before it becomes a granule.

James Jamieson and Palade then moved to slices of pancreas kept alive in a dish, which allowed a precise three-minute pulse and chases of 7, 17 and 57 minutes. They broke the cells open and separated **microsomes**, small vesicles that form from broken membranes. Label was in rough microsomes (from the ER) at the end of the pulse, peaked in smooth microsomes (from the Golgi periphery) after 7 minutes of chase, and arrived in the zymogen granule fraction later. That is a precursor-product relation: each compartment fills as the one before it empties.

The two methods disagreed at one point: fractionation put label in the granule fraction much earlier than microscopy of intact cells did, because labelled condensing vacuoles were sedimenting with the granules. The principle is that a fraction is only as good as its purity; the numbers are under Going deeper.

Keep the scope exact. These experiments, in one tissue of one species, showed the order of compartments and roughly when the labelled cohort reached each one. Jamieson and Palade argued that the kinetics were direct evidence for passage through small Golgi vesicles, but no one watched cargo ride inside a vesicle. Order and timing are what pulse-chase gives you.

## Processing along the way: glycans as a location stamp

A secretory protein enters the ER as it is made, guided by its signal sequence and SRP, as [protein sorting](/lessons/protein-sorting/) explains for the ribosome and channel and [translation](/lessons/genetic-code-and-translation/) explains for protein synthesis. Signal peptidase removes the signal in the **lumen**, the space inside the ER. Very soon after the chain enters, oligosaccharyltransferase attaches a preformed core of 14 sugars (Glc3Man9GlcNAc2) to asparagine in the sequence Asn-X-Ser/Thr. So N-glycosylation begins in the ER, not the Golgi. The sugar chemistry is in [the carbohydrates lesson](/lessons/carbohydrates/).

In the ER, glucoses are trimmed while the protein folds, under the controls described in [quality control](/lessons/quality-control-and-autophagy/). Folded cargo leaves at ER exit sites in COPII-coated carriers, long drawn as small vesicles, though some may be tubules (the coats and SNAREs are in [vesicle traffic](/lessons/vesicle-traffic/)). In mammalian cells it then passes the **ERGIC**, the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment marked by the protein ERGIC-53, before reaching the **cis** face of the Golgi, the side facing the ER. It crosses medial and **trans** cisternae (the flattened sacs of the stack) and leaves from the **trans-Golgi network** (TGN), a tubular sorting station on the far side.

The Golgi rebuilds the glycan step by step. Mannosidase I, GlcNAc transferase I, mannosidase II and GlcNAc transferase II act early; galactose and sialic acid are added later. In Chinese hamster ovary cell membranes the early enzymes partly separated from the late ones on density gradients, and the authors suggested that they work in some or all of the cis-side cisternae. The enzymes are enriched in different regions but overlap, so the glycan records which enzymes the protein has met: a rough stamp of how far it has come, not an exact address.

The tool that reads the stamp is **endoglycosidase H** (Endo H). It cuts high-mannose and hybrid glycans but not the complex glycans made after GlcNAc transferase I and mannosidase II have acted. Cut protein runs faster on a gel. Endo H resistance is a threshold in a sequence, not a switch between two organelles: it shows that a glycan has been processed past mannosidase II, not which compartment the protein is in now. In one study of normal rat hepatocytes, only about 10% of the haptoglobin inside the cells was resistant, so most of it had not yet reached those Golgi enzymes.

Some processing comes last. In human and rat pancreatic beta cells, proinsulin is cut to insulin mainly in clathrin-coated immature secretory granules, not in the Golgi stack.

### Worked problem: reading an Endo H time course

The data here are illustrative. A glycoprotein in a cultured cell line is pulse-labelled for 5 minutes and chased. At each time you immunoprecipitate it, digest with Endo H, and measure the two bands. Assume nothing is secreted or degraded in 60 minutes, so each pair sums to 1,000.

| Chase (min) | Resistant | Sensitive |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 1,000 |
| 15 | 330 | 670 |
| 30 | 550 | 450 |
| 60 | 800 | 200 |

**Step 1, fractions resistant:** 0, 33%, 55% and 80%.

**Step 2, test for first order.** If the fraction still sensitive, S, falls as S = e^(-kt), then k = -ln S / t at every time.

> k = -ln(0.67) / 15 = 0.0267 per min; -ln(0.45) / 30 = 0.0266 per min; -ln(0.20) / 60 = 0.0268 per min

The three agree, so the data fit a single first-order process with an apparent k = 0.0267 per minute. That does not mean only one physical step is involved.

Real cargoes differ: secretory proteins in rat hepatocytes and human HepG2 cells left the ER at clearly different rates, which fits a role for cargo selection as well as folding and retention in the ER (details under Going deeper).

## Through the Golgi: vesicles or maturing cisternae

Cargo is not the only thing that flows forward. ER chaperones leak out too. In 1987 Sean Munro and Hugh Pelham found that many soluble ER residents end in **KDEL** (lysine, aspartate, glutamate, leucine). In COS cells, deleting KDEL from the chaperone grp78 made it secreted, and adding grp78's last six residues to lysozyme kept lysozyme in the ER. Think of it as a return-to-sender label. A KDEL receptor in the ERGIC and Golgi binds the tag in the slightly acidic lumen there, rides back to the ER in COPI-coated vesicles, and releases its passenger at the near-neutral pH of the ER. (Yeast uses HDEL.)

How cargo crosses the stack has two pictures. In the **stable-compartment model**, each cisterna keeps its enzymes and small vesicles carry cargo from one to the next; James Rothman's laboratory reconstituted transport between Golgi compartments in a cell-free system from CHO cells, needing ATP and cytosol. In the **cisternal maturation model**, the cisterna itself carries the cargo. It forms at the cis face, its resident enzymes are swapped by COPI vesicles moving backward, and it ends as trans Golgi and TGN.

On the left, three cisternae stay in place and small vesicles carry gold cargo from one to the next. On the right, a single cisterna carrying the cargo changes from early to late as it moves down the stack while small vesicles travel backward." caption="Two pictures of how cargo crosses the Golgi. In the stable-compartment model, cisternae keep their enzymes and vesicles carry cargo forward; in the cisternal maturation model, the cisterna carries the cargo and changes character from cis to trans, while COPI vesicles move enzymes backward." />

Budding yeast made the question answerable for one organism, because its cisternae are scattered rather than stacked. In 2006 the laboratories of Benjamin Glick and Akihiko Nakano each filmed single cisternae carrying an early and a late fluorescent Golgi marker. A cisterna lost the early marker and gained the late one, and Glick's group found the rate matched cargo transit. Later imaging showed cargo staying within a cisterna as it matured. In yeast, then, maturation is seen directly. For the stacked mammalian Golgi, Glick and Alberto Luini judged that no single model explains every observation, and proposed maturation as the best candidate for a shared core.

## Two exits: constitutive and regulated secretion

The routes split at the TGN, and in some cells sorting continues in immature granules. In **constitutive secretion**, protein is released as fast as it is made. In **regulated secretion**, protein is stored at high concentration in granules until a stimulus arrives. Nearly every eukaryotic cell has the first; endocrine, exocrine and nerve cells, among others, add the second.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/secretory-pathway/constitutive-vs-regulated-exit.webp" width={1200} height={771} alt="AI-generated illustration: a secretory cell where the trans-Golgi network sends sand-coloured vesicles straight to the plasma membrane (constitutive route) and gold vesicles into stored granules (regulated route); an inset shows a granule fusing with the membrane when a stimulus arrives." caption="The routes split at the trans-Golgi network, and sorting can continue in immature granules. In a cell-free system from PC12 neuroendocrine cells, a regulated protein and a constitutive one were sorted into different vesicles as they left the TGN. Clumping of regulated cargo, such as granins, may help pull it apart from the rest; how the selection is made is still argued. In pancreatic beta cells, entry from the TGN into immature granules is not very selective: regulated proteins condense inside the granules, and lysosomal enzymes that entered with them are removed later.

A stimulus acts at the end of this route. In guinea-pig pancreatic slices, a secretagogue did not speed synthesis or transit; it mainly increased discharge. Not every secreted protein follows this whole path either: some bypass the Golgi after entering the ER, and some proteins without a signal sequence leave without entering the ER at all.

### Design problem

AtT-20 cells, a mouse pituitary tumour line, make the hormone ACTH from a larger precursor. They also carry an endogenous leukaemia virus and make its viral envelope glycoprotein. Design an experiment to show that one cell has two exit routes.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Pulse-label the infected cells, then chase, collecting the medium at intervals. Split the chase into two arms, with and without 8-bromo-cyclic AMP, a stimulus for these cells. Immunoprecipitate the viral glycoprotein, precursor ACTH and mature ACTH from medium and cells.

Expected result, as Gumbiner and Kelly reported in 1982: the viral glycoprotein and some precursor ACTH leave steadily, with little change from the stimulus, while mature ACTH is held in the cells and released four to five times more when stimulated. As a further test, Moore, Gumbiner and Kelly found that 200 micromolar chloroquine diverted new ACTH into the steady route.

Controls: equal synthesis in both arms (labelled protein in cells at the end of the pulse), viability under the stimulus, and a cell-associated count to show stored hormone falls when medium hormone rises. Limits: this is a tumour line, and the experiment does not identify the sorting signal.

## Going deeper

### The contamination history in the pancreatic slices

The granule fraction was half-maximally labelled at 17 minutes, yet in intact cells the granules carried label no earlier than 57 minutes. The cause was contamination: labelled condensing vacuoles were sedimenting with the granules. A year later the same slices showed that the step between the ER exit region and the peripheral Golgi vesicles needs energy, and is blocked by inhibitors of respiration but not of glycolysis.

### Export rates of real cargoes

Four secretory proteins in isolated rat hepatocytes left the ER with half-times from 14 to 137 minutes, and in human HepG2 cells five secreted proteins matured at rates differing at least threefold. That spread was first read as evidence against pure bulk flow, and Lodish and colleagues suggested a receptor that selects cargo, though folding and retention in the ER also contribute. For comparison, VSV G protein fused to green fluorescent protein moved from ER to Golgi in living COS cells at about 2.8% per minute (a first-order rate constant), a half-time of 0.693 / 0.028 = 24.8 minutes.

### Granin aggregation and the TGN

Granins, typical regulated cargo, stay aggregated at pH 6.4 with 1 to 10 mM calcium, conditions then believed to hold in the TGN. Later calcium probes found less free calcium in the trans-Golgi than in the medial Golgi or the ER, so how closely those test conditions match the living TGN is unclear.
