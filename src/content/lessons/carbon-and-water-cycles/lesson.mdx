## Explain: carbon moves between air, life, soil and ocean

<WordBank terms={['carbon cycle', 'photosynthesis', 'respiration', 'combustion', 'decomposition', 'evaporation', 'condensation', 'precipitation', 'transpiration', 'runoff']} />

Carbon is in the sugar, fat and protein of every living thing. Carbon atoms join in chains and rings that form the frame of these molecules.

The **carbon cycle** is the movement of carbon among the air, living things, the ocean and the soil. Carbon is also stored in rocks and in fossil fuels underground. Four key processes move carbon around the cycle.

- **Photosynthesis** is the process in which plants use radiant energy to make sugar (glucose) from carbon dioxide and water. It takes carbon out of the air.
- **Respiration** is the process in which cells break down sugar and release carbon dioxide and water. Plants, animals and decomposers all do it, day and night.
- **Decomposition** is the breakdown of dead organisms and wastes by decomposers such as bacteria and fungi. It returns carbon to the air and the soil.
- **Combustion** is burning. Burning coal, oil or natural gas releases carbon dioxide quickly.

> carbon dioxide + water + radiant energy → sugar (glucose) + oxygen

Respiration runs the other way and releases energy from the sugar. You met both in [Partners in Energy](/lessons/photosynthesis-and-respiration-partners/).

Coal, oil and natural gas formed from living things that died long ago. Their carbon stayed underground for millions of years. Burning them adds that old carbon to today’s air. The ocean also takes in some carbon dioxide at its surface and gives some back.

This cycle keeps carbon available to living things. When people add carbon to the air faster than plants and the ocean take it up, the amount in the air rises. Scientists link that rise to climate change, which can affect the health of ecosystems and which you will study in [Big Changes](/lessons/eutrophication-and-big-changes/).

## Explain: water moves, and plants help move it

You learned the water cycle in grade 6. Here is a review, plus the part living things play.

- **Evaporation** is liquid water changing into water vapor, a gas. Heat from the Sun drives it.
- **Condensation** is water vapor cooling into tiny droplets that form clouds.
- **Precipitation** is water falling from clouds as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
- **Infiltration** is water soaking into the ground.
- **Runoff** is water flowing over the land into streams and rivers. Gravity pulls it downhill.
- **Transpiration** is the release of water vapor from plant leaves. Roots take water from the soil. It moves up the stem and leaves through tiny openings in the leaves.

The water cycle makes fresh water available to every living thing. It also shapes the weather, because water vapor forms the clouds that bring rain. Moving water carries things too. Runoff from a Virginia field can carry soil, nutrients and carbon into a stream that flows to the Chesapeake Bay.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Water evaporates, condenses, falls, soaks in or runs off, and plants send it back to the air by transpiration.</Support>

## Explain: how the cycles connect

The two cycles meet inside living things.

| Process | What happens to carbon | What happens to water |
|---|---|---|
| Photosynthesis | Taken out of the air | Taken from the soil |
| Respiration | Released to the air | Released |
| Transpiration | No change | Released to the air |
| Runoff | Carried to streams | Flows to streams |

A dry summer means less water for plants. They close the tiny openings in their leaves to save water, so they take in less carbon dioxide. Nitrogen joins in too, as you will see in [Nitrogen for Life](/lessons/nitrogen-for-life/).

Now think about Hawaii. Most of Earth’s land, and most land plants, are in the Northern Hemisphere. Each northern spring, leaves grow and photosynthesis speeds up. Each fall, leaves drop, but respiration and decomposition keep going. Air at Mauna Loa mixes with air from across the north.

One thing does not cycle. Matter is used again and again, but energy comes in as sunlight and leaves as heat. You saw that in [Food Webs and Energy](/lessons/food-webs-and-energy/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Photosynthesis uses water and carbon dioxide together, so a change in one cycle changes the other.</Support>

### Design problem

Does light change how much water a plant releases through its leaves? Plan an investigation with potted plants.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do plants in bright light lose more water by transpiration than plants in shade?
- **Independent variable:** light, either under a bright lamp or on a shaded shelf, with both places kept at the same temperature.
- **Dependent variable:** the loss in mass of each pot, in grams, after 48 hours.
- **Constants:** same kind and size of plant, same pot and soil, same watering, same room temperature.
- **Control:** a pot of wet soil with no plant in each place. It shows any water lost some other way.
- **Setup:** wrap the soil surface in plastic so water can leave only through the plant.
- **Repeated trials:** three plants in each place. Find the mean loss.
- **Safety:** use a common, nontoxic houseplant, wash your hands after handling soil, and wipe up spills.

If the plants in light lose more mass than those in shade, the data support the idea that light speeds up transpiration.

</details>
