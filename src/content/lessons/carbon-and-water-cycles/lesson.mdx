> **The one idea: the air is a tub of carbon.** The carbon dioxide in the air goes up only when more flows in than flows out, and down only when more flows out than flows in.
>
> *Where it applies:* the whole air as one store (a place that holds carbon), over one year and over many years. Mauna Loa measures moving, mixed air. Its record shows the pattern the flows make, mostly from northern land plants. It does not count each flow, and its monthly averages do not show the exact day the level turns. The water cycle runs alongside and meets the carbon cycle at the tiny openings in leaves.

## Explain: the flows in and out of the tub

<WordBank terms={['carbon cycle', 'photosynthesis', 'respiration', 'combustion', 'decomposition', 'evaporation', 'condensation', 'precipitation', 'transpiration', 'runoff']} />

The first film named the flows; here is what each one does. Living things need carbon: it is in their sugar, fat and protein. Carbon atoms join in chains and rings that form the frame of these molecules.

The **carbon cycle** is the movement of carbon among the air, living things, the ocean and the soil. Carbon is also stored in rocks and in fossil fuels underground. Four key processes move it.

- **Photosynthesis** is the process in which plants, algae and some bacteria use radiant energy (light energy, such as sunlight) to make sugar (glucose) from carbon dioxide and water. It takes carbon out of the air.
- **Respiration** is the process in which cells break down sugar and release carbon dioxide and water. Plants, animals and decomposers all do it, day and night.
- **Decomposition** is the breakdown of dead organisms and wastes by decomposers such as bacteria and fungi. It returns carbon to the air and the soil.
- **Combustion** is burning. Burning coal, oil or natural gas releases carbon dioxide quickly.

> photosynthesis: carbon dioxide + water + radiant energy → sugar (glucose) + oxygen
>
> respiration: sugar + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy the cell can use

You met both in [Partners in Energy](/lessons/photosynthesis-and-respiration-partners/).

Coal, oil and natural gas formed from living things that died long ago, so burning them adds carbon that sat underground for millions of years. The ocean takes in carbon dioxide at its surface and gives some back. This cycle keeps carbon available to living things.

## Explain: water moves, and plants help move it

You learned the water cycle in grade 6. Here are six important processes in it.

- **Evaporation** is liquid water changing into water vapor, a gas, driven by heat from the Sun. Most of the water vapor in the air evaporates from the ocean.
- **Condensation** is water vapor cooling into tiny droplets that form clouds.
- **Precipitation** is water falling from clouds as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
- **Infiltration** is water soaking into the ground.
- **Runoff** is water flowing over the land into streams and rivers. Gravity pulls it downhill.
- **Transpiration** is the release of water vapor from plant leaves, through tiny openings; the water comes up from the roots.

The water cycle keeps renewing the fresh water that living things on land and in lakes and streams need. It also shapes the weather, because water vapor condenses into the clouds that bring rain.

## Explain: how the cycles connect

Beyond the leaf’s one door, the cycles also meet here:

| Process | What happens to carbon | What happens to water |
|---|---|---|
| Photosynthesis | Taken from the air and built into sugar | A little is used in the leaf. Most of the water roots take up leaves by transpiration |
| Respiration | Carbon in sugar becomes carbon dioxide, which is released | Water is made in cells |
| Transpiration | Carries no carbon, but uses the same tiny openings that let carbon dioxide in | Released to the air as vapor |
| Runoff | Carried to streams | Flows to streams |

Water flowing off a Virginia field can carry soil, nutrients and carbon into a stream that flows to the Chesapeake Bay.

### Design problem

Does light change how much water a plant releases through its leaves? Plan an investigation with potted plants.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do plants in bright light lose more water by transpiration than plants in shade?
- **Independent variable** (what you change): light, either under a bright lamp or on a shaded shelf.
- **Dependent variable** (what you measure): the loss in mass of each pot, in grams, after 48 hours.
- **Constants** (what you keep the same): same kind and size of plant, same pot and soil, same watering, same room temperature.
- **Control:** a pot of wet soil with no plant in each place. It shows any water lost some other way.
- **Setup:** so water can leave only through the plant, wrap the soil surface in plastic.
- **Repeated trials:** three plants in each place. Find the mean loss.
- **Safety:** use a common, nontoxic houseplant, wash your hands after handling soil, and wipe up spills.

If the plants in light lose more mass than those in shade, the data support the idea that light speeds up transpiration.

</details>
