It pushes oily groups together so that they are buried, and it lets many weak bonds, each easy to break and re-form, add up to a specific structure. Water's own hydrogen bonds last only trillionths of a second, and the weak bonds work at the few-Ångström scale where atoms touch. How strongly charges attract depends on whether water is between them, and pH sets which groups carry charge at all."

  - name: "Reading bond strength off distance"
    blurb: >-
      Every weak interaction is a function of how far apart two atoms sit. Hydrogen
      bonds peak around 2.8 Ångströms, van der Waals around 3–4, and both fall off
      steeply past 5. Plot energy against distance and the "sweet spot" of biology
      draws itself.
  - name: "Ethanol precipitation of DNA"
    blurb: >-
      Add ethanol to a salty DNA solution and the strands fall out as a white thread.
      Lowering the dielectric constant strengthens the ionic interactions holding
      sodium to the phosphate backbone, so the DNA stops dissolving. The abstract
      dielectric becomes a thing you can spool on a glass rod.
  - name: "Henderson-Hasselbalch as a dial"
    blurb: >-
      pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]). One pH unit above the pKa means a 10:1 ratio of base
      to acid; at the pKa they are equal. Run the equation backward to find the
      protonation state of any group, and forward to design a buffer that holds a pH
      within one unit of its pKa.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "A salt bridge (ionic interaction) between a glutamate and a lysine side chain is buried in the hydrophobic core of a folded protein, where the local dielectric constant is approximately 3. The same pair of charges at the solvent-exposed surface sits in water, with a dielectric constant of approximately 80. Using Coulomb's law (E = kq₁q₂/Dr), how does the buried salt bridge compare to the surface one at the same charge separation?"
    choices:
      - "The buried salt bridge is roughly 27-fold stronger, because lowering the dielectric constant from 80 to 3 increases the interaction energy"
      - "The buried salt bridge is roughly 27-fold weaker, because the protein interior shields charges more than water does"
      - "The two are essentially equal, because the charges and distance are identical"
      - "The surface salt bridge is stronger, because water's hydrogen bonds add extra stabilization to the ion pair"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Coulomb's law makes interaction energy inversely proportional to the dielectric
      constant D, so going from D = 80 (water) to D = 3 (protein interior) increases
      the energy by a factor of about 80/3 ≈ 27. The low-dielectric protein interior
      does NOT shield charges well, so electrostatic interactions are much stronger
      there (the 27-fold-weaker option has the physics backwards). This is the Coulomb term
      alone: a real buried salt bridge must also pay to strip water from its charges, so
      its net effect on stability can be small or even destabilizing. Calling the two equal
      ignores the dielectric term; the surface-is-stronger option is wrong because
      water screens the interaction rather than reinforcing it.

  - stem: A buried hydrophobic side chain helps hold a protein in its folded shape. Which statement best describes why?
    choices:
    - The nonpolar side chains attract each other through strong van der Waals bonds that outweigh everything else
    - Water is pushed out of ordered shells around exposed nonpolar groups and gains freedom, and tight packing and weak bonds inside the fold add to the effect
    - Burying the groups lowers the protein's entropy so much that the second law is not obeyed
    - The effect is simply the result of hydrogen bonds forming between nonpolar groups
    answer: 1
    rationale: 'The hydrophobic effect is mostly water pushing nonpolar groups together: ordered water around exposed nonpolar groups is released and gains entropy, and the packed interior with its weak bonds contributes too. The entropy picture is clearest for small groups near room temperature, and enthalpy also contributes for large surfaces and at higher temperature. Van der Waals contacts help, but they are not the main driver. Total entropy still rises once the heat released to the surroundings is counted, which is the same as ΔG < 0, so the second law is obeyed. Nonpolar groups cannot form conventional hydrogen bonds.'

  - stem: "The intracellular phosphate buffer pair H₂PO₄⁻/HPO₄²⁻ has a pKa of 6.86. If the cytoplasm is at pH 7.86, what is the approximate ratio of [HPO₄²⁻] (conjugate base) to [H₂PO₄⁻] (acid)? (Use log 10 = 1.)"
    choices:
      - "1:10 (more acid than base)"
      - "1:1 (equal)"
      - "10:1 (more base than acid)"
      - "100:1 (overwhelmingly base)"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Henderson-Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]). Here pH − pKa = 7.86 − 6.86 =
      1.0, so log([A⁻]/[HA]) = 1.0, meaning [A⁻]/[HA] = 10:1. Because the pH is one
      unit ABOVE the pKa, the deprotonated conjugate base (HPO₄²⁻) predominates
      10-fold. A 1:10 ratio would be pH one unit below pKa; 1:1 is pH = pKa; 100:1
      is pH two units above pKa.

  - stem: "Which functional group CANNOT serve as a conventional hydrogen-bond donor?"
    choices:
      - "A hydroxyl group (–OH) on serine"
      - "An amino group (–NH₂) on lysine"
      - "A methyl group (–CH₃) on a hydrophobic side chain"
      - "The amide N–H of a peptide backbone"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      A hydrogen-bond donor needs an H covalently bound to a strongly electronegative
      atom (O or N): O–H and N–H qualify. A C–H bond is at most a very weak donor, because carbon's
      electronegativity (2.55) is too close to hydrogen's (2.20) to create the
      strong partial-positive H that a conventional hydrogen bond requires. This is exactly why methyl
      groups are nonpolar and hydrophobic. The hydroxyl, amino, and backbone amide groups all
      contain O–H or N–H donors.

  - stem: "Van der Waals (dispersion) interactions between two atoms are individually very weak (under ~1 kcal/mol). Why do they nonetheless matter enormously in biology, and what is their optimal distance?"
    choices:
      - "They are negligible in biology; only covalent and ionic bonds shape macromolecules"
      - "They are optimal at ~3–4 Ångströms and become collectively decisive when thousands act over a complementary surface, as in the gecko's foot"
      - "They are strongest at long range (>10 Ångströms) and weaken as atoms approach, so they keep molecules apart"
      - "They are optimal at ~1 Ångström, the same distance as a covalent bond, which is why they are hard to distinguish from covalent bonds"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Van der Waals/dispersion forces arise from transient induced dipoles. Each is
      tiny (under ~1 kcal/mol), optimal at ~3–4 Ångströms (this lesson's '~4 Å
      scale'), and falls off steeply (as 1/r⁶) with distance while turning repulsive
      at shorter range. The gecko foot is the canonical illustration: millions of
      setae making van der Waals contacts collectively support many times the gecko's
      body weight. Calling them negligible dismisses them wrongly; the long-range
      option inverts the distance dependence; the ~1 Ångström answer confuses van
      der Waals contact (~3–4 Å) with covalent bond lengths (~1–1.5 Å).

mcatQuestions:
  - stem: "A researcher extracts genomic DNA by adding cold ethanol to an aqueous solution of DNA and salt, causing the DNA to precipitate. The backbone carries a negative charge on each phosphate, normally neutralized by counterions and screened by water. Why does ethanol promote precipitation?"
    choices:
      - "Ethanol increases the dielectric constant of the solution, strengthening the hydrogen bonds within the double helix"
      - "Ethanol lowers the dielectric constant, strengthening electrostatic attraction between the backbone and counterions, so DNA aggregates and precipitates"
      - "Ethanol forms covalent bonds with the phosphate backbone, cross-linking DNA into an insoluble network"
      - "Ethanol raises the pH above the pKa of the phosphate groups, converting them to a neutral, insoluble form"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Water's high dielectric constant (~80) screens the phosphate backbone's charges
      and keeps DNA soluble. Ethanol has a much lower dielectric constant, so adding it
      lowers the solution's overall D. By Coulomb's law (E = kq₁q₂/Dr), a lower D
      strengthens the attraction between the phosphate charges and positive counterions,
      neutralizing repulsion and letting DNA aggregate and precipitate. This is
      Coulomb's law run the other way, by changing the solvent. A raised dielectric
      constant reverses the actual change; cross-linking invents covalent chemistry;
      the pH-shift option misapplies pKa — the phosphodiester backbone stays charged
      across this range.

  - stem: "The bicarbonate buffer operates by CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻, with an effective pKa of 6.1; normal blood is pH 7.4. A patient hyperventilates, blowing off large amounts of CO₂. What is the predicted effect on blood pH, and why?"
    choices:
      - "Blood pH rises (alkalosis), because removing CO₂ shifts the equilibrium to the left, forcing H⁺ and HCO₃⁻ to recombine and lowering [H⁺]"
      - "Blood pH falls (acidosis), because removing CO₂ increases H⁺ production to compensate"
      - "Blood pH is unchanged, because the bicarbonate buffer holds pH constant regardless of CO₂"
      - "Blood pH rises (alkalosis), because CO₂ is itself a base that was lowering the pH"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      By Le Chatelier's principle, removing CO₂ (a reactant on the left) pulls the
      equilibrium leftward, consuming H⁺ and HCO₃⁻ to replace the lost CO₂ — which
      lowers [H⁺] and raises pH, a respiratory alkalosis (the lungs control [CO₂]).
      Acidosis predicts the wrong direction;
      an unchanged pH wrongly claims a buffer makes pH immune to a change in a buffer
      component; calling CO₂ itself a base misidentifies it (it generates carbonic
      acid, so it is acidifying — the pH rises because of the equilibrium shift, not
      because CO₂ is basic).

  - stem: "Glycine has a carboxyl group (pKa₁ = 2.3) and an amino group (pKa₂ = 9.6). At physiological pH (7.4), what is glycine's predominant ionization state and net charge?"
    choices:
      - "Fully protonated: –COOH and –NH₃⁺, net charge +1"
      - "Zwitterion: –COO⁻ and –NH₃⁺, net charge 0"
      - "Fully deprotonated: –COO⁻ and –NH₂, net charge −1"
      - "An equal mixture of fully protonated and fully deprotonated forms, net charge 0 on average"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Apply the rule (pH > pKa → deprotonated; pH < pKa → protonated). At pH 7.4 the
      carboxyl (pKa 2.3) is far above its pKa, so it is deprotonated to –COO⁻
      (negative); the amino group (pKa 9.6) is below its pKa, so it stays protonated as
      –NH₃⁺ (positive). The molecule therefore carries both charges at once: a
      zwitterion with net charge 0. The fully protonated form describes pH well below
      2.3; the fully deprotonated form describes pH well above 9.6; the equal-mixture
      answer wrongly invokes a 50/50 mix of the extreme forms instead of the
      singly-charged-on-each-end zwitterion that actually predominates.

    text: |
      Water is sticky and likes to hold hands with other water. Oily things don't want
      to hold hands, so the water pushes them all into one corner to get them out of the
      way. That pushing is what folds the tiny machines in your body into the right
      shapes. The pieces that make those machines work are held by little "weak grips" —
      strong enough to hold on, weak enough to let go again, like magnets you can pull
      apart. And a special number called pH tells all the parts whether to grab on or let
      go.
  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: |
      Water is a polar molecule: oxygen hogs the electrons, so it has a slightly negative
      end and slightly positive ends. That makes it stick to itself, dissolve charged
      things, and reject greasy (nonpolar) things. That last one matters most. When oily
      molecules sit alone in water, the water has to form an ordered cage around them,
      which lowers its disorder (entropy). Water doesn't stay in that state willingly, so
      it shoves the oily molecules together to free itself. This "hydrophobic effect" is
      what makes proteins fold and cell membranes form. Inside the cell, the parts that

**A protein folds itself into its working shape, often in milliseconds, with no
instructions but its own sequence — and at first glance the second law of
thermodynamics seems to forbid it.** Disorder is supposed to win: a sandcastle never builds itself.
Yet inside every cell, polypeptide chains snap into precise structures, membranes seal
themselves shut, and DNA strands find their exact partners.

The resolution is the idea most students get backwards: entropy can *build* structure,
with water's help. Not despite the second law. Because of it. The catch is *whose*
entropy. The second law is about the total: the protein, its water, and the heat they
exchange with everything else. At constant temperature and pressure, that total rises
exactly when ΔG = ΔH − TΔS for protein-plus-water is negative (ΔH is the change in
enthalpy, or heat content; T the absolute temperature; ΔS the change in entropy, the number of ways a system can be arranged, which this lesson
calls freedom). Why the same thing? Heat given off warms the surroundings and raises
their entropy by −ΔH/T, so the entropy of everything changes by ΔS − ΔH/T, which is
−ΔG/T.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Count the water, not just the molecule. A change goes forward when the free energy of the molecule *plus its water* falls (ΔG < 0). In water, each weak interaction is worth only about one to a few RT net (RT, the gas constant times the absolute temperature, ≈ 2.6 kJ/mol at 37 °C), so no single contact decides anything. The sum decides. That sum includes the freedom water gains when nonpolar surface is buried, and the charges that pH allows: one pH unit changes the free energy of a group's protonation by 2.303 RT, about 5.9 kJ/mol, the size of one weak bond.

The rule is for dilute watery solution near 25–37 °C. It says which state is favored, not
how fast a chain gets there, or whether it gets there without help.

## Water is the engine, not the stage

Water is polar. The oxygen pulls electrons far harder than the hydrogens do, so the oxygen carries a partial negative charge and each hydrogen a
partial positive. The molecule bends at about 104.5 degrees, so those charges don't
cancel: water is an electric dipole, with a minus end and a plus end.

That polarity gives water three jobs. It sticks to itself, hydrogen end to oxygen
end, in a flickering network where each bond forms and breaks in a few trillionths
of a second. It dissolves many charged things, wrapping a sodium ion in a shell of
oxygens and a chloride in a shell of hydrogens. And it rejects anything nonpolar,
refusing to make room for an oil droplet the way a crowded room refuses to absorb
someone who won't talk to anyone. Hold onto that third job.

## Weak on purpose, measured in RT

Covalent bonds *build* the cell — the backbone of a protein, the sugar-phosphate rails of
DNA — and at 100 to 1,000 kilojoules per mole they are made to last. But the cell's
work is done by bonds you can break: hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions, van der Waals
contacts. When you smell a cat, odorant molecules bind your
receptors, trigger a signal, and fall off. If that binding were covalent, you would smell
that cat for the rest of your life. Reversibility is the point.

What breaks a weak bond is heat. A particle in water jitters as water molecules hit it,
the random walk that, summed over billions of molecules, we call diffusion. Warm the water
or shrink the tracer and watch it speed up.

**RT** is the yardstick for that jostling: covalent bonds are about 40 to 400 RT, so heat
almost never breaks them, while a bond worth one or two RT breaks and re-forms all the time.

### Hydrogen bonds: who can donate

A hydrogen bond forms when a hydrogen on a strongly electronegative atom, O or N (the
**donor**), is drawn to a lone pair on another O or N (the **acceptor**).

**Predict.** Which can donate a hydrogen bond: serine's –OH, lysine's –NH₃⁺, or
alanine's –CH₃?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The –OH and –NH₃⁺ can. The –CH₃ cannot, in any useful way: carbon's electronegativity
(2.55) is too close to hydrogen's (2.20) to leave the hydrogen much positive charge, and the
methyl carbon has no lone pair to accept one. That is why methyl groups are nonpolar.

</details>

In a vacuum a hydrogen bond is worth about 10 to 40 kJ/mol. In water the donor and
acceptor were already hydrogen-bonded to water, so pairing them mostly trades one bond for
another, and the net gain is a few kJ/mol, one to a few RT.

### Charges: why water evens things out

Coulomb's law gives the energy between two charges q₁ and q₂ a distance r apart:
E = kq₁q₂/(Dr). Here k is a constant and D, the dielectric constant, measures how well the
medium screens charge: 1 for a vacuum, about 80 for water, about 2 to 4 inside a protein.
Two opposite charges 4 Å apart attract with about 350 kJ/mol in a vacuum, about 90 inside a
protein (D ≈ 4), and about 4 in water. So in a vacuum, ionic beats hydrogen bond beats van
der Waals, but in water an exposed salt bridge (an ion pair between side chains) gets at
most about 4 to 6 kJ/mol from Coulomb's law alone, measured values are usually smaller, and it
is no stronger than a hydrogen bond's net share.

**Predict.** Move a glutamate–lysine salt bridge from the surface (D ≈ 80) into the core
(D ≈ 3) at the same distance. How much stronger is the attraction, and does the protein
gain that much stability?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

By Coulomb's law alone, about 27-fold stronger (80 / 3). But to reach the core both charges
had to leave their water, and that **desolvation penalty** can cost as much as the
attraction gains. The net effect ranges from destabilizing to several kcal/mol.

</details>

Ethanol precipitation of DNA runs the same law the other way: change the water instead.
Ethanol lowers D, the attraction between the negative phosphates and counterions such as
sodium strengthens, and the DNA clumps into a white thread.

### The four-Ångström window

Hydrogen bonds and van der Waals contacts work at the same length scale: roughly three
to four Ångströms (a few ten-billionths of a meter) between atoms. Closer, atoms repel; a
little farther, the attraction fades fast. Ionic attraction reaches
farther, though water and dissolved salt damp it down. Life happens in that narrow window.

## What pays for order: the hydrophobic effect

*The "Watch first" film covers this section. Where it says the hydrophobic effect "drives"
folding, read "pays most of the bill": the ledger below shows the other terms.*

Start with one methyl group, the –CH₃ at the tip of an alanine side chain. Water next to it
cannot hydrogen-bond to it, so it keeps its hydrogen bonds by pointing them along the
surface, toward other water. That first layer has fewer ways to arrange itself than bulk
water: lower entropy. Textbooks, and the film, draw it as a cage; it is really a slightly
stiffer layer, only modestly more ordered than bulk water, but around every nonpolar group
of a protein it adds up.

**Predict.** When a protein chain folds and buries its nonpolar side chains, what is the
sign of ΔS for the chain? For the water?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The chain's entropy falls: a floppy chain that could take an astronomical number of shapes
is held in roughly one. The water's entropy rises: water that lined the buried surface is
released into the bulk. Which is bigger? The
ledger below answers it.

</details>

That release is the **hydrophobic effect**. Nonpolar groups end up together mainly because
gathering them shrinks the surface water has to line, not because they attract each other
(their van der Waals attraction is weak). Near room temperature the gain is almost all
entropy; enthalpy's share grows with temperature and at large, flat surfaces such as an
oil droplet's, where water simply loses hydrogen bonds it cannot replace.

Here is the account for folding a protein of about 100 residues near 25 °C, in round numbers
(positive opposes folding, negative favors it):

| Term | Rough size (kJ/mol) |
|---|---|
| The chain loses its freedom of shape | about +550 |
| Water freed from about 4,800 Å² of nonpolar surface buried in the core | about −500 |
| Hydrogen bonds, ion pairs and tight van der Waals packing inside the fold, counted net of the bonds those groups used to make with water | about −90 |
| **ΔG of folding** | **about −40** |

The first two rows come from standard rules of thumb: about 25 cal/mol for each Å² of
nonpolar surface buried (4,800 × 25 cal is about 500 kJ), and 4 to 7 kJ/mol of lost freedom
per residue. The total is what is measured: small proteins typically fold with ΔG between
about −20 and −60 kJ/mol. So the third row is simply what is left over (Going deeper).

Read it for two things. **The second law is safe, but not the way it is usually told.**
Near 25 °C nearly all of the −500 is the water's entropy, about −480: a little less than the
chain's +550. So the chain plus its water lose a little entropy, and the rest is paid in heat:
the bonds and packing in the third row release heat as they form, raising the entropy of the
surroundings. ΔG is negative, so the entropy of everything rises.
**The win is a small difference between big numbers:** change the burial row by 10% (50 of
its 500) and the total moves by 50 kJ/mol, more than the total itself. That is why one bad
mutation can unfold a protein, and why folded proteins are only modestly stable, a margin
that also leaves room for the flexing many proteins need to work.

The same logic builds membranes. A phospholipid with two greasy tails can't dissolve, and
it is too bulky to pack into the small sphere, a micelle, that single-tailed soaps form. So
it lines up tail-to-tail into a two-layer sheet, a bilayer, that closes on itself so no
tails are left exposed at an edge. Enzymes make the lipids and proteins insert them into
existing membranes, but no enzyme holds a bilayer together. Water does: the lipids lose
freedom when they line up, and the water freed from their tails pays for it.

## pH: the ledger for charge

Ionic attractions, and many hydrogen bonds, depend on whether a group is charged, which
depends on pH. Back to the one idea: a proton coming on or off is one more term in
the ledger, worth about one weak bond per pH unit.

Below a group's pKa, the group mostly holds its proton; above it, it mostly lets go;
at the pKa, half and half. Call the protonated form HA and the form that has lost its
proton A⁻. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation writes the rule out:
pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]).

| pH − pKa | [A⁻] : [HA] | Share deprotonated |
|---|---|---|
| −2 | 1 : 100 | about 1% |
| −1 | 1 : 10 | about 9% |
| 0 | 1 : 1 | 50% |
| +1 | 10 : 1 | about 91% |
| +2 | 100 : 1 | about 99% |

**Predict.** Glycine has a carboxyl group (pKa 2.3) and an amino group (pKa 9.6). At
pH 7.4, what form does each take, and what is the net charge?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The carboxyl's pKa is 5.1 units below the pH, far beyond the table's last row: it is
–COO⁻, more than 99.99%. The amino group's pKa is 2.2 units above the pH, so it stays
–NH₃⁺, about 99.4%. One negative, one positive: a **zwitterion**, net charge 0.

</details>

The table also explains buffers. Far from the pKa, one form has nearly run out, leaving
little to absorb added acid or base, so a simple buffer in a sealed flask works best
within about one unit of its pKa.

Blood pH lives between 7.35 and 7.45, a window one tenth of a pH unit wide, guarded
by the bicarbonate buffer (CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻), effective pKa 6.1. At 7.4 that is
1.3 units off, about 20 bicarbonate ions per dissolved CO₂ (10^1.3 ≈ 20): a poor buffer by
the sealed-flask rule. It works because blood is not sealed: breathing carries the extra
CO₂ away. Model it with bicarbonate alone, 24 mM HCO₃⁻
and 1.2 mM dissolved CO₂, and add 10 mM of strong acid; each mM of acid turns one mM of
bicarbonate into CO₂. In a sealed flask the pH falls to about 6.2 (14 mM HCO₃⁻ against
11.2 mM CO₂). If breathing keeps CO₂ at 1.2 mM, it falls only to about 7.17. Real blood does
better still: the lungs lower CO₂ in response, and hemoglobin and other buffers take
up part of the acid. The kidneys then restore the bicarbonate.

**Predict.** A patient hyperventilates until blood CO₂ halves, bicarbonate unchanged.
Which way does blood pH move?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Up, to about 7.7: a respiratory alkalosis. Removing CO₂ pulls the equilibrium left, so
[H⁺] falls (in this bicarbonate-only model, the bicarbonate used up is negligible against
24 mM). CO₂ is the acid side of the ratio, so halving it doubles [HCO₃⁻]/[CO₂], and pH rises by log 2, about 0.3.

</details>

Cross much past the window and proteins change their charges and work less well, the heart
rhythm can falter, and a patient can slide toward coma. Less than one pH unit separates
ordinary chemistry from a medical emergency. Life is organized chaos, held together by bonds
weak enough to let go, paid for by water's demand for its own freedom, and tuned by a single
number, pH.

## Going deeper

### How temperature changes the hydrophobic effect

The hydrophobic contribution to stability tends to *strengthen* with temperature over the
physiological range, so some proteins are less stable in the cold and can even
cold-denature. The cause is a positive heat-capacity change, ΔCp, on exposing nonpolar
surface: as temperature rises the entropy term shrinks and the enthalpy term grows. In a standard (convergence) model, entropy's share of the cost is
about 96% at 25 °C, 82% at 37 °C and 56% at 60 °C, while the total grows only about 4% from
25 to 37 °C. The same ΔCp bends protein stability into a two-armed curve: heat unfolds a
protein because the chain's entropy wins, and cold because the hydrophobic effect weakens.

### Which favorable terms matter most

The ledger's third row is a remainder, not a measurement, so how the favorable side splits
is still argued. Burying oily groups is widely considered the largest single contribution
to folding, but some calorimetric and mutational analyses argue that hydrogen bonds
contribute comparably.

### Favored is not the same as fast

Anfinsen proposed that the native shape is the chain's lowest-free-energy state in its
environment. Many proteins fit, but some need help to fold, and amyloid can be even more
stable than the native form. Folding avoids a random search of every shape (Levinthal's
paradox) by running downhill on a funnel-shaped energy landscape.
