## The big idea

Ask your child to dip a finger in ice water and they will pull it out fast. Now picture a seal swimming in water that cold all day. How does it stay warm?

The answer is **blubber**, a thick layer of fat just under the skin. Whales, seals and walruses all have it. Your body makes its own heat, and so does a seal's. Cold water pulls that heat away much faster than cold air does. Heat moves through fat slowly, so blubber slows the loss of the animal's own warmth. It does not make heat, and it does not "keep the cold out." It keeps the heat in.

Blubber is a **physical adaptation**, a body feature that helps an animal survive where it lives. Virginia's grade 3 standard asks children to tell physical adaptations from **behavioral** ones, things an animal does, like a seal hauling out onto a rock to rest. The mitten lets your child feel a physical one at work.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time. Never leave a child alone with the bowl of water, even for a moment. Young children can drown in an inch or two of water, so keep the bowl out of reach of younger brothers and sisters. Empty the bowl as soon as you finish.
- Each try is short. Your child can pull a hand out at any moment, and every try stops at one minute. Stop at once if a hand hurts, stings, tingles or feels numb, or if the skin looks pale, white, blue, blotchy or very red (color is harder to see on brown and Black skin, so go by how it feels). Never hold a child's hand under.
- Hands stay inside the bags and out of the water. Keep the zip bags away from babies and toddlers, since a bag over the face can suffocate. Afterward, dry and warm both hands before the next try.
- If your child has sickle cell disease or a doctor has said to keep them out of the cold, skip the hand test and do the thermometer version instead. Do the same if cold has ever made your child's fingers turn white or blue, or made their skin itch or break out in hives. If anyone's lips, face or throat swell or breathing is hard, call 911 at once.
- Use only ice cubes and water. Never use dry ice.
- Shortening makes floors and hands slippery. Wipe spills right away with paper towels, then soapy water. If anyone has a food allergy, check the shortening label. For the harder version, use warm tap water only (below 49 °C or 120 °F on the thermometer), and check it on your own wrist first.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Build the blubber mitten.** Scoop about 2 cups of shortening into a gallon zip bag. Slide a second gallon bag inside it, with the two openings together. Fold the tops over and tape them so the inner bag stays open for a hand. Squish the shortening until it surrounds the inner bag about a finger's width thick.
2. **Build the plain mitten.** Do the same with the other two bags, but put nothing between them. This mitten is the same in every way except the fat.
3. **Chill the water.** Fill the bowl about two-thirds with cold water and add two trays of ice. Wait 5 minutes. Ice water sits close to 0 °C (32 °F).
4. **Predict.** Ask, "Which hand will feel cold first? How many seconds will each hand last?" Write the guesses down.
5. **Dip and time.** Your child puts one hand in each mitten. Lower both into the ice water up to the wrist, keeping the open tops above the water. Start the timer. Your child calls out "cold" when a hand first feels cold. Write down the seconds for each hand. Stop at 60 seconds, or sooner if your child wants. The plain-mitten hand usually feels cold well before the other one. Your times will vary.
6. **Warm up and swap.** Dry both hands, warm them gently (no hot water, no heater and no hard rubbing), and wait about 5 minutes until they feel normal. Swap the mittens to the other hands and test again. This checks whether one hand is just less sensitive.
7. **Talk about it.** Ask where the warmth in the blubber hand came from. Then look at a picture of a seal or a whale and ask where its "mitten" is.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "Which hand do you think will feel cold first? Why?"
- "I wonder where the warmth in the blubber hand came from. Did the shortening make it?"
- "What do you think would happen with a thinner layer of fat? A thicker one?"
- "A seal is in the water all day. What do you think would happen to a seal with no blubber?"
- "What do you think would happen if we put fluffy fur in the mitten and it got wet?"

## Why it works

Heat always flows from a warmer place to a colder one. Water draws heat away from skin many times faster than air at the same temperature, which is why a cold swim chills you faster than a cold day. Fat conducts heat poorly, so a thick layer of it slows that flow. In a living seal, blood vessels inside the blubber narrow in cold water, sending less warm blood near the surface.

Blubber does other jobs too. It stores energy the animal can use when food is scarce, and it is lighter than seawater, which helps the animal float. It can be very thick. Weddell seals in Antarctica can carry more than 5 cm of blubber, and bowhead whales in the Arctic can carry 43 to 50 cm.

Polar bears have a thick fat layer and thick fur. On ice, the fur does most of the insulating. In cold water, the fat layer helps keep a polar bear warm too. Sea otters have no blubber at all. Their very dense fur traps air instead.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Do one round only. Your child does not need to time it, but keep the timer running yourself so the try still stops at one minute. Your child says which hand feels cold first, and you count out loud together. Afterward, ask them to point to where the blubber is on a picture of a seal. Keep the bowl on a low, steady table so they can reach in without leaning over it, and keep any toddler away from it.

### Ages 8 to 10
Run the thermometer version as a fair test. Fill two sandwich bags with the same amount of warm tap water and check that both start at the same temperature. Put one inside each mitten in the ice water. Read the water temperature in each bag every 2 minutes for 10 minutes and make a line graph. Then test other linings the same way: cotton balls, craft feathers, wet cotton and nothing.

## If it doesn't go as planned

If the plain mitten does as well as the blubber one, it may be holding a pocket of air, which also insulates. Squeeze the air out before dipping. If water gets into a mitten, keep the open tops higher above the water line. Hard, cold shortening is hard to spread; leave it at room temperature first. Throw used shortening in the trash, not down the sink.
