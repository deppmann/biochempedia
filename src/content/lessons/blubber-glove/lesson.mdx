## The big idea

In about 30 minutes, your child feels how fat slows heat loss in cold water. They practice guessing first, timing a fair test with one change, and checking the result by swapping hands.

> **Say it together:** "Your body makes its own heat, and cold water pulls heat away fast. Seals, whales and walruses have a thick layer of fat under their skin called blubber. Blubber does not make heat. It slows warmth from leaving."

Ask your child: "How would your finger feel in ice water? How does a seal swim in water that cold all day?"

Cold water pulls body heat away much faster than cold air. Blubber does not make heat or "keep the cold out"—it keeps heat in. It is a **physical adaptation**, a body feature that helps an animal survive where it lives. A **behavioral adaptation** is something an animal does, like a seal hauling out onto a rock to rest.

<SafetyNote>
- **Water:** Stay with your child the whole time and never leave them alone with the bowl. Young children can drown in an inch or two of water—keep the bowl away from children under 4 and empty it as soon as you finish.
- **Cold:** Stop every try at 1 minute (60 seconds), or sooner if your child wants. Stop at once if a hand hurts, stings, tingles or feels numb, or looks pale, white, blue, blotchy or very red (color is harder to see on brown and Black skin, so go by how it feels). Never hold a child's hand under. Use only ice cubes and water—never dry ice. If cold is unsafe for your child, see **More safety** below.
- **Choking and suffocation (under 4):** Keep zip bags, ice cubes, cotton balls and craft feathers away from children under 4. Plastic bags can suffocate, and ice cubes or small pieces can choke. **Call 911 if choking.**
- **Sharp tools, glass and heat:** Keep hands inside the bags and dry/warm them between tries. An adult handles tape scissors, the sharp metal probe of an instant-read thermometer, and warm-water bags (comfortably warm on your wrist, well below 49 °C or 120 °F). Use a plastic or metal bowl, not glass, and never a glass thermometer.
- **Slips and allergies:** Shortening is slippery. Wipe spills right away with paper towels, then soapy water, and wash hands afterward. Check the shortening label for food allergies.

<details>
<summary>More safety: cold sensitivities and epinephrine</summary>

If your child has sickle cell disease or a doctor has said to avoid cold, skip the hand test and do the thermometer version (**For older children (8 to 10)** below; it works at any age, with you reading the thermometer). Do the same if cold has ever turned fingers white or blue, or caused itching or hives. If lips, face or throat swell or breathing is hard, call 911 at once and use a prescribed epinephrine auto-injector right away.

</details>

## What to do

1. **Build the blubber mitten.** Scoop about 2 cups of shortening into a gallon zip bag. Slide a second gallon bag inside, openings lined up. Fold and tape the tops so the inner bag stays open. Have your child squish the shortening about a finger's width thick around the inner bag.
2. **Build the plain mitten.** Repeat with the other two bags and nothing between them. Squeeze out extra air. Say: "Count the bags in each mitten. Both have two bags, so the fat is the only difference."
3. **You chill the water.** Fill the bowl about two-thirds with cold water and add 2 trays of ice. Wait 5 minutes. Ice water sits close to 0 °C (32 °F).
4. **Touch before dipping.** Have your child feel the shortening bag. Ask: "Does it feel warm, or cool like the room?" They will notice it feels cool—it does not make heat.
5. **Predict, then check.** Ask: "What do you think will happen? Which hand will feel cold first, and after how many seconds?" Write down the guesses.
6. **Dip and time.** Your child slips one hand into each mitten and lowers both to the wrist, keeping the open tops above the water. Start the timer. They call out "cold" when a hand first feels cold. Note the seconds and stop at 60 seconds or sooner. Usually the plain hand feels cold well before the blubber hand; times will vary.
7. **Warm up, predict and swap.** Dry both hands and warm them gently—no hot water, no heater, no hard rubbing. Wait about 5 minutes until they feel normal. Ask: "What do you think will happen if we swap mittens to the other hands?" Swap and test again to check whether one hand is less sensitive. Empty the bowl as soon as you finish.
8. **Two-question debrief.** Ask your child two questions about what happened.
   - "Where did the warmth in the blubber hand come from—the shortening or your own hand?"
   - "Look at a picture of a seal or whale. Where is its 'mitten,' and what does it do?"

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "Which hand do you think will feel cold first? Why?"
- "I wonder where the warmth in the blubber hand came from. Did the shortening make it?"
- "What do you think would happen with a thinner layer of fat? A thicker one?"
- "A seal is in the water all day. What would happen to a seal with no blubber?"
- "What would happen if we put fluffy fur in the mitten and it got wet?"

## Why it works

Heat always flows from warmer places to colder ones. Water draws heat from skin many times faster than air at the same temperature, which is why a cold swim chills you faster than a cold day. Fat conducts heat poorly, so a thick layer slows that flow. In a living seal, blood vessels inside the blubber narrow in cold water, sending less warm blood near the surface.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/blubber-glove/seal-blubber-cutaway.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a seal resting on a rock at the water's edge, with a zoomed cutaway of its side showing a thin skin layer, a thick pale layer of blubber under it, and the darker body inside." caption="A seal's &quot;mitten&quot; is a thick layer of fat, called blubber, just under its skin. It slows the loss of the seal's own heat." />

Blubber stores energy the animal can use when food is scarce. It is lighter than seawater, which helps the animal float. Weddell seals in Antarctica can carry more than 5 cm of blubber. Bowhead whales in the Arctic can carry 43 to 50 cm.

Polar bears have both thick fat and thick fur. On ice, thick, dry fur traps air and helps keep the polar bear warm. In cold water, wet fur holds less air, so the fat layer matters more. Sea otters have no blubber—their very dense fur traps tiny air bubbles instead.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Do one round only. Keep the timer running yourself so the try stops at 1 minute. Your child calls out which hand feels cold first while you count out loud together, then points to the blubber on a seal picture. Set the bowl on a low, steady table so they reach in without leaning, and keep any child under 4 away.

### For older children (8 to 10)
Run the thermometer version as a fair test. You fill and seal two sandwich bags with equal warm tap water (comfortably warm on your wrist, well below 49 °C or 120 °F) and handle the thermometer probe to confirm both start at the same temperature. Place one inside each mitten in the ice water, read temperatures every 2 minutes for 10 minutes, and draw a line graph. Ask what they predict for other linings, then test cotton balls, craft feathers, wet cotton and nothing.

## If it doesn't go as planned

- **Both mittens feel the same:** Squeeze trapped air out of the plain mitten before dipping; air insulates too.
- **Water leaks in:** End the try at once. Dry and warm the hand. Empty or replace the wet bag. Keep the openings farther above the water next time.
- **Shortening is stiff:** Leave cold shortening at room temperature before spreading.
- **Cleanup:** Throw used shortening in the trash, not down the sink.

## Observation sheet

**Worked example.** Write a guess for each hand and which hand you think will feel cold first. Record the time when each hand first feels cold. If a hand is still comfortable at the end, write that beside the stop time. For example, write *still comfortable at 60 seconds* for the blubber hand if it never felt cold. If your child stops early, record when and why. Record the two tries separately. Times will vary.
