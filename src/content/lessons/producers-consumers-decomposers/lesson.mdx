## Explain: three jobs in an ecosystem

<WordBank terms={['producer', 'consumer', 'decomposer', 'nutrient', 'fungi', 'bacteria']} />

Every living thing in a forest does one of three jobs. These are jobs, not kinds of living things.

A **producer** is an organism that captures energy from sunlight and stores it in sugar (glucose). Trees, grasses, mosses and algae are producers. They build sugar from carbon dioxide and water. You saw how in [Photosynthesis Feeds the World](/lessons/photosynthesis-feeds-the-world/).

A **consumer** is an organism that gets energy by eating other organisms. A deer eating acorns is a first-order consumer, because it eats producers. A fox eating a mouse is a consumer that eats other consumers. Producers are eaten by consumers.

A **decomposer** is an organism that breaks down dead plants, dead animals and wastes. The main decomposers are **fungi** and **bacteria**. Fungi include mushrooms and molds. Most of a fungus is a mass of thin threads hidden in the leaves or soil. Bacteria are single cells, far too small to see without a microscope.

| Job | Gets energy from | Forest example |
|---|---|---|
| Producer | sunlight | oak tree, moss |
| Consumer | eating living organisms | deer, fox, hawk |
| Decomposer | dead matter and wastes | fungi, bacteria |

Some animals help too. Earthworms and millipedes eat dead leaves and tear them into smaller bits. Some textbooks call them decomposers, and some call them detritivores. Either way, smaller bits give fungi and bacteria more surface to work on.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Producers capture energy from sunlight, consumers eat other organisms, and decomposers break down dead matter.</Support>

## Explain: decomposers send matter around again

A fungus does not have a mouth. It releases chemicals that break down the leaf outside its body. Then it takes in the small pieces. Bacteria work in a similar way. People often say decomposers "eat" dead leaves. That word is fine if you remember how it works.

A **nutrient** is a substance, such as nitrogen, that living things need to grow. As decomposers break down a leaf, nutrients go back into the soil. In a stream, they go into the water. Plant roots take up those nutrients and use them again. Some of the leaf’s carbon goes into the air as carbon dioxide, because decomposers release it in respiration as they use food for energy. You will follow that carbon in [The Carbon and Water Cycles](/lessons/carbon-and-water-cycles/).

{/* DIAGRAM: A Virginia forest cycle with arrows: 1 Sun shining on an oak tree (producer), 2 deer eating leaves (consumer), 3 dead leaves and a dead animal on the ground, 4 fungi, bacteria and worms in the leaf litter (decomposers), 5 nutrients in the soil going back up into the tree roots */}

Matter goes around and around. Energy does not. Energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat, as you learned in [Food Webs and Energy](/lessons/food-webs-and-energy/).

Abiotic factors, or nonliving conditions such as temperature and moisture, change how fast decomposers work. In many places, warm and damp conditions help fungi and bacteria break leaves down faster. But it is not a simple rule. Too dry or too cold slows them, and one experiment found less breakdown in warmer chambers.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Decomposers return nutrients from dead matter to the soil, water and air, where producers use them again.</Support>

## Explain: when one group changes

Producers, consumers and decomposers are linked. A change in one group reaches the others.

- **Fewer producers:** plant eaters have less food. Animals that eat the plant eaters may then have less food too.
- **Fewer decomposers:** dead leaves pile up. Fewer nutrients return to the soil, so plants may grow less over time.
- **More decomposers:** leaves break down faster, but only while there are dead leaves to break down.

People change these groups too. Some actions harm them. Pesticides and pollution can kill soil life. Clearing a forest removes producers and the leaf litter that feeds decomposers. Raking away every leaf removes a source of nutrients. Other actions help. Planting trees adds producers. Leaving leaves in a corner of a yard, or making compost, feeds decomposers and gives small animals shelter.

## Elaborate: two years in a bag of leaves

Researchers sealed dried yellow birch leaves in mesh bags. In October 2011 they set the bags on the ground at three heights in the Weimer Run watershed, near Davis, West Virginia. The table shows the lowest site and the highest site. They brought back bags five times and weighed the dried leaves.

| Time after setting out (months) | Lower site: mass left (%) | Higher site: mass left (%) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (October 2011) | 100 | 100 |
| 1 (November 2011) | 68.9 | 72.0 |
| 8 (June 2012) | 70.9 | 68.9 |
| 12 (October 2012) | 54.4 | 59.7 |
| 20 (June 2013) | 51.6 | 55.8 |
| 24 (October 2013) | 41.2 | 47.8 |

Source: Atkins, Epstein and Welsch, Ecosphere, 2017, Weimer Run, West Virginia, 2011 to 2013. Each value is a mean of 8 or 9 bags, from the authors’ shared data, rounded to one decimal. Different bags were weighed each time. The 100 percent at month 0 is the starting value, not a weighing of recovered bags. This is the closest real data we found; it is not from Virginia.

1. Describe the trend at the lower site from month 0 to month 24.
2. At month 24, how many percentage points more mass was left at the higher site than at the lower site?
3. The lower site went from 68.9 to 70.9 percent between months 1 and 8. Why might that happen when different bags are weighed each time?

### Design problem

Plan an investigation to test whether moist soil makes leaves break down faster than dry soil. Use leaves, cups of soil and a balance.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do leaves lose mass faster in moist soil than in dry soil?
- **Independent variable:** moist soil or dry soil.
- **Dependent variable:** the dry leaf mass left after 4 weeks, in grams, and the percent of the starting mass.
- **Constants:** the same kind of leaf, 2.0 grams of air-dried leaves per cup, the same soil, cup size and room temperature.
- **Control:** cups of dry soil with no water added.
- **Trials:** five cups of each. Find the mean for each group.
- **Measuring:** after 4 weeks, rinse the leaves gently, let them air-dry for a week, then weigh them.
- **Safety:** wear gloves, keep cups covered with a lid that has small holes, do not sniff moldy cups, and wash your hands. Students with mold allergies should not handle the cups.

If the moist cups keep less leaf mass, moisture likely helped decomposers in this setup.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The leaves that fall each autumn do not vanish. Fungi, bacteria, worms and other small living things break them down, and the nutrients go back into the soil, where trees take them up again. Without decomposers, dead leaves would pile up and the forest would run short of nutrients.

**Science practices you used:** predicting and testing with a model, reading a data table and finding a difference, making a claim from evidence, and planning a fair test.
