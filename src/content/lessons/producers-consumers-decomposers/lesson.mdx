> **The one idea: the leaf pile is a bathtub.** Producers pour new leaves in each autumn, and consumers carry some matter along. Decomposers drain the pile back into carbon dioxide, water and nutrients that producers use again. The pile stops growing when the drain takes out as much as falls in each year.
>
> *Where it applies:* this is a simple model of **matter** (the stuff living things are made of), such as carbon and nutrients. It assumes about the same leaf fall each year and decomposers that keep removing a share of the pile. In peat bogs, cold or waterlogged ground nearly stops the drain, and dead plants keep piling up. Energy does not go around: it enters as sunlight and leaves as heat ([Food Webs and Energy](/lessons/food-webs-and-energy/)).

## Explain: three jobs in an ecosystem

<WordBank terms={['producer', 'consumer', 'decomposer', 'nutrient', 'fungi', 'bacteria', 'respiration']} />

A **producer** captures energy from sunlight and stores it in sugar (glucose) built from carbon dioxide and water ([Photosynthesis Feeds the World](/lessons/photosynthesis-feeds-the-world/)). Trees, grasses, mosses and algae are producers.

A **consumer** is an organism that gets energy by eating other organisms. The "order" counts steps from the producer. A deer eating acorns is a **first-order consumer**. A fox eating a seed-eating mouse is a **second-order consumer**.

A **decomposer** breaks down dead plants, dead animals and wastes. The main decomposers are **fungi** and **bacteria**. Fungi include mushrooms and molds; most of a fungus is a mass of thin threads hidden in the leaves or soil. Bacteria are single cells, far too small to see without a microscope.

| Job | Gets energy from | Forest example | In the bathtub |
|---|---|---|---|
| Producer | sunlight | oak tree, moss | the tap |
| Consumer | eating other organisms | deer, fox, hawk | carries matter along |
| Decomposer | dead matter and wastes | fungi, bacteria | the drain |

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Producers capture energy from sunlight, consumers eat other organisms, and decomposers break down dead matter.</Support>

**Respiration** is not breathing. It is how cells release the energy in food, and in you and most forest decomposers it uses oxygen. Carbon is a large share of a dry leaf, so, as the first film showed, much of it leaves as invisible gas ([The Carbon and Water Cycles](/lessons/carbon-and-water-cycles/)).

A **nutrient** is a substance, such as nitrogen, that living things need to grow. Nutrients are a small part of a leaf’s mass. Decomposers return them to the soil, or to the water in a stream. ### How tall does the pile get?

In a simple model, one autumn’s worth of leaves falls each year, and decomposers remove half the pile over the next year. Each new pile is half the old one plus 1. In year 2, half of 1 is 0.5, plus 1 makes 1.5:

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pile just after leaf fall (autumns of leaves) | 1 | 1.5 | 1.75 | about 1.9 | almost 2 |

The pile stops near 2, because half of 2 is 1. Real leaves do not lose the same share every year, so this model cannot tell us how big a real pile gets (Going deeper).

**Abiotic factors**, nonliving conditions such as temperature and moisture, help set how fast the drain runs; leaf type and the decomposers matter too. In many forests, warm and damp weather speeds decomposers up, and cold or very dry weather slows them down.

## Explain: when one group changes

Many changes act on the tap, the drain or both, and can reach the other groups.

- **Fewer producers (a weaker tap):** plant eaters have less food, and so may the animals that eat them. Fewer new leaves fall.
- **Fewer decomposers (a clogged drain):** dead leaves pile up. Fewer nutrients return to the soil, so plants may grow less over time.
- **More decomposers (a faster drain):** leaves break down faster, but only while dead leaves remain.

People change both sides. Clearing a forest removes producers and the new leaf litter that feeds decomposers. Planting trees adds producers. Pesticides and pollution can kill soil life and clog the drain. Raking away every leaf removes nutrients. Leaving leaves in a yard corner, or composting, feeds decomposers and shelters small animals.

### Design problem

Plan an investigation to test whether moist soil makes leaves break down faster than dry soil. Use leaves, cups of soil and a balance.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do leaves lose mass faster in moist soil than in dry soil?
- **Independent variable:** moist soil or dry soil.
- **Dependent variable:** the dry leaf mass left after 4 weeks, in grams, and the percent of the starting mass.
- **Constants:** the same kind of leaf, 2.0 grams of leaves per cup, dried until their mass stops changing, the same soil, cup size and room temperature.
- **Control:** cups of dry soil with no water added.
- **Trials:** five cups of each. Find the mean for each group.
- **Measuring:** after 4 weeks, rinse the leaves gently. Dry them the same way as the starting leaves, and weigh them each day until the mass stops changing.
- **Safety:** wear gloves, keep cups covered with a lid that has small holes, do not sniff moldy cups, and wash your hands. Students with mold allergies should not handle the cups.

If the moist cups keep less leaf mass, moisture increased mass loss in this setup. Water can also wash material out of leaves without any decomposers, so this test alone cannot show how much of the loss came from decomposers.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

In the simple model, the pile stops growing when the drain takes out as much as falls in each year. The leaf bags show how fast one kind of leaf lost mass in mesh bags over two years.

**Science practices you used:** predicting and testing with a model, reading a line graph and a data table and finding a difference, making a claim from evidence, and planning a fair test.

## Going deeper

### Jobs, not kinds

These are jobs, not kinds of living things. An earthworm helps break down dead leaves and is also food for robins. Some do not fit any job neatly: root fungi trade nutrients with living trees.

### Decomposers or detritivores?

Some textbooks call earthworms and millipedes decomposers, others detritivores. Either way, they shred the leaves.

### Why a real pile is not exactly 2 autumns

In the leaf bags, about 40 to 46 percent of the mass was gone after one year, but only about 12 to 13 percent more in the second. That fits breakdown slowing down once the easy parts of a leaf are used up. The weather also differed between years, though. If 40 to 46 percent of the pile vanished every year, the model pile would stop near 2.2 to 2.5 autumns’ worth. Older leaves drain more slowly, so a real pile probably holds more, and two years of bags cannot say how much. Worms, kept out by the mesh, would probably speed things up. Warmth does not always speed the drain either: see the story "Warmer did not mean faster."

### About the leaf-bag data

The leaves were yellow birch. The 100 percent at month 0 is the starting value, not a weighing of recovered bags. This is the closest real data we found; it is not from Virginia. Each later value is a mean of 8 or 9 bags, from the authors' shared data, rounded to one decimal. The mesh holes were about 1 millimeter wide, so earthworms and millipedes could not get in.
