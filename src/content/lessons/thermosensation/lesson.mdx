      report it? Vestergaard, Carta, Güney and Poulet worked with mice older than two months that

techniques:
  - name: "Thermal go/no-go psychophysics with catch trials"
    blurb: >-
      A head-fixed mouse licks for reward when the temperature of a Peltier plate under its paw
      changes, and catch trials with no change give the false-alarm rate. Hit and false-alarm rates
      together give d′, a sensitivity index that separates detection from how freely the animal
      licks.
  - name: "Ex vivo skin-nerve single-fibre recording"
    blurb: >-
      A patch of skin is kept alive with its nerve attached, the bath temperature is controlled,
      and spikes are recorded from single afferent fibres. It shows what the terminal sends, and
      it reveals ongoing firing that a warmer bath would hide.
  - name: "Ganglion and cortical calcium imaging"
    blurb: >-
      A genetically encoded calcium indicator reports activity in hundreds of sensory-neuron
      somata or cortical neurons at once during controlled skin stimulation. It gives population
      tuning and recruitment, at the slow time resolution of calcium signals.
  - name: "Whole-cell patch clamp with thermal ramps"
    blurb: >-
      A recombinant channel in a cell line is held at a fixed voltage while the bath temperature
      is ramped, with or without an agonist. Current versus temperature gives the apparent
      temperature sensitivity, Q10, for that state of the channel.

**Mice lacking TRPM8, the cold-and-menthol receptor, were trained for 10 days to report a gentle warming of the forepaw. They failed completely. Why would a cold receptor matter for warmth?**

If TRPM8 is the cold receptor, a mouse without it should be bad at cold, not at warm. The answer this lesson builds is that temperature is not read off one channel at a time. It is read off the activity of several groups of sensory fibres at once, including a group that reports warming by going quiet.

A few terms first. A **transducer** is the protein that turns a stimulus into an electrical signal; for temperature, the transducers are temperature-gated ion channels, which open or close as temperature changes, and no single channel reads out every temperature. A **line** here means one kind of **afferent** fibre (a sensory fibre carrying signals in from the skin) together with what it reports. A channel sits in a line; it is not itself a feeling. **Innocuous** temperatures, warm or cool, do not threaten the tissue; **noxious** ones damage or threaten to damage it, and usually hurt.

[Touch and pain](/lessons/touch-and-pain/) covers TRPV1, TRPM8 and TRPA1 at the level of necessity and sensitization, and [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/) covers how gates open and leaves heat gating open as a question. This lesson starts where those stop: how warmth, cool and noxious heat are encoded by populations of afferents and read by cortex, and what the gating evidence can and cannot say.

## The map: ranges, fibres and channels

Two kinds of afferent fibre carry the signals in this lesson. **C fibres** are thin, unmyelinated and slowly conducting. **Aδ fibres** ("A-delta") are thinly myelinated and conduct faster. Five channels come up. TRPV1 opens with noxious heat and with capsaicin, the hot molecule in chilli; TRPM8 opens with cooling and with menthol; TRPM3 and TRPA1 are two more channels tested here for noxious heat; TRPM2 is a candidate for warmth. Four temperature ranges come up, each with its own fibres and candidate channels.

| Range | What a mouse in this lesson does | Fibres that carry it | Channels tested here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cold | still avoids surfaces below 10 °C without TRPM8 | not taken up here | not TRPM8 alone; not identified here |
| Cool | reports a 0.5 °C cooling step | cool-sensitive C fibres, including a population that fires at rest | TRPM8 |
| Warm | reports a 1 °C warming step | warm-excited polymodal C fibres speed up; the cool fibres slow down | TRPM8 needed in this task; TRPV1 and TRPM2 knockouts blunt detection |
| Noxious heat | withdraws the paw | C and Aδ afferents | TRPV1, TRPM3 and TRPA1, in parallel |

Where did the channels come from? From cloning screens. Michael Caterina and colleagues in David Julius's laboratory expressed pools of sensory-neuron cDNA in cells that do not normally respond to capsaicin and screened for capsaicin-evoked calcium influx (Nature, 1997). The clone they recovered, TRPV1, encoded a nonselective cation channel that was also activated by noxious heating, which led them to suggest that it transduces painful thermal stimuli in vivo. Five years later, David McKemy, Neuhausser and Julius cloned a cold- and menthol-responsive receptor, TRPM8, from a trigeminal-neuron library made from newborn Sprague Dawley rats and characterised it in HEK293 cells and Xenopus oocytes (Nature, 2002). In the same year, Andrea Peier and colleagues in Ardem Patapoutian's laboratory reported cold and menthol activation of heterologously expressed TRPM8 (Cell, 2002). Julius and Patapoutian shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries of receptors for temperature and touch.

A **heterologous** expression screen puts a gene into a foreign cell that does not normally carry it. It tests molecular sufficiency: this protein, in this foreign cell, responds to this stimulus. It does not test whether an intact animal needs the protein, whether other proteins do the same job, or where in the protein the temperature is sensed; the field spent the next twenty years on those three questions. Need is tested by removing the gene. Mice lacking TRPM8 have profoundly reduced cold responses in neurons and fibres, yet they still avoid surfaces below 10 °C (Bautista and colleagues, Nature, 2007). That is why TRPM8 is called the main established transducer of innocuous *cooling*, not of all cold.

## Lines that fire at rest

The answer to the puzzle came from one 2020 study that ran two preparations side by side. Paricio-Montesinos and colleagues, working with Gary Lewin and James Poulet among others (Neuron, 2020), trained mice to report temperature steps on the forepaw (the next section) and recorded single afferent fibres in a forepaw **glabrous** (hairless) **skin-nerve preparation**: a patch of skin kept alive **ex vivo**, outside the animal, with its nerve attached, so spikes from single fibres can be recorded while the bath temperature is controlled. Temperature ramps ran at 1 °C per second.

Such recordings are usually run with the bath near body-surface temperature, 32 °C. Thermal imaging showed awake forepaw skin at 26 to 28 °C, so the investigators also used a 27 °C bath, while the patch of skin under the stimulator was held at the same 32 °C baseline used in the behaviour. That choice changed what they saw.

**Predict.** A fibre that is silent at rest can report a change only by starting to fire. What can a fibre that already fires slowly and steadily at resting skin temperature report that a silent fibre cannot?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A change in either direction: faster for one, slower for the other. In the 27 °C bath, about 19% of the temperature-sensitive fibres recorded were **cool-sensitive C fibres** with **ongoing activity**, firing with no stimulus at 0.2 to 6 spikes per second (Hz). At the usual 32 °C bath, no such fibres had been seen.

</details>

### The signature response

Two kinds of C fibre answered warming, in opposite directions.

| | Cool-sensitive C fibre | Warm-excited polymodal C fibre |
|---|---|---|
| Skin at rest | fires slowly and steadily (0.2 to 6 Hz in the 27 °C bath) | little ongoing firing |
| Skin cools | fires faster | not part of this story |
| Skin warms | fires **slower** | fires faster |

**Polymodal** means the fibre also responds to other kinds of stimulus, such as pressure. The table is a constructed summary of the two kinds of response, not one recording.

The left column is the signature of this system: a fibre that fires at rest can signal by slowing down. Semicircular-canal afferents in the inner ear use a similar design: they fire at rest, so one fibre can signal head rotation about its axis one way by speeding up and the other way by slowing down ([the vestibular system](/lessons/vestibular-system/)). How much a slowdown can tell depends on how much firing there was to lose. In a simple counting model, if warming removes the same fraction of spikes, a fibre that fired faster at rest says more; a fixed absolute drop does not follow that rule.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Skin temperature is read from a few afferent lines at once, not from one channel per feeling. A line that is already firing at resting skin temperature can signal in both directions: the TRPM8-dependent cool line speeds up when the skin cools and slows down when it warms. So a channel that supplies a line's activity matters for every sensation that reads that line, which is how losing a cool channel can cost warmth. And more than one channel can carry the same signal, which is how acute noxious heat survives the loss of any one of TRPV1, TRPM3 or TRPA1.

Its scope: gentle warm and cool detection in the mouse forepaw and, in its second half, acute withdrawal from noxious heat in mice. It is not a human result, it names no central circuit (none was identified), and another preparation points the other way on warmth. Where inside a channel temperature is sensed is a different question, taken up at the end.

## Measuring detection: hits, false alarms and d′

The behaviour came first in that study. Adult mice of both sexes were trained, **head-fixed** (the head held still so the stimulus stays in place), to report a temperature step on the forepaw from a 32 °C baseline, delivered by an 8 by 8 mm **Peltier** surface, a small plate whose temperature can be stepped electrically. The mouse licked to report a change. Trained mice reported a warming step of 1 °C; a separately trained cooling group reported a cooling step of 0.5 °C.

How sensitive is a mouse in such a task, when it also licks sometimes for nothing? A lick on a warming trial is a **hit**. A mouse that licked on every trial would score hits without detecting anything, so the task includes **catch trials**, with no temperature change; a lick on a catch trial is a **false alarm**. The study's analysis uses the signal-detection index d′ ("d-prime"), which compares the two rates after z, the inverse standard-normal cumulative distribution, converts each into a z score. In the standard equal-variance model with a stable criterion, a change in readiness to lick shifts both z scores equally, so their difference measures sensitivity apart from that readiness. In real sessions d′ remains useful, but it does not automatically remove learning, motor or lapse effects.

> **d′ = z(hit rate) − z(false-alarm rate)**

**Worked problem.** Here is a constructed session, not published data: 100 warming trials and 100 catch trials with no temperature change, with licks on 80 warming trials and 20 catch trials.

1. Hit rate H = 80/100 = 0.80. False-alarm rate F = 20/100 = 0.20.
2. z(0.80) = 0.8416. z(0.20) = −0.8416. (The two are mirror images because 0.80 and 0.20 are symmetric about 0.5.)
3. d′ = 0.8416 − (−0.8416) = 1.6832, so d′ ≈ 1.68, dimensionless.

It is a sensitivity index, not a temperature: 1.68 is not 1.68 °C, and nothing about the stimulus size enters the formula.

**Predict.** A second mouse licks on 80 warming trials and on 80 catch trials. Its hit rate is the same 0.80. Is it detecting warmth?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Its d′ ≈ 0, because it licks as often when nothing happens as when the plate warms. Separating sensitivity from **criterion**, how freely the animal licks, is why the index is used and why catch trials are built into the design.

</details>

## Take away the cool line

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The same study tested knockout mice in the forepaw task. TRPM8 is the main channel behind the cool fibres' response to cooling.

**Predict.** Take TRPM8 away. What should happen to the cool fibres' ongoing firing, and to the mouse's ability to report warming? Then predict the same for mice that lack TRPV1, the heat- and capsaicin-activated channel.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

| Mouse | Cool fibres' ongoing firing | Reports warming in this task? |
|---|---|---|
| Wild type | present, and warming reduces it | yes, down to a 1 °C step |
| No TRPM8 | lost | **no**: failed completely after 10 days of training |
| No TRPV1 | not reported here | yes, blunted; still detected a 1 °C step |
| No TRPM2 | not reported here | yes, blunted; threshold 2 °C rather than 1 °C |
| No TRPV1, TRPA1 or TRPM3 (all three) | not reported here | yes, with reduced sensitivity |

Without TRPM8 the cool fibres lose their ongoing firing, so warming has nothing left to reduce. The warmth deficit arises not because TRPM8 is heat-gated, but because removing it removes the cool-afferent firing whose reduction is one of the two parts of the warmth signal. (TRPM8-null mice also could not report cooling with the larger plate.) Channel loss on the warm side, single or triple, blunted detection without abolishing it.

</details>

What does failing mean here? The TRPM8-null mice were trained for 10 days with warming stimuli from 32 to 42 °C, and their hit and false-alarm rates stayed similar throughout. That alone does not identify the cause, since a mouse can fail to learn for other reasons. Two observations support a sensory explanation: the same mutants readily learned to report mechanical stimuli to the forepaw, and in wild-type mice a TRPM8 antagonist applied to the forepaw skin reduced warm detection, which recovered by the next day.

Notice how lopsided that is. TRPM8-null mice still had warm-excited fibres, in proportions similar to wild type. On the simplest picture, each line an independent alarm sufficient to drive a report, those fibres should have let the mouse detect warming less well, not fail. In this task the retained warm-excited responses were not enough, and the results support an important role for reduced cool input. Warm-excited input may also improve sensitivity, but its separate contribution was not isolated. The result does not say how the brain combines the two lines: a cool-required, warm-augmenting readout is one possible model, and a weighted sum with a threshold or a nonlinear interaction could also fit.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/thermosensation/warmth-excitation-and-silence.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: paired schematic views of mouse forepaw skin show ongoing activity in a gold cool-sensitive C fibre before warming. Mice lacking TRPM8 lose the cool-fibre activity whose reduction is one of the two parts of that signal; the study did not identify the central circuit that integrates the two signals." />

Whatever the computation, something downstream must treat a fall in the cool line as evidence of warming. These recordings did not identify that circuit, so write the finding as reduced cool input. A named disinhibitory circuit, a central synapse that inverts the sign of the cool signal, is an extension of the data, not a finding in them.

Two cautions keep the question open.

**Isolated skin.** The ongoing cool-fibre firing was recorded in isolated skin in a 27 °C bath, not in a behaving mouse. So the step from "warming slows cool fibres in the dish" to "the mouse uses that slowing" is an inference that joins two preparations, not a recording made during the task.

**Another preparation.** Yarmolinsky and colleagues imaged **trigeminal ganglion** cell bodies (the sensory neurons of the face and mouth) in anaesthetized mice expressing GCaMP5 during oral thermal stimulation (Neuron, 2016). Warmth sensors were approximately 10% of the heat-responsive cells they sampled, and acute antagonism of TRPV1 eliminated the imaged warmth responses over 35 to 43 °C in that preparation. This is a statement about sampled trigeminal ganglia during oral stimulation, not about mouse skin elsewhere or about people. It sits awkwardly beside the forepaw study, in which TRPV1-null mice still detected a 1 °C warming step. The two also differ in method: one blocked TRPV1 acutely with a drug in an anaesthetized animal, the other removed the gene from the start, and a germline knockout leaves time for development to compensate. Whether innocuous warmth rests on TRPV1-positive warm fibres or on reduced TRPM8-dependent cool input is not settled, and the answer may differ between body sites.

## Three channels for one burn: redundancy, not a chain

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Mice lacking TRPV1 keep much of their response to painful heat, so other heat sensors had to exist. Joris Vriens, Thomas Voets and colleagues reported that TRPM3 contributes heat sensitivity to a subset of small mouse sensory neurons, and that TRPM3-deficient mice had impaired avoidance of noxious heat (Neuron, 2011). TRPA1 was a third candidate.

**Predict.** Suppose the three channels worked in series, each needed to pass the signal to the next. What would each single knockout do to acute withdrawal from noxious heat? Now suppose they work in parallel, each able to carry the signal on its own. What would single knockouts do, and what would it take to remove the reflex?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In series, each single knockout would abolish withdrawal, and single knockouts do not. In parallel, single knockouts would leave the reflex and only the loss of all three would remove it. Vandewauw and colleagues made that combinatorial test (Nature, 2018), pairing recordings from isolated mouse sensory neurons and from cutaneous C and Aδ afferents ex vivo with withdrawal behaviour in vivo. Mice lacking TRPV1, TRPM3 and TRPA1 together lacked acute withdrawal from noxious heat in the reported assays. The channels are functionally **redundant** for this assay: remove one and the others carry the signal, remove all three and the acute reflex has nothing left.

</details>

The same animals retained a preference for moderate temperatures, and students regularly mangle that result. It means that whatever supports temperature preference is not exhausted by these three channels. Acute nociceptive withdrawal and graded thermal preference are different behaviours with different requirements, and a mouse that cannot do the first can still do the second.

Set this beside the cool line: losing TRPM8 cost warmth because the cool line's resting firing depends on it, whereas no single heat channel is needed for the burn.

## Reading the lines: spinal cord, ganglia and insular cortex

Between the ganglion and the cortex sit the spinal dorsal horn and the thalamus, and the codes already differ there: in mouse spinal cord, heat-responding neurons encode absolute skin temperature without adapting and receive TRPV1-positive input, while cold-responding neurons adapt quickly and encode temperature change (Ran, Hoon and Chen, Nature Neuroscience, 2016).

Wang and colleagues imaged **dorsal root ganglion** (DRG) somata, the sensory neurons for the body below the head, with two-photon microscopy during hindpaw stimulation in anaesthetized mice (Cell Reports, 2018). Raising the temperature recruited more heat-responsive neurons, a graded population signal. Cooling responses were largely ungraded, which the authors read as suggesting combinatorial cold coding: which cells respond, rather than how hard they respond. This study and the trigeminal imaging above used different ganglia, different stimulus sites and different anaesthesia, so differences in tuning between them are not yet differences between body regions.

Centrally, Vestergaard, Carta, Güney and Poulet imaged cortex in awake mice during forepaw Peltier stimulation (Nature, 2023). Posterior insular cortex responded to both cooling and warming and was **somatotopically** arranged (neighbouring parts of the body mapped to neighbouring cortex), while primary somatosensory cortex (S1) showed a widefield response only to cooling, with only sparse warm-responding neurons under two-photon imaging.

**Predict.** They then silenced posterior insula optogenetically, by driving inhibitory neurons with light in VGAT-ChR2 mice (whose inhibitory neurons carry a light-gated channel, so light silences the surrounding cortex), during the detection task. If the insula takes part in reporting temperature, should silencing it hurt warming and cooling detection alike? Should a small cooling step and a large one suffer alike?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Neither. Inhibiting posterior insula reduced warming-detection hit rates at every warming amplitude tested. It reduced cooling hit rates for 0.5 to 2 °C steps, but spared the tested 10 °C step.

</details>

### Design problem

What can that pattern establish, and what does it not?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

It establishes a causal contribution to the behaviour tested, in this preparation, at those amplitudes; because light-driven inhibition is acute and its spread incomplete, it is not proof of strict necessity. It shows less about exclusivity: the authors also inhibited primary somatosensory cortex alone, with a smaller effect, and S1 together with the insula, but other subcortical routes may carry thermal information and were not silenced.

The spared 10 °C cooling step is the informative asymmetry, and it does not show that the insula contributes nothing there. Two readings fit it. A parallel route may be sufficient when the stimulus is large, so the insula is dispensable there. Or performance at 10 °C may be at a ceiling, where removing part of the signal leaves enough to cross threshold. To separate them, vary the amplitude finely around the spared step, and add a second task where the mouse must tell two large steps apart rather than simply detect one, so the ceiling is pushed away.

The control to ask for is motor, and here the authors supply it. Driving inhibitory neurons in cortex while an animal must lick can reduce licking for reasons that are not sensory. Before reading reduced hit rates as a sensory deficit, show that false-alarm rates and lick kinematics on unaffected trial types are unchanged, or that the same light leaves an equally demanding non-thermal detection task intact. Here, inhibiting posterior insula left licking for free water and licking in an acoustic detection task unchanged, and it increased, rather than reduced, licking on no-go trials. The weaker effect of S1 inhibition is a regional comparison, not the motor control.

And the scope: mouse cortex, awake, forepaw, with a licking report. No human thermal localisation follows.

</details>

## A different question: where is the sensor inside the channel?

Everything so far has been about lines. A separate question is what, inside a channel such as TRPV1, makes it open with heat. It is a different level of explanation, and still open.

Q10 is the ratio of a process's rates measured 10 °C apart, and a steep Q10 is often treated as the signature of a temperature sensor. It is not a channel constant. Apparent Q10 is read from a plot of the logarithm of current against inverse temperature. For TRPV1 that plot has a shallow limb at lower temperatures and a steep limb at higher temperatures, each with its own apparent Q10. For rat TRPV1 expressed in HEK293T cells, recorded at −60 mV, increasing activation by resiniferatoxin, a TRPV1 agonist, reduced the apparent high-temperature Q10 from approximately 38 to approximately 3, while the low-temperature value stayed near 1.7 (Kwon and colleagues, Nature Communications, 2022). The same protein gives different numbers depending on how much agonist has already opened it, so Q10 characterises a state and an interval.

One possible reason is simple: a channel that is already mostly open cannot open much more. In an illustrative two-state model whose thermal gating is held fixed, adding an agonist drops the Q10 of the open probability steeply while the Q10 of the opening equilibrium itself does not change (Going deeper). So saturation alone can produce a drop of this size, and the drop by itself does not show a change in the sensing mechanism.

Structures sharpen the question without closing it. The first near-atomic cryo-EM structure of TRPV1, in 2013, was of a minimal construct. Full-length channels in lipid nanodiscs, imaged across temperatures in 2021, support a global conformational change followed by outer-pore rearrangement during heat-dependent opening with capsaicin present. But those activated states needed an agonist, in constructs and lipids the experimenters chose, and the authors of the 2022 ensemble work cautioned that multiple cryo-EM classes may include off-pathway states rather than only sequential intermediates.

There is also a theoretical reason not to expect a tidy sensor module. Opening is an equilibrium between closed and open states. If the heat absorbed on opening were fixed, a plot of the logarithm of that equilibrium constant against inverse temperature would be a straight line. Clapham and Miller proposed that a large gating-associated change in heat capacity can account for steep TRP-channel temperature sensitivity. A heat-capacity change makes the heat absorbed on opening itself change with temperature, so the line curves. Because the argument is independent of mechanistic detail, it does not require the temperature dependence to sit in one domain; the steepness could be spread across the protein. On that view, looking for the sensor the way one looks for a capsaicin pocket may be the wrong search.

[Nociceptors and the sodium channels of pain](/lessons/nociceptors-and-sodium-channels/) takes up what happens after transduction, and [when pain persists](/lessons/chronic-pain/) takes up what happens when these systems are altered by injury.

## Going deeper

### How much can a fall in firing say?

This is an illustrative model, not data from the study. Count one fibre's spikes over T seconds and treat the counts as Poisson (variance equal to the mean). Define a count-separation index D = |μrest − μwarm| / √[(σrest² + σwarm²)/2], the separation in units of the root-mean-square standard deviation. It is computed like d′, but not from hits and false alarms, and it is not the most an ideal observer could extract. Complete silencing from a rate r gives D = rT / √(rT/2) = √(2rT) for r > 0; when both conditions are silent, D is set to 0 by continuity.

| Resting rate | D, one fibre falling silent (T = 1 s) |
|---|---|
| 0 Hz | 0.00 |
| 0.2 Hz | 0.63 |
| 2 Hz | 2.00 |
| 6 Hz | 3.46 |

The table assumes complete silencing at each selected resting rate. The study observed warming-induced suppression, including silencing for some stimuli (a 22 to 32 °C warming step, for example), but these rows are not measured responses at each rate, nor evidence that every small warming step silences the fibre. Partial falls follow the same logic: halving a 2 Hz rate gives D = (2 − 1) / √1.5 ≈ 0.8165 for one fibre, and 0.8165 × √20 ≈ 3.65 for 20 independent fibres responding the same way. If warming removes the same fraction f of spikes, D = f√(rT) / √(1 − f/2); a fixed absolute drop instead gives D = 0.82 for 2 to 1 Hz but 0.43 for 6 to 5 Hz in 1 s. In this model, a cool fibre anywhere in the 0.2 to 6 Hz range recorded in the 27 °C bath could carry information about warming if warming reduced its firing enough. A silent fibre that starts firing at rate r gives the same D as one falling from r to silence, so in that matched comparison ongoing firing buys signalling in both directions, not extra sensitivity.

### Why two independent alarms do not fit

Again illustrative, not measured. Take an optimal observer combining two cues that are Gaussian and independent given the stimulus, each with the same variance on warm and reference trials, and assume the retained cue's statistics do not change when the other is removed. Then d′ = √(d′cool² + d′warm²). The component values are invented, not measured sensitivities of the two populations: try cool and warm values of 1.2 and 1.2, 1.5 and 0.8, or 1.0 and 1.5. Wild type then sits at d′ 1.70, 1.70 or 1.80, and a TRPM8-null mouse that kept the warm line would keep d′ 1.2, 0.8 or 1.5: blunted, not abolished. The reported result is complete failure. That excludes only this example's assumptions, not every additive readout.

### A culture comparison

Preparation matters even in a dish. In dissociated DRG cultures from male mice aged 6 to 25 weeks, warmth-sensitive neurons were about 26 ± 9% of neurons in overnight cultures and about 6 ± 3% after three days (Abd El Hay and colleagues, eLife, 2025). That roughly fourfold difference between culture protocols, measured in different groups of animals, is enough to put two laboratories into apparent disagreement about the same biology.

### Insular imaging details

Vestergaard, Carta, Güney and Poulet imaged cortex in awake Thy1-GCaMP6s mice older than two months. The grand-average insular calcium response began at about 80 ms for cooling and about 320 ms for warming, a difference the authors compare with the short-latency Aδ-fibre cool responses and longer-latency C-fibre responses recorded in earlier peripheral work. The forepaw study did not establish that Aδ fibres mediate its small innocuous-cooling reports.

### Q10, saturation and apparent enthalpy

Q10 converts to an apparent activation enthalpy. Between 35 and 45 °C, a Q10 of 38 corresponds to about 296 kJ/mol (71 kcal/mol), a Q10 of 3 to about 90 kJ/mol, and a Q10 of 1.7 to about 43 kJ/mol.

The saturation argument, in an illustrative two-state model (not fitted to TRPV1): take a gating enthalpy of 400 kJ/mol with no heat-capacity change and K = 1 at 45 °C, so K = exp[−(ΔH/R)(1/T − 1/318.15 K)], hold that thermal gating fixed, and let an agonist multiply the opening equilibrium constant K by a temperature-independent factor, with Popen = factor × K / (1 + factor × K). Between 30 and 40 °C, the Q10 of K stays at about 159 throughout, but the Q10 of the open probability falls as the agonist factor rises.

| Agonist factor | Q10 of open probability, 30 to 40 °C |
|---|---|
| 1 (no agonist) | about 146 |
| 100 | about 17 |
| 1,000 | about 2.7 |
| 10,000 | about 1.2 |

The model does not explain the resiniferatoxin measurement, from 38 to 3, quantitatively; it shows only that a drop of that size can arise without a change in mechanism.

### TRPV1 structures

The 2013 structure, from Liao, Cao, Julius and Cheng (Nature), was a 3.4 Å cryo-EM structure of a minimal functional rat TRPV1 construct in amphipols, fourfold symmetric around a pathway formed by the S5 and S6 segments and the intervening pore loop. Agonist-bound structures from the same year suggested gating at both the outer pore and a lower hydrophobic gate. The 2021 nanodisc study, of full-length rat TRPV1, was by Kwon and colleagues (Nature Structural and Molecular Biology).

### The two-state model and heat capacity

In a two-state approximation, K = Popen/(1 − Popen) = exp(−ΔG°/RT). As an illustration, take a gating heat-capacity change of 20 kJ/mol/K, with ΔH and ΔS both zero at 45 °C. Then ln K at 5, 25, 45 and 60 °C is about 22.7, 5.2, 0 and 2.5: curved rather than straight in 1/T, and rising on both sides of the minimum. With no heat-capacity change, ln K would be flat. This toy case shows only the curvature. Its channel would be almost fully open at 25 °C, which TRPV1 is not; a heat-activated channel also needs a large positive gating enthalpy near its activation range.

### What would settle it

A single construct carried through three measurements at fixed voltage: apo, lightly agonist-occupied and strongly activated, with structures and Q10 taken on the same protein in the same lipid, plus mutations tested for effects on sensing, on coupling and on general gating separately. A mutation that shifts an activation threshold does not identify a sensor until those three are told apart.
