    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Children with Miller-Dieker syndrome have a severe lissencephaly, and many of them carry a
      deletion, sometimes too small to see under the microscope, in a region of chromosome 17
      called 17p13.3. In 1993 Reiner, working with David Ledbetter, William Dobyns, C. Thomas
      Caskey and colleagues, reported isolating a gene from that region and tying its deletion to
      the disease. They named it LIS1. Its protein carried repeats resembling those of the beta
      subunit of G proteins, now called WD40 repeats, but the sequence said nothing about how the

      that LIS1 is a subunit of an enzyme that breaks down platelet-activating factor. In 1995 N.
      Ronald Morris's laboratory found that a fungal gene needed to distribute nuclei, nudF,
      encodes a close relative of LIS1. Reiner's own laboratory, at the Weizmann Institute of
      Science, went on to study how LIS1 acts on microtubules. Her gene made human lissencephaly
      a problem that cell biologists could work on.

      Some families showed a pattern that pointed to the X chromosome. Affected boys had
      lissencephaly. Affected girls and women had a milder disorder in which a band of gray matter

    story: >-
      Primary microcephaly is recessive, so it is found most often where parents are related and
      can share a rare mutation inherited from a common ancestor. Woods, a clinical geneticist,
      studied such families. In them, the affected children share long stretches of identical
      chromosome, inherited from both sides, around the broken gene. Mapping those stretches

      both encoding centrosomal proteins. Together these results turned attention to the dividing
      progenitor, and to the spindle pole in particular, as a place where human brain size is set.

techniques:
  - name: "Homozygosity mapping"
    blurb: >-
      In families where the parents are related, a child with a recessive disease carries two
      identical copies of the chromosome stretch around the broken gene. Finding the stretches
      that all affected children share narrows the search to a small region, where candidate genes
      are then sequenced. This is how ASPM and other microcephaly genes were found.
  - name: "In utero electroporation with RNA interference"
    blurb: >-
      DNA encoding a short RNA that silences one gene, plus a fluorescent marker, is injected into
      the ventricle of an embryo in the uterus and driven into nearby progenitors by electric
      pulses. Days later, the position of the glowing cells shows whether they migrated. Live
      imaging of brain slices shows which step stalled, as in the LIS1 knockdown experiments.
  - name: "Stem-cell-derived progenitors and organoids"
    blurb: >-
      Human induced pluripotent stem cells are guided into cortical neural progenitors, grown flat
      or as three-dimensional organoids that form layered tissue. Exposing them to a virus or a
      drug shows which human cell types it acts on. Organoids usually lack blood vessels, immune cells and
      a placenta, so they show what can happen, not what happens in each pregnancy.
  - name: "Brain MRI and head circumference"
    blurb: >-
      Head circumference measured with a tape is the clinical test for microcephaly. Magnetic
      resonance imaging then shows the brain's structure: a small brain with a normal-looking cortex, a
      thick smooth cortex, or a band of gray matter in the white matter. The pattern points toward
      the step that failed and the genes to test.

      Microcephaly is defined by head circumference, commonly more than two standard deviations
      below the mean for age and sex, and severe beyond three. Fewer folds on a scan describes
      lissencephaly or a simplified pattern, not the definition of microcephaly. Neurons cannot be
      counted in a living child, so a neuron count is not a clinical test. A cortex with too few
      layers describes classic lissencephaly, a migration disorder, not microcephaly.
  - stem: "Where in a dividing progenitor are the proteins of most primary microcephaly genes, such as ASPM, WDR62 and CDK5RAP2, found?"
    choices:
      - "At the centrosomes and spindle poles of the cell"
      - "On the leading process of migrating young neurons"
      - "At synapses between newly connected cortical neurons"
      - "In the extracellular matrix along the pial surface"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      These proteins localize to centrosomes and spindle poles, which is why their loss is thought
      to change how and when progenitors divide. Synapses mostly form after neurons are made and in
      place, so a protein that acts only at synapses would not set neuron number. The extracellular matrix at the pial
      surface is involved in cobblestone malformations, a positioning defect, not primary
      microcephaly. Migration genes act in migrating neurons, at the leading process and around the nucleus, and
      their loss mainly misplaces neurons, though some, such as LIS1, also affect progenitor division.
  - stem: "A woman with subcortical band heterotopia carries a loss-of-function DCX mutation on one X chromosome. Her son inherits the X that carries the mutation. What does he most likely have?"
    choices:
      - "Band heterotopia, since he inherits his mother's mosaic"
      - "Lissencephaly, since every one of his neurons lacks DCX"
      - "Microcephaly, since DCX loss stops progenitor division"
      - "A normal cortex, since his Y chromosome has a DCX copy"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A male has one X, so if it carries the mutation no neuron makes working doublecortin and most
      neurons stall: lissencephaly. The mosaic is not inherited; it arises in each female embryo
      from random X inactivation, and a son with one X cannot form one. (Rare males do have band heterotopia, when a DCX mutation arises after fertilization in only some of their cells.) The Y chromosome has no
      DCX gene, so it cannot compensate. Doublecortin acts in migrating neurons, and its loss
      misplaces neurons rather than stopping progenitor division.
  - stem: "In Miller-Dieker syndrome a deletion removes LIS1 from one copy of chromosome 17, and the other copy is normal. Children with a small deletion or inactivating mutation of LIS1 alone, also on one copy, have isolated lissencephaly. What do these children tell you about LIS1?"
    choices:
      - "The normal copy is silenced by imprinting in neurons"
      - "The deleted copy makes a toxic protein that blocks dynein"
      - "LIS1 acts only in progenitors and never in neurons"
      - "Half the usual amount of LIS1 is too little for migration"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      One normal copy remains, yet migration fails, so half the usual dose is not enough; this is
      haploinsufficiency, and small mutations that inactivate one copy also cause lissencephaly. A
      deleted copy makes no protein at all, so it cannot make a toxic one. Nothing in the result
      points to imprinting, and lissencephaly from LIS1 is not limited to the copy from one parent.
      LIS1 acts in both progenitors and migrating neurons; knocking it down in rat embryos stalls
      the nuclei of migrating neurons and stops progenitor divisions at the ventricle.
  - stem: "Human forebrain organoids are exposed to Zika virus early in their growth. Most infected cells are SOX2-positive progenitors, and two weeks later the organoids are smaller with a thinner layer of neurons. Which conclusion do these results best support?"
    choices:
      - "Zika depletes progenitors, so that fewer neurons are made"
      - "Zika blocks migration, so neurons pile up near the ventricle"
      - "Zika causes microcephaly in every infected human pregnancy"
      - "Zika kills mature neurons, and progenitors replace them"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The infected cells are progenitors, and the later loss is of the neurons they would have
      made, which fits a depleted progenitor pool. Mature neurons were not the main infected cells,
      and progenitors replacing dead neurons would not leave a thinner neuronal layer. A pure migration
      block would leave neurons near the ventricle rather than shrink the organoid, and the infected cells here are progenitors, not migrating neurons.

      The mutant has 100 × 2^5 = 3,200 founders, and the normal embryo 100 × 2^7 = 12,800, so the

**Two children can both have a malformed cerebral cortex for opposite reasons. In one, the cortex is small, but its layers are in their usual order. In the other, the cortex is thick and its surface nearly smooth, and many of its neurons sit in the wrong place.**

The first child has primary microcephaly. The second has lissencephaly, a word that means "smooth brain". Each points to one step of building a cortex that failed, and to genes needed for that step. The Zika epidemic of 2015 and 2016 showed with unusual clarity that an infection can strike the same cells as a gene.

## Number or place

Radial glia, the progenitor cells that line the fluid-filled ventricle, divide to make more of themselves and then to make neurons ([that lesson](/lessons/radial-glia-progenitors/)). Each newborn neuron then moves outward along a radial glial fiber to its layer, later-born neurons passing the earlier ones ([that lesson](/lessons/radial-migration/)). Two jobs, so two broad kinds of failure.

- **Too few neurons made.** Progenitors stop dividing early or die, and the cortex is small, though its plan can look normal. This is microcephaly.
- **Neurons made but misplaced.** Neurons stall on the way to their layer. This gives lissencephaly, or a heterotopia: a cluster of neurons sitting where they do not belong.

The first job has a property the second lacks: it multiplies. Early in development, progenitors divide symmetrically, each division making two progenitors ([that lesson](/lessons/asymmetric-division/)). Later they switch to divisions that make neurons. Each round of symmetric division doubles the pool:

> **progenitors at the switch = starting progenitors × 2^n, where n is the number of symmetric rounds**

Start from 100 progenitors.

| Symmetric rounds before the switch | Progenitors at the switch | Compared with normal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 7 (normal, in this model) | 12,800 | 1 |
| 6 (switch one round early) | 6,400 | ½ |
| 5 (switch two rounds early) | 3,200 | ¼ |

Switching one round early halves the pool, and if every progenitor present at the switch then makes the same number of neurons, it halves the neurons too. Real defects need not act on every progenitor at once, so try a second model, with a small, steady bias.

**Predict.** Suppose each division has a one-in-ten chance of being neurogenic for that round only: it gives one progenitor and one neuron instead of two progenitors, and that progenitor can divide symmetrically again next round. (A permanent switch would cost more.) Let the pool run ten rounds, longer than the table's seven. Is the pool then about 90 percent of normal, about 60 percent, or about 10 percent?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

About 60 percent. Each neurogenic division is one progenitor short, so on average a division gives 1.9 progenitors instead of 2, 5 percent short of doubling. Each round the pool reaches only 0.95 of what it should, and over ten rounds that is 0.95^10, about 0.60; simulations that draw each division at random give this on average, with single runs scattered around it. A fault in one division in ten has cost two-fifths of the pool, and the loss grows with every further round. (Those divisions also made a few neurons early, so total neuron output falls a little less than the pool.)

</details>

The second job does not multiply: a neuron that stalls is one neuron in the wrong place, and it takes no descendants with it.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Size counts the doublings; layering records the journey. A cortex's size reflects how many neurons its progenitors made and how many survived, and because the progenitor pool doubles, a small fault that recurs in each round compounds into a large loss. Its layering reports whether those neurons arrived. So a small cortex with orderly layers points first to a problem in making neurons (or in their early loss), and a cortex of near-normal size with disordered layers points to migration.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which malformations.** Those that arise while neurons are being made and placed, from early gestation through much of fetal life. It does not cover destruction of tissue already built, which can overlap with building (much of congenital Zika syndrome, or injury from too little oxygen), or disorders of folding and wiring.
- **Which measure.** "Size" means cortical volume, which tracks neuron number only roughly, since neuron density, glia and white matter also vary. Head circumference is the bedside stand-in, and a rough one.
- **A first question, not a diagnosis.** Some genes act at both steps, and Zika also destroys tissue already built; these cases are collected in [Where the sort breaks](#where-the-sort-breaks).

The rule picks up where [the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/) left off: survival can never raise neuron number above the number made. This lesson is about where the number made is set: survival factors trim the cohort, and the divisions of the progenitor pool decide how large that cohort can be.

## Too few: a tape measure and a spindle

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

### What a tape measure can tell you

Microcephaly is defined at the bedside, not under a microscope. It means a head circumference well below the average for the child's age and sex, measured from just above the eyebrows and ears around to the most prominent point at the back of the head. The World Health Organization uses a cutoff of more than two standard deviations (SD) below the mean, and calls more than three severe. For a family, this number is often the first sign that something is wrong.

How much smaller is the brain at those cutoffs? Less than you might expect. Make a rough model: treat the head as a sphere, so that head circumference grows as the cube root of brain volume, and ignore the scalp, skull and fluid that the tape also measures. Take a term newborn's head as about 34.5 cm around, with an SD of about 1.2 cm.

| Head circumference, in this model | Brain volume compared with average | Volume halvings (not counted cell divisions) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 SD below the mean (the WHO cutoff), 32.1 cm | about 0.80 | about a third of one |
| 3 SD below (severe), 30.9 cm | about 0.72 | about half of one |
| volume halved, 27.4 cm (about 6 SD below) | 0.50 | one |

So on this model a head at the cutoff holds a brain only about one-fifth smaller than average. Children with *ASPM* mutations often have far smaller heads, many SD below the mean for their age. Still, the model only compares volumes; it counts neither neurons nor lost divisions.

The cutoff also catches some healthy babies. If head sizes follow a bell curve, about 2 in 100 lie more than 2 SD below the mean. A small head is a sign, not a diagnosis, and it has many possible causes: genes; congenital infections, among them cytomegalovirus (CMV), rubella, toxoplasmosis and Zika; alcohol; and injury around birth. This lesson follows one group of genes and one virus, which act on the same cells.

### Primary microcephaly: genes at the spindle pole

Primary microcephaly (MCPH) is present at birth and inherited as a recessive trait: a child is affected when both copies of a gene are broken. That is why it is found most often in families in which the parents are related. The brain is small, and the cerebral cortex is reduced most. In most forms its layers are broadly normal, though its folds are often simpler. Some genes, including *WDR62*, can also disturb the structure of the cortex. Intellectual disability is common and varies in degree.

More than twenty MCPH genes are now known. In many studied populations the commonest cause is *ASPM*, which Woods and colleagues linked to the disease in 2002. Its name, *abnormal spindle-like, microcephaly associated*, comes from the fly gene *abnormal spindle*, which flies need to organize the spindle in dividing neural cells. Others include *MCPH1* (microcephalin), *CDK5RAP2* and *WDR62*. Most of their proteins sit at the centrosome or the spindle pole. The centrosome is the cell's main microtubule-organizing center. At mitosis the two centrosomes form the poles of the spindle that pulls the chromosomes apart.

### Why the brain?

Why would a spindle protein matter more for the brain than for other organs? One leading idea has two parts.

**Radial glia are unusual dividers.** A radial glial cell is tall and thin, and only a small patch of its surface faces the ventricle. In early divisions, the angle of the cleavage plane affects how that apical patch is shared. A tilted spindle can hand most or all of it to one daughter, and that can change what the daughters become. The patch does not decide fate on its own: a daughter without it can still divide, as a progenitor away from the ventricle. An experiment in mouse embryos tested the spindle idea: knocking down *Aspm* tilted the plane of progenitor division and shifted divisions away from the symmetric, progenitor-making kind (Fish and colleagues, 2006). A defect in how long mitosis takes can act another way: in mouse progenitors, delayed mitosis can trigger a death that depends on p53, a protein that senses stress and can halt or kill the cell.

**The cortex grows through many rounds.** If a fault tips progenitors toward the switch or toward death round after round, it compounds, as in the prediction above. Either history leaves fewer neurons, so a small cortex alone cannot say which happened. The neurotrophic lesson's question, never made or made and lost, applies here too.

The brain is the most exposed organ, not the only one. Some of the same centrosome genes, such as *CENPJ* and *CEP152*, when mutated differently, shrink the whole body as well as the brain, in Seckel syndrome.

**Predict.** A child with no working *ASPM* has a markedly small brain, but mice lacking *Aspm* have only a mild reduction. Ferrets have a folded cortex, rich in outer radial glia: progenitors that have left the ventricle but keep dividing. Knock out *Aspm* in a ferret. Should its cortex shrink less than a mouse's, about as much, or more?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

More, and by a lot. In ferrets an *Aspm* knockout reduced the cortex much more than in mice (Johnson and colleagues, 2018), which the authors attributed to ventricular radial glia being displaced from the ventricle too early.

A second, compatible reading is that a cortex that grows through more rounds of division, and through more kinds of progenitor, is more exposed to a small defect in each division. Take the same fault as before, one division in ten coming out neurogenic. After 5 rounds the pool is about 77 percent of normal, after 10 rounds about 60 percent, and after 15 rounds about 46 percent. These round counts are illustrative, not measured values for either species, and the reading remains a hypothesis. [That lesson](/lessons/human-brain-expansion/) takes up how the human cortex got large.

</details>

## Zika: how do you show that a virus causes a birth defect?

You cannot infect pregnant women to find out.

Zika is a flavivirus, a relative of dengue, spread mainly by *Aedes* mosquitoes. It was first isolated in 1947, from a monkey in the Zika Forest of Uganda, and for decades it was known for mild illness. In 2015 it spread through Brazil. Late that year, doctors in the country's northeast reported many more newborns with microcephaly than usual.

The case was built from three kinds of evidence pointing the same way.

1. **Epidemiology.** The rise in microcephaly followed the virus in time and place. A look back at a 2013 to 2014 outbreak in French Polynesia found an increase in microcephaly there too, with timing that fit infection in the first trimester.
2. **Pathology.** In 2016 Mlakar and colleagues reported Zika virus RNA and virus particles in the brain of a fetus whose mother had been infected in Brazil during pregnancy. The brain was small, with calcifications.
3. **Laboratory models.** In March 2016 the laboratories of Guo-li Ming, Hongjun Song and Hengli Tang reported that Zika infects human cortical neural progenitors grown from stem cells far more readily than it infects the stem cells or immature neurons, that it kills some of the progenitors and slows the growth of the rest. Stevens Rehen's group in Rio de Janeiro reported that the virus stunts human neurospheres and brain organoids, tissue grown in three dimensions from stem cells ([that lesson](/lessons/organoids/)).

| Evidence | What it shows | What it cannot show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epidemiology | association in time and place | cause on its own (other factors, or closer checking of newborns, can raise counts), or mechanism |
| Pathology | virus inside a damaged fetal brain | on its own, that the virus did the damage, or how often it happens |
| Cultured progenitors and organoids | which human cells the virus can infect and harm | the risk in a pregnancy |

**Predict.** The March 2016 progenitor study used MR766, a strain isolated in Uganda decades earlier and passaged in the laboratory ever since, not the virus spreading through Brazil. What would a skeptic say about the result, and what would answer the objection?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

An old laboratory strain might not behave like the epidemic virus, so its effect on progenitors might not explain the epidemic. The answer is to repeat the work with epidemic strains, and later work with them, in organoids and in pregnant mice, agreed.

</details>

In April 2016 Sonja Rasmussen and colleagues at the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention weighed this evidence against formal criteria for calling an agent a cause of birth defects. They concluded that Zika virus causes microcephaly and other serious brain defects. The pattern in affected infants, now called congenital Zika syndrome, includes severe microcephaly, calcium deposits in the brain, damage to the eye and stiff joints.

Two cautions. Much of the mechanism comes from cultured cells, organoids and mice, so it shows what the virus can do to human progenitors, not what it does in each fetus. And most infections in pregnancy do not produce microcephaly; the risk appears highest when infection comes early.

## In the wrong place: lissencephaly and the double cortex

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Lissencephaly is the other category. A normal human cortex is folded and has six layers. In classic lissencephaly it is thick, has few or no folds, and is organized into about four crude layers. Affected children usually have severe intellectual disability and epilepsy. It is classed as a failure of migration: the neurons were made, but they failed to travel.

The rule predicts a near-normal size, and in isolated lissencephaly the head is usually near normal in size at birth, though head growth often slows afterwards, so the rule fits best early. The geometry allows it. Volume is area times thickness. A smooth cortex about four to five times as thick as normal (roughly 10 to 15 mm, against 2.5 to 3 mm) needs only a quarter to a fifth of the surface area to hold the same volume of gray matter. This is only a plausibility check, since the density of neurons in the malformed cortex is not normal.

The first two genes for classic lissencephaly to be found through affected children were *LIS1* and *DCX*, the gene for doublecortin.

### LIS1: half a dose is not enough

Children with Miller-Dieker syndrome have a severe lissencephaly with distinctive facial features, and most are missing a small piece of one chromosome 17, in a region called 17p13.3. In 1993 Orly Reiner, David Ledbetter, William Dobyns and colleagues isolated a gene from that region and named it *LIS1*.

The deletion removes one copy of *LIS1*, and the other chromosome 17 is normal. Children who have lost or broken only *LIS1*, on one copy, still have lissencephaly, called isolated lissencephaly. So half the usual amount of LIS1 is too little for neurons to migrate, a state called haploinsufficiency. (Miller-Dieker is more severe: its larger deletion also removes neighbouring genes, including the one for 14-3-3ε, that are thought to add to the severity.)

The sequence did not say what LIS1 does. The clue came from a mould. *Aspergillus nidulans* grows as long filaments that must space their nuclei along their length, and mutants called *nud*, for nuclear distribution, leave the nuclei bunched. In 1995 one of them, *nudF*, turned out to encode a protein 42 percent identical to human LIS1. Other *nud* genes encode parts of cytoplasmic dynein, a motor that walks along microtubules toward their minus ends. In mammalian cells LIS1 binds dynein, together with the partner proteins NDE1 and NDEL1, and helps the motor hold on to microtubules and assemble into working complexes. A gene found through children with smooth brains had led to a motor that moves nuclei.

Why would a nucleus motor matter for migration? A migrating neuron moves in two steps. First it extends a leading process along the radial glial fiber, and its centrosome moves forward into the process. Then dynein, pulling along microtubules that run back from the centrosome to the nucleus, draws the nucleus up toward it, with help from the motor protein myosin.

**Predict.** Knock down LIS1 in the embryonic cortex, in progenitors and the neurons they are about to make. Which should fail: extending the leading process, moving the nucleus, or something earlier?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Tsai and colleagues did this in 2005, in the cortex of embryonic rats. Most new cells never started: they piled up near the ventricle in a many-branched, multipolar state instead of becoming bipolar migrating neurons. In the few that did become bipolar, the leading process kept extending, but the cell body and nucleus barely moved after it. In those cells the nucleus was the part that failed, and a nucleus that cannot be moved leaves its neuron short of its layer.

</details>

<LessonFigure src="/figures/microcephaly-and-malformations/lis1-nucleus-stalls.webp" width={1200} height={752} alt="AI-generated illustration: two schematic tracks compare a normal migrating neuron with a rare LIS1-knockdown bipolar neuron. Both have extended a long leading process, but the normal nucleus has moved far up while the knockdown nucleus is still near the bottom. An inset shows LIS1 touching dynein, NDE1 and NDEL1. Most knockdown cells, which stall as multipolar cells, are not drawn." caption="In the rare LIS1-knockdown cells that became bipolar, the leading process kept extending while the nucleus stayed behind. Most knockdown cells (not drawn) stalled as multipolar cells near the ventricle. The inset shows LIS1 binding dynein and the NudE-family proteins NDE1 and NDEL1." />

The same experiment showed something the "wrong place" label hides. Before a progenitor divides, dynein also carries its nucleus down to the ventricular surface. With LIS1 knocked down, progenitor nuclei stopped their up-and-down movement in the ventricular zone, and divisions at the ventricle were blocked. So LIS1 loss can also reduce how many neurons are made: LIS1 belongs mainly in the "wrong place" column, with one foot in "too few". The mechanics of nuclear movement are in [that lesson](/lessons/radial-migration/).

### Doublecortin: one mutation, two brains

Some families showed a striking pattern. Affected boys had lissencephaly, while their mothers or sisters had a milder disorder, subcortical band heterotopia, or "double cortex": a band of gray matter lying in the white matter beneath a nearly normal cortex. That pointed to a gene on the X chromosome. In 1998 two groups reported it in the same issue of *Cell*: Joseph Gleeson, Christopher Walsh and colleagues in Boston, and Vincent des Portes, Jamel Chelly and colleagues in Paris. The gene, *DCX*, encodes doublecortin, a protein that binds microtubules and stabilizes them in migrating neurons. It is not a motor; it is thought mainly to steady the tracks the nucleus travels on.

A male has one X chromosome. A female has two, and early in development each of her cells silences one of them at random, a process called X inactivation. The choice is inherited by that cell's descendants, so her cortex is a mosaic of neurons with one X or the other active.

**Predict.** A boy with lissencephaly and his mother with band heterotopia carry the same loss-of-function *DCX* mutation. Using X inactivation, explain both brains.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

He has one X. If it carries the mutation, no neuron makes working doublecortin, and most neurons stall: lissencephaly.

In her mosaic cortex, neurons with the normal X active migrate to the cortex. Neurons with the mutant X active stall partway and gather into a band beneath the cortex. On this reading each neuron follows its own active X, so doublecortin acts mainly within the migrating cell. An experiment in rats supports it: when DCX was silenced in some cortical neurons of embryonic rats, those neurons stalled and formed such a band.

One caveat. In those rat embryos some neighbouring neurons, with DCX intact, were also disturbed, and the sorting has not been shown neuron by neuron in human tissue.

</details>

## Where the sort breaks

Real cases do not always sort cleanly: some genes and infections affect both jobs. Here are those exceptions in one place.

| Case | Where the rule puts it | What else it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *LIS1* | wrong place: migrating neurons stall | also blocks progenitor divisions at the ventricle |
| *WDR62* | too few: a primary microcephaly gene | can also disturb the structure of the cortex |
| Zika | too few: it attacks progenitors, so fewer neurons are made | also infects young neurons and other brain cells; affected brains often show destruction, such as calcifications and a partly collapsed skull, and sometimes abnormal folding or misplaced gray matter |
| Microlissencephaly (for example, mutations in both copies of *NDE1*) | too few: the brain is small | the cortex is also smooth |

Congenital Zika syndrome is partly a failure of building and partly damage to what was built, and a cortex made and then damaged is small because tissue was lost, not because too few neurons were made.

### Design problem

You study two mouse lines. In the first, cortical progenitors switch early from symmetric proliferative divisions to neuron-producing divisions. In the second, neurons are born on schedule but cannot move their nuclei along radial glia. Predict the brain size and the layering of the cortex in each line, and say which human condition each resembles.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

In the first line the pool of founder progenitors stops doubling too soon. With fewer founders there are fewer neurons, and since the pool grows exponentially, a switch even one or two rounds early cuts neuron number by a half or more, if each founder goes on to make its usual number of neurons. The neurons that are made still migrate normally and arrive inside-out, so the cortex is small with roughly normal layers. That resembles primary microcephaly.

In the second line, if the progenitors are untouched, close to the normal number of neurons is made. But neurons whose nuclei cannot follow their leading process stall in the ventricular zone, the intermediate zone or partway through the cortical plate. The cortex has a near-normal neuron count but disordered layers, with many neurons left near the ventricle. That resembles classic lissencephaly. Real LIS1 loss is messier, because LIS1 also acts in dividing progenitors.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The Zika emergency

Brazil declared a national public health emergency in November 2015, and the World Health Organization declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on 1 February 2016.

### Two maps

The two lissencephaly genes leave different maps. Lissencephaly from *LIS1* is usually worse toward the back of the brain, and from *DCX* toward the front.

### More genes of migration

Other genes of migration include *CDK5*. Mutations in both copies of *RELN*, the human gene for Reelin, cause lissencephaly with a small cerebellum, a human counterpart of the reeler mouse ([that lesson](/lessons/reelin-and-reeler/)). The word "classic" in the main text matters: a gene for cobblestone lissencephaly, fukutin, was also reported in 1998, and cobblestone malformations are a different, pial-surface defect.

### Open questions

Why many mouse models of MCPH genes have milder microcephaly than patients is not settled, nor whether the mechanism is the same for every gene. For Zika, the entry receptor on human progenitors and the relative weight of direct killing, innate immune signaling and placental damage in the human fetus are still debated.
