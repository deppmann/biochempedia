    story: >-
      A sound straight ahead reaches both ears together; a sound off to one side reaches the
      nearer ear a fraction of a millisecond sooner. Jeffress asked what kind of neural circuit
      could read such a small difference. In a short paper in the Journal of Comparative and
      Physiological Psychology in 1948 he proposed one. Axons from the two ears run in opposite
      directions along a row of neurons, so that each neuron sits at a different extra travel
      time from each side. A neuron fires best only when inputs from both ears arrive together,
      making it a coincidence detector, so the neuron that fires most marks the delay that the

      compares activity in the two hemispheres. Jeffress's model gave the field a precise
      hypothesis to test, species by species.

**One kind of cell, the hair cell, tells you that a violin is playing an A, that the violin is to your left, and that your head has just turned toward it.**

The inner ear has two parts that share this cell. The **cochlea**, a coiled tube, analyses sound. The **vestibular organs**, three semicircular canals and two otolith organs, sense head motion and gravity. In both, a hair cell turns a tiny movement into a graded voltage; what differs is the mechanics around the cell and the circuits that read it. [Sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/) covers transduction and receptive fields in general.

## The hair bundle: force to current

On top of each hair cell sits a **hair bundle**, rows of stiff **stereocilia** graded in height like a staircase. Many vestibular hair cells also have a true cilium, the **kinocilium**, beside the tallest row; mammalian cochlear hair cells lose it as they mature. Fine **tip links** run from the tip of each shorter stereocilium to the side of its taller neighbour. Each link is made of cadherin 23 and protocadherin 15, whose ends overlap in a handshake (Sotomayor and colleagues, 2012, crystal structures).

Push the bundle toward its tallest stereocilia and the tip links tighten and pull open **mechanically gated cation channels**. Calcium imaging in neonatal rat cochlear hair cells implies that the working channels sit at the lower end of each link (Beurg and colleagues, 2009). Push the other way and the links slacken, the channels that were open at rest close, and the cell hyperpolarizes. The gating machinery belongs to [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/); in short, mutating a protein called TMC1 in mouse hair cells changed how ions pass, making TMC1 part of the pore (Pan and colleagues, 2018), though the full native complex is not solved.

Hudspeth and Corey (1977) measured this relation in hair cells of the bullfrog sacculus in vitro, recording intracellularly while moving single bundles. Deflection toward the kinocilium gave the depolarizing response. Voltages were graded, up to about 15 mV, with a peak slope near 20 mV per micrometre. Those numbers belong to that frog preparation, not to the human cochlea.

What carries the current? The top of the hair cell faces **endolymph**, a fluid rich in K+ that, in the cochlea, is held positive (by roughly +80 mV in mammals) by the **endocochlear potential** (vestibular endolymph is only slightly positive); making it is a transport problem ([membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/)). The **driving force** on an ion is the membrane potential minus that ion's equilibrium potential ([the resting membrane](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/)). In round illustrative numbers, suppose the cell sits at −50 mV and the endolymph at +80 mV, with K+ about equally concentrated on both sides of the top membrane. Equal concentrations mean K+ would be at equilibrium only with no voltage across that membrane, so the whole 130 mV pushes K+ inward. Opening channels lets K+ in, and the cell depolarizes, the reverse of most neurons, where K+ leaves.

The depolarization opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels at the base. Mouse inner hair cells lacking the L-type channel CaV1.3 showed only marginal depolarization-evoked exocytosis (Brandt, Striessnig and Moser, 2003), so Ca2+ entry drives release, as at other synapses ([synaptic transmission](/lessons/synaptic-transmission/)). The release site is a **ribbon synapse**. Mature mammalian cochlear hair cells, once lost, are not replaced ([injury and regeneration](/lessons/injury-and-regeneration/)).

## The cochlea as a frequency map, and its amplifier

Cochlear hair cells sit on the **basilar membrane**, which runs the length of the coil. Near the **base**, where sound enters, the membrane is narrow and stiff; toward the **apex** it is wider and more compliant. Sound sets up a **travelling wave** that moves from base to apex, grows until it reaches the place whose stiffness suits its frequency, peaks, and dies away. High frequencies peak near the base, low frequencies near the apex. This map of frequency onto position is **tonotopy**, and it is kept, at least as a coarse gradient, through the main relays up to primary auditory cortex. Georg von Békésy measured these waves, many in human cadaver cochleas, and received the 1961 Nobel Prize. His peaks were far broader than a living ear's tuning.

The difference is an amplifier. One row of **inner hair cells** supplies most of the auditory nerve's afferent fibres. Three rows of **outer hair cells** receive few afferents but change length when their voltage changes, **electromotility**, reported in isolated guinea-pig outer hair cells by Brownell and colleagues (1985). The motor is the membrane protein **prestin**, driven by voltage, not by ATP. On each cycle of a sound the outer hair cells push on the membrane in step, adding energy at the peak, which sharpens tuning and raises sensitivity.

Greenwood's function (1990) gives the frequency that peaks at each place in the human cochlea:

> **f = 165.4 × (10^(2.1x) − 0.88), with f in Hz and x the fraction of length measured from the apex**

**Worked problem: placing two tones.** Where do 1,000 Hz and 4,000 Hz peak? Assume a 35 mm cochlea.

*Step 1: rearrange.* x = log10(f / 165.4 + 0.88) / 2.1.

*Step 2: 1,000 Hz.* 1000 / 165.4 = 6.046; plus 0.88 is 6.926; log10 is 0.840; divided by 2.1, x = 0.400. That is 0.400 x 35 mm = 14.0 mm from the apex, 21.0 mm from the base.

*Step 3: 4,000 Hz.* 4000 / 165.4 = 24.18; plus 0.88 is 25.06; log10 is 1.399; divided by 2.1, x = 0.666. That is 23.3 mm from the apex, 11.7 mm from the base.

*Step 4: compare.* Two octaves span 23.3 − 14.0 = 9.3 mm, and the higher tone sits nearer the base.

## Two ears, two cues

The auditory nerve ends in the **cochlear nucleus** on the same side (ipsilateral; the other side is contralateral). Above that, the pathway is bilateral. Cochlear-nucleus neurons project to the **superior olivary complex** on both sides; fibres ascend to the **inferior colliculus** in the midbrain, which supplies most of the input to the **medial geniculate nucleus** of the thalamus, which relays to auditory cortex in the temporal lobe ([the map of the nervous system](/lessons/nervous-system-map/) places these levels). So deafness in one ear points to the ear, the auditory nerve or the cochlear nucleus: a lesion higher up leaves each ear represented on both sides.

The two sides meet to locate sounds. An **interaural time difference (ITD)** is the difference in arrival time between the ears. An **interaural level difference (ILD)** is the difference in sound level (intensity) between the ears, made because the head casts a sound shadow.

**Worked problem: how large is an ITD?** Treat the ears as two points 0.20 m apart, take sound speed as 343 m/s, and put a distant source at angle theta from straight ahead. The extra path to the far ear is d x sin(theta), so

> **ITD = d x sin(theta) / c**

*At 30 degrees:* 0.20 m x 0.5 / 343 m/s = 0.000292 s, or 292 microseconds.

*At 90 degrees:* 0.20 m x 1.0 / 343 m/s = 583 microseconds.

The model ignores the path around the head, so it underestimates real ITDs; the point is the scale.

Neurons in the **medial superior olive (MSO)** are excited by both cochlear nuclei and fire most when the two inputs arrive together. They handle ITDs, mainly at low frequencies, where spikes can lock to the phase of the wave, firing near the same point in the cycle, though not on every cycle. Jeffress (1948) proposed that axons of graded length act as delay lines, so each coincidence detector is tuned to one ITD. Bird brainstem circuits, such as the barn owl's, are commonly cited as fitting that scheme; in the guinea pig, McAlpine, Jiang and Palmer (2001) recorded inferior-colliculus neurons and argued for a population code compared across the two hemispheres. In the gerbil MSO itself, Brand and colleagues (2002) found no sign of a delay-line map and argued that precisely timed inhibition shapes ITD tuning. How mammals compute ITDs is still debated.

Neurons in the **lateral superior olive (LSO)** are excited by the ear on their own side and inhibited, through a relay in the trapezoid body, by the other ear, so their rate reports which ear is louder. They handle ILDs, mainly at high frequencies, where the head shadow is large. Time at low frequencies and level at high frequencies is a tendency, not a wall.

Neither cue tells above from below. The folds of the **pinna** filter sound differently by elevation, and the brain learns those spectral cues: when Hofman and colleagues (1998) reshaped adults' pinnae with moulds, elevation judgments were poor at first and then progressively recovered with the moulds in place.

## Canals, otoliths and the VOR

Vestibular hair cells use the same transduction with different mechanics. Each **semicircular canal** is a fluid-filled ring whose swelling, the ampulla, holds hair bundles in a gelatinous flap, the **cupula**. When the head rotates, endolymph lags by inertia, bends the cupula and deflects the bundles, so canals report rotation. Canals on opposite sides pair in one plane, and a turn excites one partner while inhibiting the other.

The **otolith organs** sense linear acceleration and gravity. In the **utricle**, roughly horizontal when you stand, and the **saccule**, roughly vertical, the bundles are capped by a membrane loaded with calcium carbonate crystals. Tilting or accelerating the head shears that heavier membrane across the hair cells, and the two can give the same shear, so otolith afferents alone are ambiguous. In rhesus monkeys, Angelaki and colleagues (1999) found that after canal inactivation, eye responses to translation followed the ambiguous otolith signal, supporting the view that the brain combines canal and otolith signals to tell tilt from translation.

One of the canals' main jobs is driving the **vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR)**, which keeps the eyes still in the world while the head moves. For a head turn to the left:

1. The left horizontal canal is excited; the right is inhibited.
2. The left vestibular nucleus excites the **right abducens nucleus**, whose motor neurons drive the right lateral rectus and turn the right eye rightward.
3. Abducens interneurons cross and run up the **medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF)** to the **left oculomotor nucleus**, which drives the left medial rectus and turns the left eye rightward.

Both eyes move right as the head turns left. The reflex's **gain** is eye velocity divided by head velocity. In a made-up case, a head turning at 100 degrees per second with eyes counter-rotating at 80 degrees per second gives a gain of 0.80, and the image slips across the retina at 20 degrees per second. The [cerebellum](/lessons/cerebellum/) corrects a wrong gain. Damage to one labyrinth (the vestibular organs of one inner ear) unbalances the pair: the healthy side's resting firing is no longer matched, and the brain reads the imbalance as rotation, one mechanism of vertigo.

### Design problem

Mice lacking prestin lose sensitivity and sharp cochlear tuning. A reviewer says this does not show that outer-hair-cell electromotility is the cochlear amplifier. Why is the reviewer right, and what experiment answers the objection?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Deleting a protein removes everything it does. Knockout outer hair cells had reduced stiffness, and a floppier, shorter or dying cell would also weaken amplification. So the knockout shows prestin is needed, not that its motor action is.

Remove the motor and keep the rest. Dallos and colleagues (2008) made knock-in mice whose prestin carried two substitutions, V499G and Y501H, that shifted the motor's voltage range and left little electromotility near the resting voltage, and checked that outer-hair-cell mechanics and mechanoelectric transduction were preserved. Those mice still lost cochlear amplification, supporting a requirement for prestin-based motility.

Controls to ask for: outer-hair-cell transduction currents; counts of surviving hair cells at the ages tested; thresholds across frequencies, not one tone. Limits: this is the mouse, and the result does not exclude an additional active process in the hair bundle.
