## The big idea

After a hard rain, look at a bare patch of dirt next to a lawn. The bare patch often has little channels and a fan of mud at the bottom. The grass beside it looks the same as before.

That difference is the whole activity. Rain and wind move soil from one place to another. This is called **erosion**. Plants slow it down. Leaves and stems catch falling drops before they hit the ground. Roots grip the soil and help water soak in instead of running off.

Erosion can be fast, like a flood or mudslide, or slow, like a beach wearing away a little each year. Soil forms very slowly, so it is worth keeping. Muddy runoff also carries soil into streams and, in much of Virginia, the Chesapeake Bay.

Virginia’s standards ask grade 2 children to model how plants reduce the effects of wind and water. Grade 3 and 4 children go further and plan ways to cut erosion in their schoolyard or neighbourhood.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time. You do all the cutting with scissors or a craft knife. If you use foil pans, fold over or tape the cut edges of the notch so they are not sharp.
- Wet trays of soil are heavy. You lift and carry them, and work outdoors or on a tarp so floors stay dry.
- Use bagged topsoil or garden-centre sod. Do not dig soil or grass up from the ground: roadsides and treated lawns can hold oil and chemicals, and in Virginia it is safest to make a free call to 811 before any digging.
- Cover any cuts or scrapes with a bandage, or wear gloves, before handling soil. Open bags of soil outdoors, and keep faces away from the dust. A child with asthma or allergies should stand well back while you open and pour the soil. Wash hands with soap and water afterwards.
- Some grass seed is treated with fungicide or insecticide and is often dyed a bright colour. Buy plain, untreated seed (read the bag), keep any seed away from toddlers and pets, and never eat soil or seed.
- Stay with your child on the walk too. Go only after the rain has stopped and at least 30 minutes after the last thunder, keep to your yard, schoolyard or a park path away from roads, and keep away from storm drains, steep crumbling banks, and ditches or creeks with running water. Never walk through floodwater. Watch for poison ivy, and check everyone for ticks afterwards.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Grow the grass (2 to 3 weeks ahead).** Fill one container with damp soil and sprinkle grass seed thickly on top. Perennial ryegrass is one of the fastest lawn grasses to sprout. Keep it moist in a sunny spot. Ryegrass usually sprouts in 3 to 10 days; some grasses, such as Kentucky bluegrass, can take up to a month. Or skip this step and press a piece of sod into the container on the day.
2. **Set up the slopes.** Fill the second container with the same soil to the same depth, about 5 cm. You cut a small V notch at the low end of each container so water can pour out; a child does not use the scissors or craft knife. Set the notched end of each just over the edge of a table, step or low wall, and raise the other end on books, both to the same height. Set a clear cup below each notch.
3. **Predict.** Ask your child, "Which cup will get more mud? Which will get more water?" Write both guesses down before any water is poured.
4. **Gentle rain.** Measure 2 cups (about 500 mL) of water into the watering can. Pour it slowly over the high end of the bare tray, from about 20 cm up, over about 30 seconds. Do the same for the grass tray. Use the same amount, height and speed for both.
5. **Compare the cups.** Hold both cups up to the light. The bare tray’s cup is usually brown and cloudy. The grass tray’s cup is usually much clearer, and often has less water in it. Let the cups sit 10 minutes, then measure the layer of soil on the bottom with a ruler.
6. **Big storm.** Empty the cups. Pour 2 more cups onto each tray, this time in about 5 seconds. Look at the bare soil for little channels, called **rills**, and for soil piling up at the notch. Then let the cups sit 10 minutes and measure the soil layers again.
7. **Look at the roots.** Lift one edge of the grass with a spoon. Look at how the roots and soil hang together in one clump.
8. **Erosion walk.** After the next rain, walk around your yard, schoolyard or park. Find bare spots, gullies or mud washed onto a sidewalk. Ask your child how to fix each one: plant grass, spread mulch, or put down leaves.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder which cup will look muddier. Why do you think so?"
- "What do you think will happen if we pour much faster?"
- "Where did the soil in this cup come from?"
- "What do you think the roots are doing under the grass?"
- "If this muddy water ran into a creek, what might it do to the animals there?"
- "Where outside have you seen bare dirt? What could we plant there?"

## Why it works

A falling raindrop hits bare soil hard enough to knock grains loose and splash them up. Loose grains then ride away in the thin sheet of water running downhill. Plant leaves and stems take the impact first, so the drops reach the soil more gently. Roots bind the soil together and leave small channels that help water soak in, so there is less runoff to carry soil away.

A faster pour moves more water across the surface at once, so it carries more soil and cuts rills. Farmers, road crews and stream restoration teams use the same idea when they plant cover crops, grass strips and trees along streams. Rows of trees called **windbreaks** slow the wind across fields and keep dry soil from blowing away. Soil forms slowly from rock and rotting plants: an inch of topsoil can take hundreds of years.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Do only the gentle rain and the root look. Skip measuring. Ask your child to point to the muddier cup and tell you which tray it came from. Let them draw the two cups with crayons, one brown and one clear. On the walk, find one bare spot and one grassy spot and compare them.

### Ages 8 to 10
Add a third tray covered with a layer of dead leaves or mulch, and keep everything else the same. Measure the water in each cup in mL and the soil layer in mm, and make a bar graph of the soil layers. Have your child write which tray lost the most soil and why. Then pick one bare spot at home or school, draw a plan to fix it, and check it after the next three rains.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If almost no water comes out of either tray, the soil was dry and soaked it all up. Water both trays the day before so they start equally damp. If the grass seed grew thin and patchy, the difference between the cups will be smaller; use sod or sow the seed more thickly. If water runs over the sides, make the V notch deeper or tip the trays less.
