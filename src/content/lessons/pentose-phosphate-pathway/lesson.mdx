techniques:
  - name: "Respiratory quotient (RQ = CO2/O2)"
    blurb: "Measure the ratio of CO2 exhaled to O2 consumed and you can estimate which fuel the body is mainly burning. Pure carbohydrate oxidation gives RQ ≈ 1.0; fat gives ≈ 0.7. An RQ above 1.0 signals net conversion of carbohydrate to fat: CO2 is released while oxygen-poor fat is stored. The PPP supplies part of the NADPH for that fat-making, but its own CO2 does not raise the RQ, because the NADPH it makes is later spent on reactions that use oxygen or on that net synthesis. RQ is a whole-body fuel readout, not a PPP assay."
  - name: "Isotopic carbon tracing"
    blurb: "Feed cells glucose labeled at a specific carbon (13C or 14C on C1 vs C6) and follow where the atom lands. Because the oxidative phase removes C1 of glucose as CO2 while glycolysis would keep it, the position of the released label tells you what fraction of glucose ran the PPP versus glycolysis. This is the classic way the relative flux through the two branches has been measured in different tissues, though carbon recycling through the non-oxidative phase complicates the reading."
  - name: "Enzyme assay for G6PD (the clinical screen)"
    blurb: "Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase activity is measured by following NADPH production spectrophotometrically (NADPH absorbs at 340 nm; NADP+ does not). A slower rise in A340 (absorbance at 340 nm) in a patient's red cells is the standard diagnostic for G6PD deficiency — the same initial-velocity logic from enzyme kinetics, used to decide whether certain oxidant drugs are safe to prescribe."
  - name: "Glutathione redox ratio (GSH/GSSG)"
    blurb: "The ratio of reduced glutathione to its oxidized disulfide form is a readout of a cell's antioxidant reserve, and it is held high by NADPH, largely from the PPP. Measuring GSH/GSSG (by HPLC or enzymatic recycling assays) reports whether NADPH supply, from the PPP and other sources, is keeping up with oxidative demand — it collapses when NADPH supply fails."

    choices:
      - "Because NAD+ and NADPH have different absorption spectra at 340 nm"
      - "To allow catabolism (needing NAD+) and reductive biosynthesis (needing NADPH) to run simultaneously without one draining the other"
      - "To double the cell's total store of nicotinamide cofactor"
      - "Because NADP+ cannot be reduced inside mitochondria"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "Abundant oxidized NAD+ serves catabolic dehydrogenations; abundant reduced NADPH serves reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defense.   - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: "Glucose-6-phosphate can go into glycolysis to make ATP, or into the pentose phosphate pathway, which makes no ATP at all. Instead it makes two things: NADPH, which is the cell's reducing power for building molecules and for fighting damage from reactive oxygen, and ribose-5-phosphate, the sugar RNA is built on and the starting point for DNA's deoxyribose. The first part (the oxidative phase) is irreversible: it makes two NADPH and throws away one carbon as CO2. The second part (the non-oxidative phase) is just a reversible carbon-shuffle that rearranges five-carbon sugars into six- and three-carbon sugars the cell can send back into glycolysis. Because that second part runs both ways, toward whichever products are being used up, the cell can make mostly ribose, mostly NADPH, or a mix. The main control is the enzyme G6PD, which slows down when NADPH is already high. People with weak G6PD can get a dangerous breakdown of red blood cells from fava beans or certain drugs — but that same trait is thought to give some protection against malaria."
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: "At the glucose-6-phosphate branch point, G6PD diverts carbon from glycolysis into the PPP. The oxidative phase (G6PD → lactonase → 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase) is irreversible and nets G6P + 2 NADP+ + H2O → ribulose-5-P + 2 NADPH + 2 H+ + CO2; every step runs downhill in the cell, the lactone hydrolysis strongly so, and the decarboxylation leaves no route back. The non-oxidative phase is fully reversible and amounts to 3 ribulose-5-P (via the isomerase/epimerase to ribose-5-P and xylulose-5-P) ⇌ 2 fructose-6-P + glyceraldehyde-3-P, executed by two TPP-dependent transketolase reactions (2-carbon transfers) and one transaldolase reaction (3-carbon transfer via a Schiff-base lysine). The cell keeps NAD+/NADH (~1000:1 free in the cytosol, oxidized, for catabolism) separate from NADP+/NADPH (strongly reduced, for anabolism), so that, although the two couples have nearly equal standard potentials, one stays a ready electron acceptor and the other a ready donor. Reversibility lets four operating modes balance ribose vs NADPH demand, and the primary regulator is NADP+ availability, since G6PD is product-inhibited by NADPH. Clinically, NADPH regenerates glutathione (via glutathione reductase); G6PD deficiency collapses this in RBCs (no mitochondria, no nucleus), causing oxidative hemolysis, with the allele thought to be maintained by partial malaria resistance."
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: "The PPP's architecture is a study in metabolic flexibility under a thermodynamic constraint. The oxidative segment is held far from equilibrium and committed by the strongly exergonic lactone hydrolysis and an essentially irreversible decarboxylation at 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase; oxidative flux control resides largely at G6PD, whose activity tracks the NADP+/NADPH ratio (competitive product inhibition by NADPH against NADP+), making cytosolic redox poise the main governing variable. The compartmentalization of the two nicotinamide couples (oxidized NAD+ for catabolic dehydrogenations, reduced NADPH for reductive biosynthesis and thiol-based antioxidant defense) is enforced by cofactor-specificity residues and prevents the high-capacity NAD+ pool from dissipating reductive potential. The non-oxidative segment, being near-equilibrium and reversible, functions as a carbon-rearranging exchange network (transketolase's TPP ylide chemistry for C2 transfers; transaldolase's lysine-enamine for C3 transfers) that allows the system to interconvert pentoses, hexoses, and trioses stoichiometrically (3 C5 ⇌ 2 C6 + C3) and thereby decouple NADPH production from ribose demand across the four canonical flux modes. The clinical corollary — that erythrocytes, lacking both nucleus and mitochondria, depend heavily on this NADPH source for glutathione recycling — and the balanced-polymorphism maintenance of G6PD deficiency against P. falciparum, illustrate how a housekeeping redox pathway becomes a node of selective pressure. The oncologic dimension (p53-dependent suppression of G6PD dimerization, TIGAR and PKM2 routing) shows the same control point co-opted: loss of p53 can de-repress G6PD to supply the anabolic and antioxidant demands of proliferation."

**A lymphocyte dividing to fight an infection, a liver cell turning sugar into fat, and a red blood cell hit by an oxidant drug all want different things from glucose, and one pathway serves all three.**

The dividing cell needs ribose-5-phosphate, the sugar every nucleotide is built from. The liver cell needs NADPH, the reducing power for building molecules. The red cell needs NADPH from it above all, and little ribose, to keep its antioxidant defenses loaded. None of them needs ATP from this pathway, and it makes none.

The pathway starts at a fork. At glucose-6-phosphate, phosphoglucose isomerase pulls toward glycolysis and ATP; glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase pulls toward the pentose phosphate pathway and NADPH. Dividing cells need the second road twice over: nucleotides are built on ribose, and turning ribonucleotides into DNA building blocks spends NADPH. The pathway is one of several NADPH sources, but your red blood cells depend on it as their main one, and that is where this lesson ends up.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Every NADPH the pathway makes comes from a one-way tap. Each glucose-6-phosphate that goes through it gives 2 NADPH, 1 CO₂ and 1 five-carbon sugar, and NADP⁺ is what opens the tap. The rest of the pathway is a reversible exchange that makes no NADPH and loses no carbon. It only converts five-carbon sugars to and from glycolytic sugars: 3 C5 ⇌ 2 C6 + 1 C3. So the tap sets how much NADPH the cell gets. The exchange closes the gap between the tap's fixed 2-to-1 output and the ribose the cell actually needs, and it runs in whichever direction that gap is being drained.

The rule covers the cytosolic pathway in animal cells. Other NADPH sources, and slower controls on how much G6PD a cell makes, sit outside it (Going deeper).

## Why NADPH, not NADH

NAD and NADP differ by one phosphate group, and that tag is enough for enzymes to tell them apart. Both carriers hold electrons with almost the same strength. What differs is how full each pool is. In the cytosol the free NAD⁺/NADH pool sits at roughly 1000:1 in favor of the *oxidized* form (inside mitochondria it is far more reduced), so it is kept mostly empty, ready to *accept* electrons. That is what catabolism wants: glycolysis and the citric acid cycle are constantly pulling electrons off fuel. The NADP⁺/NADPH pool sits the other way, far toward the *reduced* form, so it is kept mostly full, ready to *give* electrons. That is what anabolism wants: fatty-acid synthesis, nucleotide synthesis and glutathione regeneration all spend reducing power.

If enzymes could not tell the two apart, electrons would flow from the full pool to the empty one, like water through a pipe joining a full tank to an empty one, until both sat at the same level, and the cell would lose both an acceptor ready for catabolism and a donor ready for biosynthesis. Keeping them apart lets breakdown and building run at once (Going deeper, "Same voltage, different fill").

## The tap: the oxidative phase

The first three steps are a one-way street.

**Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD)** oxidizes C1 of glucose-6-phosphate, which turns the sugar's ring into a cyclic ester called a lactone (6-phosphoglucono-δ-lactone), and reduces the first NADP⁺ to NADPH. This is the committed, rate-limiting step, and it is the regulated one. **Lactonase** then opens the strained lactone ring to give 6-phosphogluconate. **6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase** makes the second NADPH and, in the same step, removes a carbon as CO₂, leaving the five-carbon ribulose-5-phosphate.

> **G6P + 2 NADP⁺ + H₂O → ribulose-5-P + 2 NADPH + 2 H⁺ + CO₂**

No ATP is made. Each step runs downhill in the cell, the lactone hydrolysis strongly so, and once the carbon has left as CO₂ there is no route back.

**The valve.** G6PD is product-inhibited by NADPH, so the main throttle on the tap is **NADP⁺ concentration**: feedback written into the active site lets the pathway answer moment-to-moment demand directly.

## The mismatch

The tap's 2-to-1 output is fixed, and real cells rarely need exactly that. The liver cell making fat needs a great deal of NADPH and little ribose. The dividing cell may already have plenty of NADPH and need ribose fast.

## The exchange: the non-oxidative phase

This phase is that route, and unlike the tap, every step is reversible.

Two routing enzymes set up the sugars. **Phosphopentose isomerase** turns ribulose-5-phosphate into ribose-5-phosphate, the aldose that exits toward nucleotide synthesis. **Phosphopentose epimerase** turns ribulose-5-phosphate into xylulose-5-phosphate, its epimer, which differs at a single stereocenter. Then three transfer reactions move carbons between sugars:

- **Transketolase** (the "molecular scissors") snips a two-carbon unit off xylulose-5-P and attaches it to ribose-5-P: C5 + C5 → C3 (glyceraldehyde-3-P) + C7 (sedoheptulose-7-P). It runs on thiamine pyrophosphate, the same cofactor pyruvate dehydrogenase uses.
- **Transaldolase** moves a three-carbon unit: C7 + C3 → C6 (fructose-6-P) + C4 (erythrose-4-P). It needs no external cofactor: an active-site lysine forms a Schiff base with the substrate, exactly the mechanism aldolase uses in glycolysis.
- **Transketolase again**: C4 (erythrose-4-P) + C5 (a second xylulose-5-P, also from the epimerase) → C6 (fructose-6-P) + C3 (glyceraldehyde-3-P).

Net: **three C5 sugars (one ribose-5-P and two xylulose-5-P, all made from ribulose-5-P) become two fructose-6-P and one glyceraldehyde-3-P**, or the reverse. Check the carbons: 3 × 5 = 15, and 2 × 6 + 3 = 15. Both products are glycolytic intermediates.

What decides the direction? Nothing has to. These reactions sit close to equilibrium, so they run toward whichever end is being used up. When nucleotide synthesis pulls ribose-5-phosphate away, the exchange runs backward to replace it. When the tap piles up five-carbon sugar the cell has no use for, the exchange runs forward to clear it.

## Four demands, four tallies

The tap and the exchange cover every case, the four textbook modes. Start with Mode 2, the only demand the tap meets on its own.

**Mode 2: both, in the tap's own ratio.** If the cell wants 2 NADPH for every ribose, the tap alone does the job: one glucose-6-phosphate gives 2 NADPH, 1 CO₂ and, through the isomerase, one ribose-5-phosphate. The exchange does nothing.

**Mode 1: ribose, with plenty of NADPH already.** A dividing cell that needs nucleotides fast can be in this spot. With NADPH high, the tap is nearly shut. The ribose has to come from the exchange run backward, out of fructose-6-P and glyceraldehyde-3-P pulled from glycolysis.

## The red cell: when the tap breaks

NADPH is what keeps **glutathione** reduced. Glutathione is a small tripeptide (γ-Glu-Cys-Gly) with a reactive cysteine thiol, and it is the cell's frontline antioxidant. Glutathione peroxidase spends reduced glutathione (GSH) to neutralize peroxides, turning it into the oxidized disulfide GSSG; glutathione reductase then spends NADPH to turn GSSG back into GSH.

A mature red blood cell needs little ribose, has no nucleus and no mitochondria, and gets its NADPH mainly from the PPP, so its whole glutathione cycle leans on the tap.

That is why a fava bean can be dangerous. **G6PD deficiency**, the most common human enzyme deficiency, affecting roughly 400 million people, weakens the tap itself. The gene is on the X chromosome, so males are affected more often, and its many variants differ in severity. Fava beans, certain antimalarial and sulfa drugs, and infection all push oxidative stress past what a weakened cell can handle. The cascade: G6PD↓ → NADPH↓ → GSH↓ → reactive oxygen accumulates → hemoglobin oxidizes and clumps into Heinz bodies → the damaged cells burst in the circulation or are removed by the spleen → hemolytic crisis.

If the deficiency is dangerous, why is it so common? It is thought to persist because it offers some protection against *Plasmodium falciparum*, the malaria parasite: deficient red cells seem to be a poorer home for it, and the allele's prevalence broadly overlaps the historical range of malaria. It is a balanced polymorphism, the textbook companion to sickle-cell trait, though how strong the protection is, and in whom (males with one deficient X, or heterozygous females), still differs between studies. The practical lesson: screen for G6PD before prescribing certain oxidant drugs.

## Where the NADPH goes next

Many dividing tumor cells run glycolysis hard (the Warburg effect) *and* the PPP hard, because they need NADPH for lipid and nucleotide synthesis and for surviving their own oxidative stress. Lose p53 function (TP53 is the most frequently mutated gene in human cancer) and a brake on this NADPH economy can come off (Going deeper). Next we turn to fats: first how cells take fatty acids apart, then how they build them, spending the NADPH this pathway makes.

## Going deeper

### Other NADPH sources

In many cells, malic enzyme, isocitrate dehydrogenase and folate metabolism make NADPH too. Liver and fat tissue making fatty acids get a substantial share of theirs from malic enzyme as well, which is why the red cell is the cleaner example of an NADPH-only demand, even though it can recycle only part of its carbon.

### Same voltage, different fill

To put a number on the difference in fill, take 1000:1 oxidized for NAD and about 1:100 for NADP, close to classic estimates for rat-liver cytosol. The NADP couple is then about 150 mV more reducing in the cytosol: RT/2F × ln 10⁵ ≈ 154 mV at 37 °C.

### The tally for any demand

For a demand of *n* NADPH and *r* ribose-5-phosphate, the tap must run *n*/2 times, and the exchange must run forward by (*n*/2 − *r*)/3 turns. That is zero only when *n* = 2*r* (Mode 2); a negative answer means it runs backward (Mode 1: *n* = 0, *r* = 6 gives −2).

### Why the oxidative phase does not raise the respiratory quotient

The oxidative phase itself uses no O₂, but that does not by itself raise the body's ratio of CO₂ exhaled to O₂ consumed (the respiratory quotient), because the NADPH it makes is later spent on reactions that use oxygen or on building fat. An RQ above 1 signals net fat synthesis, which spends PPP NADPH.

### Long-term control of G6PD, and how p53 acts

Over longer times, signals such as NRF2 and modifications such as SIRT2 deacetylation also change how much G6PD the cell has and how active it is. The tumor suppressor p53 can restrain G6PD by binding it and blocking its assembly into the active dimer. p53 touches the pathway in other ways too (it induces TIGAR, which can push glucose into the PPP), so its net effect depends on the cell.
