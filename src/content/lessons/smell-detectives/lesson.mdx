## The big idea

A child walks into a kitchen and says, "Cookies," before seeing a single one. The nose got there first.

Smell and taste are two of the five basic senses. The nose smells and the tongue tastes. Virginia’s kindergarten standard lists four tastes (sweet, sour, bitter, salty) and three smell words (strong, faint, pleasant). Your child uses all of them here.

The smell cups hide what each smell is, so the nose has to work alone. The taste test then shows how the two senses team up. When you chew, smells from the food rise from the back of the mouth into the nose. The brain blends them with taste into **flavour**, the full sense of what a food is like. Pinch the nose and much of that flavour fades, leaving mostly sweet or sour. Foods that seemed different can become hard to tell apart.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time. You open the extract bottles, poke the foil holes and serve the food.
- Use kitchen foods only. Skip essential oils, perfume and cleaning products, and never smell-test an unknown liquid. Some essential oils are poisonous if swallowed. If a child is choking or struggling to breathe, call 911. If a child swallows an essential oil, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222.
- Vanilla and other flavour extracts contain alcohol (vanilla extract is at least 35 percent). A few drops on cotton is plenty; put the bottle away out of reach straight after, and keep it away from children under 4. If a child swallows any, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222.
- Check every food against your child’s allergies before tasting, and use no nuts or nut products, including almond extract. At school, follow the school’s food rules and get permission from families before any tasting. Skip the tasting for a child who has trouble swallowing.
- Your child sits at a table to taste, takes small sips or 1 cm pieces, and chews and swallows each one before the next. No talking, laughing or walking with food in the mouth. Leave out the fruit pieces if any child under 4 is taking part. Smell cups are for smelling only, never eating.
- Keep cotton balls, rubber bands, foil and cups out of reach of children under 4 and pets, and throw them away afterwards. Food from the smell cups is never eaten afterwards.
- Wash hands with soap and water before handling food and after tasting. Each child gets their own cups and cotton swab, and nothing is shared or dipped twice.
- If a smell makes anyone cough, wheeze or have watery eyes, stop and move them to fresh air. If anyone struggles to breathe, call 911. A child with asthma or scent sensitivity can skip the smell cups and be the recorder.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Make the smell cups.** Put a cotton ball in each cup. Add one smell to two cups each, so every smell has a pair: a strip of peel, a pinch of coffee, a piece of cinnamon stick, 3 drops of vanilla, a crushed mint leaf. You cover each cup with foil and a rubber band, and poke 4 or 5 holes with a pencil yourself, with the cup set on a table, not held in anyone’s hand. Label the cups with letters and keep a secret key.
2. **Learn to waft.** Show your child how to hold a cup a hand’s width below the nose and wave the air toward the face. Sniff gently, never hard.
3. **Find the pairs.** Mix up the cups. Your child smells each one and puts matching cups side by side. Ask for a describing word for each: strong or faint, pleasant or not.
4. **Name the smells.** Now they guess what each smell is. Write the guesses, then peel back the foil to check. It is common to know a smell well and still not find its name.
5. **Strong or faint.** You make two new vanilla cups, one with 1 drop and one with 6 drops. Ask, "Which will smell stronger?" Then check. Rest a minute between rounds, because a smell can seem fainter after many sniffs.
6. **Predict the taste test.** For each child, you pour the two juices into separate small cups marked 1 and 2. Ask, "If you hold your nose, will you still know which juice is which?" Write the prediction.
7. **Pinch and taste.** With eyes closed, your child pinches their nose, takes a small sip and holds it in the mouth. "Which juice is it?" Then they let go of the nose while the juice is still there. Most children notice the flavour arrive when the nose opens. Sip water and repeat with the other juice, or with apple and pear pieces while your child sits still and you watch.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder which cup matches this one. What words describe it?"
- "What do you think will happen when we smell the 6-drop cup?"
- "If you hold your nose, will the juice still taste sweet?"
- "What do you think will happen when you let go of your nose?"
- "Why do you think food tastes different when you have a cold?"
- "Does everyone in the family like the same smells? How could we find out?"

## Why it works

Smell starts with smell-sensing cells high inside the nose. They respond to tiny particles, called **molecules**, that drift off things like orange peel or coffee. Smells reach these cells two ways: through the nostrils when you sniff, and from the back of the mouth when you breathe out while chewing. The second route is how food aromas join taste.

Taste buds on the tongue, the roof of the mouth and the throat sense a short list of qualities. Virginia’s kindergarten list is sweet, sour, bitter and salty. Scientists usually add a fifth, **umami**, a savoury taste found in foods like broth and cheese. Without smell, many foods taste bland. Flavour also includes texture, temperature and spicy heat, so the pinched-nose test does not remove every clue.

The tongue map, with sweet at the tip and bitter at the back, came from a misreading of a 1901 study. That study found only small differences in sensitivity across the tongue.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use three smells (six cups) with big differences, such as orange peel, coffee and mint. Show your child the real foods first, then hide them in cups and play "find the twin." For the taste test, use one pair of juices and do it once. Your child can draw a face for each smell (happy, so-so, yuck) instead of writing.

### Ages 8 to 10
Use five smells (ten cups) and add a fair test. Give each juice three times in random order. Each time, your child names the juice with the nose pinched, then again after letting go. Count correct answers. Make a table and a bar graph: correct with nose pinched, correct with nose open. Ask them to rank five vanilla cups (1 to 5 drops) from faint to strong without looking at the labels.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If the smells are too faint, add more of the food or use fewer holes in the foil, and wait 10 minutes for the smell to build. If your child guesses the juices right with the nose pinched, the colours or textures may differ: cover the cups and close eyes, or switch to two juices that taste more alike. A child who will not taste can be the tester for an adult instead.
