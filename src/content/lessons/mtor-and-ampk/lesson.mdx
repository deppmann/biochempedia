techniques:
  - name: "Drug-resistance screen in yeast"
    blurb: >-
      Spread millions of yeast cells on plates containing a drug that stops growth and keep the
      rare colonies that grow anyway. Then sort the survivors by which gene is changed. In the rapamycin
      screen the genes that turned up again and again encoded the drug's partner (FPR1) and its
      target (TOR1 and TOR2). In other screens such genes can also encode drug pumps or bypass routes.
  - name: "Affinity purification with a drug–protein bait"
    blurb: >-
      Fix FKBP12 loaded with rapamycin to a solid support, pass a tissue extract over it, and
      look for proteins that stick only when the drug is present. This is how the mammalian
      target of rapamycin was pulled out of extracts.
  - name: "Immunofluorescence co-localization"
    blurb: >-
      Fix cells, label mTOR with one fluorescent antibody and a lysosomal membrane protein with
      another, and ask whether the two signals overlap. Comparing starved and re-fed cells shows
      mTORC1 moving off and on the lysosome.
  - name: "Phospho-specific immunoblotting"
    blurb: >-
      Separate cell proteins on a gel and probe them with antibodies that bind a protein only
      when one chosen residue carries a phosphate. Phosphorylated S6 kinase reports mTORC1
      activity, and phosphorylated acetyl-CoA carboxylase reports AMPK activity, so one blot can
      read both gauges.

      mTOR's kinase domain. Yeast that lose FPR1 resist the drug because it has no partner. The
      ATP site of the kinase domain is wrong because rapamycin does not compete with ATP; it acts
      from outside the active site. The Rag GTPases carry the amino acid signal and are not the
      drug's partner. The gamma subunit of AMPK binds adenine nucleotides, not rapamycin.

    choices:
      - "They add phosphate to S6 kinase and 4E-BP1"
      - "They pump amino acids into the lysosome lumen"
      - "They speed up GTP hydrolysis on Rheb"
      - "They bring mTORC1 to the lysosome surface"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      With amino acids present the Rags bind Raptor and bring mTORC1 to the lysosome surface,
      where Rheb can activate it. Adding phosphate to S6 kinase and 4E-BP1 is the job of mTORC1
      itself; the Rags are GTPases, not kinases, and the 2008 work found they do not stimulate
      the kinase directly. Speeding up GTP hydrolysis on Rheb is what the TSC complex does, and it
      turns mTORC1 off. Pumping amino acids into the lysosome is the work of transporters; the
      Rags sit on the outer face of the membrane and carry no cargo across it.
  - stem: "Why is AMP a more sensitive signal of energy stress than ATP?"
    choices:
      - "Adenylate kinase makes AMP rise steeply as ATP dips"
      - "AMP carries more free energy per phosphate than ATP"
      - "AMP is made only once ATP has been completely used up"
      - "AMP is present at a much higher concentration than ATP"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Adenylate kinase keeps 2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP near equilibrium, so the AMP:ATP ratio rises as
      the square of the ADP:ATP ratio, and a small fall in ATP gives a large proportional rise in
      AMP. AMP is not present at a higher concentration than ATP; it is far lower, which is part
      of why its proportional change is so large. Free energy per phosphate is not what the gauge
      reads, and AMP has no phosphoanhydride bond to spend. AMP does not wait for ATP to be used
      up; it rises while ATP has barely fallen, which is the point of the design.
  - stem: "A cell has a growth factor and plenty of ATP, but amino acids are removed from its medium. Where is mTORC1, and is it active?"
    choices:
      - "Inactive on the lysosome, because TSC has switched Rheb off"
      - "Active in the cytoplasm, because Akt phosphorylates it directly"
      - "Inactive in the cytoplasm, because the Rags have let it go"
      - "Active on the lysosome, because Rheb still holds GTP"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Without amino acids the Rags switch to their inactive state and stop holding Raptor, so
      mTORC1 leaves the lysosome and is inactive in the cytoplasm, even though the growth factor
      may keep Rheb loaded with GTP. "Active on the lysosome" forgets that Rheb can activate only an
      mTORC1 that has been brought to it. "Inactive on the lysosome, because TSC has switched Rheb
      off" describes the main effect of losing the growth factor. Amino acid loss can also bring TSC to the lysosome, but the larger change here is that mTORC1 leaves it.
      "Active in the cytoplasm" is wrong because Akt acts on mTORC1 mainly through TSC and Rheb and
      cannot stand in for the amino acid signal.

    rationale: >-
      Blocking ATP synthesis raises AMP, which switches on AMPK. AMPK phosphorylates and

**The drug that protects a transplanted kidney from rejection came out of soil collected on Easter Island in 1964–65. Working out what it hits inside a cell uncovered the enzyme that decides whether a cell grows.**

Growth is expensive. Making protein uses amino acids and a large share of a cell's ATP, so a cell that starts building without supplies runs short of both. Before it commits, a cell reads two gauges. One, built around the kinase mTOR, reads growth factors and amino acids. The other, AMPK, reads energy.

## The story: an antifungal from island soil works only with a partner protein

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

From December 1964 to February 1965 a Canadian medical expedition worked on Rapa Nui (Easter Island). One member, the Montreal bacteriologist Georges Nogrady, collected soil from across the island. At Ayerst Research Laboratories in Montreal, a strain of the bacterium *Streptomyces hygroscopicus* grown from one sample was found to kill fungi. In 1972 the Ayerst microbiologist Suren Sehgal isolated the compound responsible and named it rapamycin, after the island.

Why it also stops cells from growing was answered in yeast. In Michael Hall's laboratory in Basel, Joseph Heitman spread yeast on plates containing rapamycin and kept the rare mutants that grew anyway. Most had mutations in the gene for a small protein called FPR1, the yeast version of your FKBP12. So at the doses that matter, rapamycin does little alone. It must first bind FKBP12, and the pair is the poison.

The remaining mutants had changes in one of two new genes, which the group named TOR1 and TOR2, for "target of rapamycin". The screen was published in 1991. In 1994 and 1995 several laboratories found the mammalian protein that the FKBP12–rapamycin pair binds. The name that lasted is mTOR.

mTOR is a large protein kinase. Beside its kinase domain is a small domain called FRB. Rapamycin sits between FKBP12 and FRB like glue between two blocks, and the bound FKBP12 narrows the path that substrates take to the active site. The drug does not compete with ATP. It is an allosteric inhibitor, meaning one that acts from a site other than the active site.

Yeast has two TOR genes and you have one, but in both the kinase works inside two different complexes. mTORC1, built around a partner protein called Raptor, controls growth and is the complex rapamycin blocks quickly. mTORC2, built around a partner called Rictor, is the kinase that gives Akt its second activating phosphate (the previous lesson), and it resists short rapamycin treatment.

## The idea: mTORC1 switches on only when two questions get a yes

In 1996 Hall's group reported that yeast without working TOR behave as if starved, even in rich medium: they sharply cut back the starting of new proteins and enter a resting state. Growth, they argued, is actively controlled, and TOR is the controller. What must mTORC1 hear before it gives the order? Two things, read by separate systems.

**Has the body said grow?** A growth factor such as insulin switches on Akt. Akt phosphorylates TSC2, one protein of the TSC complex, and that takes the complex out of action. The TSC complex is a GAP, the GTPase off-switch you met in the lesson on GTPase switches, and its target is a small GTPase called Rheb. With the GAP out of action, Rheb stays loaded with GTP, and Rheb-GTP is the direct activator of mTORC1. In the summer of 2003 several laboratories, Kun-Liang Guan's among them, reported within weeks of one another that TSC2 is a GAP for Rheb and that Rheb acts just upstream of mTOR.

**Are there amino acids to build with?** How cells sense amino acids was then unknown. In 2008 Yasemin Sancak, in David Sabatini's laboratory, and independently Guan's laboratory, found the link in another family of small GTPases, the Rags. Sancak's paper carried a surprise: the Rags do not raise mTORC1's kinase activity. They move it. In cells starved of amino acids, mTORC1 is spread through the cytoplasm. Add amino acids back and it gathers on one compartment, which the same laboratory identified in 2010 as the surface of the lysosome, the cell's acidic digestive compartment. Rheb is anchored to internal membranes by a lipid tail, and the lysosome surface is where the two are thought to meet.

Rheb-GTP can activate only an mTORC1 that has been brought to it. Growth factors set whether Rheb is on. Amino acids set whether mTORC1 is delivered, and their loss also brings the TSC complex to the lysosome to switch Rheb off. This is an AND gate: output only when both inputs are present. It is the standard model, not the whole story: glutamine, for one, can switch on mTORC1 by a route that does not need the Rags.

<MtorGate />

In 2011 Roberto Zoncu and colleagues reported that part of the amino acid reading is taken inside the lysosome and requires the proton pump in its membrane. Sensor proteins found since then include Sestrin2 for leucine and CASTOR1 for arginine in the cytoplasm, and the transporter SLC38A9 in the lysosomal membrane. All report to the Rags. Which sensor matters most in which tissue is not settled, and neither is where the working pool of Rheb sits.

When both answers are yes, mTORC1 phosphorylates two proteins that control the first step of translation. S6 kinase is switched on. 4E-BP1, which holds back eIF4E, the factor that grips the cap at the start of an mRNA, lets go. mTORC1 also promotes lipid synthesis and holds off autophagy, the process in which a cell digests its own contents in lysosomes to recover building blocks.

## The idea: AMPK reads AMP, because AMP moves most

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

AMPK answers a third question: is there enough ATP? You met it in the biochemistry course as a fuel gauge. Here is why the gauge watches AMP, more than ATP itself.

A healthy cell holds ATP nearly constant, so ATP is a poor alarm. The enzyme adenylate kinase interconverts the three adenine nucleotides and keeps them near equilibrium:

> **2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP, so [AMP] = K × [ADP]² / [ATP]**

Divide by [ATP] and you have the rule that matters:

> **[AMP] / [ATP] = K × ( [ADP] / [ATP] )²**

The AMP:ATP ratio goes as the square of the ADP:ATP ratio. Try round numbers, taking K as 0.5, which lies within the range of reported values. At rest, ATP is 5.0 mM and ADP is 0.5 mM, so AMP is 0.5 × 0.25 / 5.0 = 0.025 mM. Now let work use up a tenth of the ATP. ATP is 4.5 mM, ADP settles near 0.93 mM, and AMP is 0.5 × 0.86 / 4.5 = 0.096 mM. ATP fell by 10 percent and AMP rose almost fourfold. Think of a low-fuel light: the needle has barely moved and the light is on.

AMPK has three subunits. Alpha carries the kinase, beta holds the complex together, and gamma carries the sensor: sites where AMP, ADP and ATP compete to bind, so gamma reads their ratio. The main switch is one threonine in the activation loop of alpha, threonine 172, phosphorylated mainly by an upstream kinase called LKB1. A second kinase, CaMKK2, adds the same phosphate when calcium rises, with no change in AMP. That phosphate raises activity a hundredfold or more. LKB1 is active all the time. What AMP changes is AMPK itself: with AMP on gamma, the phosphate is harder for phosphatases to remove and, by most accounts, easier for LKB1 to add, and the kinase gets a further allosteric push. ADP on gamma also protects the phosphate, though at higher concentrations than AMP, and only AMP gives the allosteric push.

Once on, AMPK cuts spending and raises income. It phosphorylates and inactivates acetyl-CoA carboxylase, which slows fat synthesis and, as malonyl-CoA falls, lets fatty acids into mitochondria to be burned. It inactivates HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-setting enzyme of cholesterol synthesis. In muscle it brings more GLUT4 glucose transporters to the cell surface.

## Two gauges, one decision: energy can veto growth

AMPK phosphorylates TSC2 at sites different from Akt's, and here the phosphate makes the TSC complex more active, which drains Rheb of GTP. AMPK also phosphorylates Raptor, which inhibits mTORC1 directly.

Both gauges also write on ULK1, the kinase that starts autophagy. mTORC1 adds a phosphate that keeps it off. In the model built on work published in 2011, AMPK adds phosphates at other sites that switch it on. Later studies complicate AMPK's side of this, so hold it loosely.

You can now predict three states.

- **Fed.** Akt silences TSC, Rheb holds GTP, the Rags hold mTORC1 on the lysosome and AMPK is quiet. Protein and lipid synthesis run. Autophagy is held low.
- **No amino acids.** Rheb may still hold GTP, but the Rags release mTORC1 and it leaves the lysosome. Translation slows and autophagy starts, which returns amino acids to the cell.
- **No energy.** AMP rises and AMPK switches on. Fat and cholesterol synthesis slow, fat burning rises, glucose uptake rises in muscle, and mTORC1 is shut off through TSC2 and Raptor even when growth factors and amino acids are present.

### Design problem

A cell should build new protein only when three things are true at once: a growth factor is present, amino acids are plentiful and ATP is plentiful. Design the logic using the parts in this lesson. Say where in the cell each condition is read, and name one mutation that would let the cell ignore each condition.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Make the output "mTORC1 is active" and require two things for it: Rheb holds GTP, and mTORC1 is on the lysosome.

- **Growth factor.** Read at the plasma membrane by a receptor and relayed by Akt, which takes the TSC complex out of action so that Rheb keeps its GTP. To ignore it: lose PTEN, so that PIP3 and Akt stay on with no growth factor.
- **Amino acids.** Read in the cytoplasm and inside the lysosome by sensors that report to the Rags, which bring mTORC1 to the lysosome. To ignore it: lock RagA or RagB in its GTP form, so that mTORC1 stays on the lysosome in a starved cell.
- **ATP.** Read in the cytoplasm by the gamma subunit of AMPK, as the AMP:ATP ratio. To ignore it: lose LKB1, so that AMPK barely switches on when AMP rises.

TSC2 is where two conditions meet. A cell without it largely ignores growth factors and has lost one of AMPK's two routes to mTORC1, but it mostly keeps the amino acid check.

## When a gauge is drugged or broken

**Rapamycin.** T cells need mTORC1 to grow and divide when interleukin-2, a signaling protein of the immune system, tells them to. Rapamycin, under the drug name sirolimus, blocks that step, and it was approved in the United States in 1999 to prevent rejection of transplanted kidneys. Every other tissue uses mTORC1 too, so mouth ulcers and raised blood sugar are among its side effects.

**Tuberous sclerosis.** People born with a faulty copy of TSC1 or TSC2 develop benign growths in the brain, kidneys, skin and other organs. In cells without a working TSC brake, Rheb stays on and mTORC1 largely stops waiting for a growth factor. Drugs of the rapamycin family are used to shrink some of these growths.

**Metformin.** This is the most widely prescribed drug for type 2 diabetes, and how it works is still not fully understood. The liver, where it reduces glucose output, was long thought to be its main site of action, but the gut now looks important too. At high concentrations metformin partly inhibits complex I of the mitochondrial respiratory chain, which raises AMP and switches on AMPK. At lower, clinically relevant concentrations it has been reported to switch on AMPK at the lysosome without changing AMP. Whether AMPK is the reason blood glucose falls is also argued. Mice lacking AMPK in the liver still respond to the drug, and other targets have been proposed in the liver and in the gut.
