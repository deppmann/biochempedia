techniques:
  - name: "Drug-resistance screen in yeast"
    blurb: >-
      Spread millions of yeast cells on plates containing a drug that stops growth and keep the
      rare colonies that grow anyway. Then sort the survivors by which gene is changed. In the rapamycin
      screen the genes that turned up again and again encoded the drug's partner (FPR1) and its
      target (TOR1 and TOR2). In other screens such genes can also encode drug pumps or bypass routes.
  - name: "Affinity purification with a drug–protein bait"
    blurb: >-
      Fix FKBP12 loaded with rapamycin to a solid support, pass a tissue extract over it, and
      look for proteins that stick only when the drug is present. This is how the mammalian
      target of rapamycin was pulled out of extracts.
  - name: "Immunofluorescence co-localization"
    blurb: >-
      Fix cells, label mTOR with one fluorescent antibody and a lysosomal membrane protein with
      another, and ask whether the two signals overlap. Comparing starved and re-fed cells shows
      mTORC1 moving off and on the lysosome.
  - name: "Phospho-specific immunoblotting"
    blurb: >-
      Separate cell proteins on a gel and probe them with antibodies that bind a protein only
      when one chosen residue carries a phosphate. Phosphorylated S6 kinase reports mTORC1
      activity, and phosphorylated acetyl-CoA carboxylase reports AMPK activity, so one blot can
      read both gauges.

    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      With amino acids present the Rags bind Raptor and bring mTORC1 to the lysosome surface,
      where Rheb can activate it. Adding phosphate to S6 kinase and 4E-BP1 is the job of mTORC1
      itself; the Rags are GTPases, not kinases, and the 2008 work found they do not stimulate
      the kinase directly. Speeding up GTP hydrolysis on Rheb is what the TSC complex does, and it
      turns mTORC1 off. Pumping amino acids into the lysosome is the work of transporters; the
      Rags sit on the outer face of the membrane and carry no cargo across it.
  - stem: "Why is AMP a more sensitive signal of energy stress than ATP?"
    choices:
      - "Adenylate kinase makes AMP rise steeply as ATP dips"
      - "AMP carries more free energy per phosphate than ATP"
      - "AMP is made only once ATP has been completely used up"
      - "AMP is present at a much higher concentration than ATP"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Adenylate kinase keeps 2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP near equilibrium, so the AMP:ATP ratio rises as
      the square of the ADP:ATP ratio, and a small fall in ATP gives a large proportional rise in
      AMP. AMP is not present at a higher concentration than ATP; it is far lower, which is part
      of why its proportional change is so large. Free energy per phosphate is not what the gauge
      reads, and AMP has no phosphoanhydride bond to spend. AMP does not wait for ATP to be used
      up; it rises while ATP has barely fallen, which is the point of the design.

    rationale: >-

**The drug that protects a transplanted kidney from rejection came out of soil collected on Easter Island in 1964–65. Working out what it hits inside a cell uncovered the enzyme that decides whether a cell grows.**

The first film sketched mTORC1's two-signal gate at the lysosome; here is the evidence behind it, and the energy gauge that can override it.

## How a soil drug uncovered the growth controller

From December 1964 to February 1965 a Canadian medical expedition worked on Rapa Nui (Easter Island), where Montreal bacteriologist Georges Nogrady collected soil across the island. At Ayerst Research Laboratories in Montreal, a *Streptomyces hygroscopicus* strain grown from one sample killed fungi. In 1972 Ayerst microbiologist Suren Sehgal isolated the compound and named it **rapamycin**, after the island.

Why it stops cell growth was answered in yeast. In Michael Hall's Basel laboratory, Joseph Heitman plated yeast on rapamycin and collected rare resistant mutants. Most carried mutations not in a kinase, but in a small protein called **FPR1**—the yeast version of human **FKBP12**.

**Predict.** If losing FPR1 makes yeast resistant to rapamycin at working doses, how must the drug block growth?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Rapamycin does little alone at working doses. It must first bind FKBP12 (FPR1 in yeast), and the **FKBP12–rapamycin pair** is the active poison.

</details>

The remaining mutants altered *TOR1* and *TOR2*, for "target of rapamycin" (published 1991). In 1994 and 1995 several labs identified the mammalian protein bound by FKBP12–rapamycin: **mTOR**.

mTOR is a large protein kinase with a small **FRB domain** beside its kinase domain. Rapamycin glues FKBP12 to FRB, so bound FKBP12 narrows substrate access to the active site. It does not compete with ATP; it is an **allosteric inhibitor**, acting from outside the active site.

Yeast has two TOR genes and humans have one, yet in both the kinase works in two complexes. **mTORC1**, built around **Raptor**, controls growth and is blocked quickly by rapamycin. **mTORC2**, built around **Rictor**, gives Akt its second activating phosphate (the previous lesson) and resists short rapamycin treatment.

## Two signals, one gate at the lysosome

Growth is expensive: making protein uses amino acids and a large share of cellular ATP. In 1996 Hall's group reported that yeast without working TOR act starved even in rich medium—cutting back translation initiation and entering a resting state. Growth, they argued, is actively controlled, and TOR is the controller.

**Track 1: Growth factors.** A signal such as insulin activates Akt, releasing a brake through a double negative:

> **Growth factor → Akt ⊣ TSC complex (GAP) ⊣ Rheb-GTP → mTORC1**

Akt phosphorylates **TSC2** in the **TSC complex**, taking that complex out of action. The TSC complex is a **GAP** (the GTPase off-switch from the lesson on GTPase switches) for the small GTPase **Rheb**. Without its GAP, Rheb stays loaded with GTP, and **Rheb-GTP** directly activates mTORC1. In summer 2003 several labs, Kun-Liang Guan's among them, reported within weeks of one another that TSC2 is Rheb's GAP and Rheb acts upstream of mTOR.

**Track 2: Amino acids.** In 2008 Yasemin Sancak (David Sabatini's lab) and independently Guan's lab traced amino acid sensing to the **Rag** small GTPases. Sancak's surprise was that the Rags bind Raptor and *move* mTORC1 without raising its kinase activity directly. In amino-acid-starved cells, mTORC1 spreads through the cytoplasm; restore amino acids and it gathers on one organelle, identified by the same lab in 2010 as the **lysosome**, the cell's acidic digestive compartment. Because a lipid tail anchors Rheb to internal membranes, the lysosome surface is where Rheb-GTP and mTORC1 are thought to meet.

Rheb-GTP can activate only an mTORC1 that has been brought to it. Thus the lysosome acts as an **AND gate**: growth factors keep Rheb on, and amino acids deliver mTORC1 (amino acid loss also brings TSC to the lysosome to switch Rheb off).

**Predict.** Suppose Rheb loses the lipid tail that anchors it to membranes, while everything else is normal. In a cell given insulin and plenty of amino acids, is mTORC1 on?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In the standard model, largely **off**. Akt still keeps Rheb loaded with GTP and the Rags still bring mTORC1 to the lysosome, but Rheb-GTP is no longer held where mTORC1 is delivered. The two signals have to meet in the same place.

</details>

## Why AMP moves most: adenylate kinase and AMPK's switch

Here is the equilibrium behind the fuel gauge you met in the biochemistry course and in the second film.

Because a healthy cell holds ATP nearly constant, ATP is a poor alarm. **Adenylate kinase** keeps the three adenine nucleotides near equilibrium:

> **2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP, so [AMP] = K × [ADP]² / [ATP]**

Divide by `[ATP]` for the governing rule:

> **[AMP] / [ATP] = K × ( [ADP] / [ATP] )²**

The AMP:ATP ratio varies as the **square** of the ADP:ATP ratio. Take round numbers with `K = 0.5` (within reported values):

- **At rest:** ATP is 5.0 mM and ADP is 0.50 mM, so `[AMP] = 0.5 × 0.25 / 5.0 = 0.025 mM` (total adenine pool `ATP + ADP + AMP = 5.525 mM`).
- **After ATP drops 10%:** ATP is 4.5 mM. Rather than ADP jumping the full 0.5 mM to 1.0 mM, adenylate kinase converts part of that ADP into AMP and ATP while holding the total pool at `5.525 mM`. ADP settles near `0.93 mM`, and `[AMP] = 0.5 × 0.93² / 4.5 ≈ 0.096 mM`.

ATP fell 10 percent, yet AMP rose almost fourfold—like a low-fuel light tripping while the needle barely moved.

**Predict.** Suppose K were 1.0 instead of 0.5, and the ADP:ATP ratio changed in the same way as in the example. Would the fold rise in the AMP:ATP ratio be larger?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. K multiplies the AMP:ATP ratio in both states, so it cancels when you compare them. The fold rise depends only on how much the ADP:ATP ratio changed, squared.

</details>

**AMPK** has three subunits: **alpha** carries the kinase, **beta** holds the complex together, and **gamma** holds binding sites where AMP, ADP and ATP compete, reading their ratio. Full activation requires phosphorylation of **threonine 172 (Thr172)** in alpha's activation loop—boosting activity a hundredfold or more—added mainly by the upstream kinase **LKB1**.

LKB1 is active all the time, so AMP changes **AMPK itself**: AMP on gamma makes the Thr172 phosphate harder for phosphatases to remove and, by most accounts, easier for LKB1 to add, plus a direct allosteric push. (*Going deeper* covers ADP protection and calcium activation by **CaMKK2**.)

**Predict.** A cell lacking LKB1 cannot phosphorylate Thr172 via the energy route. If ATP drops and AMP spikes, does AMPK reach full activity?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. LKB1 adds most of the Thr172 phosphate, the source of the hundredfold or greater boost. Without it there is little phosphate for AMP to shield, and AMP's allosteric push alone gives only weak activity. (CaMKK2 can still add the phosphate if calcium rises.)

</details>

Active AMPK cuts spending and raises income: it phosphorylates and inactivates **acetyl-CoA carboxylase** (slowing fat synthesis and lowering malonyl-CoA so fatty acids enter mitochondria to burn), inactivates **HMG-CoA reductase** (rate-setting enzyme of cholesterol synthesis), and in muscle brings more **GLUT4** glucose transporters to the surface.

## Two gauges, one decision: predicting cell states

Putting both halves of the rule together—mTORC1 needs growth factors and amino acids, while AMPK reads rising AMP to conserve energy and veto it—shows how energy overrides growth.

AMPK blocks mTORC1 twice: it phosphorylates **TSC2** at activating sites different from Akt's (draining Rheb of GTP) and phosphorylates **Raptor** to inhibit mTORC1 directly. Both gauges also write on **ULK1**, the autophagy-initiating kinase (*Going deeper*).

| State | Growth factor / Akt | Amino acids / Rags | Energy / AMPK | Rheb & mTORC1 | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Fed** | On (TSC silenced) | On (Rags bind Raptor) | High ATP (AMPK off) | Rheb-GTP; mTORC1 on lysosome | **mTORC1 ON:** protein & lipid synthesis run; autophagy low. |
| **No amino acids** | On | Off (Rags release mTORC1) | High ATP (AMPK off) | Rheb may hold GTP (loss can draw TSC); mTORC1 in cytoplasm | **mTORC1 OFF:** translation slows; autophagy starts, returning amino acids. |
| **No energy** | On | On | Low ATP, high AMP (**AMPK ON**) | AMPK activates TSC2 (Rheb-GDP) & brakes Raptor | **mTORC1 OFF (vetoed):** fat & cholesterol synthesis slow; fat burning & muscle glucose uptake rise. |

**Predict.** A cell lacks TSC2, keeping Rheb GTP-bound without growth factors. Will rapamycin still block mTORC1? Why?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Largely, yes. FKBP12–rapamycin binds mTOR's FRB domain, downstream of TSC2 and Rheb, and narrows substrate access whatever Rheb's state. The block is not total: rapamycin stops S6 kinase phosphorylation more completely than 4E-BP1 phosphorylation.

</details>

### Design problem

A cell should build new protein only when three things are true at once: a growth factor is present, amino acids are plentiful and ATP is plentiful. Design the logic using the parts in this lesson. Say where in the cell each condition is read, and name one mutation that would let the cell ignore each condition.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Make the output "mTORC1 is active" and require two things for it: Rheb holds GTP, and mTORC1 is on the lysosome.

- **Growth factor.** Read at the plasma membrane by a receptor and relayed by Akt, which takes the TSC complex out of action so that Rheb keeps its GTP. To ignore it: lose PTEN, so that PIP3 and Akt stay on with no growth factor.
- **Amino acids.** Read in the cytoplasm and inside the lysosome by sensors that report to the Rags, which bring mTORC1 to the lysosome. To ignore it: lock RagA or RagB in its GTP form, so that mTORC1 stays on the lysosome in a starved cell.
- **ATP.** Read in the cytoplasm by the gamma subunit of AMPK, as the AMP:ATP ratio. To ignore it: lose LKB1, so that AMPK barely switches on when AMP rises.

TSC2 is where two conditions meet. A cell without it largely ignores growth factors and has lost one of AMPK's two routes to mTORC1, but it mostly keeps the amino acid check.

</details>

## When a gauge is drugged or broken

**Rapamycin.** T cells need mTORC1 to grow and divide when **interleukin-2**, an immune signaling protein, stimulates them. Rapamycin (**sirolimus**) blocks that step and was approved in the United States in 1999 to prevent kidney transplant rejection. Because other tissues use mTORC1 too, mouth ulcers and raised blood sugar are among its side effects.

**Tuberous sclerosis.** People born with a faulty copy of *TSC1* or *TSC2* develop benign growths in the brain, kidneys, skin and other organs. Without a working TSC brake, Rheb stays on and mTORC1 largely stops waiting for a growth factor; rapamycin-family drugs shrink some of these growths.

**Metformin.** The most widely prescribed type 2 diabetes drug still has an unsettled mechanism. The liver (reducing glucose output) was long thought its main site, though the gut now looks important too. At high concentrations metformin partly inhibits mitochondrial complex I, raising AMP and activating AMPK. At lower, clinically relevant concentrations it has been reported to activate AMPK at the lysosome without changing AMP. Whether AMPK explains the blood-glucose drop is also argued: mice lacking liver AMPK still respond, and other liver and gut targets have been proposed.

## Going deeper

### Amino acid sensors upstream of the Rags

In 2011 Roberto Zoncu and colleagues reported that part of the amino acid reading occurs inside the lysosome and requires its membrane proton pump. Sensors found since include **Sestrin2** (leucine) and **CASTOR1** (arginine) in the cytoplasm, and the lysosomal membrane transporter **SLC38A9**—all reporting to the Rags. Which sensor matters most in which tissue, and where the working pool of Rheb sits, are not settled.

### Beyond AMP: ADP and calcium inputs to AMPK

**ADP** on the gamma subunit also protects Thr172 from phosphatases, though at higher concentrations than AMP, and only AMP gives the allosteric push. Separately, **CaMKK2** phosphorylates Thr172 when calcium rises, with no change in AMP.

### AMPK and ULK1 in autophagy

mTORC1 phosphorylates **ULK1** to keep autophagy off. In the 2011 model, AMPK phosphorylates other sites on ULK1 to switch autophagy on, but later studies complicate AMPK's side of this wiring, so hold it loosely.
