    blurb: >-
      Tie a band around a limb tightly enough to stop arterial inflow, or loosely enough to stop
      only venous return, and see where blood collects; press along a vein and see which way it
      refills. Harvey used these on the human arm to show the direction of flow.
  - name: "Isolated heart and heart-lung preparations"
    blurb: >-
      Keep a heart beating outside the body (Frank's frog hearts) or with only its lungs
      (Starling's dogs), and control its filling and the resistance it pumps against. Because
      nervous control is removed and the experimenter sets filling, load and the perfusing fluid, changes in output can be traced to the
      heart itself.
  - name: "Electrocardiography"
    blurb: >-
      Record from electrodes on the skin the small voltages the heart produces as excitation
      spreads through it. Willem Einthoven's string galvanometer made reliable human recordings
      possible early in the twentieth century, work recognized with the 1924 Nobel Prize in
      Physiology or Medicine; the QRS complex marks the depolarization of the ventricles.
  - name: "Intraventricular and vascular pressure recording"
    blurb: >-
      Place pressure catheters in a ventricle, an artery or both and record pressure through each
      beat. Comparing two regions of one ventricle at the same moment, as Wang and colleagues did
      in anesthetized pythons, shows whether a chamber without a full wall works as one pump or two.

An adult human holds about 5 litres of blood, yet at rest each side of the heart pumps roughly that much every minute. The first film showed the left side paying far more pressure for that same flow. What follows adds the pressures, the valves, and animals that solve it another way.

> **The one idea.** At a given flow, the pressure a circuit costs depends on how hard it is to push through, its **resistance**, just as water needs more push through a narrow pipe than a wide one: **pressure drop = flow × resistance**.
>
> *Scope:* beat-averaged pressures in a steady state. The rule is bookkeeping, not a claim about control: in a living body the heart and vessels set the flow together, and changing a resistance can change the flow. Within one beat, each valve follows the pressures on either side of it at that moment.

## Bulk flow, open and closed

Diffusion is quick over micrometres but far too slow over centimetres ([size, scale and surface](/lessons/size-and-scaling/)), so a large animal relies on **bulk flow**: a pump drives the whole fluid, and diffusion does only the last short step. A **circulatory system** is that pump, the fluid and the spaces it flows through.

In an **open circulatory system**, the fluid, called **hemolymph** because it is blood and tissue fluid in one, leaves the vessels and flows through the **hemocoel**, a set of body spaces that bathe the organs directly. In a **closed circulatory system**, blood stays inside vessels, and exchange happens across the thin walls of **capillaries**, the smallest vessels.

Insects such as grasshoppers and Drosophila are open: a tubular heart along the back pumps hemolymph forward, and it drifts back over the organs into the heart through small valved openings, the ostia. It carries little oxygen, because air reaches the tissues mostly through air tubes, the tracheae ([gas exchange](/lessons/gas-exchange/)). Most molluscs, such as snails and clams, are also open.

Open and closed do not split invertebrates from vertebrates. Earthworms are closed, with muscular vessels that pump. Squid and octopuses, which are molluscs, are closed too, with two **branchial hearts** that push blood through the gills and a systemic heart that sends it to the body. All vertebrates are closed. (See [animal body plans](/lessons/animal-body-plans/).)

## Through the mammalian heart, valve by valve

An **atrium** is a receiving chamber and a **ventricle** a pumping chamber. An **artery** carries blood away from the heart and a **vein** carries it toward the heart, whatever its oxygen content. "Right" and "left" mean the animal's own right and left. In a mammal after birth, the route is:

1. The superior and inferior **venae cavae** bring oxygen-poor blood from the body to the right atrium.
2. It passes the **tricuspid valve** into the right ventricle.
3. The right ventricle pushes it through the **pulmonary valve** into the pulmonary trunk and arteries, to the lungs.
4. The **pulmonary veins** return oxygen-rich blood to the left atrium.
5. It passes the **mitral valve** into the left ventricle.
6. The left ventricle pushes it through the **aortic valve** into the aorta, and on to the body.

So the pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood and the pulmonary veins oxygen-rich blood. Each valve is a one-way door: it opens when pressure behind it exceeds pressure ahead, and closes when that reverses.

**Systole** is a chamber's contraction phase and **diastole** its relaxation phase. For the adult human left ventricle at rest, one beat has four phases (pressures in **mmHg**, millimetres of mercury):

- **Filling** (diastole). Atrium above ventricle, so the mitral valve is open and blood flows in; the atrium's own contraction adds the last part.
- **Isovolumic contraction** (systole; same volume). Ventricle above atrium, so the mitral valve shuts; still below the aorta, so the aortic valve stays shut.
- **Ejection** (systole). Ventricle passes aortic pressure (about 80 mmHg), the aortic valve opens, and blood leaves; both peak near 120 mmHg.
- **Isovolumic relaxation** (diastole). Ventricle falls below the aorta, so the aortic valve shuts; both stay shut until ventricular pressure drops below atrial pressure and the mitral valve opens again.

So in "120 over 80 mmHg", the 80 is the aorta's lowest pressure, not the ventricle's, which falls to a few mmHg as it fills. The right ventricle runs the same cycle against the lungs' low resistance: its pulmonary valve opens once its pressure passes pulmonary-artery pressure, and it peaks near 25 mmHg, about a fifth of the left's.

## One circuit or two

In **single circulation**, blood passes through the heart once per loop; in **double circulation**, twice: once on a **pulmonary circuit** to the gas-exchange organ and back, and once on a **systemic circuit** to the rest of the body and back.

**Teleost fish** (most bony fish, such as trout) have single circulation, the arrangement the first film priced, with one atrium filling the one ventricle.

**Birds and mammals** have four chambers that keep the circuits fully separate, each with its own pump.

**Frogs** have two atria and one ventricle. The right atrium receives body blood, including oxygen-rich blood from the skin; the left receives lung blood. The ventricle feeds a **pulmocutaneous circuit** (lungs and skin, since frog skin exchanges gas too) and a systemic circuit; the two streams are thought to stay partly separate in it, with some mixing.

**Pythons** have one ventricle, partly divided by a muscular ridge that lets it run at two different pressures during contraction. You will work the numbers in the design problem.

**Crocodilians** have four chambers, but the right ventricle opens into both the pulmonary artery and a left aorta. A small opening, the **foramen of Panizza**, joins the left aorta to the right aorta, which the left ventricle fills at body pressure. So the left aorta usually holds more pressure than the right ventricle makes, keeping the left aortic valve shut.

**Predict.** If resistance to flow into the lungs rises, where can right-ventricle blood go?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Right ventricular pressure climbs until it passes left-aortic pressure; the left aortic valve then opens and part of the output leaves by the left aorta, bypassing the lungs: a **shunt**.

</details>

None of these hearts is a step toward the mammal's: a frog heart that sends blood to the skin, or a crocodile heart that bypasses the lungs, works well in its own animal.

## Harvey's numbers, redone

Beyond the second film's ligatures, Harvey pressed along a vein to show that its valves pass blood only toward the heart. His argument did not show how arteries connect to veins: Marcello Malpighi saw capillaries in the frog lung in 1661.

**Worked problem.** Redo Harvey's calculation for an illustrative adult human at rest in a steady state, with a heart rate of 75 beats per min, an end-diastolic volume (EDV, the ventricle's volume when full) of 120 mL and an end-systolic volume (ESV, the volume left after ejection) of 50 mL. **Ejection fraction** is the share of the full volume each beat ejects.

> **stroke volume = EDV - ESV**   and   **cardiac output = heart rate x stroke volume**   and   **ejection fraction = stroke volume / EDV**

1. *Stroke volume.* 120 mL - 50 mL = 70 mL per beat.
2. *Cardiac output.* 75 beats per min x 70 mL per beat = 5,250 mL per min, or 5.25 L per min, for one ventricle; the two pump in series, so their outputs are not added.
3. *Ejection fraction.* 70 / 120 = 0.583, or 58.3 percent.
4. *Harvey's comparison.* Assume 5.0 L of blood in the body. A volume equal to all of it passes through one side of the heart in 5.0 / 5.25 = 0.95 min, about 57 seconds. That is an average turnover time; some blood takes short routes and some longer ones. In half an hour that side pumps 5.25 x 30 = 157.5 L, more than 30 times the body's blood; in a day, 5.25 x 1,440 = 7,560 L. The second film's halved stroke volume still gives about 79 L in half an hour (35 x 75 x 30 mL), nearly 16 times the body's blood.

## The rate dial: pacemaker and conduction

The second film named the rate dial of a healthy mammalian heart, the **sinoatrial (SA) node**, near the entry of the superior vena cava, but not how it keeps time. Its cells drift slowly upward in voltage to the threshold for an impulse, fire, and start again; that drift, the **prepotential**, sets the heart rate. Two mechanisms are thought to drive the drift: currents across the cell membrane, and rhythmic release of calcium inside the cells. How much each contributes is still debated ([the action potential](/lessons/action-potential/) covers how impulses are made).

Excitation spreads across both atria, then reaches the **atrioventricular (AV) node**, where conduction slows.

**Predict.** Why would a heart slow its own signal here?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The delay lets the atria finish contracting before the ventricles start. Without it the two would contract almost together, and the atria's push, the last part of filling, would come too late.

</details>

Excitation then runs down the bundle of His, its right and left branches, and the Purkinje fibres in the ventricle walls.

Besides the second film's nerves (sympathetic speeds the SA node, the parasympathetic vagus slows it), circulating adrenaline raises the rate. The control circuits, including the baroreflex, are in [the hypothalamus and autonomic system](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/), Otto Loewi's frog hearts in [transmitter systems](/lessons/transmitter-systems/), and how adrenaline speeds the pacemaker inside the cell in [cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/).

## The stroke volume dial: filling, contractility and load

The second film named three inputs to stroke volume, but not which way filling turns the dial.

**Predict.** In Starling's dog heart-lung preparation, the heart is cut off from its nerves and a reservoir sets how much blood returns to the right atrium. Raise the reservoir. What does output do?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It rises to match. Patterson, Piper and Starling reported in 1914 that when more blood returned, the heart filled further and its output rose; Otto Frank had found in frog hearts in 1895 that, over a range, more filling gave a stronger contraction. With nerves removed, the change came from the heart itself.

</details>

**Preload** is how much the ventricle is filled and stretched before it contracts. By the **Frank-Starling relation**, over the normal range stroke volume rises along a curve as end-diastolic volume rises. Sympathetic stimulation raises **contractility**, the strength of contraction at a given filling, and so lifts the whole curve. The third input, **afterload**, is the pressure the ventricle ejects against; vessel resistance helps set it ([blood and vessels](/lessons/blood-and-vessels/)).

### Design problem

Wang and colleagues (Journal of Experimental Biology, 2003) reported mean arterial pressures of 75.7 cm H2O in the systemic circulation and 11.6 cm H2O in the pulmonary artery of conscious Python molurus. Does one ventricle mean one pressure? Convert the values to mmHg (1 mmHg = 1.36 cm H2O), say what they imply about how the ventricle works, and design a measurement that would test your answer.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Convert: 75.7 / 1.36 = 55.7 mmHg, and 11.6 / 1.36 = 8.5 mmHg. The systemic value is about 6.5 times the pulmonary one (75.7 / 11.6 = 6.5, the same ratio in either unit).

One open chamber would push the same peak pressure into both outflows while their valves are open. A gap in mean pressure alone could come from how the two sets of arteries drain between beats, but Wang's systolic peaks were far apart too, so the ventricle behaves as two regions during contraction. Wang and colleagues' simultaneous recordings inside the ventricle of anesthetized snakes placed the difference at the muscular ridge, and Jensen and colleagues (Journal of Experimental Biology, 2010), imaging python hearts by ultrasound and angioscopy, saw the ridge press against a structure called the bulbuslamelle during systole and divide the ventricle by function.

To test it, record pressure in both regions at once through the beat: separate systolic peaks support functional division. Compare a frog. Separate pressures do not rule out some mixing of blood, so measure that separately.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Python pressures

Among reptiles with an undivided ventricle, pressure separation like the python's is known mainly in pythons and monitor lizards; in other snakes studied, such as vipers and garter snakes, it does not occur.

### The crocodile shunt can be driven

Resistance to flow into the lungs can rise actively. Connective-tissue nodules in the right ventricle's pulmonary outflow tract, the cog-tooth valve, move under active control and restrict flow to the lungs, raising right ventricular pressure toward the shunt threshold (Axelsson and colleagues, Journal of Experimental Biology, 1996; Franklin and Axelsson, Nature, 2000).

### Fish chambers and the bird-mammal split

Blood reaching a teleost heart first collects in a sinus venosus before the atrium, and the ventricle ejects into the bulbus arteriosus, an elastic outflow rich in smooth muscle rather than heart muscle. Its low stiffness damps pressure swings before the delicate gill capillaries (Andersen and Østbye, Genes, 2025; Matsuki and colleagues, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA, 2026).

Birds and mammals reached four chambers independently; birds keep the right arch of the aorta, mammals the left (see [reading phylogenetic trees](/lessons/reading-phylogenetic-trees/)).

### Pacemaker and conduction detail

In rabbit SA node tissue, Brown and DiFrancesco (Journal of Physiology, 1980) recorded a current active over the pacemaker voltage range that adrenaline increased.

A textbook schematic for adult humans places the AV delay described above inside the PR interval on an electrocardiogram, an interval that also includes the spread across the atria.
