## The big idea

Children reach into pockets and toy boxes and know what they have grabbed without looking. That everyday skill is the sense of touch at work.

People have five basic senses, and each goes with a body part: eyes for sight, ears for hearing, nose for smell, tongue for taste and skin for touch. Skin can tell rough from smooth, hard from soft, and warm from cold. It covers the whole body, but fingertips feel the finest detail.

The feely bag takes sight away, so touch has to do the job alone. Your child finds out what touch can tell them (shape, texture, warm or cold, heavy or light) and what it cannot (colour, words printed on a label). Then they sort describing words to the senses they belong to.

Virginia’s kindergarten standard asks children to match each sense to its body part and to use the senses to describe things. It gives word lists for each sense, and this activity uses them.

<SafetyNote>
- Stay with your child the whole time. Pack the bag yourself and check every object before it goes in.
- Nothing sharp, hot, frozen, breakable or alive goes in the bag. A spoon from the fridge is fine; skip ice and anything from the freezer.
- Use objects too big to fit inside a toilet-paper tube, and check soft ones such as the sponge and mitten squashed flat too. The tube is a bit wider and longer than the official under-3 test cylinder, so it is a cautious check. Keep marbles, beads, coins, batteries, magnets and other small things away from children under 4.
- Check for allergies before you choose objects. Leave out balloons and rubber gloves if anyone has a latex allergy. Use craft-store feathers, not ones found outside.
- For the two-point test, you bend the paper clip and fold a small piece of tape over each cut end so the tips are blunt, then test them on your own hand first. Touch lightly, only enough to dent the skin. Test hands, arms and feet only, never the face, lips, eyes or ears. With a class, children ask before touching a partner, and anyone can say "stop."
- Shake out and look over any pinecone or stone from outside for insects, spiders and sap before it goes in the bag. Wash hands with soap and water afterwards if any objects came from outside.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Fill the bag.** Out of sight, put 8 to 10 objects in the pillowcase. Choose a mix: rough and smooth, hard and soft, heavy and light. Keep the two room-temperature spoons and the fridge spoon aside for later.
2. **Feel and describe.** Your child puts one hand in and holds one object without looking. Before guessing, they give you three describing words, such as "bumpy, hard, light." Write the words down.
3. **Guess, then look.** Now they guess and pull it out. Ask, "What did your hand know that your eyes don’t?" and "What do your eyes know that your hand didn’t?" Touch knows warm and heavy; eyes know colour.
4. **Two spoons.** Put the metal spoon and the wooden spoon in the bag. Ask, "They have been in the same room all day. Will one feel colder?" Then your child feels both. Most children find the metal one feels colder. Both are at room temperature, but metal draws heat out of fingers faster.
5. **The fridge spoon.** Dry the cold spoon with a towel in front of your child, then hand it to them with their eyes closed. Ask, "Is it wet or dry?" Many children say wet. Wipe it on a dry towel together and check the towel. It usually stays dry. On a humid day, water from the air can form on a cold spoon, the way it does on a cold glass; if the towel gets damp, that is a real finding too.
6. **Feel with other skin.** With your child’s eyes closed, brush the feather on a fingertip, then the back of the hand, an elbow and a foot. Ask, "Can you feel it? Where can you feel it best?"
7. **Sort the words.** Write sense words on cards: sweet, sour, bitter, salty, rough, smooth, hard, soft, cold, warm, loud, high, low, bright, dull, strong, faint. Lay out five cards labelled eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin, and sort. "Soft" fits both skin and ears, so it can go in two places.
8. **Two points or one (ages 8 to 10).** You unbend a large paper clip into a U with the ends 1 cm apart and tape over each cut end so the tips are blunt. With your child’s eyes closed, you touch either one end or both ends to a fingertip, then the back of the hand, then the forearm. Mix one and two at random. They say "one" or "two." Repeat with the ends 2 cm and then 4 cm apart.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder what this is. What three words describe it before you guess?"
- "What do you think will happen if we swap the metal spoon for the wooden one?"
- "Is this spoon wet or just cold? How could we find out without looking?"
- "Can you feel the feather on your elbow? Where do you think you will feel it best?"
- "What do you think will happen when I touch your arm with two points close together?"
- "Which sense would tell you this orange is orange? Which would tell you it smells sweet?"

## Why it works

Skin holds several kinds of sensors called **receptors**, special cells or nerve endings that each respond mainly to one kind of change. Some respond to pressure and vibration. Others respond to warmth, to cold, or to damage that we feel as pain.

Fingertips have many touch receptors packed close together. That is why they can tell two points apart when the points are only a few millimetres apart. On the forearm, the points often need to be several centimetres apart before they feel like two, and the back of the hand falls between the palm and the forearm. Exact numbers vary between people and with how the test is done.

Skin does not measure the temperature of an object directly. It senses its own temperature, which drops faster when heat leaves it quickly. Metal pulls heat away much faster than wood, so it feels colder at the same temperature. Skin also has no sensor for wetness. Research suggests the brain judges wetness from cold and touch signals together, which is why a cold, dry spoon can feel damp.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Use five or six objects with big differences: a pinecone, a sponge, a stone, a mitten, an orange. Ask for one describing word instead of three. For the word sort, use picture cards (an eye, an ear, a nose, a tongue, a hand) and read the words aloud. Skip the two-point test. Your child can draw the object they liked best instead of writing.

### Ages 8 to 10
Do the two-point test as a fair test: same paper clip, same gentle press, ten tries on each spot with one or two points mixed at random. Count correct answers and make a bar graph for fingertip, back of hand and forearm. Measure, with a ruler, the smallest gap that still feels like two on each spot. On a fingertip, also try a gap smaller than 1 cm, such as 5 mm. Ask them to predict how a toe will compare first, then test only the toe, never the lips or face.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

If your child peeks, turn it into a rule they enforce: they can close their eyes and you hold the bag. If guessing gets too easy, use objects of the same shape, such as a tennis ball, an orange and a smooth stone. In the two-point test, children sometimes say "two" for a single point. That is normal. It is why you mix one and two points and count correct answers.
