techniques:
  - name: "Bottom-trawl fish survey"
    blurb: >-
      A boat pulls a net along the river bottom for the same short time at many places,
      and scientists count the fish caught. The counts become a survey index that shows whether a
      fish is getting more or less common from year to year.
  - name: "DNA diet study"
    blurb: >-
      Scientists collect bits of DNA from inside a fish's gut and match them to known species. The
      matches show what kinds of animals and plants the fish ate, even when the food is too broken

  - name: "Aquarium experiment"
    blurb: >-
      Fish are kept in several tanks that differ in one condition, such as how salty the water is.
      Comparing survival and growth across the tanks shows how that one condition affects the fish.

**In the 1970s and 1980s, people put blue catfish into Virginia’s James, Rappahannock and York river systems. Today these big fish are common in parts of those rivers.**

Blue catfish are native to the Mississippi River basin and other waters outside Virginia’s Bay rivers. People stocked them here to give anglers big fish to catch. Their numbers grew fast in the 1990s. What could this new fish mean for the crabs, small fish and native catfish already living there?

## Explain: follow the change

<WordBank terms={['habitat', 'population', 'land use', 'habitat destruction', 'pollution', 'native species', 'invasive species', 'competition']} />

- A **habitat** is where an organism lives and finds the food, water and shelter it needs ([more on habitats](/lessons/habitat-and-niche/)). A **population** is every member of one species in one area at one time.
- **Land use** means how people use land: for farms, homes, roads or forest. Rain can carry fertilizer and manure from farm fields into streams.
- Taking wood, coal, fish or gravel changes an ecosystem. Digging gravel out of a stream changes the streambed.
- **Habitat destruction** removes or ruins habitat, such as filling a marsh. Animals there must move or die, so local populations often drop.
- **Pollution** is harmful material added to air, water or soil. Extra fertilizer can feed fast algae growth. Later, the water can run low on oxygen ([follow the steps](/lessons/eutrophication-and-big-changes/)).

Habitat destruction, pollution and invasive species are three ways people disrupt ecosystems. A food web links species by who eats whom ([food webs](/lessons/food-webs-and-energy/)), so one change can spread: some populations go up, and others go down.

People and ecosystems also affect each other through climate, water supply, air quality, energy production, ocean acidification and waste management. Ocean acidification means ocean water becomes more acidic as it takes in carbon dioxide from the air.

Human effects are not always negative. In Virginia’s coastal bays, people spread eelgrass seeds. A 2020 study reported that underwater grass returned and the total mass of fish rose ([Helping Ecosystems Recover](/lessons/restoring-ecosystems/)).

## Explain: a new fish and shared food

A **native species** lives in a place naturally, without people bringing it. An **invasive species** is brought to a new place, spreads and causes harm to native species or people; being new is not enough.

| | Blue catfish | White catfish |
|---|---|---|
| Native to Virginia’s Bay rivers? | No (Mississippi River basin and other waters) | Yes |
| How it got here | People stocked it: James and Rappahannock in the 1970s, York River system in 1985 | Already here |
| What it eats | Plants and animals, including fish and sometimes blue crabs | Prey similar to what blue catfish eat |

Blue catfish can grow about as long as many adults are tall.

The first film showed **competition**: species need the same limited resource, such as food or space ([more on competition](/lessons/competition-and-cooperation/)). No fight is required. Scientists report that the two catfish eat similar prey, so they may compete.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/people-change-habitats/competition-without-a-fight.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two underwater views of the same river bottom. On the left, two pale white catfish swim near plenty of small fish and crabs. On the right, a much larger blue catfish swims beside the same two white catfish, and only a few small fish and one crab are left. No fish is fighting." caption="Competition needs a shared, limited resource, but it does not need a fight." />

Shared food is like pizza at a party: a new guest can leave less for others. Unlike pizza, river prey reproduce, move and change with the seasons.

If blue catfish eat many blue crabs, fewer may remain for other crab eaters, including people.

Compare each fish only with itself, because the two indexes use different math and river areas. Three more cautions. Values from 2015 on were adjusted for a new boat and net. The years are not evenly spaced. And the white index was 0.06 in 1994 but 1.31 in 1995, so the size of a drop depends on which years you pick.

Worked comparison: the blue index went from 8.97 in 1995 to 54.42 in 2022.

**later index ÷ earlier index = 54.42 ÷ 8.97 ≈ 6.1**

So the 2022 blue index was about six times the 1995 index. That is a pattern, not its cause.

### Design problem

A town plans homes beside a creek. Some people want lawns to the water. Others want a strip of plants along the bank as habitat. Plan a test with soil trays: does a plant strip keep soil out of the creek?

**Predict 5.** Which tray should release less soil: bare soil or soil with a plant strip? What result would change your mind?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does a strip of grass at the low end of a soil tray reduce soil washed off by rain?
- **Independent variable:** a grass-sod strip 10 cm wide at the low end, or bare soil.
- **Dependent variable:** the runoff soil mass, in grams, after the water dries in a pan.
- **Control:** bare-soil trays with no grass strip.
- **Constants:** the same soil, tray size, tilt, and 1 liter of water poured from the same watering can for 1 minute.
- **Repeated trials:** three trays of each kind; find each mean.
- **Safety:** wear gloves, wipe spills so no one slips, and wash your hands.
- **Meaning:** less soil from grass trays supports keeping plants along the bank. A tray is a model, not a real creek.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

When people change an ecosystem, populations may rise or fall. In the James River surveys, blue catfish became more common while native white catfish became rarer. Similar prey makes competition possible, even without a fight, but the survey shows a pattern, not its cause. Land use, habitat destruction and pollution can also change populations, and restoration can help.

**Science practices you used:** reading a graph and table, finding a mean, comparing change over time, testing a model and naming its limits, planning a fair test, and making a claim from evidence.

## Going deeper

### How the catfish survey is done

Each bottom tow lasts five minutes and is made in daytime, from September to November.

### Eelgrass restoration in numbers

People spread about 74.5 million eelgrass seeds in Virginia’s coastal bays. The 2020 study reported that underwater grass grew back across 3,612 hectares.

### The longest blue catfish

The U.S. Geological Survey lists the longest blue catfish at 165 cm.
