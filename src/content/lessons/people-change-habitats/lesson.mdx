## Explain: people are part of ecosystems

<WordBank terms={['habitat', 'population', 'land use', 'habitat destruction', 'pollution', 'native species', 'invasive species', 'competition']} />

Your lunch, your tap water and the wood in your house all came from ecosystems. People are living parts of ecosystems. We depend on them for food, water, energy and other resources.

A **habitat** is the place where an organism lives and finds the food, water and shelter it needs ([more on habitats](/lessons/habitat-and-niche/)). A **population** is all the members of one species living in one area at the same time.

People change habitats in many ways:

- **Land use** is how people use an area of land, such as for farms, homes, roads or forest.
- Farming changes fields. Rain can carry fertilizer and manure from fields into streams.
- Taking resources from nature, such as wood, coal, fish or gravel, changes habitats too. Digging gravel out of a stream changes the shape of the streambed.

Some of these actions disrupt ecosystems:

- **Habitat destruction** is damage that removes or ruins a habitat, such as filling in a marsh to build homes. Animals that lived there must move or die, so their populations there often drop.
- **Pollution** is harmful material added to air, water or soil. Extra fertilizer in a river can feed fast algae growth, and later the water can run low on oxygen ([the steps are here](/lessons/eutrophication-and-big-changes/)).
- Bringing in an invasive species is a third kind, explained next.

A food web links species by who eats whom ([food webs](/lessons/food-webs-and-energy/)). So a change in one population can spread through the whole web. Some populations go up, and others go down.

People and ecosystems also affect each other through climate, water supply, air quality, energy production, ocean acidification and waste management. Ocean acidification means ocean water becoming more acidic as it takes in carbon dioxide from the air.

Not all human impacts are negative. In Virginia’s coastal bays, people spread eelgrass seeds. A 2020 study reported that underwater grass grew back there, and the total mass of fish there rose ([Helping Ecosystems Recover](/lessons/restoring-ecosystems/)).

## Explain: a new fish in the river

A **native species** is a species that lives in a place naturally, without people bringing it. An **invasive species** is a species brought to a new place that spreads and causes harm to native species or to people. Being new is not enough; it must spread and cause harm.

| | Blue catfish | White catfish |
|---|---|---|
| Native to Virginia’s Bay rivers (James, Rappahannock, York)? | No. Native to the Mississippi River basin and other waters | Yes |
| How it got here | People stocked it: James and Rappahannock in the 1970s, York River system in 1985 | Already here |
| What it eats | Plants and animals, including fish and sometimes blue crabs | Prey similar to what blue catfish eat |

Blue catfish can get very big, about as long as many adults are tall.

**Competition** is when two or more species need the same resource, such as food or space, and there is not enough for all of them ([more on competition](/lessons/competition-and-cooperation/)). Competition does not need a fight. If blue catfish eat many of the small fish and crabs that white catfish also eat, less food is left for the white catfish. Scientists report that the two catfish eat similar prey, so they may compete.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/people-change-habitats/competition-without-a-fight.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: two underwater views of the same river bottom. On the left, two pale white catfish swim near plenty of small fish and crabs. On the right, a much larger blue catfish swims beside the same two white catfish, and only a few small fish and one crab are left. No fish is fighting." caption="Competition does not need a fight. If blue catfish eat many of the small fish and crabs that white catfish also eat, less food is left for the white catfish." />

A river’s food supply is a bit like a pizza at a party. If a new guest eats a lot, less is left for everyone else. The comparison breaks because a river’s food grows back, and its amount changes with the seasons.

Effects can spread. If blue catfish eat many blue crabs, fewer crabs may be left for other crab eaters, including people.

### Design problem

A town plans new homes beside a creek. Some people want lawns down to the water. Others want to keep a strip of plants along the bank as habitat. Plan a test, using trays of soil, of whether a strip of plants keeps soil out of the creek.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does a strip of grass at the low end of a soil tray reduce the soil washed off by rain?
- **Independent variable:** a strip of grass sod 10 cm wide at the low end, or bare soil.
- **Dependent variable:** the mass of soil in the runoff, in grams, after the water dries in a pan.
- **Control:** trays of bare soil with no grass strip.
- **Constants:** the same soil, tray size, tilt, and 1 liter of water poured from the same watering can for 1 minute.
- **Repeated trials:** three trays of each kind; find each mean.
- **Safety:** wear gloves, wipe up spills so no one slips, and wash your hands.
- **Meaning:** less soil from the grass trays supports keeping plants along the bank. A tray is a model, not a real creek.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

When people brought blue catfish into Virginia rivers, the new fish spread. In the James River surveys, blue catfish became far more common while native white catfish became rarer. The two eat similar prey, so they may compete for food without fighting. Land use, habitat destruction and pollution change populations too.

**Science practices you used:** reading a data table and finding a mean, comparing changes over time, testing a model and naming its limits, planning a fair test, and making a claim from evidence.

## Going deeper

### How the catfish survey is done

The survey uses five-minute daytime bottom tows, made from September to November each year. The catfish index uses those tows. It counts older fish, age 1 and up, judged by length.

### Eelgrass restoration in numbers

In Virginia’s coastal bays, people spread about 74.5 million eelgrass seeds. The 2020 study reported that underwater grass grew back over an area of 3,612 hectares.

### The longest blue catfish

The U.S. Geological Survey lists the longest blue catfish at 165 cm.
