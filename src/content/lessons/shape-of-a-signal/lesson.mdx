      - "It rises in steps as neighbouring subunits phosphorylate each other"

    rationale: >-
      At a high spike rate calmodulin is more often bound to two neighbouring subunits at once,

  - stem: "Which arrangement makes a pathway's activity rise and fall repeatedly while the stimulus stays steady?"

      - "Positive feedback that acts without delay"
      - "Negative feedback that acts after a delay"
      - "Positive feedback that acts after a delay"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Negative feedback that acts after a delay corrects too late, so if the feedback is strong enough activity overshoots in each
      direction and oscillates, as NF-κB does with IκBα and as the three-repressor ring does in E. coli. Negative feedback

**A cell reads four things in a signal besides which molecule it is: how much, how long, how often and where.** In PC12 cells—a line of rat adrenal tumour cells that grow nerve-like processes with nerve growth factor—one growth factor orders division and another orders differentiation, both through the same kinase, ERK. The first film introduced how a downstream clock decodes duration and spike rate; here are the wiring, proteins and evidence behind it.

## The idea: a signal has a shape, and the cell reads it

Calcium, cyclic AMP, ERK and NF-κB recur across pathways with different outcomes. Like a one-button doorbell sending different messages with a short press, a long press or rapid taps, a cell reads four features:

- **How much.** Signal size, as in dose-response curves and the switches of [signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/).
- **How long.** Minutes, or hours.
- **How often.** Repeated spikes with varying gaps.
- **Where.** Cell surface, internal membrane, or beside one target rather than another.

Reading the last three takes a **decoder**: a downstream part whose own timing or position makes it answer one pattern and ignore another.

<Support>**The one idea.** Cells read duration and frequency against the timing of a decoder; delayed negative feedback makes pulses, and location restricts which targets answer. **Scope:** This covers how cells decode and shape signals beyond molecule identity and dose. Delayed negative feedback oscillates only when feedback is strong enough and turnover is fast enough; liquid-like protein clusters are established in reconstituted membranes and still debated in living cells.</Support>

## How long: a part that arrives late can time a signal

[The lesson on Ras and MAPK](/lessons/ras-to-mapk/) gave the PC12 result: epidermal growth factor (EGF) activates ERK briefly and cells divide, whereas nerve growth factor (NGF) keeps ERK active much longer and cells stop dividing and grow processes. Wiring sets that length: in PC12 cells, NGF engages a positive feedback loop that EGF does not. As a direct test, PC12 cells engineered to carry extra EGF receptors keep ERK active longer with EGF and differentiate.

PC12 cells proved that ERK duration changes cell fate; the clearest molecular mechanism was worked out for **c-Fos** in fibroblasts. ERK turns on immediate early genes from [signaling to the nucleus](/lessons/signaling-to-the-nucleus/), including *c-fos*. As the first film showed, ERK reaches c-Fos by a slow route (transcribing and translating short-lived c-Fos protein) and a fast route (ERK and an ERK-activated kinase phosphorylating near c-Fos's C-terminal end to protect it from breakdown). Picture a clinic calling your name twenty minutes after sign-in: only people still waiting are seen. After a brief signal ERK is off when c-Fos arrives, so c-Fos is made and lost; after a long signal c-Fos builds up and, as a transcription factor, activates later genes.

## How often: a decoder that holds on between spikes can count

[Calcium signaling](/lessons/calcium-signaling/) showed that calcium often arrives as brief spikes; in many cell types a stronger stimulus gives more frequent spikes of about the same height. Building on the first film's leaky-bucket picture, a fast-on decoder that loses activity with a fixed half-time multiplies its remaining activity by 1/2 each half-time, so decay is exponential:

> **fraction left at the next spike = (1/2) ^ (gap between spikes ÷ half-time of the decoder)**

With a 20-second half-time, spikes 60 seconds apart span three half-times (1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2), leaving one eighth (12.5%). Spikes 10 seconds apart span half a half-time, leaving about 70 percent to build on.

**Predict.** A decoder has a 40-second half-time and spikes arrive every 80 seconds. What fraction stays behind before the next spike—and if a mutation halves the half-time to 20 seconds, what fraction stays at the same 80-second gap?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

At 40 s, 80 s is 2 half-times, leaving (1/2)² = 1/4 (25%). At 20 s, 80 s is 4 half-times, leaving (1/2)⁴ = 1/16 (6.25%)—four times less, not half, because decay is exponential.

</details>

One molecular decoder is **calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase II (CaMKII)**, twelve kinase subunits folded shut at rest. Calcium-bound calmodulin binds a subunit, unfolds it and activates it. An active subunit can phosphorylate its neighbour—locking it into a calcium-independent active state after calcium falls—*only while the neighbour also holds calmodulin* to keep its site exposed. Rare spikes let calmodulin leave before adjacent subunits are loaded together; fast spikes catch more neighbours loaded at once, so autonomous activity climbs in steps. In 1998 Paul De Koninck and Howard Schulman delivered timed pulses of calcium and calmodulin to surface-fixed CaMKII and saw calcium-independent activity rise with pulse rate. That is a test-tube result.

Transcription factors follow the same off-rate rule. In 1998 Ricardo Dolmetsch, Keli Xu and Richard Lewis clamped calcium spikes at chosen spacings in a human T-cell line. Closely spaced spikes activated three factors, NFAT and NF-κB among them; widely spaced spikes activated only NF-κB. A likely reason is off-rate: NFAT exits the nucleus within minutes once calcium drops, whereas in B cells NF-κB stayed in the nucleus long after a brief spike ended.

**Predict.** If a mutation slowed NFAT's nuclear export tenfold without changing how fast calcium drives it in, how would NFAT's response to widely spaced spikes change?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

NFAT would answer widely spaced spikes too: slower export lengthens its nuclear persistence so active NFAT bridges longer gaps.

</details>

Not every frequency-sensitive system peaks under continuous stimulation; some need quiet gaps to recover. The brain releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone (**GnRH**) in pulses. In rhesus monkeys whose hypothalamic lesions cut off their own GnRH, one pulse an hour restored pituitary release of LH and FSH, whereas continuous GnRH shut release down via pituitary desensitization (down-regulation). Medicine uses this inversion: **leuprolide**, a slow-release GnRH relative, holds levels steady so that after an initial surge the pituitary goes quiet and testosterone falls, which is the aim in prostate cancer. A drug can change a signal's shape as well as its size.

## Pulses from feedback: a loop with a delay keeps its own time

In [signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/) negative feedback pulled pathways back to baseline. As the second film showed, adding a delay makes the correction land late so activity overshoots, and it can oscillate when feedback is strong and switch-like enough and proteins are short-lived enough.

The same **NF-κB** that bridges widely spaced calcium spikes drives its own pulses under a steady inflammatory cue. Once IκB is destroyed, as in [Toll-like receptors and NF-κB](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb/), nuclear NF-κB induces IκBα, which after a delay exports NF-κB back out; while the stimulus persists, new IκBα is destroyed and the cycle repeats, as single-cell films of fluorescent NF-κB showed in some cell lines (Nelson and colleagues, 2004). Knockout cells with a computer model showed that IκBα drives the oscillation while IκBβ and IκBε damp it (see Going deeper).

**p53** and **MDM2** also form a negative feedback loop. [Stress signals](/lessons/stress-signals/) covered Galit Lahav's 2004 single-cell films in Uri Alon's lab: p53 arrives in pulses of similar size, with larger radiation doses giving more pulses on average. MDM2 alone is not enough: a 2008 study from Lahav's lab found the pulses are driven by repeated restarts from upstream kinases ATM and Chk2 and kept uniform by a second feedback loop through Wip1.

**Predict.** After moderate radiation, human cancer cells left to pulse p53 recovered and resumed dividing. Lahav's lab then used **nutlin-3** (which blocks MDM2) on a schedule to hold p53 steady at the height of one pulse. Should steady-p53 cells recover the same way, or choose a different fate?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A different fate: cells held steady at one-pulse height often stopped dividing for good. Even at equal peak size, steady versus pulsing p53 altered cell fate.

</details>

Is a delayed negative-feedback ring enough on its own to make a clock? Michael Elowitz and Stanislas Leibler tested that in *E. coli* by wiring three repressors—proteins that switch genes off—into a ring, each silencing the next. Because protein synthesis takes time, each off-order lands late and the three chase one another around the ring; a green fluorescent readout oscillated with a period of hours in at least 40 percent of filmed cells. The clock was noisy, and it ran.

## Where: the same kinase means something else in another place

By our guiding rule, timing is half the story: **location also restricts which targets answer.** As the second film showed for [cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/) and [adaptors and scaffolds](/lessons/adaptors-and-scaffolds/), **A-kinase anchoring proteins (AKAPs)** park PKA beside specific targets so a local rise in cAMP reaches the PKA parked nearby and may never reach PKA parked elsewhere. A receptor can likewise signal from the cell surface and again from an endosome after internalization, the subject of the next lesson.

A cell can also build a place on demand—on a slide. In 2016 Michael Rosen's and Ronald Vale's laboratories placed twelve purified components from early T-cell signaling ([antigen receptors and checkpoints](/lessons/antigen-receptors-and-checkpoints/)) on an artificial membrane with no cell. Once a kinase phosphorylated the receptor tail, membrane-bound **LAT** and its partners gathered into liquid-like clusters via many weak contacts between repeated binding sites ([protein interaction modules](/lessons/protein-interaction-modules/)). Inside, kinases such as ZAP70 were concentrated and phosphatases such as CD45 excluded, favouring a signal that stays on.

**Predict.** On that artificial membrane, if you mutate LAT so it keeps only one binding site for its partners instead of several repeated sites, what happens to clustering and local signaling?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Clustering breaks down, because liquid-like clusters need multivalent weak contacts linking partners into a network. Without clusters sorting kinases from phosphatases, the local signaling boost is lost.

</details>

Separate test tube from cell. In a human T-cell line, LAT mutants that clustered more activated more ERK, yet proving a bright cellular spot is a liquid phase is harder. **Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)** bleaches one spot with a laser and times brightness returning as unbleached molecules enter. Because FRAP measures molecular exchange, proteins binding and leaving a fixed scaffold recover just like a liquid droplet. For anchored kinases, place is established; for droplets, it is established in the test tube and still argued over in the cell.

### Design problem

Two hormones raise calcium in the same cell. The first must switch on gene A and not gene B. The second must switch on gene B and not gene A. You may not add a second messenger. Design how the two calcium signals differ in time, and say what property each gene's activator must have so that it answers only its own pattern.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

With decoders that switch on fast and off slowly, spike rate alone will not do it. In the T-cell experiment, fast spikes switched on everything that slow spikes did, and more. To get two answers that exclude each other, make the signals differ in length and in height together.

Let hormone 1 give one tall, brief spike. Let hormone 2 give a low plateau that lasts.

Gene A's activator needs a high threshold and a long memory. It ignores the low plateau, switches on at once when a tall spike comes, and stays on long after calcium has fallen. NF-κB, once its inhibitor has been destroyed, behaves this way.

Gene B's activator needs a low threshold and a short memory. It is sensitive enough to answer the plateau, but it reverses within minutes when calcium falls, so one brief spike is over before enough of it has collected in the nucleus to start transcription. NFAT behaves this way.

Dolmetsch, Lewis and their colleagues saw this split in B cells in 1997. A large, brief rise in calcium activated NF-κB, and a low, sustained rise activated NFAT.

</details>

## Going deeper

### The GnRH experiment

The rhesus monkey pulse-versus-continuous experiment was done in 1978 by Ernst Knobil's laboratory (Belchetz, Plant, Nakai, Keogh and Knobil, *Science* 202:631–633), showing pituitary desensitization and down-regulation under continuous infusion.

### Who modelled NF-κB oscillations

In 2002 Alexander Hoffmann, Andre Levchenko, Martin Scott and David Baltimore paired a computer model with IκB-knockout cells, concluding that IκBα drives the oscillation while IκBβ and IκBε damp it.

### Testing whether a bright spot is a liquid

In 2019 David McSwiggen, Mustafa Mir, Xavier Darzacq and Robert Tjian reviewed FRAP in living cells and wrote: "Even proteins that engage in stable, high-affinity binding interactions can show recovery."
