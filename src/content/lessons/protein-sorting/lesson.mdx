techniques:
  - name: "Cell-free translation with microsomes and protease protection"
    blurb: >-
      Messenger RNA is translated in a cell extract with or without ER vesicles (microsomes), and
      the products are treated with protease, with or without detergent. A protein that survives
      protease only while the membranes are intact is inside the vesicles. Adding membranes during
      or after translation tells you whether entry needs the chain to be growing.
  - name: "Salt extraction and add-back"
    blurb: >-
      Membranes are washed in high salt to strip loosely bound proteins, tested for a lost
      activity, then given back a purified candidate factor. If a factor purified from the wash restores the activity,
      it is a strong candidate for what the wash removed. This is how SRP was shown to be the selective ribosome-binding
      activity.
  - name: "Import into isolated organelles"
    blurb: >-
      A radiolabelled precursor made in vitro is incubated with purified mitochondria or
      chloroplasts, then treated with protease. Protected, processed protein has been imported.
      Uncouplers, ATP depletion or a drug that locks the cargo folded test what import needs.
  - name: "Signal transplantation and mutagenesis"
    blurb: >-
      A candidate signal is fused to a passenger protein that normally stays in the cytosol, or
      the signal is mutated residue by residue, and the fusion's location is read by microscopy or
      import. Moving a passenger shows that a signal is sufficient; losing location after a
      deletion shows that it is necessary.

**Made in a test tube with no membranes, a secretory protein comes out longer than the one the cell secretes.**

Chapter 1 of the film answered why; here is what it leaves out. The text uses the field's words for the film's: the "address" is a **signal sequence**, reading "as the chain grows" is **cotranslational**, and reading "after release" is **post-translational**.

## The address

Almost every protein in a eukaryotic cell starts on a ribosome in the **cytosol**, the fluid outside the organelles (a few are made inside mitochondria and chloroplasts from their own genes), yet thousands must reach the ER, mitochondria, chloroplasts, peroxisomes or the nucleus. The inside of a membrane-bound organelle is its **lumen** (in mitochondria and chloroplasts the innermost space is called the matrix and the stroma, respectively).

A signal sequence is a continuous stretch of amino acids that carries sorting information, like a zip code on a parcel. A protein with no signal stays in the cytosol by default, which gives the field its two tests. If moving a stretch onto a protein that normally stays in the cytosol moves that protein, the stretch is **sufficient** to sort it. If deleting the stretch leaves the protein in the cytosol, it is **necessary**.

Which way a membrane protein faces, its **topology**, is set at insertion ([membrane organization and topology](/lessons/membrane-organization-topology/)); nuclear import, through pores rather than across a membrane, has [its own lesson](/lessons/nuclear-transport/). Here the question is how a protein reaches the right membrane at all.

## Into the ER while being made

The amino terminus leaves the ribosome first ([translation](/lessons/genetic-code-and-translation/)), and the ER signal is a hydrophobic stretch, usually near the amino terminus. Here is the experiment behind each of chapter 1's four steps.

1. **Recognition.** SRP is a complex of six proteins and an RNA. *Evidence:* in a translation extract made from wheat germ, SRP stopped the growth of preprolactin chains at about 70 residues: the 30-residue signal, just clear of the ribosome, plus roughly 40 residues still hidden in its tunnel. Adding ER vesicles released the stop. (In other extracts SRP only slows chain growth for a while; Going deeper.)
2. **Docking.** *Evidence:* washing dog-pancreas ER vesicles in high salt removed their ability to bind ribosomes making prolactin, and purified SRP restored it (Walter and Blobel).
3. **Handover.** Chapter 1's two "hands" are **GTPases**, subunits of SRP and of its receptor that bind GTP and then hydrolyse it. *Evidence:* in the bacterial versions of the two GTPases (3D viewer), the GTP sites face each other, so each helps the other hydrolyse GTP and the complex comes apart.
4. **Cleavage.** **Signal peptidase** works on the lumenal side of the membrane. Many membrane proteins keep an uncleaved signal that stays in the membrane as an anchor.

**Predict.** A signal's position sets the earliest moment it can be read. A secretory protein is only 60 residues long, with its signal at residues 1 to 20, and about 40 residues stay hidden in the ribosome. When has the whole signal cleared the ribosome, and what does that leave SRP?

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only when the chain is about 60 residues long (20 + 40), that is, when it is finished. SRP has almost no time to catch it, and very short secretory proteins like this can enter after release by an SRP-independent route (Going deeper). A signal at the carboxyl end is the extreme case: it leaves the ribosome only as the chain is released, too late for SRP but not for readers that work after release.

</details>

SRP is the main reader at the ribosome, not the only one, and some ER proteins, such as tail-anchored ones, enter after release (Going deeper).

## After release: mitochondria, chloroplasts, peroxisomes

Chapter 1 gave the idea and its mitochondrial and peroxisomal cases; here are the readers for all three organelles. They work away from the ribosome's exit: **TOM** on the mitochondrial outer membrane, **TOC** on the chloroplast outer envelope, and **PEX5** in the cytosol. Their cargo is usually read after release. The reader's place does not settle the timing alone; the exceptions are in Going deeper.

The table sets the four routes side by side; they differ most in the last two rows.

**Mitochondria: what the methotrexate result proves.** In chapter 1's experiment (Eilers and Schatz, 1986), methotrexate binds the passenger, the enzyme dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), and holds it folded. **Predict.** You give isolated mitochondria a matrix precursor, with or without an uncoupler (a chemical that collapses the membrane potential), then add protease. Which sample gives a protected band slightly smaller than the precursor?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Only without the uncoupler. Then the presequence is pulled through the TIM23 complex, is often cut off in the matrix, and the membranes shield the chain from protease. With the uncoupler, complete TIM23-mediated import and processing are blocked; the precursor may bind or partly engage the machinery, but that is not matrix entry. Protected, processed protein that disappears with an uncoupler is the standard sign of real import.

</details>

**Predict.** Chapter 1 left a question. Some ribosomes making mitochondrial proteins sit on the mitochondrial surface. Could their presequences be read before the chain is finished?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

They could: the ribosome is now within TOM's reach. Being close makes early reading possible, not certain. Studies of ribosomes near mitochondria suggest that some mitochondrial proteins may be imported while still being made (Going deeper).

</details>

**Chloroplasts** follow the mitochondrial pattern, through TOC in the outer envelope and **TIC** in the inner. Finished precursors of the Rubisco small subunit entered purified intact chloroplasts and were processed (Chua and Schmidt, 1978). Which ATP-driven motor pulls hardest is still debated (Going deeper).

**Peroxisomes: the exception in the last rows.** PEX7 reads PTS2 cargo as PEX5 reads PTS1. The evidence that cargo can stay folded came in 1995: Walton, Hill and Subramani found that mammalian peroxisomes imported proteins held folded by disulfide bonds or chemical cross-links, and even gold particles coated with signal-bearing protein.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Most likely it stays in the cytosol. PEX5 holds the free carboxyl end of a PTS1 deep in its groove (3D viewer), so an SKL buried inside the chain should not be read. This is an inference from the structure, not a cited experiment; a PTS2 elsewhere in the protein could still send it to peroxisomes.

</details>

## How do you know where a protein went?

Here is the protection logic as numbers.

### Worked problem: reading a protection gel

Here is a made-up data set, patterned on the Blobel and Dobberstein experiments. A secretory protein's messenger RNA is translated in reticulocyte lysate, an extract of immature rabbit red blood cells that makes protein from added messenger RNA. Some reactions include **microsomes**, sealed vesicles formed from ER membrane when cells are broken up. You read band intensity in arbitrary units. The precursor, still carrying its signal, runs at 26 kDa and a processed form at 23 kDa.

- **Lane 1, no microsomes.** 26 kDa = 100. After protease: nothing.
- **Lane 2, microsomes present during translation.** 26 kDa = 40, 23 kDa = 60. After protease: 23 kDa = 57, 26 kDa = 0. After protease plus detergent: nothing.
- **Lane 3, microsomes added after translation.** 26 kDa = 100, no 23 kDa. After protease: nothing.

Assume the added protease cannot cross an intact microsome membrane and that the bands are proportional to the amount of protein.

**Predict.** In lane 2, 40 units kept the full 26 kDa size. Were those chains inside the vesicles?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. After protease no 26 kDa band survives, so every unprocessed chain was outside and was digested. Those chains kept their signal because they never entered: the scissors are on the inside.

</details>

**Step 1: how much was processed, and was it protected?** In lane 2, 60 of 100 units are processed, so 60 percent of chains lost their signal. Of those 60, 57 survive protease:

> protected fraction = 57 / 60 = 0.95, or 95 percent

So nearly all processed chains are inside sealed vesicles.

**Step 2: was it the membrane?** Detergent dissolves the vesicles and the 23 kDa band vanishes. The protein is not resistant to protease on its own; the membrane protected it.

**Step 3: does timing matter?** This is chapter 1's predict, as data. In lane 3 there is no processing and no protection. In this assay, translocation needs ongoing translation.

**Step 4: how big was the signal?** The shift is 26 − 23 = 3 kDa. With an average residue mass of about 110 Da:

> residues removed ≈ 3,000 Da / 110 Da per residue ≈ 27 residues

That fits a signal of the usual size; preprolactin's is 30 residues.

Each classic result is one of the two tests. **Sufficient:** the first 53 residues of a yeast mitochondrial precursor carried mouse DHFR into the matrix of yeast mitochondria (the film's mouse enzyme), and SKL added to the end of a bacterial enzyme sent it into peroxisomes of mammalian cells. **Necessary:** deleting the short region at luciferase's tail, at most twelve residues, left luciferase in the cytosol.

Three warnings keep predictions honest. First, sufficiency for one passenger is not universality. Second, SKL is a specific signal; only a few conservative substitutions kept it working in luciferase. Third, position matters: a signal that works at one end may fail at the other or inside the chain. When a protein carries two signals, the outcome is empirical. Sorting continues past the ER, through [vesicle traffic](/lessons/vesicle-traffic/) and [the secretory pathway](/lessons/secretory-pathway/); lipid tails that pin proteins to membranes are in [lipid anchors](/lessons/lipid-anchors-and-addresses/).

### Design problem

Protein Y has an amino-terminal amphipathic helix and ends in SKL. Where does it go, and how would you test it?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Predict first. The amino-terminal helix looks like a matrix presequence and SKL is a PTS1, so Y could go to mitochondria, peroxisomes or both. The presequence emerges first and, if accessible, may win, but both signals are usually read after release, so treat that only as a hypothesis.

Build four GFP fusions: full-length Y, Y without the helix, Y without SKL, and Y without both. Put GFP where it hides neither signal, for example just after the helix, so SKL stays at the free carboxyl end. Image each in cultured cells with mitochondrial and peroxisomal markers, and score colocalization. Expect the double deletion in the cytosol. If deleting the helix moves Y to peroxisomes and deleting SKL leaves it in mitochondria, each signal works and the helix dominates in the full protein.

Then test import directly. Incubate radiolabelled Y with isolated mitochondria, with and without an uncoupler, and treat with protease. Protected, processed Y that disappears with the uncoupler means import, not sticking to the surface. To show it reached the matrix, open the outer membrane by swelling and repeat the protease test. A similar protease test on isolated peroxisomes checks the SKL route.

</details>

## Going deeper

### How the signal idea was found

In 1971 Günter Blobel and David Sabatini proposed that proteins bound for the ER carry a signal that sends the ribosome making them to the membrane. In 1972 César Milstein's group translated the messenger RNA for an antibody light chain in a cell-free system and got a product slightly larger than the light chain that myeloma cells secrete, which they proposed was a precursor. In 1975 Blobel and Bernhard Dobberstein tested it directly, showing that the extra piece is an address read while the chain is still on the ribosome.

### SRP details from the cell-free experiments

The 70-residue stop came from Peter Walter and Blobel in 1981 and belongs to preprolactin in the wheat germ system; in reticulocyte lysate SRP was later found to cause only a transient pause (Wolin & Walter, J Cell Biol 109:2617, 1989). In 1982 Reid Gilmore, Walter and Blobel used SRP as bait to isolate a 72,000-dalton receptor protein. Signal peptidase is a different enzyme from the SRP receptor: the SRP affinity column used to isolate the receptor separated the two activities. A 2016 structure of an active Sec61 showed the signal sitting in the channel's lateral gate after displacing one of its helices.

### Other roads into the ER

In budding yeast, rapid SRP depletion in 2018 left many proteins with cleavable signals still targeted while being made, although proteins with transmembrane domains generally needed SRP. Some proteins, especially in yeast, cross after translation through Sec61 with its partners Sec62 and Sec63; very short secretory proteins, whose signal clears the ribosome only as the chain finishes, can enter this way. Tail-anchored proteins, whose only hydrophobic segment is at the very end, are inserted after translation by other machinery (the GET, or TRC40, route).

### Mitochondria: early reading, carriers and the conducting path

In human cells, nearly a fifth of mitochondrial proteins have been reported to be imported while still being made. Such studies map ribosomes near mitochondria, so they show more directly where a protein is made than when it crosses: a sizeable minority appear to be made on the mitochondrial surface, and some may be imported during synthesis. Metabolite carriers of the inner membrane have internal signals and use a separate **TIM22** route. A 2023 study of the yeast TIM23 complex placed the protein-conducting path along its Tim17 subunit rather than along Tim23, as older diagrams show; the complex keeps its name.

### Chloroplast motors

In the stroma, chaperones such as Hsp70 and Hsp93 have long been proposed as the ATP-driven motor; recent structures implicate another, Ycf2-FtsHi, at the inner envelope, and which does most of the work is still debated. The table says the cargo crosses largely unfolded, not that it must unfold, because some folded or partly folded passengers have been reported to cross the envelope.

### The chloroplast precursor experiment and its correction

Nam-Hai Chua and Gregory Schmidt's 1978 precursors, from pea and spinach, were 4,000 to 5,000 daltons larger than the mature protein. In that paper the precursor from the alga *Chlamydomonas* did not enter those higher-plant chloroplasts, but a later study (Mishkind, Wessler and Schmidt, 1985) found it imported efficiently by spinach and pea chloroplasts, so the negative result did not hold.

### How PEX5 crosses

In *Xenopus* egg extracts in 2022, PEX5 went all the way into the lumen with its cargo, and was unfolded when pulled back out. How the membrane passes large folded cargo without leaking small solutes, and whether there is a stable pore at all, is still open.
