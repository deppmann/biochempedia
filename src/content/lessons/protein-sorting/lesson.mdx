techniques:
  - name: "Cell-free translation with microsomes and protease protection"
    blurb: >-
      Messenger RNA is translated in a cell extract with or without ER vesicles (microsomes), and
      the products are treated with protease, with or without detergent. A protein that survives
      protease only while the membranes are intact is inside the vesicles. Adding membranes during
      or after translation tells you whether entry needs the chain to be growing.
  - name: "Salt extraction and add-back"
    blurb: >-
      Membranes are washed in high salt to strip loosely bound proteins, tested for a lost
      activity, then given back a purified candidate factor. If the factor restores the activity,
      it is what the wash removed. This is how SRP was shown to be the selective ribosome-binding
      activity.
  - name: "Import into isolated organelles"
    blurb: >-
      A radiolabelled precursor made in vitro is incubated with purified mitochondria or
      chloroplasts, then treated with protease. Protected, processed protein has been imported.
      Uncouplers, ATP depletion or a drug that locks the cargo folded test what import needs.
  - name: "Signal transplantation and mutagenesis"
    blurb: >-
      A candidate signal is fused to a passenger protein that normally stays in the cytosol, or
      the signal is mutated residue by residue, and the fusion's location is read by microscopy or
      import. Moving a passenger shows that a signal is sufficient; losing location after a
      deletion shows that it is necessary.

**Made in a test tube with no membranes, a secretory protein comes out longer than the one the cell secretes.**

In 1971 Günter Blobel and David Sabatini proposed that proteins bound for the ER carry a signal that sends the ribosome making them to the membrane. In 1972 César Milstein's group translated the messenger RNA for an antibody light chain in a cell-free system and got a product slightly larger than the light chain that myeloma cells secrete, which they proposed was a precursor. In 1975 Blobel and Bernhard Dobberstein tested the idea directly: the extra piece is an address, read while the chain is still on the ribosome, and cut off once the protein has arrived. This lesson is about such addresses: what they look like, who reads them, and what happens when you move one.

## The idea: a zip code in the sequence

Almost every protein in a eukaryotic cell starts on a ribosome in the **cytosol**, the fluid that fills the cell outside the organelles. (A few are made inside mitochondria and chloroplasts from their own genes.) Yet a typical cell must deliver thousands of different proteins to the ER, mitochondria, chloroplasts in plants, peroxisomes and the nucleus. The inside of a membrane-bound organelle is its **lumen** (in mitochondria and chloroplasts the innermost space is called the matrix and the stroma).

The cell solves this with a **signal sequence**: a continuous stretch of amino acids that carries sorting information. Think of a zip code printed on the parcel. A protein with no signal stays in the cytosol by default, and a signal attached to a protein that normally stays in the cytosol can send it elsewhere. That second fact is the most useful test in this whole field: if moving a stretch of sequence moves the protein, the stretch is sufficient to sort it.

The signals differ by destination, and so does the timing:

- **ER**: a hydrophobic stretch, usually near the amino terminus, read *while the chain is being made* (cotranslational).
- **Mitochondrial matrix**: an amino-terminal presequence that tends to form an **amphipathic helix**, with positive charges on one face and hydrophobic residues on the other (see [protein structure](/lessons/protein-3d-structure/)). Read *after* translation.
- **Chloroplast stroma**: an amino-terminal transit peptide, read after translation.
- **Peroxisomal matrix**: usually a carboxyl-terminal tripeptide such as serine-lysine-leucine (SKL), called PTS1, or less often an amino-terminal PTS2. Read after translation.

How a membrane protein ends up facing the right way, its **topology**, is set at insertion; [membrane organization and topology](/lessons/membrane-organization-topology/) covers that. Here the question is how a protein reaches the right membrane at all. Nuclear import, which goes through pores instead of across a membrane, has [its own lesson](/lessons/nuclear-transport/).

## Into the ER while being made

Ribosomes and translation belong to [the genetic code and translation](/lessons/genetic-code-and-translation/); you need only that the amino terminus leaves the ribosome first. The ER route runs as a cycle, and each step was pinned down in a cell-free experiment.

1. **Recognition.** The **signal recognition particle (SRP)**, a complex of six proteins and an RNA, binds the hydrophobic signal as it emerges from the ribosome. In 1981 Peter Walter and Blobel showed, translating preprolactin in a wheat germ cell-free system, that SRP made elongation stop at a chain of about 70 residues: the 30-residue signal plus roughly 40 residues hidden inside the ribosome. Those numbers belong to preprolactin in that system; in reticulocyte lysate SRP was later found to cause only a transient pause (Wolin & Walter, J Cell Biol 109:2617, 1989).
2. **Docking.** The SRP-ribosome complex binds the **SRP receptor** on the ER membrane. Walter and Blobel found that washing dog-pancreas ER vesicles in salt removed their ability to bind ribosomes making prolactin, and purified SRP restored it. In 1982 Reid Gilmore, Walter and Blobel used SRP as bait to isolate a 72,000-dalton receptor protein.
3. **Handover.** SRP and its receptor both carry GTP-binding subunits. With GTP bound they hold together and pass the ribosome to the **Sec61 channel**, the pore through which the chain enters the ER. GTP hydrolysis then separates SRP from its receptor, and elongation resumes with the chain threading through. A 2016 structure of an active Sec61 showed the signal sitting in the channel's lateral gate after displacing one of its helices.
4. **Cleavage.** **Signal peptidase** on the lumenal side cuts off the signal. It is a different enzyme from the SRP receptor: the 1982 affinity column separated the two activities.

The GTP step works as a timer. A complex that commits only with GTP bound, and comes apart only when GTP is hydrolysed, can wait until the ribosome is safely on Sec61 before letting go.

### Worked problem: reading a protection gel

Here is a made-up data set, patterned on the Blobel and Dobberstein experiments. A secretory protein's messenger RNA is translated in reticulocyte lysate. You read band intensity in arbitrary units. The precursor runs at 26 kDa and a processed form at 23 kDa.

- **Lane 1, no microsomes.** 26 kDa = 100. After protease: nothing.
- **Lane 2, microsomes present during translation.** 26 kDa = 40, 23 kDa = 60. After protease: 23 kDa = 57, 26 kDa = 0. After protease plus detergent: nothing.
- **Lane 3, microsomes added after translation.** 26 kDa = 100, no 23 kDa. After protease: nothing.

Assume the protease cannot cross an intact membrane and that the bands are proportional to the amount of protein.

**Step 1: how much was processed, and was it protected?** In lane 2, 60 of 100 units are processed, so 60 percent of chains lost their signal. Of those 60, 57 survive protease:

> protected fraction = 57 / 60 = 0.95, or 95 percent

So nearly all processed chains are inside sealed vesicles, while every unprocessed chain is outside and digested.

**Step 2: was it the membrane?** Detergent dissolves the vesicles and the 23 kDa band vanishes. The protein is not resistant to protease on its own; the membrane protected it.

**Step 3: does timing matter?** In lane 3 there is no processing and no protection. In this assay, translocation needs ongoing translation.

**Step 4: how big was the signal?** The shift is 26 − 23 = 3 kDa. With an average residue mass of about 110 Da:

> residues removed ≈ 3,000 Da / 110 Da per residue ≈ 27 residues

That fits a signal of the usual size; preprolactin's is 30 residues.

Tail-anchored proteins, whose only hydrophobic segment is at the very end, are inserted after translation by other machinery.

## After translation: mitochondria, chloroplasts, peroxisomes

The other organelles read finished, released chains. Each needs a receptor at the outer surface, a way across, and a source of energy, and they differ in whether the cargo must unfold.

**Mitochondria.** A matrix protein's presequence is recognised by the **TOM complex** of the outer membrane and passed to the **TIM23 complex** of the inner membrane. Two energy sources drive it: the membrane potential across the inner membrane, which pulls on the positively charged presequence, and ATP used by mitochondrial Hsp70 in the matrix motor. Many presequences are then cut off. The cargo must unfold. In 1986 Eilers and Schatz showed that methotrexate, which holds a presequence-DHFR fusion folded, blocked its import. Two cautions. Metabolite carriers of the inner membrane have internal signals and use a separate **TIM22** route. And a 2023 study of the yeast complex placed the protein-conducting path in Tim17, not in Tim23 as older diagrams show.

**Chloroplasts.** Transit peptides are read by **TOC** in the outer envelope and **TIC** in the inner. In 1978 Nam-Hai Chua and Gregory Schmidt showed that finished precursors of the Rubisco small subunit from pea and spinach, 4,000 to 5,000 daltons larger than the mature protein, entered purified intact chloroplasts and were processed. In that paper the precursor from the alga *Chlamydomonas* did not enter those higher-plant chloroplasts, but a later study (Mishkind, Wessler and Schmidt, 1985) found that the algal precursor is imported efficiently by spinach and pea chloroplasts, so the negative result did not hold. Recent structures implicate an ATP-driven motor, Ycf2-FtsHi, at the inner envelope.

**Peroxisomes.** PTS1 cargo is bound in the cytosol by the receptor **PEX5**; PTS2 cargo by **PEX7**. Peroxisomes break the unfolding rule: in 1995 Walton, Hill and Subramani found that mammalian peroxisomes imported proteins held folded by cross-links, and even gold particles coated with signal-bearing protein. In *Xenopus* egg extracts in 2022, PEX5 went all the way into the lumen with its cargo, and was unfolded when pulled back out. Insertion of loaded receptor has been described as ATP-independent, but recycling PEX5 needs ubiquitin and ATP, so sustained import is not free.

## Reading and rewriting signals

Signals are modular, so you can predict and test. Put the first 53 residues of a yeast mitochondrial precursor on mouse DHFR, and the fusion enters the matrix of yeast mitochondria. Add SKL to the end of a bacterial enzyme, and it goes to peroxisomes in mammalian cells. Delete the short region, at most twelve residues, at luciferase's tail, and it stays in the cytosol.

Three warnings keep predictions honest. First, sufficiency for one passenger is not universality. Second, SKL is a specific signal; only a few conservative substitutions kept it working in luciferase. Third, position matters: a signal that works at one end may fail at the other or inside the chain. When a protein carries two signals, the outcome is empirical. Sorting continues past the ER, through [vesicle traffic](/lessons/vesicle-traffic/) and [the secretory pathway](/lessons/secretory-pathway/); lipid tails that pin proteins to membranes are in [lipid anchors](/lessons/lipid-anchors-and-addresses/).

### Design problem

Protein Y has an amino-terminal amphipathic helix and ends in SKL. Where does it go, and how would you test it?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Predict first. The amino-terminal helix looks like a matrix presequence and SKL is a PTS1, so Y could go to mitochondria, peroxisomes or both. The presequence emerges first and, if accessible, may win. Treat that as a hypothesis.

Build four GFP fusions: full-length Y, Y without the helix, Y without SKL, and Y without both. Image each in cultured cells with a mitochondrial marker and a peroxisomal marker, and score colocalization. Expect the double deletion in the cytosol. If deleting the helix moves Y to peroxisomes and deleting SKL leaves it in mitochondria, each signal works and the helix dominates in the full protein.

Then test import directly. Incubate radiolabelled Y with isolated mitochondria, with and without an uncoupler, and treat with protease. Protected, processed Y that disappears with the uncoupler means true matrix import, not sticking to the surface. A similar protease test on isolated peroxisomes checks the SKL route.
