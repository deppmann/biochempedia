## The big idea

A child who eats green beans has seen only one moment in a bean plant’s life. Growing one from seed shows the whole story, and it ends where it began: with a bean.

A bean plant goes through these stages in order. A seed takes in water and sprouts, a step called **germination**. Roots and a stem grow, then leaves. Flowers form, and pollen reaches the centre of each flower. That centre swells into a pod, which is the plant’s **fruit**, and new seeds ripen inside it. Then the plant dies. Bush beans are **annuals**, plants that live for just one growing season.

Virginia’s grade 2 standard lists exactly these stages, from seed to death. In kindergarten, children are not expected to name the stages in order. For them, it is enough to notice that the plant gets bigger, grows more leaves, and makes pods and seeds.

<SafetyNote>
- Check for food allergies first. If a child is allergic to beans or other legumes, ask their doctor before they handle the seeds or eat the pods. Stay with your child while planting and handling seeds. Keep loose seeds, soaked or dry, away from children under 4, who can choke on them or push them into a nose or ear. You snip extra seedlings and set up any grow light, and keep its cord and plug away from water.
- Use seeds labelled untreated. Treated seeds have a bright pink, blue or green coating that contains pesticide. If you already have them, do not bring them into a classroom. At home, an adult plants them, keeps them away from children, and everyone washes hands.
- Do not eat raw bean pods or seeds, and do not taste the soaked seed. Raw beans, green beans included, contain a natural toxin (a lectin) that can cause vomiting, diarrhoea and stomach pain. Boil the green pods you pick before anyone eats them, and keep the dry seeds for planting. Germany’s food-safety agency advises boiling fresh beans, green beans included, for at least 30 minutes, and not steaming them. Use that time for the pods you pick. If a child is choking or struggling to breathe, call 911. If a child swallows a raw bean or a treated seed, call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222 (free, any hour).
- Wash hands with soap and water after handling soil. Dampen potting mix before scooping it to keep dust down, and scoop away from faces.
- Outdoors, use sun protection and pick a garden spot away from roads and from lawns treated with chemicals.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Look inside a seed.** Soak one extra bean overnight. Help your child peel off the skin and split it in half. With a magnifying glass, look for the tiny leaves tucked inside. Ask, "What do you think this will become?" Then throw the bean away, because it is raw and not for eating, and wash hands.
2. **Plant.** Fill each pot with potting mix to about 2 cm below the rim. Push 2 or 3 seeds about 3 cm deep, cover them, and water until water drips out the bottom. Label the pot with the date. Put it in the sunniest window you have, or outdoors after the last spring frost.
3. **Predict.** Ask your child to guess how many days until something shows above the soil, and to draw what they think it will look like. Write the guess down.
4. **Visit once a week.** On the same day each week, measure the tallest plant in centimetres from the soil to the top, draw it, and count the leaves. Water when the top of the soil feels dry. When plants are about 5 cm tall, keep the strongest one or two in each pot, and you snip off the rest at the soil.
5. **Watch the stages.** In warm weather, a hooked stem usually pushes up in 5 to 14 days. Leaves follow within days. Small white or purple flowers usually open about 4 to 7 weeks after planting, sometimes later indoors or in cool weather. Tiny pods show up within a week or two of the first flowers. Before the flowers open, ask, "What do you think will grow where the flowers are?"
6. **Let some pods ripen.** Green beans are ready to pick about 7 to 9 weeks after planting. Leave at least three pods on the plant. Over the next 3 to 6 weeks, they turn yellow, then dry and papery, and the seeds inside rattle. Ask, "Will the new seeds look like the one we planted?" Then open a dry pod and compare.
7. **Make a life-cycle wheel.** Divide a paper plate into five slices. Your child draws, in order around the circle: seed, sprout, leafy plant, flowers, pods with seeds. Draw an arrow from the last slice back to the first.

With a class, give each group one pot and one ruler, and keep a shared wall chart of heights.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check.

- "I wonder what comes out of the seed first, a root or a leaf. What do you think?"
- "How many centimetres do you think it will grow by next week?"
- "What do you think will happen if we forget to water it for a week?"
- "Where do you think the pods will grow? Look for clues on the plant."
- "I wonder if the new beans will be bigger, smaller or the same as the one we planted."
- "What do you think will happen to the plant after the pods dry?"

## Why it works

A bean seed holds a tiny plant and two thick seed leaves packed with stored food. Warmth and water wake it. The root comes out first, then a hooked stem pulls the seed leaves up into the light. Once the true leaves open, the plant makes its own food by **photosynthesis**, using light, water and carbon dioxide from the air.

When the seed sprouts, the root comes out first, then a hooked stem pulls the seed leaves up into the light." />

Bean flowers mostly pollinate themselves. In most flowers, pollen has already reached the centre of the same flower by the time it opens, which is why beans set pods on a windowsill with no bees. Each fertilized ovule becomes a seed, and the surrounding ovary becomes the pod.

Timing depends on the variety and, above all, on temperature. Seeds in cold soil sprout slowly and may rot. Very hot days can make flowers drop before they set pods. Seed packets list "days to harvest," which means green pods. Dry, ripe seeds take several weeks longer.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Measure with string: hold a piece against the plant, cut it, and tape it in the journal, one string per week. Line the strings up to see the plant grow. Or count how many blocks tall it is. Use only drawings and three words: sprout, flower, pod. Let your child do the watering and pick the day of the week for visits.

### Ages 8 to 10
Keep a table of height in centimetres and number of leaves each week, then draw a line graph of height. Try a fair test: plant two pots the same way, then put one in a sunny window and one in a dim corner. Change only the light. Record which flowers first and how many pods each makes.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

No sprouts after two weeks usually means the soil was too cold or too wet, and the seeds rotted. Dig one up to check, then replant in a warmer spot and water less. Tall, pale, floppy plants need more light: move them to the sunniest window, or outdoors for a few hours at first and a little longer each day. If flowers drop without making pods, heat or dry soil is the usual cause, and more flowers often follow.
