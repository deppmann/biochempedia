## The big idea

A child who eats green beans has seen only one moment in a bean plant’s life. Growing one from seed to seed shows the whole story, ending right back where it began: with a bean. Along the way, your child practises looking closely, measuring each week, guessing before checking, and waiting for a slow result.

**Say it together:** "A bean seed holds a tiny baby plant with a packed lunch. With water, warmth and light, it grows leaves, flowers and pods full of new beans. When the old plant dies, those new beans can start the circle again."

A bean plant moves through these stages in order. First, the seed takes in water and sprouts—a step called **germination**. Roots and a stem grow, then leaves. Next, flowers form and pollen reaches the centre of each flower. That centre swells into a pod—the plant’s **fruit**—with new seeds ripening inside. Finally, the plant dies. Bush beans are **annuals**: plants that live for one growing season.

<details>
<summary>For teachers: Virginia standards</summary>

Virginia’s grade 2 standard lists these exact flowering-plant stages in order, from seed to death. In kindergarten, children are not expected to name the stages in order; it is enough for them to notice that the plant gets bigger, grows more leaves, and makes pods and seeds.

</details>

<SafetyNote>
- **Supervision, choking & allergies:** Check for food allergies first; if a child is allergic to beans or other legumes, ask their doctor before they handle seeds or eat pods. Stay with your child while planting, handling seeds, and opening dry pods. Keep loose seeds (dry, soaked, or freshly picked) and any small measuring blocks away from children under 4. Small objects can choke a child or get stuck in a nose or ear.
- **Skip tasting and wash hands.** Never taste any part of the plant during the activity. Raw beans, including green beans, contain a natural toxin called a lectin. Wash hands with soap and water after handling soil, seeds, or plants. Dampen potting mix before scooping to keep dust down, and scoop away from faces.
- **Emergency help:** Call 911 if a child is choking or struggling to breathe. If a child swallows a raw bean or a treated seed, call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222 (free, any hour).
- **Tools, glass & heat (adult only):** An adult snips extra seedlings, cuts measuring string, holds any glass magnifying glass (or uses plastic), and sets up any grow light with its cord and plug away from water. An adult handles the stove and boiling water if you eat picked green pods—boil fresh green beans for at least 30 minutes (Germany’s food-safety agency advises boiling, not steaming, for at least 30 minutes). Keep dry seeds for planting.
- **Use untreated seeds.** Check the packet for the word “untreated.” Treated seeds have a bright pink, blue or green pesticide coating. Leave them out of this activity and keep them out of classrooms and away from children. If anyone handles them, wash hands.
- **Choose an outdoor spot.** Plant where you have permission, away from roads and lawns treated with chemicals. Check the weather, use sun protection, and check for ticks afterward.
</SafetyNote>

## What to do

1. **Peek inside a seed.** Soak one extra bean overnight. Help your child peel off the skin and split the bean in half. Look closely (with a plastic magnifying glass if you have one, or with an adult holding a glass one). Your child will notice two thick halves and tiny folded leaves inside. Ask, *"What do you think this will become?"* Throw the soaked bean away right after, because it is raw, and wash hands.
2. **Plant.** Dampen the potting mix and fill each pot to about 2 cm below the rim. Push 2 or 3 seeds about 3 cm deep, cover them, and water until water drips out the bottom. Label the pot with the date. Put it in your sunniest window, or outdoors after the last spring frost.
3. **Predict first.** Ask, *"How many days do you think it will take before something pokes through the soil? Draw what you think we’ll see first."* Write down the guess and the planting date. When the first sprout appears, compare the day and what it looks like with your child’s guess and drawing.
4. **Visit once a week.** Pick a day for your visits. Fill in the first row of the observation sheet together. Each week, measure the tallest plant in centimetres from the soil to the top, count the leaves, and draw it. Water when the top of the soil feels dry. When plants reach about 5 cm tall, keep the strongest one or two in each pot, and you snip off the rest at the soil.
5. **Watch each stage.** In warm weather, your child will usually notice a hooked stem pushing up in 5 to 14 days, then leaves within days. Small white or purple flowers usually open about 4 to 7 weeks after planting, sometimes later indoors or in cool weather. Before flowers open, ask, *"What do you think will grow right where these flowers are?"* Once the first flowers open, look for tiny pods over the next week or two.
6. **Let pods ripen and compare.** Green beans are ready to pick about 7 to 9 weeks after planting. Leave at least three pods on the plant. Over the next 3 to 6 weeks, they turn yellow, then dry and papery, and the seeds rattle inside. Before opening a dry pod together, ask, *"Will the new seeds look like the one we planted?"* Open it and line up a new dry seed beside one from the packet.
7. **Build a life-cycle wheel and debrief.** Divide a paper plate into five slices. Your child draws, in order around the circle: seed, sprout, leafy plant, flowers, pods with seeds. Draw an arrow from the last slice back to the first. Close with two questions: *"Show me on your wheel where our new beans came from."* and *"If we planted one of these new beans next season, what would happen?"*

With a class, give each group one pot and one ruler, and keep a shared wall chart of heights.

## Questions to ask while you do it

Ask your child to guess first, then check what really happens.

- "I wonder what comes out of the seed first—a root or a leaf. What do you think?"
- "How many centimetres do you think it will grow by next week?"
- "What do you think will happen if we forget to water it for a week?"
- "Where do you think the pods will grow? Look for clues on the plant."
- "I wonder if the new beans will be bigger, smaller or the same as the one we planted."
- "What do you think will happen to the plant after the pods dry?"

## Why it works

A bean seed holds a tiny plant between two thick seed leaves packed with stored food. Warmth and water wake it. The root comes out first, then a hooked stem pulls the seed leaves up into the light. Once the true leaves open, the plant makes its own food by **photosynthesis**, using light, water and carbon dioxide from the air.

Two small insets show a seed with its root coming out first, and then a hooked stem pulling the seed leaves up out of the soil." caption="Inside a soaked bean is a tiny plant between two thick seed leaves. When the seed sprouts, the root comes out first, then a hooked stem pulls the seed leaves up into the light." />

Bean flowers mostly pollinate themselves. In most flowers, pollen has already reached the centre of the same flower by the time it opens. That is why beans set pods on a windowsill with no bees. Each fertilized ovule becomes a seed, and the surrounding ovary becomes the pod.

Timing depends on the variety and, above all, on temperature. Seeds in cold soil sprout slowly and may rot. Very hot days can make flowers drop before they set pods. Seed packets list "days to harvest," which means green pods; dry, ripe seeds take several weeks longer.

## Easier and harder

### Ages 5 to 7
Measure with string: hold a piece against the plant, let an adult cut it, and tape it in the journal—one string per week. Line the strings up to see the plant grow, or count how many blocks tall it is. Use only drawings and three words: sprout, flower, pod. Let your child do the watering and pick the day of the week for visits.

### Ages 8 to 10
Keep a table of height in centimetres and number of leaves each week, then draw a line graph of height. Try a fair test: plant two pots the same way, then put one in a sunny window and one in a dim corner. Change only the light. Record which flowers first and how many pods each makes.

## If it doesn’t go as planned

No sprouts after two weeks usually means the soil was too cold or too wet, and the seeds rotted. Dig one up to check, then replant in a warmer spot and water less. Tall, pale, floppy plants need more light: move them to the sunniest window, or outdoors for a few hours at first and a little longer each day. If flowers drop without making pods, heat or dry soil is the usual cause, and more flowers often follow.
