techniques:
  - name: "Cell-free reconstitution with depletion and add-back"
    blurb: >-
      Break cells open, keep the soluble extract, and find conditions under which the process runs
      in a tube. Then fractionate the extract to find a single component the reaction requires, remove it with an antibody to
      show the activity is lost, and add the pure protein back to show it returns. This is how
      cytochrome c and APAF-1 were tied to caspase activation.
  - name: "Double-mutant (epistasis) analysis"
    blurb: >-
      Combine two mutations with opposite effects in one animal and see which effect wins. The gene
      whose mutant effect shows through usually acts later in the pathway, when both are loss-of-function mutations in one linear pathway. In the worm this placed ced-9
      before ced-4 and ced-3.
  - name: "Electron microscopy of fixed tissue"
    blurb: >-
      Cut very thin sections of fixed tissue and image them with electrons to see membranes and
      organelles. Kerr used it to show that the fragments of an apoptotic cell are wrapped in intact
      membrane with recognisable organelles inside, which set this death apart from necrosis.
  - name: "Co-immunoprecipitation"
    blurb: >-
      Pull one protein out of a cell extract with an antibody and identify what comes out with it.
      Pulling out BCL-2 brought down its partner BAX, an early sign that members of the family
      bind one another.

    choices:

      - "The cell stops dividing for good but keeps its place and its shape"
      - "The cell shrinks into membrane-wrapped parcels that neighbours eat"
      - "The cell fuses with a neighbour and hands its contents over intact"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Apoptosis is an orderly dismantling: the cell shrinks and is packed into membrane-wrapped
      parcels that neighbours eat, so nothing leaks and usually no inflammation follows. Swelling until the
      membrane bursts and the contents spill is necrosis. Fusing with a neighbour is not a form of
      cell death. A cell that stops dividing but keeps its place and shape is alive, not dying.
  - stem: "Which statement describes how a caspase cuts its substrates?"
    choices:
      - "It cuts after an aspartate, using a cysteine in its active site"
      - "It cuts after a glutamate, using a cysteine in its active site"
      - "It cuts after an aspartate, using a serine in its active site"
      - "It cuts after a cysteine, using an aspartate in its own active site"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The name stands for cysteine-dependent aspartate-specific protease: the active-site residue is
      a cysteine and the cut falls just after an aspartate in the substrate. Cutting after a
      cysteine with an aspartate reverses the two roles. Cutting after a glutamate with a cysteine
      gets the enzyme's residue right but misses the aspartate that defines the family. Cutting after an aspartate with a
      serine confuses caspases with the serine proteases of digestion.

      - "They open the inner mitochondrial membrane and let protons leak back in"
      - "They bind APAF-1 in the cytosol and assemble it into the apoptosome"
      - "They cut the executioner caspases and start the demolition phase"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Activated BAX and BAK open the outer mitochondrial membrane, which releases cytochrome c from
      the space between the two membranes. Opening the inner membrane to protons describes an
      uncoupler of oxidative phosphorylation, not these proteins. Assembling APAF-1 into the
      apoptosome is done by cytochrome c with dATP. Cutting the executioner caspases is done by
      initiator caspases, and BAX and BAK are not proteases.
  - stem: "A cell makes only a shortened FADD that keeps its death domain but lacks its death effector domain. What happens when Fas ligand binds Fas on this cell?"
    choices:
      - "Fas fails to cluster, because FADD is what binds the ligand outside the cell"
      - "Fas clusters and recruits caspase-9 in place of caspase-8"
      - "Fas clusters and recruits caspase-8 directly, with no need for FADD"
      - "Fas clusters and binds FADD, but caspase-8 is not recruited"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      The ligand clusters Fas from outside, and the death domain of the shortened FADD can still
      bind the clustered tails. Without the death effector domain FADD cannot hold the precursor of
      caspase-8, so no initiator is gathered and the signal stops. Fas does not fail to cluster,
      because clustering is done by the trimeric ligand and not by FADD. Fas cannot recruit
      caspase-8 directly, because the receptor has a death domain, the caspase has death effector
      domains, and only FADD has one of each. Caspase-9 is gathered by the apoptosome, not by a
      death receptor.
  - stem: "A lymphoma cell line makes about ten times the normal amount of BCL-2 and survives DNA damage that kills normal B cells. Which treatment would most directly restore its death after DNA damage?"
    choices:
      - "Adding more cytochrome c to the space between the mitochondrial membranes"
      - "Adding a small molecule that fills the BH3-binding groove of BCL-2"
      - "Adding an inhibitor that blocks the active site of caspase-9"
      - "Adding a growth factor that raises the activity of the kinase Akt"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Extra BCL-2 soaks up the BH3-only proteins made after damage and keeps BAX and BAK restrained.
      A small molecule that fills the BH3-binding groove of BCL-2 frees BAX and BAK, so the membrane opens.

    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Removing cytochrome c abolishes the activation and adding it back restores it, so it is
      required, and the lack of activation without dATP shows the nucleotide is required too.
      Cytochrome c is not sufficient with no nucleotide, since that condition gave nothing. Nothing
      here tests whether caspase-3 cuts cytochrome c, and removing a mere substrate would not block

  - stem: "APAF-1, a protein of about 130 kDa, runs as a single copy on a sizing column when purified alone. After incubation with dATP and cytochrome c (about 12 kDa), it runs as a complex of about 1,000 kDa in which each APAF-1 holds one cytochrome c. About how many copies of APAF-1 does the complex contain?"
    choices:
      - "83 copies"
      - "7 copies"
      - "8 copies"
      - "14 copies"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Each unit of the complex is one APAF-1 plus one cytochrome c, about 130 + 12 = 142 kDa, and
      1,000 divided by 142 is about 7, the number of spokes in the apoptosome. Eight copies comes
      from dividing by the mass of APAF-1 alone and rounding up, which leaves out the cytochrome c;
      eight units of 142 kDa would weigh over 1,100 kDa. Fourteen counts every protein chain in the

      dividing by the mass of cytochrome c alone.
  - stem: "In the worm C. elegans, animals that lack ced-9 function die during development because many cells that normally live undergo programmed death. Animals that lack ced-3 are healthy and show almost none of the normal programmed deaths. Animals that lack both genes are healthy and show almost no programmed deaths. Which conclusion about the two gene products do these results support?"
    choices:
      - "CED-9 acts apart from CED-3, in a second pathway"
      - "CED-9 acts after CED-3 and is switched on by it"
      - "CED-3 acts before CED-9 and is needed to produce it"
      - "CED-3 acts after CED-9 and is held in check by it"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Loss of ced-9 kills cells only if ced-3 is present, so the deaths caused by losing CED-9 are
      carried out by CED-3. CED-3 therefore acts after CED-9, and working CED-9 holds it in check
      (through CED-4, as other experiments showed). If CED-9 acted after CED-3 and were switched on
      by it, the double mutant would look like the ced-9 mutant and die. If the two acted in
      separate pathways, removing ced-3 would not rescue the animals. If CED-3 acted first and were
      needed to produce CED-9, losing ced-3 would cause the same excess death as losing ced-9, and
      it does the opposite.

  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      Initiator caspases are monomeric zymogens whose activation is driven by dimerisation on an
      oligomeric platform: the DISC, built from death domain and death effector domain contacts, or
      the heptameric APAF-1 apoptosome, whose assembly needs cytochrome c bound to the WD40

      intrinsic pathway. In a given cell it tends to be rapid and to involve most mitochondria,
      which makes the response all or none at the level of the single cell. The threshold is set by
      the amounts, binding preferences and locations of BCL-2 family members, a modern form of Korsmeyer's rheostat. Selectivity among the guards
      matters in the clinic: a compound that blocks BCL-xL as well as BCL-2 depletes platelets,

      An open question is how BAX and BAK are activated. In the direct model, argued by Letai and

**Cytochrome c, the small protein you met carrying electrons to complex IV, has a second job: once it leaves the mitochondrion, it orders the cell to take itself apart.** The first film showed why irreversible cuts place control at assembly platforms; here we trace the genetic evidence, caspase enzymology, and both death routes.

## The idea: a cell can die by accident or by program

In the early 1960s John Kerr, a doctoral student in London, tied off blood vessels to part of a rat's liver and, over the following years, found two deaths in one tissue. In **necrosis**, death by injury, sheets of cells swelled, burst and spilled their contents, often causing inflammation. In the second death, single cells shrank, grew dense and broke into parcels wrapped in unbroken membrane; neighbours ate the parcels, and usually no inflammation followed.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/death-receptors-bcl2-caspases/necrosis-vs-apoptosis.webp" width={1200} height={590} alt="AI-generated illustration: two panels of three neighbouring cells. In necrosis the middle cell swells, bursts and spills its contents. In apoptosis the middle cell shrinks, dense, and breaks into small parcels wrapped in intact membrane, one of which a neighbour has eaten." caption="Necrosis bursts injured cells and spills their contents, whereas apoptosis packs dying cells into intact, membrane-wrapped parcels that neighbours eat, usually without inflammation." />

Kerr published that death in 1971 as "shrinkage necrosis", and in 1972, with Andrew Wyllie and Alastair Currie in Aberdeen, named it **apoptosis**, Greek for the falling of leaves—claiming it as something a cell does routinely. Animals sculpt embryos, match losses to births in adult tissues, and clear infected or self-reactive immune cells. Diseases split accordingly: too little death in cancers and autoimmune disease, and too much in some forms of neurodegeneration, where apoptosis is one of several ways neurons die.

Proof of a genetic program came from the self-fertilising hermaphrodite worm *Caenorhabditis elegans*, which makes 1,090 body cells and loses the same 131 every time. Robert Horvitz's laboratory isolated four core mutants:

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Wild type | All 131 deaths occur | Balanced program |
| *ced-3* or *ced-4* | Nearly all fail | Killer machinery |
| *ced-9* | Massive excess death; lethal | Brake blocking death |
| *egl-1* | Nearly all fail | Releases the *ced-9* brake |

In **double-mutant epistasis**, combining two loss-of-function mutations with opposite effects in one linear pathway usually reveals their order: the mutation that acts later shows through, because an upstream change cannot alter a broken downstream step.

**Predict.** A worm loses both *ced-9* (brake) and *ced-4* (killer). Does it suffer massive excess death, or almost none?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Almost none, and it survives. Losing CED-9 kills cells only when CED-4 is intact, placing *ced-4* downstream of *ced-9*.

</details>

Opposite-phenotype double mutants placed *egl-1* before *ced-9*, and *ced-9* before *ced-4* and *ced-3*; further work showed CED-4 switches on CED-3. Each has human relatives: BCL-2 (CED-9), APAF-1 (CED-4) and caspases (CED-3).

## The killer is an enzyme kept as a precursor

CED-3's human relatives are **caspases** ("cysteine-dependent aspartate-specific proteases"): each uses an active-site cysteine to cut target proteins just after an aspartate.

The first film showed why caspases are stored as inactive precursors (**zymogens**, like digestive proteases) in two apoptotic classes. (A third group, inflammatory caspases such as caspase-1, drives a different, inflammatory death.) **Initiator caspases** (caspase-8, caspase-9) are monomers too dilute in cytosol to pair stably until an oligomeric platform crowds them to dimerize. **Executioner caspases** (caspases 3, 6 and 7) are pre-paired dimers locked shut by an uncleaved linker between their large and small subunits until an initiator—or another active caspase—cuts that linker.

Active executioners drive the **demolition phase**, cleaving hundreds of proteins. Cutting the inhibitor of a DNA-cutting enzyme frees it to cleave DNA between nucleosomes (the protein spools DNA is wound on) into a ladder roughly 200 base pairs apart; dismantling nuclear and cytoskeletal supports condenses chromatin, rounds the cell and blebs its surface.

Unlike a kinase cascade—where a phosphate added by a kinase is removed by [a phosphatase](/lessons/phosphorylation-switch)—no enzyme rejoins a cut peptide bond. Inhibitor proteins such as **XIAP** can restrain an active caspase, yet nothing undoes a cut already made, putting primary control upstream at initiator gathering.

## Killed from outside: a death receptor gathers caspase-8

The first film animated the outside route (**extrinsic pathway**); here are its binding domains and genetic proof.

It starts at a **death receptor**, a membrane protein whose cytosolic tail carries a **death domain**, a fold that binds other death domains. On **Fas**, trimeric **Fas ligand** presented by a killer T cell clusters receptors so the adaptor **FADD** can dock. Like [Grb2](/lessons/adaptors-and-scaffolds), FADD has no enzyme activity and two ends: its death domain holds Fas, and its **death effector domain** holds procaspase-8. Together they form the **death-inducing signaling complex (DISC)**, where crowded procaspase-8 copies pair up, become active and cut executioners, with no kinase or second messenger.

<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Without clustering, isolated receptor tails cannot pack FADD and procaspase-8 close enough for dimerization.

</details>

Shigekazu Nagata's Osaka laboratory cloned human Fas in 1991 from a killing antibody, then linked two autoimmune mouse strains—swollen lymph nodes and lupus-like disease—to both ends of the pair: *lpr*, kept since the 1970s (Fas defect, 1992) and *gld* (Fas ligand mutation, 1994), where surplus lymphocytes stayed alive after immune responses. Another death-domain receptor, **TNFR1** (for TNF), usually activates NF-κB for survival and kills only when that arm fails—"the anti-death, death pathway" of [the NF-κB lesson](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb).

An outside order uses a surface receptor to gather caspase-8, but danger starting inside the cell—such as damaged DNA or lost survival signals—needs an internal platform; the second film shows how the mitochondrion and BCL-2 family decide.

## Killed from inside: a series of checks and balances

The inside route, the **intrinsic pathway**, is decided at the outer mitochondrial membrane: the BCL-2 family balance decides whether mitochondria release cytochrome c—and **BCL-2** turned up first in human cancer. In follicular lymphoma (a B-cell cancer), a piece of chromosome 18 joined to chromosome 14 places BCL-2 beside an antibody gene; Yoshihide Tsujimoto in Carlo Croce's lab (1984) and Stanley Korsmeyer's lab (1985) cloned the junction, and David Vaux, Suzanne Cory and Jerry Adams in Melbourne (1988) proved BCL-2 keeps cells from dying rather than making them divide.

In 1993 Korsmeyer's laboratory found the opposition: **BAX**, which resembles BCL-2, binds to it and speeds death. The family works at the outer mitochondrial membrane in three classes:

| Class | Examples | Role at outer membrane | Effect |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Effectors** | BAX, BAK | Open the outer membrane once activated | Pro-death |
| **Guards** | BCL-2, BCL-xL | Hold BAX and BAK in a hydrophobic groove | Pro-survival |
| **Sensors (BH3-only)** | BIM, BID, PUMA, NOXA, BAD | Fill a guard's groove via one short **BH3 helix**; some (BIM, cut BID) also activate BAX/BAK directly | Pro-death |

Each sensor reads its own cue: after DNA damage, p53 (previous lesson) switches on PUMA and NOXA, whereas the survival kinase [Akt](/lessons/pi3k-akt-insulin) phosphorylates BAD and switches it off. Korsmeyer read the family as a **rheostat**: guards and effectors coexist in healthy cells, and their proportion, binding preferences and locations set the death threshold (see *Going deeper* on direct vs. indirect activation).

**Predict.** Growth factor is removed from a dependent cell, dropping Akt activity. Tracing Akt → BAD → guards → BAX/BAK, is the outer mitochondrial membrane more or less likely to open?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

More likely. This is a chain of three inhibitions: Akt normally inhibits BAD, BAD inhibits the guards, and guards inhibit BAX and BAK; lower Akt frees BAD to occupy guards and release BAX/BAK.

</details>

When BAX and BAK open that membrane, [cytochrome c](/lessons/oxidative-phosphorylation) escapes alongside **SMAC**, which frees active caspases from inhibitors such as XIAP. Xiaodong Wang's laboratory proved cytochrome c's role in 1996 in a tube: adding dATP to HeLa cytosol (spun free of nuclei, mitochondria and membranes, though hypotonic lysis had leaked cytochrome c from ruptured mitochondria) switched on caspase-3; antibody depletion stopped it and pure cytochrome c restored it. In 1997 they purified **APAF-1** (CED-4's relative), showing that cytochrome c and dATP assemble seven APAF-1 copies into the **apoptosome** wheel that gathers caspase-9.

The two routes meet: active caspase-8 cuts **BID**, whose cut piece goes to the mitochondrion and releases cytochrome c (Junying Yuan and Xiaodong Wang labs, 1998); later work showed it does so by setting off BAX and BAK.

**Predict.** Fas ligand triggers a modest pulse of caspase-8 in a cell making excess BCL-2. How does excess BCL-2 affect the cut-BID branch?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Excess BCL-2 can soak up cut BID and restrain BAX/BAK, blocking or blunting outer-membrane opening and cytochrome c release.

</details>

### Design problem

A cell must be able to destroy itself within hours, and must never do so by accident. Design the safeguards. Say in what form the killing enzyme is kept, what holds it off, and why you would require two separate events before it is released. Then say what a tumour gains by making too much BCL-2, and how a small molecule shaped like a BH3 helix would take that gain away.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

For speed, store executioners as zymogens cut only by an initiator gathered on a platform whose parts sit apart: APAF-1 in cytosol, cytochrome c behind the outer mitochondrial membrane.

Require two separate events: stress must induce BH3-only sensors, and there must be enough to tie up guards set separately by survival signals. If noise produces each in one cell in a thousand independently, both arise by chance in one cell in a million. Once most mitochondria open, irreversible cuts make the outcome usually all or none.

Extra BCL-2 in a tumour soaks up damage-induced BH3-only sensors so the cell lives. A BH3-shaped small molecule fills BCL-2's groove, displacing them so BAX and BAK open the membrane and kill the stressed tumour cell.

</details>

## When the brakes are too strong, or the machinery is missing

Follicular lymphoma carries too strong a brake: the t(14;18) translocation drives excess BCL-2, so B cells outlive their time, and further mutations can then make one a cancer. **Venetoclax**—approved in the United States in 2016 for a form of chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, not follicular lymphoma—fills BCL-2's BH3 groove while sparing BCL-xL (see *Going deeper*).

Deleting genes in **knockout mice** tests the reverse. Most mice lacking APAF-1 die before or around birth and their cells resist many death signals; mice lacking caspase-9 develop malformed brains with too many cells. So the inside route matters. Yet these embryos still pass **gastrulation** (when body layers form) and build organs, and many cells that should die still die: in the toe webbing of APAF-1 knockouts, cells die without the look of apoptosis, by a death that needs no known caspase.

A separate backup sits on the outside route. When TNFR1 is engaged and caspase-8 is blocked (as some viruses arrange), kinases **RIPK1** and **RIPK3** assemble, RIPK3 phosphorylates **MLKL**, and MLKL breaks the plasma membrane so the cell swells and bursts on instruction: **necroptosis**. Caspase-8 normally restrains it, in part by cutting RIPK1.

**Predict.** A mouse embryo lacking the killer caspase-8 dies in development. What happens if RIPK3 is deleted too, and why?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

It survives. Without caspase-8 cutting RIPK1, unchecked necroptosis kills the embryo; removing RIPK3 shuts that backup off—the same double-mutant logic as in the worm.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Direct or indirect activation of BAX and BAK?

In the **direct model** (Letai and colleagues, 2002), some BH3-only proteins briefly bind and switch on BAX or BAK directly while others only occupy guards. In the **indirect model** (a Melbourne group, 2007), BH3-only proteins act mainly by neutralizing guards, leaving freed BAX and BAK active. Later evidence supports elements of both, and the exact structure of the BAX/BAK membrane pore remains unsettled.

### Guard selectivity and DISC stoichiometry

An earlier compound that blocked both BCL-xL and BCL-2 depleted platelets, which depend on BCL-xL to survive; venetoclax avoids this by sparing BCL-xL. Separately, crystal structures of Fas–FADD death domains (Scott et al., 2009) define the fold contact while a later structure showed a different arrangement, so DISC diagrams illustrate domain contacts rather than fixed copy numbers.
