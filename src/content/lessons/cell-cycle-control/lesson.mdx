techniques:
  - name: "DNA-content flow cytometry with EdU and phospho-H3"
    blurb: >-
      Fixed cells are stained with a DNA dye (propidium iodide plus RNase, or DAPI) and passed one
      at a time past a laser, giving a histogram with a 2C and a 4C peak. A short EdU pulse marks
      cells in S phase, and an antibody to histone H3 phosphorylated on serine 10 marks mitotic
      cells, separating states that DNA content alone lumps together.
  - name: "Temperature-sensitive mutants and shift experiments"
    blurb: >-
      A mutant protein works at a permissive temperature and fails at a restrictive one. Shifting
      a growing culture and following single cells shows where cells stop (the arrest or
      termination point) and, from which cells finish their cycle first, when the gene was needed
      (the execution point).
  - name: "Cell fusion and cytoplasm transfer"
    blurb: >-
      Two cells in different phases are fused so their nuclei share one cytoplasm, or cytoplasm
      from one cell is injected into another. Each nucleus is scored separately for DNA synthesis
      or chromosome condensation. The result shows a transferable activity and whether a nucleus
      can respond to it, not the molecule responsible.
  - name: "Cycling egg extracts"
    blurb: >-
      Crushed Xenopus eggs give a cytoplasm that runs through repeated cycles in a tube. Messenger
      RNA can be destroyed and added back, and cyclin added at known concentrations, so the cause
      of entry into and exit from mitosis can be tested directly.

    choices:
      - "2C DNA, and the cell is now haploid"
      - "4C DNA, and the cell is still diploid"
      - "4C DNA, and the cell is now tetraploid"
      - "2C DNA, and the cell is still diploid"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Replication doubles the DNA from 2C to 4C, but each chromosome is now two sister chromatids
      held together, so the number of chromosome sets is unchanged and the cell is still diploid.
      Calling it tetraploid confuses doubling DNA with doubling chromosome sets. A 2C diploid cell
      is a G1 (or G0) cell, which has not replicated yet. A 2C haploid cell does not arise in a somatic
      cycle; halving the sets happens in meiosis.
  - stem: "Which statement correctly describes the two partners in a cyclin-CDK complex?"
    choices:
      - "The CDK is the kinase, and the cyclin level rises and falls"
      - "The CDK level rises and falls, and the cyclin level stays even"
      - "The cyclin is the kinase, and the CDK is its regulatory partner"
      - "Both partners are kinases that phosphorylate each other in turn"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The CDK carries the catalytic site and is inactive alone; the cyclin is a regulatory subunit
      whose level rises through part of the cycle and falls when it is destroyed, switching the
      kinase on and off. Making the cyclin the kinase reverses the roles; cyclins have no kinase
      domain. The partners do not phosphorylate each other in turn; only the CDK is a kinase. In
      most cycles CDK levels stay fairly even and it is the cyclins that oscillate, which is how
      cyclin was found.
  - stem: "A fission-yeast strain lacks Wee1, the kinase that puts an inhibitory phosphate on Cdc2. Compared with wild type, what do you expect?"
    choices:
      - "Cells divide larger, because Cdc25 has no phosphate to remove"
      - "Cells copy their DNA twice, because S phase repeats itself"
      - "Cells arrest in G2, because Cdc2 can never be switched on"
      - "Cells divide smaller, because Cdc2 is switched on earlier"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Wee1 holds Cdc2 off by phosphorylating it; without the brake, Cdc2-cyclin B becomes active
      before the cell has grown to its usual size, so cells enter mitosis early and divide small,
      the phenotype that named wee1. An arrest in G2 is what loss of the activator, Cdc2 or Cdc25,

      inhibitory kinase. Repeated S phase is a failure of once-per-cycle replication control,

  - stem: "In a culture doubling every 24 h, 55% of cells are in G1. A student multiplies 0.55 by 24 h and says G1 lasts 13.2 h. What is wrong?"
    choices:
      - "The estimate is too short, because cells in G1 are often quiescent"
      - "The estimate is too short, because mitotic cells are counted twice"
      - "The estimate is too long, because young cells are overrepresented"
      - "Nothing, because a fraction of cells always equals a fraction of time"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Each division turns one old cell into two newborn cells, so a growing culture holds twice
      as many cells at the start of the cycle as at the end, and early phases take up a larger
      share of the cells than of the time; the age-distribution model gives about 11.1 h. Equating
      the two fractions is true only for a population that is not growing. Quiescent cells would
      inflate the 2C peak and make the naive estimate even longer, not shorter. Mitotic cells are
      not counted twice; each cell is one event in the cytometer.
  - stem: "A budding-yeast temperature-sensitive mutant arrests with large buds and an undivided nucleus. After a shift to the restrictive temperature, cells that were already in S phase also arrest with large buds in the same cycle. What can you conclude about when the gene acts?"
    choices:
      - "It acts at mitosis, because that is where the cells collect"
      - "It acts in G1 only, because large buds form after the G1 step"
      - "It has no fixed time, because the arrest shape is the same"
      - "It may act at or after S phase, though cells stop at mitosis"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Cells that had passed the gene's execution point when shifted would finish this cycle and
      stop in the next one. These cells stop in the current cycle although they were already in S,
      so the function was still needed at or after that time, and the arrest at mitosis is simply
      where the missing work is first noticed. Reading the arrest stage as the time of action is
      the error the execution point was defined to avoid. Placing it in G1 only would require
      S-phase cells to escape into the next cycle, which they did not. A uniform arrest shape is
      what defines a cdc mutant; it does not mean the gene lacks a time of action.

      The G1 nucleus begins replication in S-phase cytoplasm, so the cytoplasm carries an

  - stem: "In cycling Xenopus egg extracts with protein synthesis blocked, a nondegradable cyclin B was added at known concentrations. **Fuse a cell that is copying its DNA with a cell that has not started yet, and the second nucleus begins copying early. Fuse it with a cell that has already finished, and that nucleus refuses.**

Something in the cytoplasm says "copy now", and something in the nucleus remembers whether it already has. This lesson measures those states, then follows the fusion, frog and yeast experiments to the engine behind them: a kinase whose partner rises and falls.

## Phases, and how you count them

The cycle has four phases. G1 is the gap between mitosis and the start of DNA replication. In S phase the genome is copied (the mechanics belong to [DNA replication](/lessons/dna-replication/)). G2 is the gap after copying and before mitosis. In M phase, mitosis sorts the duplicated chromosomes into two nuclei, and cytokinesis, the split of the cell itself, usually follows. Mammalian cells can also leave G1 for G0, a resting state they enter when conditions change and can leave again.

The first measurement is DNA content. One C is the DNA of one haploid genome, so a diploid cell has 2C in G1 and 4C in G2 and M. Doubling the DNA does not double the ploidy: a G2 cell is still diploid, with each chromosome made of two sister chromatids. To read DNA content, fix the cells and stain them with propidium iodide, a dye that fluoresces when bound to nucleic acid, plus RNase, because the dye also binds RNA. A flow cytometer measures each cell's fluorescence one at a time and builds a histogram: a 2C peak, a 4C peak, and S-phase cells smeared between.

The histogram has two blind spots. G0 cells sit in the 2C peak with G1, and mitotic cells sit in the 4C peak with G2. Two more stains fix the second; G0 still needs its own marker, such as Ki67. EdU, a thymidine analogue, is built into DNA made during a short pulse and is then detected by click chemistry with a fluorescent tag, so it marks cells in S. An antibody to histone H3 phosphorylated on serine 10 marks condensed mitotic chromosomes. Saldivar and colleagues combined all three, read by image-based cytometry rather than flow, to separate phases in human RPE-1 and MCF10A cells (Science, 2018).

### Worked problem: fractions are not durations

The numbers here are illustrative. Suppose an asynchronous culture of a diploid human cell line doubles every 24 h, every cell is cycling, and none die. Of 10,000 cells, 5,500 fall in the 2C peak, 2,000 in the 4C peak and 2,500 between. A 30-minute EdU pulse labels 25% of cells, which agrees that the cells between the peaks are in S. And 3% are phospho-H3 positive; assume the stain marks only mitotic cells.

**Step 1.** Fractions: G1 0.55, S 0.25, G2 plus M 0.20, of which M is 0.03.

**Step 2.** The tempting move is to multiply by 24 h: 13.2, 6.0 and 4.8 h. That is wrong. Each division turns one old cell into two newborn ones, so a growing culture always holds twice as many cells just born as cells about to divide. Early phases are overrepresented.

**Step 3.** For a culture in steady exponential growth, the time a cell takes to pass the first fraction F of the population, ordered by age, is

> t = -T × log2(1 - F/2), where T is the doubling time

- End of G1 (F = 0.55): t = -24 × log2(0.725) = 24 × 0.464 = 11.13 h.
- End of S (F = 0.80): t = -24 × log2(0.600) = 17.69 h, so S lasts about 6.55 h (working from unrounded values).
- Start of M (F = 0.97): t = -24 × log2(0.515) = 22.98 h. M lasts 24 - 22.98 = 1.02 h, and G2 lasts 22.98 - 17.69 = 5.29 h.

Check: 11.13 + 6.55 + 5.29 + 1.02 = 23.99 h, 24 h within rounding. The naive estimate made G1 two hours too long and G2 plus M (6.31 h) one and a half hours too short. The model assumes identical phase lengths in every cell and no resting or dying cells, so treat these as estimates.

## Asking the cell: fusion and cytoplasm transfer

In 1970 Rao and Johnson fused cultured HeLa cells taken from different phases, so that two nuclei shared one cytoplasm. As later reviews describe the results, a G1 nucleus in a cell fused with an S-phase cell started replicating early, while a G2 nucleus in the same situation did not. Two conclusions follow. S-phase cytoplasm carries an activity that can start replication. And a nucleus that has already replicated cannot respond until it has been through mitosis, which is how DNA gets copied once per cycle and no more. (The molecular basis, licensing of replication origins in G1, belongs to [DNA replication](/lessons/dna-replication/).)

Fusing a mitotic cell with an interphase cell had a different outcome: the interphase nucleus condensed its chromosomes prematurely (Johnson and Rao, 1970), and how the chromosomes looked depended on the phase of that nucleus. Mitotic cytoplasm is dominant.

A frog experiment pointed the same way. A frog oocyte waits in prophase of its first meiotic division until progesterone tells it to resume. In 1971 Yoshio Masui and Clement Markert took cytoplasm from progesterone-treated oocytes of the leopard frog *Rana pipiens* and injected it into untreated oocytes, which then matured. Removing the donor's nucleus did not prevent the activity. They called it maturation-promoting factor (MPF). Smith and Ecker reported a similar transfer in the same year. The activity was later found in mitotic cells too.

Both kinds of experiment reveal an activity, not a molecule.

## Asking the genes: cdc mutants in two yeasts

In 1970 Leland Hartwell, Joseph Culotti and Brian Reid described temperature-sensitive mutants of budding yeast, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, that grow at a low temperature and stop at a higher one, each with a uniform shape. They called them cdc, for cell division cycle. The bud reports the stage: none in G1, small in S, large by mitosis.

Their key idea was to separate two times. A car that runs out of fuel stops where the tank ran dry, not at the station you drove past. The **execution point** is when a gene's function is normally performed. The **termination point**, or arrest point, is where cells collect when it is not. Shift a growing culture to the restrictive temperature and follow single cells: cells already past the execution point finish this cycle and stop in the next, while cells not yet past it stop in this one. The fraction that finishes tells you where the execution point lies. In the 1970 paper, the *cdc-3* mutant kept budding and dividing its nucleus while its cells failed to separate, so steps of the cycle can come apart. One warning: their budding-yeast *cdc-2*, which stopped at mitosis, is not the fission-yeast *cdc2* below. The budding-yeast gene that matters here is *CDC28*, needed at Start, the commitment point in G1.

Paul Nurse used fission yeast, *Schizosaccharomyces pombe*, a rod that divides in the middle, so length tells you its age. In 1975 he described a mutant that divided at a smaller size than normal. The mutation changed when division happened, not whether. Most later small-cell mutants mapped to a gene named *wee1*; one mapped to *cdc2*, whose other alleles block mitosis. So *cdc2* behaves as an accelerator and *wee1* as a brake. By 1981 Nurse and Yvonne Bissett concluded that *cdc2* acts both in G1 and in G2. In 1982 David Beach, Barbara Durkacz and Nurse found that budding-yeast DNA carrying *CDC28* rescued a fission-yeast *cdc2* mutant: two distant yeasts, one job.

## Cyclins meet Cdc2: one engine

In 1983 Tim Hunt's group followed new proteins in dividing embryos of the sea urchin *Arbacia punctulata*. One built up through each cycle and was destroyed at each division; they named it cyclin and saw similar proteins in another sea urchin and a clam. Disappearing at division is a correlation. Tests came next. Clam cyclin A mRNA injected into *Xenopus* oocytes made them resume meiosis (Swenson, Farrell and Ruderman, 1986). In *Xenopus* egg extracts that cycle in a tube, destroying the messenger RNA stopped the cycles in interphase and adding back cyclin mRNA restored them, while a cyclin that could not be destroyed held the extract in mitosis (Murray and Kirschner, with Solomon, 1989).

The lines met in 1988. Lohka, Hayes and Maller purified MPF from *Xenopus* eggs, and antibodies to fission-yeast Cdc2 recognised its 32 kDa protein (Gautier and colleagues), and Dunphy and colleagues reached Cdc2 by a separate route; in 1990 the other component proved to be cyclin B. MPF is Cdc2, now called CDK1, bound to cyclin B.

The logic, as it is now taught:

- **CDKs** (cyclin-dependent kinases) are the catalytic subunits, inactive alone. **Cyclins** are regulatory subunits that switch them on and steer them to targets; cyclin levels rise and fall while CDK levels stay fairly even. In mammals, cyclin D with CDK4 or CDK6 acts in G1, cyclin E with CDK2 at the G1/S transition, cyclin A with CDK2 in S, and cyclins A and B with CDK1 in G2 and M. Growth-factor signals reach cyclin D (see [Ras to MAPK](/lessons/ras-to-mapk/)), and the Rb brake it releases is in [cancer genetics](/lessons/cancer-genetics/).
- **Wee1** adds an inhibitory phosphate to CDK1, holding the complex off while cyclin accumulates; the phosphatase **Cdc25** removes it (the chemistry is in [the phosphorylation switch](/lessons/phosphorylation-switch/)).
- **CDK inhibitor proteins**, such as p21 and p27 in mammals, bind the complex and block it.
- Two **ubiquitin ligases** destroy regulators: the SCF at several points, notably around G1/S, and the APC/C in mitosis, where destroying cyclin B ends M phase ([ubiquitin and proteolysis](/lessons/ubiquitin-and-proteolysis/)).

Active CDK1 also activates Cdc25 and inhibits Wee1, two positive loops that make mitotic entry a switch ([signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/)). In *Xenopus* egg extracts, entry needed about 32 to 40 nM of added nondegradable cyclin B, but an extract already in mitosis stayed there down to about 16 to 24 nM, and unreplicated DNA raised the entry threshold. Read the fusion results in this light: mitotic cytoplasm is full of active CDK1-cyclin B, enough to condense any nucleus it meets.

### Design problem

Using cell fusion, design experiments to ask whether S-phase cytoplasm can drive replication in another nucleus, and why a G2 nucleus might not respond.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Synchronise cultured mammalian cells in G1, S and G2 and confirm each population by DNA content. Pre-label one partner before fusing, for example with a nuclear dye or a long label in the S cells, so that every nucleus in a fused cell can be identified.

Fuse S with G1, and S with G2. Give a short pulse of a DNA precursor (radioactive thymidine or EdU) and score each nucleus separately. Expected result, as in Rao and Johnson's 1970 work: G1 nuclei start replicating early, G2 nuclei do not.

Controls: same-phase fusions (G1 with G1, G2 with G2), which test whether the fusion itself starts or blocks replication, and unfused neighbours on the same slide.

Interpretation: S cytoplasm carries a replication-starting activity, and a nucleus that has replicated cannot answer it again before mitosis. A third pairing, mitotic with interphase, should give premature chromosome condensation, with an appearance that depends on the phase of the interphase nucleus.

Limit: fusion identifies an activity and a nuclear competence, not the molecules. Naming them needs biochemistry or genetics.

</details>
