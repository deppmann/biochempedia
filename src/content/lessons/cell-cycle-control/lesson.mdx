summary: "Every eukaryotic cell cycle runs on one conserved engine: a cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) switched on by a cyclin partner. scientists:
  - name: "Leland Hartwell"
    contribution: >-
      With Joseph Culotti and Brian Reid, isolated temperature-sensitive cell-division-cycle (cdc)
      mutants of budding yeast and used time-lapse microscopy to separate when a gene acts from
      where a mutant cell stops.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Hartwell, Culotti & Reid, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 66:352 (1970); Hartwell, Mortimer, Culotti & Culotti, Genetics 74:267 (1973)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Hartwell chose baker's yeast, Saccharomyces
      cerevisiae, at UC Irvine; at the University of Washington his laboratory used its bud, which reports the stage of the cycle: no bud in G1, a small bud as DNA
      is copied, a large bud by mitosis. The group collected temperature-sensitive mutants, which
      grow at a low temperature and fail at a higher one, and kept those that stopped with one
      uniform shape. In the 1970 paper with Joseph Culotti and Brian Reid, they followed single cells
      by time-lapse photomicroscopy after the temperature shift. That let them define two points
      for each gene: the execution point, when its function is normally performed, and the
      termination point, where cells collect when it is not. One mutant kept budding and dividing
      its nucleus though its cells never separated, so steps of the cycle could come apart. By
      1973 the collection held 148 mutants in 32 complementation groups. One gene, CDC28, became
      the handle on Start, the point in G1 where a yeast cell commits to dividing. Hartwell shared
      the 2001 Nobel Prize with Tim Hunt and Paul Nurse.
  - name: "Paul Nurse"
    contribution: >-

      Durkacz found that budding-yeast CDC28 does the same job as fission-yeast cdc2.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Nurse, Nature 256:547 (1975); Beach, Durkacz & Nurse, Nature 300:706 (1982); Lee & Nurse, Nature 327:31 (1987); Nurse, Nobel Lecture (2001)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Fission yeast, Schizosaccharomyces pombe, grows as a rod and divides in the middle, so the
      length of a cell tells you how far through its cycle it is. In 1975, working in the
      Department of Zoology in Edinburgh, Nurse described a temperature-sensitive strain that
      divided at a smaller size than wild type. A mutant that blocks division shows a gene is
      needed; one that brings division forward shows a gene sets its timing. Most later small-size
      mutants mapped to a gene named wee1, and one mapped to cdc2, whose other mutant alleles block
      mitosis. With Yvonne Bissett he concluded in 1981 that cdc2 is needed both in G1 and in G2.
      In 1982, with David Beach and Barbara Durkacz, he found that budding-yeast DNA carrying CDC28
      rescued a fission-yeast cdc2 mutant, though the two yeasts are distant relatives. In 1987,
      with Melanie Lee, a human cDNA rescued the same mutant. They concluded that elements of
      cell-cycle control were likely conserved from yeast to humans.
  - name: "Tim Hunt"
    contribution: >-
      With Evans, Rosenthal, Youngblom and Distel, found in sea-urchin embryos a
      protein made through each cycle and destroyed at each division, and named it cyclin.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Evans, Rosenthal, Youngblom, Distel & Hunt, Cell 33:389 (1983); Pines & Hunt, EMBO J. (1987); Hunt, Nobel Lecture (2001)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Fertilized sea-urchin eggs divide quickly, and in Arbacia punctulata the early divisions
      need continuous protein synthesis, programmed by messenger RNAs the mother stored in the
      egg. Hunt and his co-authors labelled the newly made proteins and followed them through the
      first divisions. One built up through each cycle and vanished
      at each division, and the 1983 paper reported similar proteins in eggs of another sea urchin,
      Lytechinus pictus, and in oocytes of the surf clam Spisula solidissima. The name cyclin
      described the behaviour. The paper did not show what cyclin does, and a protein that
      disappears at division could be a result of division rather than a cause. Tests followed.
      In 1987 Jonathan Pines and Hunt injected synthetic sea-urchin cyclin mRNA into Xenopus
      oocytes, and the oocytes matured. In 1990 Hunt was among the authors who found B-type
      cyclins in purified frog MPF, tying his oscillating protein to the frog activity and to the
      yeast kinase.
  - name: "Yoshio Masui"
    contribution: >-
      With Clement Markert, showed that cytoplasm from frog oocytes treated with progesterone
      makes untreated oocytes mature when injected, and called the activity maturation-promoting
      factor.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Masui & Markert, J. Exp. Zool. 177:129 (1971); Smith & Ecker, Dev. Biol. 25:232 (1971)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      A frog oocyte waits in the prophase of its first meiotic division until a hormone,
      progesterone, tells it to resume. Masui and Markert, at Yale University, worked with oocytes
      of the leopard frog Rana pipiens stripped of their follicle cells. They took cytoplasm from
      oocytes treated with progesterone and injected it into untreated ones, and the recipients
      matured. In Rana the activity was already in the donor cytoplasm before the nucleus broke down. Removing the donor's nucleus did not stop the
      activity from forming. They named it maturation-promoting factor, or MPF. L. Dennis Smith
      and Richard Ecker reported cytoplasmic transfer of maturation in Rana pipiens in the same
      year. What they had was an activity in a mixture, measured by its effect. In 1988 highly
      purified MPF from Xenopus eggs was shown to contain the frog relative of yeast Cdc2.

techniques:
  - name: "DNA-content flow cytometry with EdU and phospho-H3"
    blurb: >-
      Fixed cells are stained with a DNA dye (propidium iodide plus RNase, or DAPI) and passed one
      at a time past a laser, giving a histogram with a 2C and a 4C peak. A short EdU pulse marks
      cells in S phase, and an antibody to histone H3 phosphorylated on serine 10 marks mitotic
      cells (and some late-G2 cells), separating states that DNA content alone lumps together.
  - name: "Temperature-sensitive mutants and shift experiments"
    blurb: >-
      A mutant protein works at a permissive temperature and fails at a restrictive one. Shifting
      a growing culture and following single cells shows where cells stop (the arrest or
      termination point) and, from which cells finish their cycle first, when the gene was needed
      (the execution point).
  - name: "Cell fusion and cytoplasm transfer"
    blurb: >-
      Two cells in different phases are fused so their nuclei share one cytoplasm, or cytoplasm
      from one cell is injected into another. Each nucleus is scored separately for DNA synthesis
      or chromosome condensation. The result shows a transferable activity and whether a nucleus
      can respond to it, not the molecule responsible.
  - name: "Cycling egg extracts"
    blurb: >-
      Crushed Xenopus eggs give a cytoplasm that runs through repeated cycles in a tube. Messenger
      RNA can be destroyed and added back, and cyclin added at known concentrations, so the cause
      of entry into and exit from mitosis can be tested directly.

practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "Which statement correctly describes the two partners in a cyclin-CDK complex?"
    choices:
      - "The CDK is the kinase, and the cyclin level rises and falls"
      - "The CDK level rises and falls, and the cyclin level stays even"
      - "The cyclin is the kinase, and the CDK is its regulatory partner"
      - "Both partners are kinases that phosphorylate each other in turn"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The CDK carries the catalytic site and is inactive alone; the cyclin is a regulatory subunit
      whose level rises through part of the cycle and falls when it is destroyed, switching the
      kinase on and off. Making the cyclin the kinase reverses the roles; cyclins have no kinase
      domain. The partners do not phosphorylate each other in turn; only the CDK is a kinase. In
      most cycles CDK levels stay fairly even and it is the cyclins that oscillate, which is how
      cyclin was found.
  - stem: "Cycling frog egg extracts are given a cyclin B that the APC/C cannot destroy. Everything else in the extract is normal. What do you expect?"
    choices:
      - "The extract stays in interphase, because cyclin B is never used up"
      - "The extract enters mitosis and stays there, because CDK1 stays active"
      - "The extract cycles faster, because cyclin B builds up sooner"
      - "The extract copies its DNA again, because mitosis is skipped"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Cyclin B binding switches CDK1 on, so the extract enters mitosis as usual. Leaving mitosis needs the APC/C to destroy cyclin B and switch CDK1 off; with a cyclin that cannot be destroyed, CDK1 stays active and the extract stays in mitosis, as Murray, Solomon and Kirschner found. Staying in interphase would follow from too little cyclin, not from cyclin that lasts. Faster cycling would need cyclin to be both made and removed, and here removal is blocked. Copying DNA again would need the extract to leave mitosis and relicense its origins, which it cannot do while CDK1 stays active.
  - stem: "A fission-yeast strain lacks Wee1, the kinase that puts an inhibitory phosphate on Cdc2. Compared with wild type, what do you expect?"
    choices:
      - "Cells divide larger, because Cdc25 has no phosphate to remove"
      - "Cells copy their DNA twice, because S phase repeats itself"
      - "Cells arrest in G2, because Cdc2 can never be switched on"
      - "Cells divide smaller, because Cdc2 is switched on earlier"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Wee1 holds Cdc2 off by phosphorylating it; without the brake, Cdc2-cyclin B becomes active
      before the cell has grown to its usual size, so cells enter mitosis early and divide small,
      the phenotype that named wee1. An arrest in G2 is what loss of the activator, Cdc2 or Cdc25,
      would give. Larger cells would follow from slower activation, not from losing the
      inhibitory kinase. Repeated S phase is a failure of once-per-cycle replication control,
      not the expected result of an early mitosis.
  - stem: "A budding-yeast temperature-sensitive mutant arrests with large buds and an undivided nucleus. After a shift to the restrictive temperature, cells that were already in S phase also arrest with large buds in the same cycle. What can you conclude about when the gene acts?"
    choices:
      - "It acts at mitosis, because that is where the cells collect"
      - "It acts in G1 only, because large buds form after the G1 step"
      - "It has no fixed time, because the arrest shape is the same"
      - "It may act at or after S phase, though cells stop at mitosis"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Cells that had passed the gene's execution point when shifted would finish this cycle and
      stop in the next one. These cells stop in the current cycle although they were already in S,
      so the function was still needed at or after that time, and the arrest at mitosis is simply
      where the missing work is first noticed. Reading the arrest stage as the time of action is
      the error the execution point was defined to avoid. Placing it in G1 only would require
      S-phase cells to escape into the next cycle, which they did not. A uniform arrest shape is
      what defines a cdc mutant; it does not mean the gene lacks a time of action.
  - stem: "A diploid human cell has finished S phase and is in G2. What are its DNA content and its ploidy?"
    choices:
      - "2C DNA, and the cell is now haploid"
      - "4C DNA, and the cell is still diploid"
      - "4C DNA, and the cell is now tetraploid"
      - "2C DNA, and the cell is still diploid"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Replication doubles the DNA from 2C to 4C, but each chromosome is now two sister chromatids
      held together, so the number of chromosome sets is unchanged and the cell is still diploid.
      Calling it tetraploid confuses doubling DNA with doubling chromosome sets. A 2C diploid cell
      is a G1 (or G0) cell, which has not replicated yet. A 2C haploid cell does not arise in a somatic
      cycle; halving the sets happens in meiosis.

      Cells pile up with 4C DNA while the mitotic marker falls, so they have copied their DNA but are not entering mitosis: a G2 arrest.   - stem: "Cultured mammalian cells are synchronised in G1, S or G2, and S-phase cells are given a nuclear label before fusion.     rationale: >-

  - stem: "An asynchronous culture of a diploid cell line doubles every 20 h with no cell death and no resting cells. DNA-content flow cytometry places 50% of cells in the 2C peak. **In the summer of 1982, at Woods Hole, Tim Hunt's group found a protein in dividing sea-urchin eggs that built up through each cell cycle and vanished at every division.**

The first film, above, asks why a cell destroys its engine's partner, a cyclin, every time it divides; the answer is how a cell makes its cycle run forwards only, and copy its genome exactly once. The text keeps the film's names (the cyclin sawtooth, the brake, the rise and fall) and adds what it leaves out: the evidence, the other cyclin-CDK pairs and the harder cases.

## The record: a sawtooth and a snap

The first film draws the simplest case: two schematic traces on one time axis, for a cycle with strong brake control, such as the first cycle of a frog embryo or the frog egg extracts met below. The exact waveform depends on organism and stage; later rapid frog cleavage cycles show smoother kinase oscillations.

- **Cyclin.** A sawtooth: it climbs steadily and drops sharply at each division, because it is destroyed.
- **Kinase activity.** The activity of the mitotic kinase that cyclin switches on (first measured as an activity called MPF, maturation-promoting factor) does not follow the ramp: it stays low, snaps up just before mitosis, and crashes with cyclin.

A schematic, not data: the exact waveform depends on organism and stage." />

The two traces pose three puzzles.

1. Why does the kinase need cyclin at all?
2. If cyclin climbs steadily, why does activity lag behind and then jump?
3. Why does the cell destroy cyclin to end mitosis, instead of simply switching the kinase off?

One idea answers all three.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** CDK activity rises and falls once per cycle. Rising activity carries the cell from G1 through S and into mitosis; brakes and checkpoints decide when it may rise. At the metaphase-to-anaphase transition one enzyme, the APC/C, tags two targets for destruction: securin, so the sister chromatids separate, and cyclin B, so activity falls and the cell can leave mitosis. Low activity resets the replication origins and high activity starts copying, so the genome is copied once per cycle.

This is an organizing model, not a single dial. "CDK activity" stands for several cyclin-CDK pairs that rise in turn (the table below), and phosphatases, checkpoints and protein destruction shape each step. The closest thing to a single dial is a fission-yeast cell engineered to run on one fused cyclin-CDK: S phase starts at a lower level of its activity and mitosis at a higher one. Meiosis and endocycles (cycles that copy DNA without dividing) bend the pattern, and endocycles turn out to test it (Copy once, below).

## The engine: a kinase and a partner that comes and goes

The cycle has four phases. G1 is the gap between mitosis and the start of DNA replication. In S phase the genome is copied (the mechanics belong to [DNA replication](/lessons/dna-replication/)). G2 is the gap after copying and before mitosis. In M phase, mitosis sorts the duplicated chromosomes into two nuclei, and cytokinesis, the split of the cell itself, usually follows. Mammalian cells can also leave G1 for G0, a resting state they enter when conditions change and can leave again.

**CDKs** (cyclin-dependent kinases) are the catalytic subunits, and they are inactive alone. **Cyclins** are regulatory subunits that switch them on and steer them to targets. That answers the first puzzle. CDK levels stay fairly even while most cyclin levels rise and fall (cyclin D tracks growth signals most closely), so the rise and fall of each cyclin switches its kinase on and off. A bound cyclin is needed but not enough.

Different cyclin-CDK pairs carry the rise through different stretches of the cycle. In mammals:

| Stretch of the cycle | Cyclin | CDK |
|---|---|---|
| G1 | D | CDK4 or CDK6 |
| G1/S transition | E | CDK2 |
| S | A | CDK2 |
| G2 and M | A and B | CDK1 |

This is the usual division of labour, not a strict requirement: CDKs can stand in for one another (Going deeper).

A schematic of mammalian cells; the pairings are the usual division of labour, not a strict rule." />

The mitotic pair has an older name. MPF, the activity that makes frog oocytes mature and is also found in mitotic cells, is CDK1 (first named Cdc2, from fission yeast) bound to cyclin B.

## Holding the rise back, and letting it go

Now the second puzzle: if cyclin B climbs steadily and switches CDK1 on, why does activity lag and then jump? Because a cyclin-CDK pair can be built and still be held off. To be active, CDK1 needs four things at once:

- a cyclin bound;
- an **activating phosphate**, which a CDK-activating kinase (CAK) adds to a loop near the active site;
- **no inhibitory phosphates**: Wee1 adds one near the ATP site, on tyrosine 15, and in animal cells the related kinase Myt1 adds one on threonine 14 as well as tyrosine 15;
- **no inhibitor protein** bound.

The names to hold on to are the brake, Wee1, and its release, Cdc25; the rest is detail.

**The inhibitory phosphate is a brake.** Wee1 keeps phosphorylating new CDK1-cyclin B complexes through G2, as the first film shows, so inactive complexes pile up. The phosphatase Cdc25 takes them off (the chemistry is in [the phosphorylation switch](/lessons/phosphorylation-switch/)).

**Inhibitor proteins are a second brake.** In mammals, p21 and p27 bind CDK2 and CDK1 complexes and block them without any chemistry; the structure of p27 lying across cyclin A-CDK2 is in the 3D viewer on this page. (With cyclin D-CDK4/6 they act differently; Going deeper.) One important way to remove an inhibitor is to destroy it. Around G1/S, cyclin E-CDK2 phosphorylates p27, the SCF ubiquitin ligase tags it for the proteasome (Sic1 is the yeast counterpart), and with its brake gone cyclin E-CDK2 switches fully on.

**The release is a switch.** Once a little CDK1 escapes its brake, it phosphorylates Cdc25, making it more active, and Wee1, making it less active. These two positive loops are the snap in the first film: each active complex frees more. A switch built this way also has memory: once mitosis starts, a small dip in cyclin will not reverse it ([signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/)).

**Predict.** A fission-yeast cell is engineered to make much more Cdc25 than normal. When does it divide, and at what size?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Earlier and smaller. With more of the phosphatase, the brake comes off before the cell reaches its usual size, CDK1 snaps on early, and the cell enters mitosis small. Losing Wee1 gives the same result, which is how that gene got its name: *wee*.

</details>

### A decision in G1

Before any of this, a cell in G1 has to decide whether to divide at all. That decision is the **restriction point** (in yeast, **Start**), and a cell passes it only if it has enough nutrients and growth factors. Growth-factor signals raise cyclin D ([Ras to MAPK](/lessons/ras-to-mapk/)). Cyclin D-CDK4/6 begins to phosphorylate **Rb**, a protein that holds the E2F transcription factors off. Freed E2F switches on genes for S phase, including cyclin E, and cyclin E-CDK2 phosphorylates Rb further. In the classical model that loop is another switch: once it flips, the cell finishes the cycle even if the growth factors are taken away (later work adds a second switch; see Going deeper). Rb's role in cancer is in [cancer genetics](/lessons/cancer-genetics/). Strictly, this is a commitment point, not a check that a step is finished.

### Checkpoints pull the same levers

A **checkpoint**, in Leland Hartwell and Ted Weinert's term, holds the cycle until an earlier step is finished or repaired. The three you need all act on the engine:

- **DNA damage in G1.** p53 builds up and switches on the gene for p21. p21 binds and blocks CDK2 complexes, and the cell waits in G1.
- **Damaged or unfinished DNA in G2.** The sensor kinases ATM and ATR, through Chk1 and Chk2, inactivate Cdc25 (and Chk1 activates Wee1), so CDK1 keeps its inhibitory phosphate and the cell waits in G2. In frog egg extracts, unreplicated DNA raised the amount of cyclin needed to enter mitosis.
- **An unattached chromosome in mitosis.** Unattached kinetochores, the sites where chromosomes grip the spindle, switch on the spindle assembly checkpoint. It stops the APC/C and its partner Cdc20 from destroying securin and cyclin B, so the cell waits before anaphase, often in prometaphase. Attachment normally silences the signal, but some errors escape it: a merotelic kinetochore, gripped from both poles, can silence it and enter anaphase misattached. (Some other APC/C targets, such as cyclin A, are still destroyed.)

**Predict.** Cells lacking p53 are irradiated in G1. What happens to them?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

They lose the main burst of p21, so the lasting G1 stop fails and many enter S phase with damaged DNA. Other damage responses can delay them briefly, and they can still be stopped later, at the G2 checkpoint, which acts through Cdc25 and Wee1. Losing the G1 stop is one reason p53 is the most often mutated gene in human cancers.

</details>

## The fall: destruction makes it one way

Falling activity carries the cell out of mitosis. The G1 and G2 brakes and checkpoints act on the rise, and the spindle checkpoint holds back the fall. Each is a pause: a phosphate can be removed, an inhibitor let go, a checkpoint silenced. The way down is different, and it answers the third puzzle; the first film shows it as one enzyme tagging two proteins. Two ubiquitin ligases tag regulators for destruction by the proteasome ([ubiquitin and proteolysis](/lessons/ubiquitin-and-proteolysis/)): the SCF, met above, and the APC/C, which acts from mitosis through G1. Once kinetochore checkpoint signaling has been silenced, the APC/C with Cdc20 tags securin and cyclin B. Losing securin frees separase, the enzyme that cuts cohesin, the protein ring holding sister chromatids together, so the sisters separate (in vertebrates, losing cyclin B also lifts CDK1's own block on separase). Losing cyclin B drops CDK1 activity, phosphatases strip the mitotic phosphates, and the cell leaves mitosis. Then the APC/C with Cdh1 keeps mitotic cyclins low through G1, so activity starts the next cycle from the bottom.

Third, freed separase cuts the cohesin ring so sister chromatids separate, and cyclin B is broken down in the proteasome, leaving CDK1 inactive." caption="Once the spindle checkpoint is silenced, the APC/C with Cdc20 tags two targets: securin, so separase cuts cohesin and the sisters separate, and cyclin B, so CDK1 activity falls and the cell leaves mitosis. As the first film says, a destroyed protein has to be made again. Destruction, together with feedback switches, is a main reason the cycle's big transitions run one way (at G1/S the Rb-E2F switch adds its own memory). Destroying a brake drives the cycle forward; destroying cyclin resets it.

The first film's frog egg extract test shows that a cyclin that cannot be destroyed holds the extract in mitosis. A second test asks whether low activity alone would do.

**Predict.** Vertebrate cells in mitosis are blocked from destroying cyclin B. A drug that inhibits CDK1 is added briefly, and they start to leave mitosis. Then the drug is washed out. What happens?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

They go back toward mitosis. Cyclin B was never destroyed, so once the drug was gone CDK1 switched back on. After a short treatment, low activity on its own was only a pause; destroying cyclin is what normally keeps activity low and makes the exit one way. Longer inhibition can lock cells out of mitosis even with cyclin B present, so feedback helps too (Potapova and colleagues: Nature, 2006, and 2009).

</details>

## Copy once: low activity licenses, high activity fires

Before an origin of replication can fire, it must be **licensed**: loaded with the helicase that will later unwind the DNA there. Licensing needs low CDK activity; firing a licensed origin needs S-phase CDK activity, together with a second kinase, DDK. High CDK activity from S phase to mitosis also blocks relicensing in yeast, and contributes in animal cells, which add further locks, such as the protein geminin and destruction of the licensing protein Cdt1 (the details belong to [DNA replication](/lessons/dna-replication/)). So, as the first film shows, an origin can fire at most once per cycle; many licensed origins never fire and are copied by forks from their neighbours. In an ordinary cycle the way back to low activity, and to new licences, is through mitosis, where cyclin is destroyed. The prediction below shows another way.

**Predict.** Fission-yeast cells lack their mitotic B-cyclin, Cdc13. They finish S phase, but CDK activity then falls without any mitosis. What happens to their DNA?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

They copy it again, as the first film shows: repeated S phases without dividing. Cells that overproduce the CDK inhibitor Rum1 do the same (Hayles and colleagues, 1994; Moreno and Nurse, 1994). Some normal cells use this on purpose: endocycles are the natural version.

</details>

Before anyone knew of CDKs, cell fusion showed that a replicated nucleus does not simply start again. In 1970 Rao and Johnson fused cultured HeLa cells taken from different phases, so that two nuclei shared one cytoplasm. As later reviews describe the results, a G1 nucleus in a cell fused with an S-phase cell started replicating early, while a G2 nucleus in the same situation did not. Two conclusions follow. S-phase cytoplasm carries an activity that can start replication, which in the rule's terms fires the origins a G1 nucleus has licensed. And under these fusion conditions a G2 nucleus did not start another round, which fits licensing: its fired origins cannot be licensed again until activity falls, normally at mitotic exit (the Cdc13 and Rum1 cells and endocycles show it can happen without mitosis).

Fusing a mitotic cell with an interphase cell had a different outcome: the interphase nucleus condensed its chromosomes prematurely (Johnson and Rao, 1970), and how the chromosomes looked depended on the phase of that nucleus. Mitotic cytoplasm is dominant: it is full of active CDK1-cyclin B, which, with the kinases and phosphatases it controls, can condense an interphase nucleus from G1, S or G2.

### Design problem

Using cell fusion, design experiments to ask whether S-phase cytoplasm can drive replication in another nucleus, and why a G2 nucleus might not respond.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Synchronise cultured mammalian cells in G1, S and G2 and confirm each population by DNA content (2C or 4C, where 1C is the DNA of one haploid genome; see Measuring the states below), using a mitotic marker such as phosphorylated histone H3 to show that the 4C cells are in G2 and not M. Pre-label one partner before fusing, for example with a nuclear dye or a long label in the S cells, so that every nucleus in a fused cell can be identified.

Fuse S with G1, and S with G2. Give a short pulse of a DNA precursor (radioactive thymidine or EdU) and score each nucleus separately. Expected result, as in Rao and Johnson's 1970 work: G1 nuclei start replicating early, G2 nuclei do not.

Controls: same-phase fusions, where G1 with G1 and G2 with G2 test whether fusion itself starts replication and S with S tests whether fusion blocks it, plus unfused neighbours on the same slide.

Interpretation: S cytoplasm carries a replication-starting activity, and under these fusion conditions a replicated G2 nucleus does not initiate another round in S-phase cytoplasm. A third pairing, mitotic with interphase, should give premature chromosome condensation.

Limit: fusion identifies an activity and a nuclear competence, not the molecules. Naming them needs biochemistry or genetics.

</details>

## How the engine was found: frogs, yeast and sea urchins

The model came from three lines of work that began apart and met in one complex. Frog oocytes gave an activity: in 1971 Yoshio Masui and Clement Markert injected cytoplasm from progesterone-treated oocytes into untreated ones, which then matured, and named the activity maturation-promoting factor (MPF). Like cell fusion, this revealed an activity, not a molecule. Yeast genetics gave the genes: budding-yeast *CDC28*, needed at Start, and fission-yeast *cdc2*, an accelerator needed both in G1 and in G2, with *wee1* as its brake; *CDC28* and then a human gene could each stand in for *cdc2*. Sea urchins gave cyclin (published in 1983), which the frog egg-extract tests in The fall confirmed in 1989. The lines met in 1988, when highly purified MPF from *Xenopus* eggs proved to contain the frog relative of Cdc2, and in 1990, when its other component proved to be cyclin B.

One idea from the yeast work is still how mutants are read. In 1970 Leland Hartwell, Joseph Culotti and Brian Reid described temperature-sensitive mutants of budding yeast, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, that grow at a low temperature and stop at a higher one, each with a uniform shape. They called them cdc, for cell division cycle. The bud reports the stage: none in G1, small in S, large by mitosis.

Their key idea was to separate two times. Picture a car that must fill up at one station on every lap. If the station closes just after the car has filled up, it finishes this lap and stalls on the next; if it closes just before, the car stalls later on this lap, well past the station. The station is the **execution point**: the last time in the cycle the gene's function is needed. Where the car stalls is the **termination point**, or arrest point: where cells collect when the function fails. Shift a growing culture to the restrictive temperature and follow single cells: the fraction that finishes this cycle tells you where the execution point lies.

Reading a population needs one more tool: telling, in one frozen moment, which phase each cell is in. The first film connected the activity cycle to one round of replication, so DNA amount constrains the phase without identifying it exactly. ## Measuring the states

The first measurement is DNA content. One C is the DNA of one haploid genome, so a diploid cell has 2C in G1 and 4C in G2 and M. Doubling the DNA does not double the ploidy: a G2 cell is still diploid, with each chromosome made of two sister chromatids. To read DNA content, fix the cells and stain them with propidium iodide, a dye that fluoresces when bound to nucleic acid, plus RNase, because the dye also binds RNA. A flow cytometer measures each cell's fluorescence one at a time and builds a histogram: a 2C peak, a 4C peak, and S-phase cells smeared between.

The histogram has two blind spots. G0 cells sit in the 2C peak with G1, and mitotic cells sit in the 4C peak with G2. Two more stains help. EdU, a thymidine analogue, is built into DNA made during a short pulse and is then detected by click chemistry with a fluorescent tag, so it marks cells in S and separates them from the edges of the 2C and 4C peaks, where early and late S cells blur into G1 and G2. An antibody to histone H3 phosphorylated on serine 10 marks condensing mitotic chromosomes. It is close to a mitotic marker but not exact: the mark begins in late G2, spreads across chromosomes as they condense and fades during anaphase. G0 is harder, and no single stain marks it cleanly (see Going deeper).

Of 10,000 cells, 5,500 fall in the 2C peak, 2,000 in the 4C peak and 2,500 between. A 30-minute EdU pulse labels 25% of cells, which agrees that the cells between the peaks are in S. And 3% are phospho-H3 positive; assume the stain marks every mitotic cell and nothing else.

## Going deeper

### The extract thresholds

In *Xenopus* egg extracts with protein synthesis blocked, entry into mitosis needed about 32 to 40 nM of added nondegradable cyclin B, but an extract already in mitosis stayed there down to about 16 to 24 nM (Sha and colleagues, 2003). Two thresholds with a gap between them are the mark of a switch with memory; the numbers belong to that assay, not to every cell.

### A second switch in G1

The Rb-E2F loop is not the only memory at the restriction point. Recall that the APC/C with Cdh1 keeps mitotic cyclins low through G1. In human MCF10A cells, Cappell and colleagues (2018) found a second G1 switch with memory: the switching-off of APC/C-Cdh1, held in place by a double-negative loop with EMI1 (knocking down EMI1 abolished the memory).

### Beyond the usual pairings

Mouse embryos lacking CDK2, CDK3, CDK4 and CDK6 still develop to mid-gestation, with CDK1 binding all the cyclins. CDK inhibitor proteins are also less simple with cyclin D-CDK4/6 than with CDK2 and CDK1 complexes: they help that complex assemble, and tyrosine-phosphorylated p27 can leave it active.

### Marker fine print

Cells out of the cycle lose Ki67, a protein made in cycling cells, but its level falls gradually through G1 and G0, so it does not mark G0 cleanly. Saldivar and colleagues combined DNA content, EdU and phospho-H3, read by image-based cytometry rather than flow, to separate phases in human RPE-1 and MCF10A cells (Science, 2018).

### The three lines in full

**Frogs.** A frog oocyte waits in prophase of its first meiotic division until progesterone tells it to resume. Masui and Markert's oocytes were from the leopard frog *Rana pipiens*, and removing the donor's nucleus did not prevent the activity.

**Fission yeast.** Paul Nurse used *Schizosaccharomyces pombe*, a rod that divides in the middle, so length tells you its age. In 1975 he described a mutant that divided at a smaller size than normal. The mutation changed when division happened, not whether. Most later small-cell mutants mapped to a gene named *wee1*; one mapped to *cdc2*, whose other alleles block mitosis. In 1982 budding-yeast DNA carrying *CDC28* rescued a fission-yeast *cdc2* mutant: two distant yeasts, one job. In 1987 a human gene did the same.

**Sea urchins.** In work done in the summer of 1982 and published in 1983, Tim Hunt's group followed new proteins in dividing embryos of the sea urchin *Arbacia punctulata*. Cyclin's disappearance at division was a correlation, so tests came next. In *Xenopus* egg extracts, destroying the messenger RNA stopped the cycles in interphase and adding back cyclin mRNA restored them (Murray and Kirschner, 1989).

### Yeast names and early mutants

In the 1970 paper, the *cdc-3* mutant kept budding and dividing its nucleus while its cells failed to separate, so steps of the cycle can come apart. A naming warning: Hartwell's budding-yeast *cdc-2*, which stopped at mitosis, is not the fission-yeast *cdc2*. Nurse and Yvonne Bissett concluded in 1981 that *cdc2* acts both in G1 and in G2, and the 1982 *CDC28* rescue was by David Beach, Barbara Durkacz and Nurse.

### Who found MPF's parts

Smith and Ecker reported a cytoplasm transfer like Masui and Markert's in the same year, 1971. Hunt's group saw similar proteins in another sea urchin and a clam, and clam cyclin A mRNA injected into *Xenopus* oocytes made them resume meiosis (Swenson, Farrell and Ruderman, 1986). The nondegradable-cyclin experiment was by Murray, Solomon and Kirschner (1989). In 1988 Lohka, Hayes and Maller purified MPF from *Xenopus* eggs; antibodies to fission-yeast Cdc2 recognised its 32 kDa protein (Gautier and colleagues), and Dunphy and colleagues reached Cdc2 by a separate route.
