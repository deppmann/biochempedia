techniques:
  - name: "DNA-content flow cytometry with EdU and phospho-H3"
    blurb: >-
      Fixed cells are stained with a DNA dye (propidium iodide plus RNase, or DAPI) and passed one
      at a time past a laser, giving a histogram with a 2C and a 4C peak. A short EdU pulse marks
      cells in S phase, and an antibody to histone H3 phosphorylated on serine 10 marks mitotic
      cells (and some late-G2 cells), separating states that DNA content alone lumps together.
  - name: "Temperature-sensitive mutants and shift experiments"
    blurb: >-
      A mutant protein works at a permissive temperature and fails at a restrictive one. Shifting
      a growing culture and following single cells shows where cells stop (the arrest or
      termination point) and, from which cells finish their cycle first, when the gene was needed
      (the execution point).
  - name: "Cell fusion and cytoplasm transfer"
    blurb: >-
      Two cells in different phases are fused so their nuclei share one cytoplasm, or cytoplasm
      from one cell is injected into another. Each nucleus is scored separately for DNA synthesis
      or chromosome condensation. The result shows a transferable activity and whether a nucleus
      can respond to it, not the molecule responsible.
  - name: "Cycling egg extracts"
    blurb: >-
      Crushed Xenopus eggs give a cytoplasm that runs through repeated cycles in a tube. Messenger
      RNA can be destroyed and added back, and cyclin added at known concentrations, so the cause
      of entry into and exit from mitosis can be tested directly.

    choices:
      - "The CDK is the kinase, and the cyclin level rises and falls"
      - "The CDK level rises and falls, and the cyclin level stays even"
      - "The cyclin is the kinase, and the CDK is its regulatory partner"
      - "Both partners are kinases that phosphorylate each other in turn"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The CDK carries the catalytic site and is inactive alone; the cyclin is a regulatory subunit
      whose level rises through part of the cycle and falls when it is destroyed, switching the
      kinase on and off. Making the cyclin the kinase reverses the roles; cyclins have no kinase
      domain. The partners do not phosphorylate each other in turn; only the CDK is a kinase. In
      most cycles CDK levels stay fairly even and it is the cyclins that oscillate, which is how

  - stem: "Cycling frog egg extracts are given a cyclin B that the APC/C cannot destroy. Everything else in the extract is normal. What do you expect?"
    choices:
      - "The extract stays in interphase, because cyclin B is never used up"
      - "The extract enters mitosis and stays there, because CDK1 stays active"
      - "The extract cycles faster, because cyclin B builds up sooner"
      - "The extract copies its DNA again, because mitosis is skipped"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Cyclin B binding switches CDK1 on, so the extract enters mitosis as usual. Leaving mitosis needs the APC/C to destroy cyclin B and switch CDK1 off; with a cyclin that cannot be destroyed, CDK1 stays active and the extract stays in mitosis, as Murray, Solomon and Kirschner found. Staying in interphase would follow from too little cyclin, not from cyclin that lasts. Faster cycling would need cyclin to be both made and removed, and here removal is blocked.   - stem: "A fission-yeast strain lacks Wee1, the kinase that puts an inhibitory phosphate on Cdc2. Compared with wild type, what do you expect?"
    choices:
      - "Cells divide larger, because Cdc25 has no phosphate to remove"
      - "Cells copy their DNA twice, because S phase repeats itself"
      - "Cells arrest in G2, because Cdc2 can never be switched on"
      - "Cells divide smaller, because Cdc2 is switched on earlier"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Wee1 holds Cdc2 off by phosphorylating it; without the brake, Cdc2-cyclin B becomes active
      before the cell has grown to its usual size, so cells enter mitosis early and divide small,
      the phenotype that named wee1. An arrest in G2 is what loss of the activator, Cdc2 or Cdc25,

  - stem: "A budding-yeast temperature-sensitive mutant arrests with large buds and an undivided nucleus. After a shift to the restrictive temperature, cells that were already in S phase also arrest with large buds in the same cycle. What can you conclude about when the gene acts?"
    choices:
      - "It acts at mitosis, because that is where the cells collect"
      - "It acts in G1 only, because large buds form after the G1 step"
      - "It has no fixed time, because the arrest shape is the same"
      - "It may act at or after S phase, though cells stop at mitosis"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Cells that had passed the gene's execution point when shifted would finish this cycle and
      stop in the next one. These cells stop in the current cycle although they were already in S,
      so the function was still needed at or after that time, and the arrest at mitosis is simply
      where the missing work is first noticed. Reading the arrest stage as the time of action is
      the error the execution point was defined to avoid. Placing it in G1 only would require
      S-phase cells to escape into the next cycle, which they did not. A uniform arrest shape is

  - stem: "A diploid human cell has finished S phase and is in G2. What are its DNA content and its ploidy?"
    choices:
      - "2C DNA, and the cell is now haploid"
      - "4C DNA, and the cell is still diploid"
      - "4C DNA, and the cell is now tetraploid"
      - "2C DNA, and the cell is still diploid"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Replication doubles the DNA from 2C to 4C, but each chromosome is now two sister chromatids
      held together, so the number of chromosome sets is unchanged and the cell is still diploid.
      Calling it tetraploid confuses doubling DNA with doubling chromosome sets. A 2C diploid cell

**Every eukaryotic cell, from yeast to human, runs its cycle on one conserved engine: a cyclin-dependent kinase, or CDK, that works only while a cyclin partner is bound. The cell makes cyclin and the kinase switches on; it destroys cyclin and the kinase switches off.**

That engine has two jobs to get right. The genome must be copied exactly once, and the copies must be split safely between two cells. So the engine comes with brakes that hold it off, checkpoints that keep it waiting until a step is done, and protein destruction that resets it and makes each step one way. This lesson builds that model first, then shows the experiments that found it, and ends with how you measure which phase a cell is in.

## One engine: a kinase whose partner rises and falls

The cycle has four phases. G1 is the gap between mitosis and the start of DNA replication. In S phase the genome is copied (the mechanics belong to [DNA replication](/lessons/dna-replication/)). G2 is the gap after copying and before mitosis. In M phase, mitosis sorts the duplicated chromosomes into two nuclei, and cytokinesis, the split of the cell itself, usually follows. Mammalian cells can also leave G1 for G0, a resting state they enter when conditions change and can leave again.

**CDKs** (cyclin-dependent kinases) are the catalytic subunits, and they are inactive alone. **Cyclins** are regulatory subunits that switch them on and steer them to targets. Full activity also needs an activating phosphate added by a CDK-activating kinase (CAK). Cyclin levels rise and fall through the cycle while CDK levels stay fairly even, so the rise and fall of each cyclin switches its kinase on and off.

In mammals, cyclin D with CDK4 or CDK6 acts in G1, cyclin E with CDK2 at the G1/S transition, cyclin A with CDK2 in S, and cyclins A and B with CDK1 in G2 and M. This is the usual division of labour, not a strict requirement: CDKs can stand in for one another (see Going deeper). Growth-factor signals reach cyclin D (see [Ras to MAPK](/lessons/ras-to-mapk/)), and the Rb brake it releases is in [cancer genetics](/lessons/cancer-genetics/).

The mitotic pair has an older name. Maturation-promoting factor, or MPF, the activity that makes frog oocytes mature and is also found in mitotic cells, is CDK1 (first named Cdc2, from fission yeast) bound to cyclin B. The engine is conserved: a budding-yeast gene and a human gene can each stand in for the fission-yeast one. The experiments that showed this come later in the lesson.

## Brakes, checkpoints and a one-way reset

**Brakes.** Wee1 (with the related kinase Myt1 in animal cells) adds an inhibitory phosphate to CDK1, holding the complex off while cyclin accumulates; the phosphatase Cdc25 removes it (the chemistry is in [the phosphorylation switch](/lessons/phosphorylation-switch/)). CDK inhibitor proteins, such as p21 and p27 in mammals, bind CDK2 and CDK1 complexes and block them. (With cyclin D-CDK4/6 they act differently; see Going deeper.)

**A switch.** Active CDK1 also activates Cdc25 and inhibits Wee1, two positive loops that make mitotic entry a switch ([signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/)). Once entry starts, the switch is all or none. In frog egg extracts, entering mitosis took more cyclin B than staying in it, so the extract's state depended on where it had come from.

**Checkpoints.** Brakes let a cell wait; checkpoints decide when it must. Leland Hartwell and Ted Weinert named them checkpoints: controls that hold the cycle until a step is finished. At the restriction point in G1 (Start in yeast), a cell commits to dividing only if it has enough nutrients and growth factors. In G2, if DNA is damaged or replication is incomplete, checkpoint pathways act on Wee1 and Cdc25 and keep CDK1 off, so the cell does not enter mitosis; in frog egg extracts, unreplicated DNA raised the amount of cyclin needed to enter. In mitosis, the spindle assembly checkpoint holds back the APC/C until every chromosome is attached to the spindle. Each checkpoint works by acting on the engine.

**Destruction.** Two ubiquitin ligases tag regulators for destruction by the proteasome ([ubiquitin and proteolysis](/lessons/ubiquitin-and-proteolysis/)). The SCF acts at several points, notably around G1/S. The APC/C acts from mitosis through G1: with Cdc20 it targets securin, which lets sister chromatids separate, and then cyclin B, which ends M phase; with Cdh1 it keeps mitotic cyclins low through G1. A destroyed protein cannot be switched back on; it has to be made again. That is what makes each transition one way and resets the engine for the next cycle.

The evidence is direct. In frog egg extracts that cycle in a tube, a cyclin that could not be destroyed held the extract in mitosis. Making cyclin drives entry; destroying it permits exit.

## Copy once: what cell fusion revealed

Fuse a cell that is copying its DNA with a cell that has not started yet, and the second nucleus begins copying early. Fuse it with a cell that has already finished, and that nucleus refuses. Something in the cytoplasm says "copy now", and something in the nucleus remembers whether it already has.

In 1970 Rao and Johnson fused cultured HeLa cells taken from different phases, so that two nuclei shared one cytoplasm. As later reviews describe the results, a G1 nucleus in a cell fused with an S-phase cell started replicating early, while a G2 nucleus in the same situation did not. Two conclusions follow. S-phase cytoplasm carries an activity that can start replication. And a nucleus that has already replicated cannot respond until it has been through mitosis, which is how DNA gets copied once per cycle and no more. Part of the molecular basis is licensing: replication origins are licensed only in late mitosis and G1. In yeast, high CDK activity from S phase to mitosis blocks relicensing, and animal cells add other blocks, so a replicated nucleus cannot be licensed again until it has been through mitosis (the details belong to [DNA replication](/lessons/dna-replication/)).

Fusing a mitotic cell with an interphase cell had a different outcome: the interphase nucleus condensed its chromosomes prematurely (Johnson and Rao, 1970), and how the chromosomes looked depended on the phase of that nucleus. Mitotic cytoplasm is dominant. Read this in light of the engine: mitotic cytoplasm is full of active CDK1-cyclin B, which, with the kinases and phosphatases it controls, can condense an interphase nucleus from G1, S or G2.

### Design problem

Using cell fusion, design experiments to ask whether S-phase cytoplasm can drive replication in another nucleus, and why a G2 nucleus might not respond.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Synchronise cultured mammalian cells in G1, S and G2 and confirm each population by DNA content (2C or 4C, explained under Measuring the states below), using a mitotic marker such as phosphorylated histone H3 to show that the 4C cells are in G2 and not M. Pre-label one partner before fusing, for example with a nuclear dye or a long label in the S cells, so that every nucleus in a fused cell can be identified.

Fuse S with G1, and S with G2. Give a short pulse of a DNA precursor (radioactive thymidine or EdU) and score each nucleus separately. Expected result, as in Rao and Johnson's 1970 work: G1 nuclei start replicating early, G2 nuclei do not.

Controls: same-phase fusions, where G1 with G1 and G2 with G2 test whether fusion itself starts replication and S with S tests whether fusion blocks it, plus unfused neighbours on the same slide.

Interpretation: S cytoplasm carries a replication-starting activity, and a nucleus that has replicated cannot answer it again before mitosis. A third pairing, mitotic with interphase, should give premature chromosome condensation, with an appearance that depends on the phase of the interphase nucleus.

Limit: fusion identifies an activity and a nuclear competence, not the molecules. Naming them needs biochemistry or genetics.

## How the engine was found: frogs, yeast and sea urchins

The model came from three lines of work that began apart and met in one complex.

A frog oocyte waits in prophase of its first meiotic division until progesterone tells it to resume. In 1971 Yoshio Masui and Clement Markert took cytoplasm from progesterone-treated oocytes of the leopard frog *Rana pipiens* and injected it into untreated oocytes, which then matured. Removing the donor's nucleus did not prevent the activity. They called it maturation-promoting factor (MPF), and it was later found in mitotic cells too. Like cell fusion, this revealed an activity, not a molecule.

In 1970 Leland Hartwell, Joseph Culotti and Brian Reid described temperature-sensitive mutants of budding yeast, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, that grow at a low temperature and stop at a higher one, each with a uniform shape. They called them cdc, for cell division cycle. The bud reports the stage: none in G1, small in S, large by mitosis.

Their key idea was to separate two times. A car that runs out of fuel stops where the tank ran dry, not at the station you drove past. The **execution point** is when a gene's function is normally performed. The **termination point**, or arrest point, is where cells collect when it is not. Shift a growing culture to the restrictive temperature and follow single cells: cells already past the execution point finish this cycle and stop in the next, while cells not yet past it stop in this one. The fraction that finishes tells you where the execution point lies. The budding-yeast gene that matters here is *CDC28*, needed at Start, the commitment point in G1.

Paul Nurse used fission yeast, *Schizosaccharomyces pombe*, a rod that divides in the middle, so length tells you its age. In 1975 he described a mutant that divided at a smaller size than normal. The mutation changed when division happened, not whether. Most later small-cell mutants mapped to a gene named *wee1*; one mapped to *cdc2*, whose other alleles block mitosis. So *cdc2* behaves as an accelerator and *wee1* as a brake. *cdc2* acts both in G1 and in G2. In 1982 budding-yeast DNA carrying *CDC28* rescued a fission-yeast *cdc2* mutant: two distant yeasts, one job. In 1987 a human gene did the same.

In 1983 Tim Hunt's group followed new proteins in dividing embryos of the sea urchin *Arbacia punctulata*. One built up through each cycle and was destroyed at each division; they named it cyclin. Disappearing at division is a correlation, so tests came next. In *Xenopus* egg extracts that cycle in a tube, destroying the messenger RNA stopped the cycles in interphase and adding back cyclin mRNA restored them (Murray and Kirschner, 1989), and the nondegradable cyclin described above held the extract in mitosis.

The lines met in 1988, when highly purified MPF from *Xenopus* eggs proved to contain the frog relative of Cdc2. In 1990 its other component proved to be cyclin B. MPF is Cdc2, now called CDK1, bound to cyclin B.

## Measuring the states

The first measurement is DNA content. One C is the DNA of one haploid genome, so a diploid cell has 2C in G1 and 4C in G2 and M. Doubling the DNA does not double the ploidy: a G2 cell is still diploid, with each chromosome made of two sister chromatids. To read DNA content, fix the cells and stain them with propidium iodide, a dye that fluoresces when bound to nucleic acid, plus RNase, because the dye also binds RNA. A flow cytometer measures each cell's fluorescence one at a time and builds a histogram: a 2C peak, a 4C peak, and S-phase cells smeared between.

The histogram has two blind spots. G0 cells sit in the 2C peak with G1, and mitotic cells sit in the 4C peak with G2. Two more stains help. EdU, a thymidine analogue, is built into DNA made during a short pulse and is then detected by click chemistry with a fluorescent tag, so it marks cells in S and separates them from the edges of the 2C and 4C peaks, where early and late S cells blur into G1 and G2. An antibody to histone H3 phosphorylated on serine 10 marks condensing mitotic chromosomes. It is close to a mitotic marker but not exact: the mark begins in late G2 and is removed from anaphase on. G0 is harder, and no single stain marks it cleanly (see Going deeper).

### Worked problem: fractions are not durations

The numbers here are illustrative. Suppose an asynchronous culture of a diploid human cell line doubles every 24 h, every cell is cycling, and none die. Of 10,000 cells, 5,500 fall in the 2C peak, 2,000 in the 4C peak and 2,500 between. A 30-minute EdU pulse labels 25% of cells, which agrees that the cells between the peaks are in S. And 3% are phospho-H3 positive; assume the stain marks every mitotic cell and nothing else.

**Step 1.** Fractions: G1 0.55, S 0.25, G2 plus M 0.20, of which M is 0.03.

**Step 2.** The tempting move is to multiply by 24 h: 13.2, 6.0 and 4.8 h. That is wrong. Each division turns one old cell into two newborn ones, so a growing culture always holds twice as many cells just born as cells about to divide. Early phases are overrepresented.

**Step 3.** For a culture in steady exponential growth, the time a cell takes to pass the first fraction F of the population, ordered by age, is

> t = -T × log2(1 - F/2), where T is the doubling time

- End of G1 (F = 0.55): t = -24 × log2(0.725) = 24 × 0.464 = 11.13 h.
- End of S (F = 0.80): t = -24 × log2(0.600) = 17.69 h, so S lasts about 6.55 h (working from unrounded values).
- Start of M (F = 0.97): t = -24 × log2(0.515) = 22.98 h. M lasts 24 - 22.98 = 1.02 h, and G2 lasts 22.98 - 17.69 = 5.29 h.

Check: 11.13 + 6.55 + 5.29 + 1.02 = 23.99 h, 24 h within rounding. The naive estimate made G1 two hours too long and G2 plus M (6.31 h) one and a half hours too short. The model assumes identical phase lengths in every cell and no resting or dying cells, so treat these as estimates.

## Going deeper

### The extract thresholds

In *Xenopus* egg extracts with protein synthesis blocked, entry into mitosis needed about 32 to 40 nM of added nondegradable cyclin B, but an extract already in mitosis stayed there down to about 16 to 24 nM (Sha and colleagues, 2003). Unreplicated DNA raised the entry threshold. Two thresholds with a gap between them are the mark of a switch with memory; the numbers belong to that assay, not to every cell.

### Beyond the usual pairings

Mouse embryos lacking CDK2, CDK3, CDK4 and CDK6 still develop to mid-gestation, with CDK1 binding all the cyclins. CDK inhibitor proteins are also less simple with cyclin D-CDK4/6 than with CDK2 and CDK1 complexes: they help that complex assemble, and tyrosine-phosphorylated p27 can leave it active.

### Marker fine print

Cells out of the cycle lose Ki67, a protein made in cycling cells, but its level falls gradually through G1 and G0, so it does not mark G0 cleanly. Saldivar and colleagues combined DNA content, EdU and phospho-H3, read by image-based cytometry rather than flow, to separate phases in human RPE-1 and MCF10A cells (Science, 2018).

### Yeast names and early mutants

In the 1970 paper, the *cdc-3* mutant kept budding and dividing its nucleus while its cells failed to separate, so steps of the cycle can come apart. A naming warning: Hartwell's budding-yeast *cdc-2*, which stopped at mitosis, is not the fission-yeast *cdc2*. Nurse and Yvonne Bissett concluded in 1981 that *cdc2* acts both in G1 and in G2, and the 1982 *CDC28* rescue was by David Beach, Barbara Durkacz and Nurse.

### Who found MPF's parts

Smith and Ecker reported a cytoplasm transfer like Masui and Markert's in the same year, 1971. Hunt's group saw similar proteins in another sea urchin and a clam, and clam cyclin A mRNA injected into *Xenopus* oocytes made them resume meiosis (Swenson, Farrell and Ruderman, 1986). The nondegradable-cyclin experiment was by Murray, Solomon and Kirschner (1989). In 1988 Lohka, Hayes and Maller purified MPF from *Xenopus* eggs; antibodies to fission-yeast Cdc2 recognised its 32 kDa protein (Gautier and colleagues), and Dunphy and colleagues reached Cdc2 by a separate route.
