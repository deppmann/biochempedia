practiceQuestions:
  - stem: "Why does the cell expend ATP during ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation, given that hydrolysis of a peptide bond is itself exergonic?"
    choices:
      - "The ATP is consumed to directly cleave the peptide bonds inside the 20S core"
      - "The ATP is used to activate ubiquitin (E1) and to unfold and thread substrates through the 19S cap, paying for specificity and processivity rather than for bond breaking"
      - "The proteasome stores the ATP as a phosphorylated intermediate that is later released as heat"
      - "ATP is required to synthesize new ubiquitin molecules for every round of degradation"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "Peptide-bond hydrolysis releases energy, so the cell is not paying to break bonds. ATP is spent at E1 (activating ubiquitin via a thioester) and at the 19S cap (recognizing, unfolding, and threading the tagged substrate into the 20S barrel). The principle is that ATP buys specificity and processivity. The 20S core cuts bonds without ATP, no high-energy phosphointermediate is stored as heat, and ubiquitin is recycled by DUBs rather than resynthesized each cycle."
  - stem: "A K63-linked polyubiquitin chain is attached to a target protein. Based on the ubiquitin code, what is the most likely consequence?"
    choices:
      - "Cleavage of the target into short peptides"
      - "Export of the target out of the cell"
      - "Immediate degradation by the 26S proteasome"
      - "A nonproteolytic outcome such as DNA repair, membrane trafficking, or immune signaling"
    answer: 3
    rationale: "Linkage type encodes meaning. K48-linked chains (>=4 ubiquitins) are the canonical proteasomal destruction signal; K63-linked chains instead drive nonproteolytic processes including DNA damage repair, trafficking, and immune/inflammatory signaling. Generation of short peptides is the result of proteasomal degradation (a K48 outcome), and ubiquitin chains are not a general signal for secretion out of the cell."

    choices:
      - "Transferred to alpha-ketoglutarate; requires pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)"
      - "Transferred directly to ammonia; requires NAD+"
      - "Transferred to oxaloacetate; requires biotin"
      - "Transferred to pyruvate; requires thiamine pyrophosphate"
    answer: 0
    rationale: "Aminotransferases move the alpha-amino group onto alpha-ketoglutarate, generating glutamate (the nitrogen collection point) and the corresponding alpha-keto acid.   - stem: "An infant presents with hyperammonemia. Laboratory studies show LOW plasma citrulline and HIGH urinary orotic acid. Which urea cycle enzyme is most likely deficient?"
    choices:
      - "Argininosuccinate lyase"
      - "Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS-I)"
      - "Ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC)"
      - "Arginase"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "OTC condenses carbamoyl phosphate with ornithine to make citrulline. When OTC is deficient, citrulline is made poorly (low citrulline), and the carbamoyl phosphate that accumulates spills into cytosolic pyrimidine synthesis, raising orotic acid. CPS-I deficiency also lowers citrulline but does NOT raise orotic acid (carbamoyl phosphate is not made in excess). Arginase and argininosuccinate lyase defects produce different metabolite patterns and do not give this low-citrulline/high-orotate triad."
  - stem: "Which pair of amino acids is purely ketogenic, and what is the underlying reason a purely ketogenic amino acid cannot yield net glucose?"
    choices:
      - "Glutamate and glutamine, because they enter as alpha-ketoglutarate"
      - "Aspartate and asparagine, because they enter as oxaloacetate"
      - "Alanine and serine, because they enter as pyruvate"
      - "Leucine and lysine, because they enter as acetyl-CoA or acetoacetyl-CoA, which cannot be turned back into pyruvate or into net oxaloacetate"
    answer: 3
    rationale: "Leucine and lysine are the only two purely ketogenic amino acids: leucine's carbons enter as acetyl-CoA and acetoacetyl-CoA, and lysine's as acetoacetyl-CoA. Because pyruvate dehydrogenase is irreversible, acetyl-CoA cannot be converted back to pyruvate, and each turn of the TCA cycle takes in two carbons from acetyl-CoA and gives off two as CO2, so it never makes extra oxaloacetate and there is no net path to glucose. Alanine and serine (pyruvate), glutamate/glutamine (alpha-ketoglutarate), and aspartate/asparagine (oxaloacetate) all enter as glucogenic intermediates and can yield net glucose."

  - stem: "A muscle biopsy from a fasting marathon runner shows net export of alanine to the blood, while the liver shows increased glucose output. A tracer placed on the amino nitrogen of muscle alanine later appears in urea. Which sequence best describes this nitrogen's journey?"
    choices:
      - "Muscle alanine is oxidized to CO2 in the liver, releasing its nitrogen as nitrate"
      - "Muscle alanine to liver, where alanine is transaminated to pyruvate and its nitrogen enters glutamate, then is released as ammonium and fixed by CPS-I into the urea cycle"
      - "Muscle alanine is converted directly to urea in the muscle and exported to the kidney"
      - "Muscle alanine donates its nitrogen to oxaloacetate in the liver, forming aspartate that is excreted unchanged"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "In the glucose-alanine cycle, muscle packages nitrogen as alanine and ships it to the liver. Glutamate dehydrogenase then releases ammonium, which CPS-I fixes into carbamoyl phosphate to begin the urea cycle, so the tracer nitrogen ends up in urea (some can also enter as aspartate's nitrogen at argininosuccinate synthetase). Muscle lacks the urea cycle, aspartate is not excreted intact (its nitrogen is the second nitrogen of urea), and nitrogen is not excreted as nitrate."

  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: "Cells don't just build proteins, they also destroy them on a schedule, and which proteins die is a controlled decision. A small protein called ubiquitin gets attached to a doomed protein as a tag. A chain of ubiquitin built one specific way (called K48) is a 'destroy me' signal, and a giant machine called the proteasome shreds the tagged protein into small pieces. Three enzymes, E1, E2, and E3, add the tag, and the E3 is the one that decides who gets tagged. When the amino acids from broken-down proteins are burned for fuel, their nitrogen becomes ammonia, which is poisonous, so the liver locks it into a safe molecule called urea using a five-step loop, the urea cycle. The carbon skeletons left over are not wasted; they feed back into the pathways that make energy or glucose. Some are 'glucogenic' (can become glucose) and a couple, leucine and lysine, can only become fat-type fuel."
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: "Protein turnover is regulated proteolysis, and the central paradox is that the cell spends ATP to degrade proteins even though peptide bond hydrolysis is exergonic; ATP pays for marking and unfolding, not for bond breaking. Ubiquitin (76 aa, C-terminal Gly-Gly) is conjugated to substrate lysines through an E1 (ATP-dependent activation, thioester intermediate), E2 (conjugation), E3 (substrate recognition, the specificity step) cascade. Linkage type is a code: K48 polyubiquitin (>=4) targets the 26S proteasome; K63 signals trafficking, DNA repair, and immunity; mono-Ub often directs trafficking or chromatin changes. The 26S proteasome uses 19S caps to recognize, unfold, and ATP-thread substrate into the 20S core, whose protease sites face inward. Catabolism of the amino acids themselves begins with PLP-dependent transamination onto alpha-ketoglutarate (forming glutamate) and oxidative deamination by glutamate dehydrogenase, releasing ammonium. Ammonia is detoxified in the liver via the urea cycle: CPS-I (rate-limiting, NAG-activated, mitochondrial, 2 ATP), OTC, argininosuccinate synthetase (aspartate donates the second N, ATP to AMP), argininosuccinate lyase (yields fumarate, linking to TCA), and arginase (regenerates ornithine, a catalyst). The carbon skeletons enter at seven points; 13 amino acids are purely glucogenic, only Leu and Lys are purely ketogenic, and five are mixed."
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: "The ubiquitin-proteasome system illustrates that selectivity, not thermodynamics, is the regulated variable in intracellular proteolysis. The combinatorial expansion from ~2 E1s to ~40 E2s to >600 E3s places substrate discrimination in the E3 layer, and the polyubiquitin linkage topology (K48, K63, K11, K6, branched and mixed chains) constitutes a true post-translational code decoded by linkage-specific ubiquitin-binding domains and DUBs. Conditional stability is exploited in signaling: IkB degradation as the activating step for NF-kB, and the PHD/VHL axis as an O2-tuned degron in which prolyl hydroxylation generates the VHL recognition determinant (the 2019 Nobel paradigm), enabling graded HIF-1alpha stabilization. The 26S holoenzyme couples ATPase-driven unfolding and translocation (19S Rpt subunits) to processive threading into the 20S chamber, where proteolysis by N-terminal threonine nucleophiles is sequestered. Nitrogen catabolism is organized around glutamate as the funnel, with the hepatic urea cycle integrated thermodynamically and metabolically with the TCA cycle via the aspartate-argininosuccinate shunt (the Krebs bicycle): fumarate to malate to OAA regenerates aspartate and returns its carbon (recycling, not net gluconeogenic carbon), while CPS-I's NAG dependence provides feedforward sensing of nitrogen and acetyl-CoA load. Inborn errors map cleanly onto enzyme, accumulating metabolite, and compartment (orotic aciduria in OTC deficiency reflects carbamoyl phosphate overflow into cytosolic pyrimidine synthesis), and the glucogenic/ketogenic partition is dictated by the irreversibility of pyruvate dehydrogenase and the two-in, two-out carbon stoichiometry of the TCA cycle, which forbid net gluconeogenesis from acetyl-CoA-yielding skeletons (Leu, Lys)."

**Right now, inside you, proteins are being destroyed on purpose.** Your body turns over roughly 250 grams of protein a day, tearing down and rebuilding what looks like a stable adult. Rudolf Schoenheimer showed this in the 1930s: rats fed nitrogen-labeled amino acids built the heavy tag into proteins throughout the body. He called it "the dynamic state of body constituents."

The clocks differ wildly. Ornithine decarboxylase can be gone in about 10 minutes. Hemoglobin lasts as long as its red blood cell, about 120 days. The crystallins at the core of your eye's lens are as old as you are, never replaced. Cells set many of these clocks deliberately.

Here is the puzzle Irwin Rose could not let go of. Breaking a peptide bond is downhill. It is slow without an enzyme, but a protease removes the slowness without ATP: trypsin in your gut spends none. Yet the cell spends ATP to destroy its own proteins. What is the ATP buying?

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson: choose, then split by atom.** In the ubiquitin-proteasome system, the cell spends ATP to *mark* and *unfold* the proteins it destroys, never to cut their bonds, and the marking step (the E3 ligase) is where the choice is made. When an amino acid freed by that destruction is burned instead of reused, its atoms take two roads. **Nitrogen is a liability:** it is passed from carrier to carrier (glutamate, then alanine or glutamine in the blood) and is caged in the liver as urea, which takes one nitrogen from free ammonium and one from aspartate. **Carbon is an asset:** the skeleton enters central metabolism at one of seven doors, and only the part that enters at a door other than acetyl-CoA or acetoacetyl-CoA can become net glucose.

This is the adult human picture, liver and muscle, fed or fasting. The split applies only to amino acids that are burned. Urea carries most waste nitrogen, not all: the kidney excretes some as ammonium, and some leaves as creatinine and uric acid. Lysine and threonine bypass the usual first step of nitrogen removal, and birds and many reptiles excrete uric acid, not urea.

## Choosing: the ubiquitin tag

Rose, Avram Hershko and Aaron Ciechanover found the answer in reticulocyte extracts, and it earned the 2004 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The tag is **ubiquitin**, a 76-amino-acid protein about 96% identical from yeast to humans. Its C-terminal Gly-Gly forms an isopeptide bond (an amide bond to a side-chain amine, not to the backbone) with a lysine on the doomed protein.

Three enzymes write the tag. **E1** activates ubiquitin, spending ATP (to AMP plus pyrophosphate) to form a thioester to its own cysteine. **E2**, the conjugating enzyme, carries the activated ubiquitin. **E3**, the ligase, picks the address: it chooses the target and gets the ubiquitin onto it. Count them: roughly 2 E1 enzymes, about 40 E2s and more than 600 E3s. Specificity lives in the E3s. PROTAC drugs exploit this: they recruit an E3 ligase to a protein it would normally ignore, condemning that protein to the proteasome.

**What does an E3 read?** A degron, a feature that marks the target for destruction. The cleanest example is the N-end rule: half-life set by the first residue. A yeast test protein starting with methionine, glycine or alanine lasted more than 20 hours; the same protein starting with arginine or phenylalanine was gone in about two or three minutes. One residue, hundreds of fold.

**The chain is a code.** Ubiquitin has lysines of its own, so ubiquitins can be chained; K48 means a link through ubiquitin's lysine 48. A chain of four or more K48-linked ubiquitins is the classic destruction signal. A K63 chain usually means "signal, do not destroy": DNA repair, trafficking, immune signaling. Other linkages and single ubiquitins carry other messages.

## Destroying: the proteasome

Tagged proteins go to the **26S proteasome**, one or two 19S caps on a 20S core. The caps recognize the K48 chain, unfold the protein and thread it in, using ATP. The core's active sites face strictly inward, so bystanders are rarely chewed up. Short peptides come out, and deubiquitinases (DUBs) recycle the ubiquitin.

### Destruction as a switch

NF-κB sits trapped by its inhibitor IκB. When a signal arrives, IκB is phosphorylated, tagged with a K48 chain and destroyed, which frees NF-κB to enter the nucleus and switch on inflammatory and immune genes. Destroying the inhibitor is the activation.

Oxygen sensing, the subject of the 2019 Nobel Prize in Medicine, also runs on regulated destruction. In normal oxygen, a prolyl hydroxylase uses O₂ and α-ketoglutarate to hydroxylate HIF-1α, and the VHL E3 ligase recognizes the hydroxyproline and sends HIF-1α to the proteasome.

Multiple myeloma plasma cells churn out so much antibody that they are addicted to clearing misfolded protein. Block the proteasome with bortezomib (a reversible inhibitor) and misfolded protein piles up, killing these cells first.

## From destruction to fuel: most amino acids are reused

Peptidases cut the proteasome's peptides into free amino acids, which join those from food in one pool. The roughly 250 g of body protein broken down each day is rebuilt, and an adult in nitrogen balance burns about as much as they eat, typically around 80 g, so about three-quarters of the amino acids leaving the pool go back into protein (250 of about 330 g). The surplus is burned after a protein-rich meal or during a fast. Turnover by itself is nearly a closed loop; urea comes mainly from burning the surplus (plus a small obligatory loss), and that is where nitrogen and carbon part ways.

## The nitrogen road

Free ammonia is neurotoxic. At body pH nearly all of it is ammonium ion (NH₄⁺); only about 1 to 3% is NH₃, and here and in the film "ammonia" means both. So the cell does not let the amino group fall off; it hands it from carrier to carrier first.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

None. The enzyme only trades:

> alanine + α-ketoglutarate ⇌ pyruvate + glutamate

The ¹⁵N is now on glutamate, still an amino acid. **Transamination** moves nitrogen; it removes none. It is reversible, so the same enzymes also help build several non-essential amino acids, such as alanine and aspartate.

</details>

For most amino acids, aminotransferases collect the amino group onto α-ketoglutarate, so **glutamate** is the collection point. They all use pyridoxal phosphate (PLP, vitamin B6). The main way to release the nitrogen is a second move, oxidative deamination by **glutamate dehydrogenase** in liver mitochondria (glutaminase and a few other enzymes free some ammonium directly):

> glutamate + NAD⁺ (or NADP⁺) + H₂O ⇌ α-ketoglutarate + NH₄⁺ + NAD(P)H + H⁺

The reaction can run both ways; in liver the urea cycle drains the ammonium, so it runs toward release.

Muscle and other tissues ship nitrogen to the liver in two nontoxic carriers. In the **glucose-alanine cycle**, muscle puts nitrogen onto pyruvate from glycolysis, making alanine; the liver takes it off and turns the pyruvate into glucose for muscle and brain. In a fast this route carries the nitrogen of muscle protein breakdown, and some of the liver's new glucose carbon comes from muscle amino acids. **Glutamine**, the most abundant amino acid in blood, carries two nitrogens; glutaminase in liver and kidney releases one as ammonium.

### Caging the nitrogen: the urea cycle

The liver now holds waste nitrogen in two forms: free ammonium, and the amino group of aspartate, made when glutamate hands its nitrogen to oxaloacetate. Urea, H₂N–CO–NH₂, has two nitrogens, and the cycle Hans Krebs and Kurt Henseleit found in 1932 takes **one from each source**. It starts in the mitochondrion, where much of the liver's ammonium is made and where the rest arrives, so free ammonium meets CPS-I without leaving the compartment.

**Step 1, the first nitrogen (mitochondrion).** Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS-I) joins ammonium to bicarbonate, spending two ATP, to make carbamoyl phosphate: the committed, rate-limiting step. CPS-I works only with N-acetylglutamate bound, which the liver makes from acetyl-CoA and glutamate faster when amino acids are plentiful. So the cycle speeds up when nitrogen arrives.

**Step 2, onto the carrier (mitochondrion).** Ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) hands the carbamoyl group to ornithine, making citrulline, which leaves for the cytosol.

**Predict.** Ornithine has just taken on the carbamoyl group. Does the liver need a fresh ornithine for every urea it makes?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Ornithine comes back out at step 5. Krebs and Henseleit spotted this in liver slices: a little ornithine supported far more urea than it could have made itself. Used at one step and returned at another, ornithine is a wheel, not a fuel. This was the first metabolic cycle ever described.

</details>

**Step 3, the second nitrogen (cytosol).** Argininosuccinate synthetase joins citrulline to aspartate, making argininosuccinate. Aspartate's amino group is urea's second nitrogen. This step spends one ATP but cuts it to AMP and pyrophosphate, and the pyrophosphate is then split, so it costs two high-energy bonds.

**Step 4, the carbon leaves (cytosol).** Argininosuccinate lyase cuts argininosuccinate into arginine and fumarate. Aspartate's nitrogen stays on arginine; its four carbons leave as fumarate.

**Step 5, urea out, ornithine back (cytosol).** Arginase adds water to arginine, releasing urea and regenerating ornithine, which returns to the mitochondrion.

**Where the fumarate goes: the Krebs bicycle.** Fumarate becomes malate, then oxaloacetate, which takes an amino group from glutamate and becomes aspartate again for step 3. So the urea cycle and the citric acid cycle are two wheels on one chain. The film says glucose-building carbon comes out of the second wheel, and that needs correcting. Oxaloacetate can become glucose, but the loop only returns the carbon aspartate brought in. A fasting liver's net new glucose carbon comes from elsewhere: the skeletons of glucogenic amino acids from broken-down protein, and glycerol from fat.

### When the cycle breaks

When the cycle fails, ammonia rises and astrocytes swell as they trap it in glutamine, causing brain edema. OTC deficiency, the most common urea cycle disorder, shows a triad: high ammonia, low citrulline (OTC makes it) and high urinary orotic acid. Why orotic acid? The cytosol builds the ring of the pyrimidine bases C, T and U from carbamoyl phosphate and aspartate. Carbamoyl phosphate that OTC cannot use spills out of the mitochondrion into that pathway, and its intermediate, orotic acid, reaches the urine. Treatment: restrict protein, give scavengers (sodium benzoate, sodium phenylbutyrate) that escort nitrogen out by other routes, supply arginine (except in arginase deficiency), and dialyze in a crisis.

## The carbon road: seven doors

Stripped of nitrogen, the skeleton is carbon the cell will not waste. All 20 amino acids enter central metabolism at seven doors, some at more than one.

| Door | Net glucose? | Who enters here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pyruvate | Yes | Alanine, serine, glycine, cysteine; part of tryptophan |
| α-Ketoglutarate | Yes | Glutamate, glutamine, proline, arginine, histidine |
| Succinyl-CoA | Yes | Valine, isoleucine, methionine, threonine |
| Fumarate | Yes | Part of phenylalanine and tyrosine; aspartate, when it gives its nitrogen to urea |
| Oxaloacetate | Yes | Aspartate, asparagine |
| Acetyl-CoA | No | Leucine; part of isoleucine, threonine and tryptophan |
| Acetoacetyl-CoA | No | Leucine, lysine; part of phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan |

*Acetoacetyl-CoA here stands for acetoacetate and its activated form.*

**The rule:** only the first five doors give net glucose, because each can become oxaloacetate, the starting point for glucose. Acetyl-CoA cannot. Pyruvate dehydrogenase is irreversible, so it cannot go back to pyruvate, and each turn of the citric acid cycle takes in two carbons from acetyl-CoA and gives off two as CO₂, so it never makes extra oxaloacetate. Acetoacetyl-CoA is two acetyl units, so the same count applies.

Only two amino acids are purely ketogenic: **leucine** (acetyl-CoA and acetoacetyl-CoA) and **lysine** (acetoacetyl-CoA). Five are mixed: **isoleucine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and tyrosine**. The other 13 are purely glucogenic.

The succinyl-CoA door explains two vitamins. Its four amino acids are broken down to propionyl-CoA. A biotin enzyme carboxylates it to methylmalonyl-CoA, and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, a B12 enzyme, rearranges that into succinyl-CoA. Cofactors fingerprint the step: PLP for amino-group transfer, biotin for carboxylation, B12 for the rearrangement.

Break one of these enzymes and you get an inborn error of metabolism; each is rare, but as a group they are not. In 1902 Archibald Garrod argued that alkaptonuria, which turns urine black and stains cartilage, is inherited: the first recognized inborn error. PKU (defective phenylalanine hydroxylase) is caught by newborn screening and managed by a low-phenylalanine diet. In MSUD (defective branched-chain keto-acid dehydrogenase), branched-chain amino acids and their keto acids build up, the urine smells of maple syrup, and the excess leucine is neurotoxic.

## The whole lesson in one bird

The bar-tailed godwit flies 11,000 km nonstop in about eight days. It burns fat for energy and metabolic water, and it breaks down some of its own muscle protein, probably for glucose, for anaplerosis (refilling citric acid cycle intermediates) and for more water. The nitrogen leaves as uric acid, which costs almost no water. Choose, then split by atom: the E3s pick what dies, the nitrogen is caged, the carbon is kept.

## Going deeper

The main line above does not depend on these details.

### The rest of the ubiquitin code

Ubiquitin has seven internal lysines (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, K63). K11 chains, made by the cell-cycle ligase APC/C, also send proteins to the proteasome. Linear (M1) chains, built through ubiquitin's N-terminal methionine, help switch on NF-κB. A single ubiquitin often directs trafficking or changes a protein's partners, as on histones. K48 chains of four or more are the classic signal, not the only one: shorter or mixed chains, and several single ubiquitins, can also target a protein.

### More on the N-end rule

The rule was found in 1986 by Andreas Bachmair, Daniel Finley and Alexander Varshavsky. Later work added a layer: many of the "stable" first residues become degradation signals once they are N-terminally acetylated.

### More on the proteasome

The 20S core is a barrel of 28 subunits in four stacked rings, and its peptides are about 3 to 22 residues long. A second route, autophagy into lysosomes, handles much bulk turnover, whole organelles, and aggregates the proteasome cannot take. Besides bortezomib (reversible), myeloma is treated with carfilzomib (irreversible) and oral ixazomib.

### Transaminases on a liver panel

ALT and AST leak into the blood when liver cells are injured, which is why these transaminases sit on standard liver panels. AST also comes from muscle and heart.

### What urea really costs

Four high-energy bonds per urea is the textbook gross cost. The NADH made at malate dehydrogenase in the bicycle (and at glutamate dehydrogenase) offsets part of it, so the net cost is lower; textbooks differ on the exact figure.

### Threonine in humans

The textbook classes threonine as mixed, and this lesson and the sorter follow that. In adult humans the route that yields acetyl-CoA (threonine dehydrogenase) is minor, and most threonine goes to propionyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA.

### A label is not net glucose

The two CO₂ that leave in a turn of the citric acid cycle are not the same two carbons that entered. So an acetyl-CoA carbon can turn up in glucose as an isotope label, because over later turns it trades places with oxaloacetate carbons. What matters is the count: two carbons leave as CO₂ for every two that enter, so no net glucose is made.

### Treating PKU

Besides the low-phenylalanine diet, PKU can be managed with sapropterin, a synthetic form of the enzyme's cofactor, tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4), or with pegvaliase, an injected enzyme that breaks down phenylalanine.
