techniques:
  - name: "Time-shift assay"
    blurb: >-
      Partners sampled at different times are crossed: hosts from one time point are challenged
      with parasites from the past, the same time and the future. A steady trend across time
      points suggests an arms race; a peak or dip on contemporary partners with no trend suggests
      fluctuating selection.
  - name: "Resurrection ecology"
    blurb: >-
      Dormant eggs and spores are recovered from dated sediment layers and hatched or used to
      infect, so that past host and parasite populations can be tested against each other and
      against present ones.
  - name: "Clonal-diversity manipulation in chemostats"
    blurb: >-
      A prey population is started either as one clone, with no genetic variation for selection
      to act on, or as several clones. Comparing the population cycles of the two treatments
      tests whether prey evolution changes the dynamics.
  - name: "Within-strain service manipulation"
    blurb: >-
      The same partner strain is made to stop providing its service, for example by replacing
      nitrogen with argon around rhizobial nodules. Any host response can then be tied to the
      service rather than to other genetic differences between strains.

**A fungus that helps a plant grow in poor soil can slow the same plant down in rich soil.** So is the fungus a mutualist or a parasite? This lesson treats that as a question you answer with data: the sign of an interaction is the net effect on each partner's fitness, and it can change with conditions. It then asks a harder one: when two species interact for many generations, how can you tell whether each has evolved in response to the other? That reciprocal change, each species altering the selection on the other, is what **coevolution** means. The lesson covers how mutualisms stay honest, how hosts and parasites escalate or cycle, and how rapid evolution can feed back on population cycles.

## The idea: interaction signs are outcomes

Ecologists name interactions by the net effect on each partner's **fitness**, its survival and reproduction relative to the alternative. In a **mutualism** (+,+) both partners gain. In **commensalism** (+,0) one gains and the other shows no net change. In **parasitism** (+,-) the parasite gains and the host loses. Predation, covered in [predation](/lessons/predation/), shares the parasite's sign but usually kills the prey outright.

Treat each sign as the reading on a balance. A zero in commensalism is a net value: small costs and small benefits can cancel, and Mathis and Bronstein's 2020 review argued that commensalism can persist as a stable outcome rather than a waystation to something else.

Because the sign is a net, it can move. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, the most widespread kind, grow into plant roots, take carbon from the plant and supply soil nutrients, especially phosphorus. (Ectomycorrhizal fungi on many forest trees mainly supply nitrogen.) Johnson, Graham and Smith (1997) argued that when the plant's costs exceed its benefits, as can happen when nutrients are plentiful, the fungus might be considered parasitic from the plant's side. The same pair sits somewhere on a **mutualism-parasitism continuum**, and where depends on the soil.

Microbes show it too. Shapiro, Williams and Turner (2016) found that in early tests *Escherichia coli* infected with the filamentous phage M13 grew faster and reached higher density than uninfected cells, which they read as a facultative mutualism; after experimental evolution in treatments that allowed both vertical and horizontal spread, the phage no longer helped the ancestral strain, even though the infected bacteria evolving alongside it grew fastest. A virus is usually filed as a parasite; the measurement said otherwise there.

## Mutualisms and how hosts keep partners honest

A mutualism has a weak point. If a partner can take the reward and skip the service, that partner may leave more offspring than an honest one, and **cheaters** could spread. Rhizobia are bacteria that live in root nodules of legumes and fix atmospheric nitrogen for the plant in exchange for carbon. What stops non-fixing strains from taking over?

One answer is **host sanctions**: the host reduces the fitness of partners that do not deliver. Kiers, Rousseau, West and Denison (2003) tested this in soybean, *Glycine max*, with *Bradyrhizobium japonicum*. Comparing a fixing strain with a non-fixing one would be confounded, because the strains differ at many genes. So they kept the strain and removed the service, replacing the nitrogen in the air around roots or single nodules with argon. Rhizobial reproductive success fell by about 50% in nodules that could not fix. Reduced oxygen supply to those nodules was implicated as a possible mechanism; it was not shown to be the cause. The 50% figure belongs to soybean in that study, not to every legume.

A zoomed nodule below shows the same rod-shaped bacteria in both cases." caption="Kiers and colleagues kept the Bradyrhizobium strain the same and changed only the service, replacing the nitrogen in the air around roots or single nodules with argon so those nodules could not fix." />

"Sanction" names a measured effect, not a decision. The plant does not judge. Plants that cut the reproduction of rhizobia in non-fixing nodules, by whatever mechanism, leave fewer cheaters in the soil, and that effect is what selection can maintain.

Sanctions also depend on context. Fronk and colleagues (2025) grew *Lotus japonicus* with fixing and non-fixing rhizobia. Under nitrogen fertilization the plants no longer discriminated between them, and they reduced their overall investment, making small nodules with fewer rhizobia. When the plant gets nitrogen from the soil, the rhizobium's service is worth less, and the line between partner and freeloader blurs.

## Parasites, hosts and two kinds of coevolution

Matching traits are tempting evidence. A long-tongued moth and a deep flower look like a matched pair. Daniel Janzen's 1980 note "When is it coevolution?" set a stricter standard: **coevolution** is evolutionary change in one population in response to another, followed by an evolutionary response in the second. A good fit can arise when only one partner evolved, so the fit alone is not the evidence.

Hosts and parasites with short generations, such as bacteria and phages or water fleas and their microparasites, give some of the clearest tests, because they evolve quickly and have strong effects on each other. Two kinds of dynamics are distinguished:

- An **arms race** is escalation: host resistance and parasite infectivity both rise through time, in repeated rounds, though escalation can slow or stop.
- **Fluctuating selection** is cycling: whichever host genotype is common is attacked most, so rare genotypes gain, and the advantage keeps shifting. This is negative frequency-dependent selection, whose arithmetic is in [population genetics](/lessons/population-genetics/).

The analogy is two chess players. In an arms race both keep getting stronger, so a player from today would beat either player from last year. In fluctuating selection each player has learned only the other's current habits, so whichever player is ahead does best against today's version of the other, and there is no long-run gain.

A **time-shift assay** puts that analogy to work. You freeze samples through time and cross hosts from one time with parasites from the past, the present and the future. In one time-shift study of a bacterium and its lytic phages, most phages gave patterns consistent with frequency-dependent selection, and some also showed directional selection (Betts and colleagues, 2014). In a sequencing study, a phage that coevolved with its host evolved faster at the molecular level and diverged more among replicate populations than one evolving on a host held at a constant genotype (Paterson and colleagues, 2010). Phage biology itself is in [bacterial and phage genetics](/lessons/bacterial-and-phage-genetics/).

Natural systems can be tested across time when dormant stages are stored. Decaestecker and colleagues (2007) used dormant *Daphnia* eggs and parasite spores from dated layers of pond sediment, a method called **resurrection ecology**. Parasites had adapted to their hosts within a few years, and **virulence**, the harm done to an infected host, increased through the record. In that Daphnia system the result ran against the common claim that parasites evolve toward harmlessness, though it comes from one host and one parasite. Myxoma virus in Australian rabbits is the classic field case: after its release around 1950 its virulence fell to intermediate levels, not to zero.

## Ecology and evolution on one clock: rotifers and algae

In classical predator-prey models, the predator peak lags the prey peak by about a quarter of a cycle: prey rise, predators feed and multiply, prey crash, predators starve. The **phase lag** is the delay between peaks as a fraction of the cycle's period. Yoshida, Jones, Ellner, Fussmann and Hairston (2003) grew the rotifer *Brachionus calyciflorus* on the alga *Chlorella vulgaris* in nitrogen-limited chemostats, with the algae as either one clone or several. One clone cannot evolve by selection among clones; several can. They reported that single-clone prey gave short cycles with roughly the classical quarter-cycle lag, while multiclonal prey gave long cycles with predator and prey peaks largely out of step. The worked calculation of a phase lag from peak times is in Going deeper.

The lag alone does not prove that algae evolved. A long lag could have other causes. The causal case came from manipulating clonal diversity, and from a measured trade-off: algal clones that resisted grazing were poorer competitors for nutrients.

The feedback runs like this. Grazing favours defended clones; as they spread, food for rotifers falls; rotifers decline; with grazing relaxed, faster-growing undefended clones regain ground, and the cycle repeats. That trade-off is the same kind you met in [competition](/lessons/competition/), and the general case is in [selection in an ecological context](/lessons/selection-in-ecological-context/). One point needs care: the 2003 paper showed prey evolution and had not investigated predator evolution. This is eco-evolutionary feedback, not demonstrated coevolution.

### Design problem

You have frozen samples of a bacterium and its phage taken every two weeks for 20 weeks. Design a test that separates an arms race from fluctuating selection.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Revive hosts and phage from all 11 time points (weeks 0, 2, 4 up to 20). For each phage time point, challenge hosts from the past, the same time and the future, for example phage from week 10 against hosts from weeks 4, 10 and 16. Measure infection as the proportion of host colonies a phage lyses, with several colonies per host-phage pairing.

Predictions. Under an arms race, phage infect past hosts best and future hosts worst, and hosts resist past phage best; the effect grows steadily with the time gap. Under fluctuating selection, infection shows a peak or a dip on contemporary hosts, depending on which partner is ahead, with no long-run trend.

Controls. Run several replicate lines, since one line could follow either path by chance. Add Paterson's constant-host treatment, phage evolving on a host held at one genotype, so you can tell reciprocal change from phage-only adaptation. Betts and colleagues (2014), using a time-shift design with *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* PAO1, found patterns consistent with frequency-dependent selection for five of six phages. The result applies to those laboratory populations and says nothing about phage therapy.

## Going deeper

### Worked problem: reading a phase lag from peak times

**Worked problem.** Here is an invented data set. Assume the cycles are regular, the period does not change during the run, and each predator peak is matched with the prey peak just before it. (In the real experiment the treatments did not share a period; here they do, to keep the arithmetic clean.)

- Run A: prey peak on days 10, 40 and 70; rotifers peak on days 17.5, 47.5 and 77.5.
- Run B: prey peak on the same days; rotifers peak on days 25, 55 and 85.

1. *Period.* 40 - 10 = 30 days, and 70 - 40 = 30 days. The period is 30 days.
2. *Lag in run A.* 17.5 - 10 = 7.5 days, so 7.5 / 30 = 0.25 cycle. That is the classical quarter lag.
3. *Lag in run B.* 25 - 10 = 15 days, so 15 / 30 = 0.50 cycle. Predator peaks sit at prey troughs: **antiphase**.

> **phase lag (cycles) = delay between peaks (days) / period (days)**

4. *Compare.* Run A's 0.25 is the classical quarter lag. The real single-clone runs came close to it, with a mean lag of 0.32 cycle (range 0.26 to 0.36). Run B's 0.50 is antiphase, near the long multiclonal cycles, whose mean lag was 0.56 cycle (range 0.41 to 0.68). Run B resembles the evolving-prey runs.

### Time-shift and sequencing details

The time-shift study of Betts, Kaltz and Hochberg (2014) used *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* PAO1 and six lytic phages, and five of the six gave patterns consistent with frequency-dependent selection, while two of the six also showed directional selection. Paterson and colleagues (2010) let the phage phi2 evolve with *Pseudomonas fluorescens* SBW25 or with a host held at a constant genotype. Sequencing showed faster phage molecular evolution, and more divergence between replicate populations, when the host coevolved.

### The worm, probiotic and phage-therapy bridge

For human health, these laboratory and field systems show mechanisms, not treatments. Pathogen variants that escape host defences, such as those in [Toll-like receptor signalling](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb/) and [antigen receptors](/lessons/antigen-receptors-and-checkpoints/), can leave more descendants, which puts those defences under reciprocal selection. Experiments in the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans* (Rafaluk-Mohr and colleagues, 2018) found that a mildly parasitic gut bacterium, newly paired with the worm, coevolved with its host toward a protective, mutually beneficial partnership when a pathogen was present. That is a worm result, not evidence for any human probiotic or phage therapy.
