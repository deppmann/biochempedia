summary: "The nervous system is built of separate cells, neurons, that pass signals to one another across specialized contacts, and that is why development has to build connections between cells. techniques:
  - name: "Golgi impregnation (the black reaction)"
    blurb: >-
      Tissue is hardened in potassium dichromate and then placed in silver nitrate, and silver
      chromate crystals fill a small, unpredictable fraction of cells. Because so few cells are
      marked, the cell body, dendrites and axon of each one can be followed through a thick
      section.
  - name: "Nissl staining"
    blurb: >-
      A basic dye such as cresyl violet binds the RNA-rich material of the cell body and the DNA of
      the nucleus. It marks every neuron's cell body, so it is good for counting cells and seeing
      layers, but it leaves axons and dendrites almost unstained.
  - name: "Serial sectioning and reconstruction"
    blurb: >-
      A fixed embryo is cut into a complete, ordered series of sections, and the shapes are rebuilt
      in drawings or models. It shows where cells lie and how tissues are arranged at each stage,
      but not how any one cell moved or changed.
  - name: "Transmission electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Electrons pass through an ultrathin section of tissue stained with heavy metals, resolving
      structures far smaller than light can. It showed two separate membranes and a narrow cleft at
      chemical synapses.

**Stain every cell in a slice of brain and you cannot follow any one of them: a crowd of cell bodies, and between them a tangle of threads that no eye can follow. Stain only a few cells, but completely, and each one stands out with every branch.**

That second kind of stain arrived in 1873, and it turned an old question into one that could be argued with evidence. Is the nervous system built of separate cells, like liver or skin, or is it one continuous network of fused fibres? It took about eighty years, a stain nobody fully understands, a lot of embryos and finally the electron microscope to settle it. This lesson follows the evidence in the order it came, and asks one thing of each piece: could it reach the place where the question is decided?

## Touch or fusion: a question about 20 nanometres

There were two answers on offer. The **neuron theory** said each nerve cell is a separate cell, bounded by its own membrane, which extended the cell theory (that tissues are built of cells) to the nervous system; signals pass between cells at points of contact. The **reticular theory** said the fine branches fuse into one continuous net.

Under the neuron theory, the place where one neuron acts on another is a specialized contact. It came to be called a **synapse**, a word Charles Sherrington introduced in 1897, decades before anyone had seen the gap. At a **chemical synapse**, one cell releases a chemical messenger that acts on the next; at an **electrical synapse**, current passes straight through channels joining the two cells.

So the whole argument comes down to one spot, a point where two fine branches meet: one fused cytoplasm, or two membranes facing each other across a gap? How big is that spot, compared with what could be seen? Two numbers matter most: the 200-nanometre blur and the 20-nanometre gap.

| What | Size | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Smallest separation the best light microscope can show, side to side | about 200 nanometres (0.2 micrometres) | two points closer than this blur into one |
| The same limit in depth, focusing up and down | roughly 0.5 to 1 micrometre, depending on the optics | several times worse than side to side |
| A stained section, examined by refocusing | 100 micrometres or more thick | many branches lie above and below one another |
| The gap between two cells at a chemical synapse, as measured later | about 20 nanometres | about ten times below the limit |
| The gap between membranes at an electrical synapse | a few nanometres | further below the limit still |

Golgi and Cajal did not know how narrow the gap was, only that where branches met they could not see one.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** A touch is not a fusion. Neurons are separate cells, each bounded by its own membrane, and they pass signals across specialized contacts. The difference between touch and fusion lies at the membrane where two branches meet: either an open, unrestricted cytoplasmic bridge runs through the junction, or two membranes face each other across a gap (about 20 nanometres at a chemical synapse; a few nanometres at an electrical synapse, crossed only by size-limited channels). Evidence that bears directly on the membrane at a contact, by imaging it or by testing what crosses it, can settle the question. Evidence about whole cells, embryos or signal direction can make one answer more likely, but cannot decide it.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which nervous systems.** Vertebrates and many other animals, but not all. A few animals really do have fused neurons; see [Going deeper](#when-neurons-do-fuse).
- **What it does not say.** It does not say that neurons act alone. Coupled cells exchange ions and small molecules through channels. They do not form one unrestricted cytoplasm: each keeps its own membrane (see [A pore is not a fusion](#a-pore-is-not-a-fusion)).

That is why this lesson sets up the rest of the course. In vertebrates, development has to wire separate cells together, each growing its branches toward the right partners. Where neurons depend on a target's limited supply of a factor, each separate cell can win or lose its share, the subject of [the lesson on the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/). And separate, polarized cells joined at specialized contacts are why the direction of signal flow can be traced from cell to cell.

## To see one cell, stain almost none

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Before anyone could argue about contacts, they needed to see whole cells. The stains of the nineteenth century coloured what they reached. A Nissl stain, developed by the German psychiatrist and neuropathologist Franz Nissl, marks every cell body and nucleus in the section. It is excellent for counting cells and seeing layers, but axons and dendrites, the long thin processes where the question of fusion lives, stay almost invisible.

Camillo Golgi's black reaction worked the other way. He hardened small blocks of nervous tissue in potassium dichromate, then moved them into silver nitrate. Most cells stay pale. A few fill with a black deposit from cell body to the finest twig, so in a thick section one neuron and many of its branches can be traced against a yellowish background, although branches that leave the section are cut off.

This is **sparse labelling**: a few cells, filled completely, apparently at random. Golgi could not choose which cells were marked, and the fraction varies from piece to piece of tissue. Think of a forest at night with lights strung on a handful of trees. If every tree were lit you would see a glow. With only a few lit, you can see each lit tree's shape.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/neuron-doctrine/nissl-vs-golgi-stain.webp" width={1200} height={780} alt="AI-generated illustration: two panels of the same kind of tissue patch. A Nissl stain marks every cell body but leaves branches almost invisible; a Golgi stain fills a few separate neurons, showing cell body and many branches in black against a pale yellowish background. The Golgi cells are drawn as tidy examples; real processes are often cut off at the section edge." caption="Nissl stain marks every cell body and nucleus but leaves axons and dendrites almost invisible. Why one neuron takes the stain and its neighbour does not is still unknown." />

**Predict.** If sparse staining lets you see one cell whole, could you stain even fewer cells and so see whether two touching branches are fused?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Sparse labelling answers one question well: whose branch is this? Near the cell body, each black branch can be traced back to its own cell. It cannot answer the other question, touch or fusion, for two reasons.

1. **Both partners are rarely stained.** A contact has two sides. As an illustration, not a measured rate, suppose cells take the stain independently and 1 cell in 20 is stained. Then both sides of a given contact are stained only 1 time in 400, and even starting from a stained cell, its partner is stained only 1 time in 20. Staining fewer cells makes this worse, not better. Where only one side is stained, the pale partner could be a separate cell the stain missed or a fused part the stain did not reach. That single cells fill without their neighbours filling is itself a hint of separateness, but while no one knows why the stain takes, it cannot decide the matter.
2. **Resolution does not depend on how many cells are stained.** Even where two black branches do meet, the gap between them is still far below what light can show.

In the forest at night, a few lit trees show you each tree's shape. Where two lit branches cross in the dark, though, you still cannot tell whether they are grafted together.

</details>

## One slide, two readings

Santiago Ramón y Cajal came to the stain in 1887, when the Madrid histologist Luis Simarro showed him Golgi preparations. From 1888, working first on the cerebellum of birds, Cajal described axon branches that end close to other cells without joining them.

How could two careful observers look at similar preparations and disagree? Go back to the scale table: where two fine branches touch, or merely pass close at different depths in a thick section, the image cannot tell contact from fusion. Each camp filled that blind spot with what its theory predicted. Golgi read the tangle of fine axon branches as a continuous net. For Cajal, the free endings he found over and over were the evidence, and an unseen connection was no evidence at all.

Neither could win at the contact itself, so Cajal changed where he looked: to embryos.

## Evidence from development: fibres grow out of single cells

An adult brain is a thicket. An embryo is a young wood: fewer cells, shorter branches, and little myelin, the fatty sheath that wraps many mature axons and can block the stain. There a whole cell fits in a section and its branches can be counted.

Cajal was not the first to argue from embryos. Wilhelm His cut embryos into complete, numbered series of sections and rebuilt their shapes in drawings and models. The **neural tube**, the hollow tube of tissue that becomes the brain and spinal cord, showed dividing cells next to its central cavity and young nerve cells farther out. In the late 1880s His argued that each nerve fibre grows out from a single cell, which he called a **neuroblast**. If a fibre is the outgrowth of one cell, it began as part of that cell and not as a strand of a net.

**Predict.** Suppose every nerve fibre does grow out of one cell. Does that rule out Golgi's net?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Outgrowth shows where a fibre begins, not how it ends. A fibre could grow out of one cell and then fuse with another at its far end. Rival developmental views also held that nerve fibres form from chains of cells, or from bridges already present between cells. So the embryo evidence made separate cells more likely without settling the question.

Where fibres come from was largely settled by watching. In 1907 Ross Harrison kept pieces of frog embryo neural tube alive in a drop of lymph and watched living nerve fibres grow out of them, the subject of [the lesson on the growth cone](/lessons/growth-cone/). How a fibre ends still had to wait for an instrument that could see the membrane.

</details>

In 1890, in the spinal cord of chick embryos, Cajal described swellings at the tips of young axons, some cone-shaped, some fringed with spikes. He named the structure the **growth cone** and read it as the advancing end of a fibre that lengthens behind it. Every one of his specimens was fixed and dead, so the motion was an inference from a series of still shapes.

Cajal then added a rule about direction, which Arthur van Gehuchten in Belgium proposed independently. By the **law of dynamic polarization**, a signal enters a neuron through its dendrites and cell body and leaves through its axon. Cajal pointed to the retina and the olfactory bulb, where it is obvious which end receives the outside world and most cells line up in that order. The law is a useful generalization, not a rule without exceptions (see [Going deeper](#exceptions-to-one-way-flow)). Cajal argued that a chain of separate, polarized cells explains one-way signalling better than a diffuse net. In a chain, each cell has a receiving end and a sending end, so the order of the cells sets a direction; continuity alone sets none, though a net could still have polarized parts. This matches the point from the simple reflex circuit that the direction of information flow matters.

By 1906 most anatomists favoured separate cells, though not all. That year Golgi and Cajal shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their work on the structure of the nervous system. In his lecture Golgi still argued against the doctrine, and he could: no light microscope could show the gap. Separateness was the better explanation of the evidence, but nobody had yet seen it.

## The direct test: look at the membrane

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

None of that evidence reached the membrane at a point of contact. Settling the question took an instrument that could show it.

**Predict.** Suppose you could see a single membrane where an axon ending meets another cell. Sketch what you would see if Golgi were right, and what you would see if Cajal were right.

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If the branches are fused, the outer membrane runs on unbroken around both, no membrane crosses the junction, and the cytoplasm carries on through it. If the cells are separate, two membranes face each other across a gap, and each cell's cytoplasm stops at its own membrane.

</details>

About half a century after the Nobel Prize, the transmission electron microscope, applied to thin sections of nervous tissue, became that instrument. In 1955 Eduardo De Robertis and H. Stanley Bennett examined synapses in frog sympathetic ganglia and the earthworm nerve cord. In 1956 Sanford Palay examined the rat brain. At the chemical synapses they examined they saw the second sketch: the membranes of two cells stay separate across a cleft that those first reports measured at 10 to 20 nanometres (about 20 nanometres is the figure usually quoted now), with small vesicles crowded in the terminal (we now know they hold the chemical messenger).

At a contact between fine branches, Golgi and Cajal had seen the same blur, and each could claim it. The electron microscope replaced the blur with two membranes, and the question was settled.

## A pore is not a fusion

Some neurons are joined more closely than this. At an electrical synapse, the two membranes come within a few nanometres of each other, and channels called **gap junctions** cross both. Each cell supplies half of each channel, a ring of proteins called a hemichannel, and the two halves dock across the gap. In vertebrates the ring is six connexins; most invertebrates use innexins, and the innexin rings solved so far have eight. Ions and small molecules pass straight from one cell's cytoplasm into the next. Gap junctions join some neurons, especially in development.

**Predict.** Does this bring back Golgi's network? What would you inject into one cell to find out?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Each cell keeps its own membrane, and the channels can close. A vertebrate connexin pore is roughly 1.5 nanometres across at its narrowest in the structures solved, and passes ions and small molecules, generally up to about a kilodalton; what passes depends on the connexin and the molecule (a dalton is about the mass of one hydrogen atom). Inject two tracers. A small one known to pass the junction, such as Neurobiotin, reaches coupled neighbours; Lucifer yellow passes some junctions but poorly through others. A 10-kilodalton dextran, nearly 5 nanometres across, stays in the injected cell. In a **syncytium**, a single cell with many nuclei sharing one cytoplasm, the dextran would spread too, given time.

</details>

In vertebrates, Golgi's network, in the sense he meant it, does not exist; coupling through regulated pores does. A touch is not a fusion, and neither is a pore.

## Putting it together

Most of this evidence bore on the same question, at different distances from the membrane.

Read down the last column. Most of the earlier methods, by light microscope or by lesion, left the same thing open, what happens at the contact, until electron microscopy reached it, first at chemical synapses in the 1950s and at electrical ones in the 1960s, with dye fills showing what the pores pass. The rest of this course builds on that answer.

### Design problem

Suppose neurons were fused into a continuous network. What would you predict if you injected one neuron with a dye too large to pass through gap junctions, or if you cut one axon? Design one test that was available in 1900 and one available today, and say what each theory predicts.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**The 1900 test: cut a fibre and trace the damage.** Since Augustus Waller's work in 1850 it had been known that when a nerve fibre is cut, the part beyond the cut degenerates. In the 1870s and 1880s Bernhard von Gudden and his student, the Swiss psychiatrist Auguste Forel, made lesions in young animals and followed which cells wasted away afterwards. If neurons are separate units, the wasting should stay within the cells whose fibres were cut, each cell body and its fibres behaving as one unit. If the grey matter were one fused net, there would be no reason for damage to follow the outline of single cells. Forel found that the wasting stayed confined to particular groups of cells and their processes, and took this as evidence that each nerve cell and its fibres form a unit. The test was never decisive. No reticularist claimed that neighbouring fibres in a nerve were fused, so a single cut fibre could not tell the theories apart, and damage can sometimes cross from one neuron to the next even though they are separate.

**Today's test: fill one cell and look at its edge.** Use the two tracers from the gap-junction section. Under the neuron theory the 10-kilodalton dextran, too large for gap junction channels, fills the injected cell, every branch of it, and stops at its membrane; under the reticular theory it would flow on into the network. The small tracer is the control: it may reach a few coupled neighbours, and a connexin blocker should stop that. Then fix the tissue and examine the stained cell's contacts by electron microscopy, which shows two membranes separated by a narrow cleft at each chemical synapse. A large dye that stays in one cell is strong evidence of a separate compartment, and the micrographs confirm it.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Stain chemistry

A Nissl dye such as cresyl violet carries a positive charge, so it sticks to negatively charged RNA and DNA. Neurons pack their cell bodies with ribosome-rich membranes, which is why the dye concentrates there. The blocks stayed in the dichromate for days before the silver bath. Where the two meet inside a cell, an insoluble salt crystallizes:

> **2 Ag⁺ + CrO₄²⁻ → Ag₂CrO₄ (silver chromate, which looks black in the cell)**

### Gerlach, Golgi and the changing reticulum

The reticular theory was older than Golgi's stain. In the early 1870s the German anatomist Joseph von Gerlach, using other stains, proposed that the fine branches of nerve cells fuse into a continuous net. In Golgi's preparations dendrites seemed to end freely, and he thought they mainly served nutrition. The thin branches of axons, by contrast, seemed to merge into a diffuse network through the grey matter, and that network of axon branches was his 'reticulum'. His account changed over the years, but it would be wrong to say he thought every dendrite fused with the next.

### Separate cells and the cell theory

A continuous membrane boundary is what makes a cell a separate unit (see [the lesson on membrane structure](/lessons/membrane-structure-function/)), and separate compartments are what allow each one to be controlled on its own, the theme of [the introductory lesson on biochemistry and cell biology](/lessons/intro-biochemistry-cell-biology/).

### Not everyone was convinced

From the late 1890s the doctrine was contested by Apáthy, Bethe and Held, and Franz Nissl, whose stain opens this lesson, published a book-length attack on it in 1903.

### Exceptions to one-way flow

The olfactory bulb, one of Cajal's own examples of polarized cells, was later found to have synapses from one dendrite onto another. Cajal himself later revised the law, as "axipetal polarization", allowing the signal to bypass the cell body.

### Names, priority and the three signs

Wilhelm Waldeyer is usually credited with the word 'neuron', in 1891. The electron-microscope observations match the 'three signs of a synaptic specialization': small vesicles in the presynaptic terminal, a narrow cleft, and a thickened postsynaptic membrane. Palay and George Palade had already reported vesicles in mammalian synapses at a 1954 meeting, so priority is hard to rank.

### When neurons do fuse

The squid's giant axon is formed by the fusion of axons from many cells in the giant fibre lobe, so many cell bodies share one axonal cytoplasm (J. Z. Young, 1930s). In 2023 volume electron microscopy showed that neurons in the nerve net of a comb jelly, a marine animal from one of the earliest-branching animal lineages, share one continuous membrane and form a syncytium.
