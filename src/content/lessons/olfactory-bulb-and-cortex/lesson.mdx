**In the olfactory bulb, smell arrives as a map: receptor neurons of one type converge on a glomerulus, so each odour lights up a fixed pattern of glomeruli. Piriform cortex does not keep that map. An odour activates neurons scattered across the cortex, yet the scattered pattern still carries which odours are chemically alike, and connections inside the cortex are thought to let it gain learned meaning.** That transformation, from a map of receptor identity to a distributed population code shaped by experience, is the main subject of this lesson. Reviews of piriform cortex describe odour quality as coded sparsely and in a distributed way, and put learning and memory at the centre of how odours are perceived (Gottfried, 2010; Wilson and Sullivan, 2011).

[Smell and taste](/lessons/smell-and-taste/) ended with receptor neurons converging on glomeruli and the bulb projecting to piriform cortex. This lesson starts with the cortex's code and then goes back to the bulb circuits that shape what the cortex receives, nearly all of it in mice. The question at each stage is what the circuit keeps of the receptor map, what it adds, and what it gives up. How the receptor choice and the axon wiring arise is in [olfactory receptor choice](/lessons/olfactory-receptor-choice/), [partners, layers and self-avoidance](/lessons/partners-layers-self-avoidance/) and [topographic maps](/lessons/topographic-maps/).

## A cortex with no map, and what it still represents

Mitral and tufted axons reach piriform cortex without a thalamic relay. Stettler and Axel (2009) imaged odour responses in mouse piriform cortex in vivo and found that an odour activated a **dispersed** ensemble, neurons scattered across the area and not gathered in a patch, and that individual neurons had discontinuous receptive fields. Their result is not a statement that piriform carries no chemical information. It says that the arrangement is not a map of the kind seen in [topographic maps](/lessons/topographic-maps/).

Sosulski and colleagues (2011) asked whether the dispersal begins in the wiring. They labelled the output axons of a single glomerulus in adult mice with a tracer and imaged where they went. In piriform cortex the axons projected diffusely with no apparent spatial preference. In the cortical amygdala the same glomerulus gave spatially stereotyped patches. So dispersal belongs to one target, not to the whole bulb. Diffuse is also not the same as random: later projection mapping found spatial structure in how bulb neurons route their axons, though not a map of odour chemistry (see Going deeper).

If the anatomy is diffuse, is chemistry lost? Pashkovski and colleagues (2020) imaged piriform layer 2/3 neurons and bulb axon boutons, the terminals of mitral and tufted cells, in mice breathing at a controlled sniff rate and tested with fixed panels of odours. Both bulb inputs and piriform populations had correlated activity that tracked chemical relationships between odours. The piriform populations clustered related odours more strongly than the bulb inputs did.

This is a different kind of map. A spatial map says that similar odours are active in nearby places. A representational geometry says that similar odours evoke similar population patterns wherever the cells sit. Piriform cortex has the second kind without the first.

## What cortical connections add: recognition and learned meaning

Why scatter an odour across the cortex? Piriform pyramidal cells are linked to each other by association fibres, recurrent connections within the cortex, and reviews of the field treat those connections as a place where experience is stored. Strengthening the synapses among neurons that are active together is thought to bind a scattered ensemble into a familiar odour object. That would help an animal tell a familiar odour from similar ones, and let the whole ensemble respond even when part of the input is missing, a process called pattern completion (Wilson and Sullivan, 2011).

The cortex's own network does change the code. When Pashkovski and colleagues blocked synaptic output from piriform neurons with a genetically targeted silencing tool, which attenuates the cortex's associational network, the cortical relationships between odours became more like the bulb's. Piriform also sends feedback to the bulb and connects to other olfactory areas, so this shows that piriform network activity is needed for the change, not that local connections alone produce it. Passive odour experience could also reshape the cortical relationships.

If an odour is an ensemble, what happens if you create one without an odour? Choi and colleagues (2011) expressed channelrhodopsin in subsets of layer 2/3 piriform neurons in awake mice and activated them with light. Pairing the activation with a reward produced one learned behaviour, and pairing it with a shock produced the opposite. Small ensembles were enough to drive a learned response, though a weaker one than larger ensembles, though a weaker one than larger ensembles, and mice could learn to discriminate different activated subpopulations. (For optogenetic tools, see [hacking the cell](/lessons/hacking-the-cell/); for learned associations, [synaptic plasticity](/lessons/synaptic-plasticity/) and [memory consolidation](/lessons/memory-consolidation/).)

What this shows is that arbitrary piriform activity can acquire learned behavioural meaning, which is sufficiency for an association. It does not show that the mice smelled something, and the authors withheld the claim that their artificial network reproduced natural odour processing. The ensemble sizes tested are not the size of a natural odour representation; the numbers, and the light-pulse arithmetic, are in Going deeper.

### Design problem

Piriform responses to odours are scattered across the cortex. Design experiments that test whether the scattered activity still carries odour chemistry, and whether any extra structure comes from the bulb input or from the cortex's own network. Say what the results would not show.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

*Is there a spatial map?* Image piriform neurons during a fixed odour panel and ask whether neurons near each other share responses more often than distant ones. If they do not, there is no spatial map.

*Is there structure anyway?* Compare the population patterns for every pair of odours, and ask whether chemically related odours give more similar patterns than unrelated ones.

*Where does the structure come from?* Image bulb axon boutons in piriform cortex with the same panel and compare their similarity structure with the cortical one. If the cortex clusters related odours more strongly, the cortex has added something.

*Does the cortical network add it?* Block synaptic output from piriform neurons and ask whether the cortical similarity structure becomes more like the bulb's. Because piriform also feeds back to the bulb and reaches other olfactory areas, a change shows that piriform network activity is needed, not that local connections alone produce it.

*What stays unproven.* A fixed panel does not sample all odours, fluorescence is not a full record of spikes, and similar neural patterns do not show that the mouse perceives the odours as similar; that needs a behavioural test.

</details>

## How the bulb shapes what the cortex receives

Before the cortex can scatter and sharpen an odour, the bulb has already reshaped it. **Mitral and tufted cells** are the principal output neurons of the mouse main olfactory bulb. Each sends a primary dendrite into one glomerulus. Cells that send theirs into the same glomerulus are **sister cells**. Around and beneath them sit interneurons: **periglomerular cells**, GABAergic cells that work in the glomerular layer, and **granule cells**, which lack axons and contact the lateral dendrites of mitral and tufted cells.

The key contact is a **reciprocal dendrodendritic synapse**, one dendrite signalling to another and back. A mitral or tufted dendrite releases glutamate onto the interneuron, and the interneuron releases GABA onto the same dendrite. A reviewer of the rodent literature (Burton, 2017) summarized the consequences: the loop can inhibit the cell that excited it (recurrent inhibition), and because one interneuron touches many output cells, it can inhibit neighbours that share it (lateral inhibition). This resembles the lateral inhibition of [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/), built from dendrites, but the bulb is not a retina. Recordings in rats found that each mitral cell is influenced by a handful of spatially scattered glomeruli, not a centre-surround field of neighbours (Fantana and colleagues, 2008), and glomerular-layer circuits also spread inhibition across the bulb and set its gain (Banerjee and colleagues, 2015). The same review concluded that granule cells alone do not account for all odour-evoked inhibition of mitral and tufted cells, so do not treat granule cells as the whole story.

## Timing within the breath, and sister cells

Bulb output is a pattern of when cells fire as well as which cells fire. A review of bulb coding concluded that the relative timing of activity in the bulb conveys odour information, although whether and how downstream circuits use it has been debated (Uchida and colleagues, 2014). The two output types already differ in timing. In mice, tufted cells respond with shorter latency over a wider range of odour concentrations and project mainly to anterior olfactory cortex, while mitral cells project to all olfactory cortical areas (Igarashi and colleagues, 2012), and the two lock to opposite phases of the sniff cycle (Fukunaga and colleagues, 2012). They are parallel channels, not copies.

Sister cells make a sharp test of timing. Do cells that share one glomerulus send the same message? Dhawale and colleagues (2010) recorded in adult anaesthetized mice and used a light-based method to find which recorded mitral and tufted cells received input from one parent glomerulus, so sisters were defined by their input and not by how they responded to odours. Counting mean spikes, sister responses were correlated. Looking at spike timing relative to the respiration cycle, odours changed the timing of sisters differently. Rate made them look alike, and timing did not. A shared glomerulus is a shared address, not a shared message.

Read the result within its limits. The units were pooled mitral and tufted cells, which fire at different sniff phases, and the mice were anaesthetized, so some timing differences may reflect cell type or state. Timing differences also do not tell you which synapses produced them, so the experiment does not show that granule-cell inhibition did. In awake mice, sisters of one genetically identified glomerulus also gave diverse responses (Arneodo and colleagues, 2018), so sisters can differ in more than timing.

## Representations that drift

The distributed code is also not fixed. Schoonover and colleagues (2021) recorded single units in mouse piriform cortex over days to weeks, and odour-evoked responses drifted: a classifier trained on the first recording day approached chance performance by about a month. Daily exposure to an odour slowed the drift. This is an active question rather than a settled rule; a 2026 review reads drift on multiple timescales as a sign that piriform cortex may encode temporal context as well as odour identity (Friedrich and Zenke, 2026).

Drift does not mean that an animal's smell changed. A decoder is a tool the experimenter holds, and a downstream circuit may read a moving population consistently. The finding does change what a cortical ensemble can mean, because the fixed address of the textbook ensemble is not what these recordings show. How to tell a changing code from a changing recording is worked through in Going deeper.

## Smell loss in people, and a learned odour map

Two applications sit beside this circuit story and are brief here. Early in the COVID-19 pandemic, Brann and colleagues (2020) found the ACE2 entry gene in non-neuronal olfactory cells and localized ACE2 protein to sustentacular support cells in mouse dorsal epithelium, not in sensory neurons, which suggested non-neuronal mechanisms. Khan and colleagues (2021) examined adult postmortem tissue from people who had died with COVID-19 and found sustentacular cells to be the main olfactory mucosal target, with no evidence of infection of sensory neurons or the bulb parenchyma in their samples, though they allowed that mechanisms may differ between people. Do not teach infection of olfactory neurons and spread along their axons into the brain as the usual route; current evidence suggests neuronal infection is rare.

Poorer smell identification also predicted later disease in cohort studies: Parkinson disease in the Health ABC study (Chen and colleagues, 2017) and Alzheimer dementia in a North Manhattan cohort (Devanand and colleagues, 2015). These are associations, not diagnoses, and smell loss has other causes such as chronic rhinosinusitis.

A different route to odour structure skips the brain. Lee, Mayhew and colleagues (2023) trained a graph neural network on approximately 5,000 labelled molecules and represented every molecule as a point in 256 learned dimensions, a principal odor map. In a test on new molecules rated by a human panel, the frozen model's description profile was closer to the panel's average than the median panellist's was for just over half of the molecules. This is a computational embedding of structure and reported odour, not anatomy of the bulb or cortex.

## Going deeper

This section holds the particulars behind the main line: preparations, numbers and worked arithmetic.

### Preparations behind the main results

Sosulski and colleagues electroporated a dextran tracer into a single glomerulus of an adult mouse, waited five days, and imaged the labelled output axons in fixed, flattened cortex and in excised amygdala.

Pashkovski and colleagues imaged male mice 8 to 16 weeks old. The mice were semi-paralysed and ventilated, in a state the authors called wakefulness on EEG criteria, with an imposed 8 Hz sniff cycle, and cortical neurons were tested with three panels of 22 odours (the bulb-bouton comparison used one of them, called the tiled panel). The 2020 imaging did not freely sample odours, and fluorescence is not a full record of spikes.

Dhawale and colleagues recorded in mice 60 to 400 days old, anaesthetized with ketamine and xylazine.

### Diffuse, but not random

Diffuse axons leave open how individual synapses are chosen, because a tracer image is not a connectivity matrix. Later, barcoded sequencing of the projections of thousands of single bulb neurons (Chen and colleagues, 2022) found that the wiring is not random after all: bulb neurons aimed at one anterior-posterior position in piriform also project to matched targets outside it. That is spatial structure in routing, not a map of odour chemistry.

### How small an artificial ensemble can be

In Choi and colleagues' experiments, ensembles of as few as about 300 channelrhodopsin-expressing neurons could drive a learned response, weakly (flight in about 30 percent of aversive-task trials at about 300 cells and about 95 percent above 500). The figure of 300 counts cells that expressed channelrhodopsin, and only about 40 percent of such cells showed an activity marker after light, so the number of neurons actually driven is uncertain. It is the smallest effective ensemble tested here, with a weaker response than larger ensembles.

### Worked problem, part a: the light-pulse train

**Worked problem, part a: the stimulus.** The aversive-conditioning photostimulus used 20 Hz pulses of 25 ms each. Suppose, as an exercise, a train lasts 3 s with one pulse starting each period. The 3 s is our assumption.

*Step 1: period.* 1/20 s = 50 ms.
*Step 2: pulses.* 20 s⁻¹ x 3 s = 60 pulses.
*Step 3: off-time per period.* 50 ms - 25 ms = 25 ms.
*Step 4: duty cycle.* 25/50 = 0.50.
*Step 5: light-on time.* 60 x 25 ms = 1,500 ms = 1.5 s.
*Check:* 3 s x 0.50 = 1.5 s.

These are stimulus quantities. They do not say that neurons fired 60 spikes.

### Sisters in the awake bulb

In awake mice, sister cells of one genetically identified glomerulus, M72, gave temporally diverse excitatory and inhibitory responses to most of its ligands, and stereotyped early responses only to its strongest ligand (Arneodo and colleagues, 2018).

### Drift: the numbers, and a changing code or a changing recording

In Schoonover and colleagues' recordings, a classifier trained on the first recording day approached chance performance by 32 days, and fear conditioning did not stabilize the responses. The 32 days is the point where this classifier approached chance, not a time constant.

A classifier trained on day 1 piriform responses decodes odours well on day 1 and near chance a month later. Design the experiments that distinguish a changing neural code from a changing recording, and say what the result does not show.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

*Is the recording stable?* Check that the same units are tracked: compare waveform shape, isolation quality and baseline firing across days, and repeat with a subset that is clearly the same cells.

*Is the failure about the decoder?* Train a new decoder on each day and compare it with the frozen day-1 decoder. If daily decoders work and the frozen one fails, odour information is present every day while the mapping changed. If daily decoders also fail late, the odour information is lost or hidden, so check recording quality, odour delivery and the animal's state before reading anything into it.

*Does behaviour drift too?* Test the animal's discrimination of the same odours on the same days. If behaviour holds steady while the decoder fails, the behaviour tolerates the change, though this does not say which cells or readout keep it stable.

*What controls drift?* Compare animals with daily exposure, with fear conditioning and with neither, as the paper did, and ask which factor changes the rate.

*What stays unproven.* A failed decoder alone does not show a changed percept, and a change in code does not show which synapses changed.

### Worked problem, part b: the odour map's prospective test

**Worked problem, part b: the prospective test.** A human panel characterized 400 new odorants, and 323 were retained for the final evaluation with no retraining.

*Step 1: fraction retained.* 323/400 = 0.8075, or 80.75 percent.
*Step 2: what 53 percent counts.* For 53 percent of evaluated molecules, the model's profile was closer to the panel mean than the median panellist's was.
*Step 3: how many molecules.* 0.53 x 323 = about 171. It is not classification accuracy.

The map did not predict suprathreshold intensity as concentration varied, and the roughly 500,000-molecule picture plots predictions.

### Smell and later disease: the numbers

In the Health ABC study of 2,462 community-dwelling people aged 70 to 79 at enrolment, the lowest tertile of a smell identification test had a Parkinson disease hazard ratio of 4.8 against the highest tertile (95% CI 2.0 to 11.2, from only 42 incident cases; Chen and colleagues, 2017).
