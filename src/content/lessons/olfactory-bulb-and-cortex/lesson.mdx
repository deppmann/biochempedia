summary: >-
  In a simple model, each piriform neuron listens to several scattered glomeruli and fires when
  enough are active together, so odours that share more glomeruli share more neurons, wherever
  those neurons sit. That is why a scattered code still carries chemistry: the olfactory bulb hands
  piriform cortex an orderly map of receptor identity, and the cortex scatters it, so an odour
  activates neurons spread across the cortex. You follow one glomerulus into the cortex, work the overlap
  arithmetic, predict what imaging should find and see where the cortex's own network adds
  structure, then meet artificial ensembles that acquire meaning, a code that drifts, the bulb's
  inhibition and timing, and human smell loss. Afterwards you can tell a spatial map from a
  representational geometry, say what an artificial cortical ensemble does and does not show, and
  read sister-cell data.

hook: >-
  The bulb hands piriform cortex a tidy map of receptor identity, and the cortex scatters it. How
  can a scattered pattern still carry which odours are alike, and come to mean something?

scientists:
  - name: "Richard Axel"
    contribution: >-
      With Dan Stettler, Gloria Choi and Carl Schoonover, his laboratory imaged dispersed odour ensembles in mouse piriform cortex (2009), paired artificial ensembles with outcomes (2011) and recorded how piriform odour responses drift (2021).
    profileSourceAnchor: "Stettler & Axel, Neuron (2009); Choi et al., Cell (2011); Schoonover et al., Nature (2021)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      The receptor map in the bulb is orderly, so a reasonable guess was that the cortex would
      keep the order. Stettler and Axel looked at mouse piriform cortex with optical imaging in
      vivo and reported the opposite arrangement: an odour activated a spatially dispersed
      ensemble of neurons, and individual neurons had discontinuous receptive fields. If the
      cortex was not a map, what could it be? Choi and colleagues turned the question around in
      2011. They made channelrhodopsin-expressing ensembles in awake mice and paired their
      activation with a reward or a shock, and the mice acquired opposite learned behaviours. In
      2021 Schoonover, Ohashi, Axel and Fink recorded single piriform units across weeks and found
      that the responses to an odour did not stay put. Taken together the three papers added strong
      evidence that the piriform odour code is distributed, can be taught, and moves. They
      also left the open question of what a reader of such a code would have to do.
  - name: "Sandeep Robert Datta"
    contribution: >-
      With Dara Sosulski and colleagues, traced single-glomerulus output into piriform cortex and cortical amygdala (2011); with Stan Pashkovski and colleagues, showed structured chemical relationships in mouse piriform populations (2020).
    profileSourceAnchor: "Sosulski et al., Nature (2011); Pashkovski et al., Nature (2020)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      A wiring diagram can say whether a cortex is built to keep inputs apart. Sosulski, Bloom,
      Cutforth, Axel and Datta put a tracer into a single glomerulus of an adult mouse and, five
      days later, imaged where its output went in fixed tissue. In piriform cortex the labelled
      axons spread diffusely, with no apparent spatial preference. In the cortical amygdala the
      same glomerulus gave stereotyped patches. The difference showed that dispersal is a property
      of one target, not of the bulb. Nine years later Pashkovski and colleagues asked whether
      a dispersed projection still carries chemistry. They imaged piriform neurons and bulb
      axon terminals in mice, using three panels of 22 odours for the cortical neurons, and compared how
      similar the population responses to different odours were. Chemical relationships were
      present in both, and the cortex clustered related odours more strongly. The paper separated
      two ideas that are easy to merge: the absence of a spatial map and the absence of structure.
  - name: "Brian Lee and Emily Mayhew"
    contribution: >-
      Joint first authors of the 2023 Science paper that learned a 256-dimensional odour map from molecular structure and tested it on molecules rated by a human panel.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Lee, Mayhew et al., Science (2023), A principal odor map unifies diverse tasks in olfactory perception."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Can a molecule's structure predict what people say it smells like? Lee, Mayhew and colleagues trained a graph neural network, a
      model that reads a molecule as atoms joined by bonds, on approximately 5,000 molecules that
      had odour labels. Every molecule became a point in a space of 256 learned coordinates, which
      the authors called a principal odor map. Then they did the harder thing. They had a human
      panel rate 400 new molecules, kept 323 for the final test after exclusions, and compared the
      frozen model with the individual panellists. The map does not come from the nose or the
      brain. It is a computational description of structure and reported odour, and the paper is
      careful about what it did not predict, such as how intensity changes with concentration.
  - name: "David Brann"
    contribution: >-
      First author of the 2020 Science Advances analysis that located the genes for SARS-CoV-2 entry in non-neuronal cells of the olfactory epithelium rather than in olfactory neurons.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Brann et al., Science Advances (2020), Non-neuronal expression of SARS-CoV-2 entry genes in the olfactory system suggests mechanisms underlying COVID-19-associated anosmia."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Early in the COVID-19 pandemic, many patients lost their sense of smell, and a natural
      explanation was that the virus attacked olfactory neurons. Brann and colleagues tested a
      narrower question using uninfected tissue: which olfactory cells make the host proteins the
      virus uses to enter cells? They combined bulk sequencing of human and non-human primate olfactory mucosa with
      single-cell datasets from human and mouse, new mouse sequencing, and tissue staining.
      The entry gene ACE2 was found in non-neuronal cells, mainly support cells, and ACE2 protein was
      localized to sustentacular cells in the dorsal part of the mouse epithelium, rather than in
      olfactory sensory neurons. Expression is not infection, and the authors framed their result
      as suggesting mechanisms. It moved the working hypothesis from the neuron to its support
      cells, and later work on tissue from patients could test it directly.

techniques:
  - name: "In vivo recording with optical identification of input"
    blurb: >-
      Light is used to find which output neurons receive input from one glomerulus, and extracellular
      electrodes then record their spikes during odour presentation. The method defines sister
      cells by their input, independently of how they respond to odours.
  - name: "Optogenetic ensemble activation"
    blurb: >-
      A light-gated channel is expressed in a subset of neurons, and light then drives them whatever
      the nose is doing. It tests whether an activity pattern is sufficient to drive a learned
      behaviour. It does not show that the same neurons are needed for natural smelling.
  - name: "Longitudinal recording and cross-day decoding"
    blurb: >-
      The same preparation is recorded over days to weeks, and a classifier trained on one day is
      tested on later days. A fall in accuracy means the mapping from activity to odour changed, and
      it needs controls to separate a changing code from a changing recording.
  - name: "Single-cell RNA sequencing"
    blurb: >-
      Transcripts are counted in individual cells, which are then grouped into types by what they
      express. It shows which cells express a gene such as ACE2; it cannot by itself show infection.

practiceQuestions:
- stem: Single glomeruli of adult mice were labelled with a tracer and the output axons were imaged in fixed tissue.
    Which result did Sosulski and colleagues report?
  choices:
  - Stereotyped patches in both piriform cortex and cortical amygdala, following the bulb's order
  - Diffuse spread in piriform cortex without spatial preference, and stereotyped patches in cortical amygdala
  - Diffuse spread in both piriform cortex and cortical amygdala, with no patches anywhere
  - Stereotyped patches in piriform cortex, and diffuse spread in the cortical amygdala
  answer: 1
  rationale: The glomerular axons spread through piriform cortex with no apparent spatial preference but formed
    spatially stereotyped patches in the cortical amygdala, so the dispersal belongs to a target and not to the
    whole bulb. The reversed pattern swaps the two targets. The all-diffuse choice would make the cortical amygdala
    result disappear. The all-patchy choice would predict a patchy piriform map, which the 2009 imaging and 2011
    single-glomerulus tracing did not find.
- stem: Each toy piriform neuron samples two different glomeruli at random and fires only if both are active.
    Odour A activates 10 glomeruli. Odour B also activates 10, and 6 of them are shared with A. In a large population,
    what fraction of the neurons that respond to A would you expect also to respond to B?
  choices:
  - About 60 percent, the same as the fraction of shared glomeruli
  - About a third, because both of a neuron's inputs must fall among the shared glomeruli
  - None, because the responsive neurons are scattered across the cortex
  - All of them, because every A-responsive neuron has at least one input in common with B
  answer: 1
  rationale: A-responsive neurons are those with both inputs among A's 10 glomeruli, 45 pairs. Those that also
    respond to B have both inputs among the 6 shared glomeruli, 15 pairs, so 15/45 = 1/3. The 60 percent choice
    assumes cortical overlap copies glomerular overlap, but a neuron needing two coincident inputs roughly squares
    the shared fraction. The none choice treats scattered position as erasing similarity, yet position never enters
    the calculation. The all-of-them choice is wrong twice, since 6 of the 45 pairs lie wholly among A's 4 unshared
    glomeruli, and one shared input alone would not reach threshold anyway.
- stem: In awake mice, channelrhodopsin was expressed in a subset of layer 2/3 piriform neurons, and light activation
    of the ensemble was paired with a reward in one group and a shock in another. Which conclusion does this design
    support?
  choices:
  - Piriform activity is required for every odour-guided behaviour that mice can show
  - An arbitrary small ensemble can gain learned appetitive or aversive meaning through pairing
  - The ensemble is the natural, permanent cortical representation of one particular odour
  - The mice perceived the light as the smell of the odour that usually excites those neurons
  answer: 1
  rationale: Pairing arbitrary ensemble activation with outcomes produced opposing learned behaviours, which is
    evidence of sufficiency for a learned association. The subjective-smell choice claims a perceptual quality that
    the authors specifically withheld. The natural-and-permanent choice treats an imposed pattern as the natural
    code, and later recordings show that natural responses drift. The requirement choice is a necessity claim, but
    activation experiments cannot show necessity.
- stem: In the mouse olfactory bulb, a mitral cell dendrite releases glutamate onto a granule cell dendrite, and
    the granule cell releases GABA back onto the same dendrite. One granule cell contacts many mitral and tufted
    cells. Which consequence does this arrangement allow?
  choices:
  - The loop can inhibit the cell that excited it and also inhibit neighbouring output cells
  - Granule cells relay the mitral cell's signal to piriform cortex through their own axons
  - The loop excites neighbouring output cells, so an active glomerulus recruits its neighbours
  - Granule cells account for all odour-evoked inhibition of mitral and tufted cells
  answer: 0
  rationale: A reciprocal dendrodendritic synapse lets the interneuron inhibit the dendrite that excited it (recurrent
    inhibition), and because one granule cell touches many output cells it can inhibit neighbours that share it
    (lateral inhibition). The relay choice fails because granule cells lack axons. The excitation choice reverses
    the sign, since the interneuron releases GABA. The all-inhibition choice overstates the case, because a review
    of the rodent literature concluded that granule cells alone do not account for all odour-evoked inhibition.
- stem: In adult anaesthetized mice, mitral and tufted cells that share a parent glomerulus were recorded during
    odour presentation. Which summary matches what Dhawale and colleagues reported?
  choices:
  - Sister cells matched in both rate and timing, so they behave as copies of one input line
  - Sister cells differed in both rate and timing, so a shared glomerulus gives no shared sensory input
  - Sister cells had correlated mean-rate responses but differed in timing against respiration, so rate alone misses
    information
  - Sister cells differed in mean rate but matched in timing, so rate carries the glomerular identity
  answer: 2
  rationale: The reported pattern was correlated odour responses by mean spike rate together with odour-dependent
    differences in spike timing relative to respiration, which means a rate-only reading discards some of the output.
    The matched-in-both choice is the redundant-copies idea the data argue against. The choice with matched timing
    reverses the finding. The choice that denies shared input confuses the output code with the input, because sisters
    were defined by sharing a glomerulus.
- stem: Suppose, in made-up data, a classifier trained on day 1 of piriform recordings falls to chance by day 32,
    while a classifier retrained on each day separates the same odours well on that day. What is the best reading?
  choices:
  - Downstream circuits must have lost the ability to read the changing code
  - The odours became indistinguishable to the mouse itself by day 32
  - The electrodes moved, so the later recordings hold no odour information at all
  - Each day's code still separates odours, but the map from activity to odour changed
  answer: 3
  rationale: A daily-retrained decoder that works shows odour information is present each day, and a frozen decoder
    that fails shows the mapping between activity and odour changed. The indistinguishable choice jumps from a decoder
    to a percept, and behaviour would have to be tested. The moved-electrode choice contradicts the good same-day
    decoding, although unit tracking still needs checking. The downstream-failure choice asserts a change in a reader
    that the data do not measure.

mcatQuestions:
- stem: In a made-up imaging data set from mouse piriform cortex, neurons that respond to odour A are scattered
    across the imaged field, and two neurons 100 µm apart are no more likely to share a response than two neurons
    1 mm apart. The population patterns for two related esters correlate at r = 0.7, while the patterns for an ester
    and an amine correlate at r = 0.1. Which statement is justified?
  choices:
  - Piriform cortex carries no chemical information, because the responsive neurons are scattered
  - Piriform cortex has a spatial map of odours, because the related esters give correlated patterns
  - Piriform cortex lacks a spatial map of odours, yet its population patterns still carry chemical similarity
  - Each glomerulus sends patchy input to piriform cortex, which explains the correlated ester patterns
  answer: 2
  rationale: Distance does not predict shared responses, so there is no spatial map, but related odours give more
    similar population patterns, so the geometry carries chemistry, the pattern Pashkovski and colleagues reported
    for real piriform populations. The no-information choice confuses the absence of a spatial map with the absence
    of structure. The spatial-map choice confuses representational geometry with location, since correlated patterns
    can come from cells anywhere. The patchy-input choice contradicts single-glomerulus tracing, which found diffuse
    projections to piriform cortex and stereotyped patches only in the cortical amygdala.
- stem: In made-up data, bulb axon boutons and piriform neurons are imaged with the same odour panel. In both, related
    odours give more similar patterns than unrelated ones, but piriform regroups some odour pairs, so its similarity
    matrix matches the bouton matrix at only r = 0.5. After synaptic output from excitatory piriform neurons is
    blocked with a genetically targeted silencing tool, the match rises to r = 0.8, while the bouton matrix is
    unchanged. Which conclusion is best supported?
  choices:
  - Local recurrent connections within piriform cortex alone produce the cortex's regrouping of odours
  - Piriform network activity is needed for the cortex to reshape the odour relationships of its input
  - The bulb inputs carry no chemical relationships, so all of the structure is made in the cortex
  - Silencing made the mice unable to tell the related odours apart
  answer: 1
  rationale: With piriform output blocked, the cortical similarity matrix moves toward the bouton matrix, so the
    reshaping depends on piriform network activity, the direction Pashkovski and colleagues reported. The
    local-connections choice goes too far, because piriform output also reaches the bulb and other olfactory areas
    that connect back to piriform cortex, so this manipulation cannot isolate local connections. The no-chemistry
    choice ignores the bouton patterns, which already group related odours. The discrimination choice jumps from
    neural similarity to behaviour, which these data do not measure.
- stem: A hypothetical optogenetic experiment drives a piriform ensemble with 10 ms light pulses at 40 Hz for 2
    s, one pulse starting every period. These are stimulus quantities, not spike counts. What are the total light-on
    time and the duty cycle?
  choices:
  - 0.08 s of light in total, with a duty cycle of 0.04
  - 1.60 s of light in total, with a duty cycle of 0.80
  - 0.40 s of light in total, with a duty cycle of 0.20
  - 0.80 s of light in total, with a duty cycle of 0.40
  answer: 3
  rationale: The period is 1/40 s = 25 ms, the train has 40 per second x 2 s = 80 pulses, the light is on for 80
    x 10 ms = 800 ms = 0.80 s, and the duty cycle is 10/25 = 0.40, which also equals 0.80 s over 2 s. The 0.40 s
    choice counts only half the pulses, as if the train lasted 1 s. The 1.60 s choice doubles the on-time, which
    would need 20 ms pulses. The 0.08 s choice slips a factor of ten in the pulse width.

  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: >-
      In the mouse bulb, mitral and tufted cells that share a parent glomerulus are sister cells.
      They can have correlated mean odour responses and different spike timing relative to
      respiration. Reciprocal dendrodendritic synapses with granule cells give recurrent and lateral
      inhibition, and granule cells are not the only source of odour-evoked inhibition. Piriform
      cortex receives diffuse, apparently unpatterned projections from a single glomerulus, whereas
      the cortical amygdala receives stereotyped patches. Population analyses show that piriform
      activity still carries chemical relationships, stronger clustering than in bulb input, and
      that its geometry changes with experience. Arbitrary ensembles can acquire learned valence, and
      responses to an odour drift over weeks.
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      Stage by stage, the olfactory pathway trades a map for a distributed code. Bulb sister cells
      dissociate rate from respiration-relative timing in adult anaesthetized mice, so the glomerular
      identity label does not fix the output code. Single-glomerulus tracing shows diffuse piriform
      and stereotyped cortical amygdala projections, and two-photon imaging in 8 to 16 week male
      mice under imposed 8 Hz sniffing shows chemical relationships represented in bulb boutons and
      sharpened in piriform, with the geometry shifting toward bulb-like structure when associative
      transmission was attenuated. Optogenetically imposed ensembles of about 300 channelrhodopsin-expressing neurons weakly supported
      learned appetitive or aversive responses, and single-unit responses drifted so that a day-1
      classifier approached chance by 32 days, with daily exposure slowing drift. Open question: what
      readout stays stable on top of a population that moves, and does behaviour drift with the code?

anecdotes:
  - title: "Dispersed, except in one place"
    source: "Sosulski, Bloom, Cutforth, Axel & Datta, Nature (2011), Distinct representations of olfactory information in different cortical centres."
    text: >-
      When the labelled axons from one glomerulus were followed into piriform cortex, they spread
      widely with no apparent spatial preference. It would have been easy to conclude that the bulb
      projects at random. The same experiment imaged the cortical amygdala and found that the
      axons from a single glomerulus formed stereotyped patches there. One tracer in one adult
      mouse glomerulus therefore showed two different rules in two different targets. A diffuse
      pattern is a fact about piriform cortex, and the cortical amygdala is a reminder that the
      bulb is not wired as a single random projection.
  - title: "The three hundred neurons"
    source: "Choi, Stettler, Kallman, Bhaskar, Fleischmann & Axel, Cell (2011), Driving opposing behaviors with ensembles of piriform neurons."
    text: >-
      In awake mice, activating ensembles of channelrhodopsin-expressing piriform neurons with light
      was paired with a reward or a shock, and the animals learned opposite responses. The authors
      reported that ensembles of as few as about 300 channelrhodopsin-expressing neurons supported a learned response, and that mice
      could learn to tell separate activated subpopulations apart. They also withheld the
      conclusion that their artificial network reproduced natural odour processing. The number is
      the smallest effective ensemble tested in this artificial experiment, not a count of the neurons in a natural odour
      representation.
  - title: "Fifty-three percent is not accuracy"
    source: "Lee, Mayhew et al., Science (2023), A principal odor map unifies diverse tasks in olfactory perception."
    text: >-
      The headline number from the prospective test of the odour map is easy to misread. A panel
      characterized 400 new molecules and 323 were retained after exclusions. For 53 percent of the
      evaluated molecules, the frozen model's profile was closer to the panel mean than the median
      individual panellist's profile was. That makes it a statement about being typical of a human
      panel. It does not say the model named the right smell 53 percent of the time, and the large
      published map of roughly half a million molecules plots predictions.

**In the olfactory bulb, smell arrives as a map: receptor neurons of one type converge on a glomerulus, so each odour lights up a characteristic pattern of glomeruli at a given concentration. Piriform cortex does not keep that map. An odour activates neurons scattered across the cortex, yet chemically similar odours still evoke similar patterns there. The cortex's own network then reshapes those patterns, so related odours cluster more strongly than in the bulb's input, and a pattern can acquire learned meaning.** Reviews of piriform cortex describe odour quality as coded sparsely and in a distributed way, and put learning and memory at the centre of how odours are perceived (Gottfried, 2010; Wilson and Sullivan, 2011).

[Smell and taste](/lessons/smell-and-taste/) ended with receptor neurons converging on glomeruli and the bulb projecting to piriform cortex. This lesson follows that projection into the cortex, nearly all of it in mice. The question at each stage is what the circuit keeps of the receptor map, what it adds, and what it gives up. How the receptor choice and the axon wiring arise is in [olfactory receptor choice](/lessons/olfactory-receptor-choice/), [partners, layers and self-avoidance](/lessons/partners-layers-self-avoidance/) and [topographic maps](/lessons/topographic-maps/).

## What the bulb sends, and the puzzle

Each olfactory receptor neuron makes one type of receptor, and neurons of one type send their axons to the same small knot of synapses in the bulb, a **glomerulus** (in mice, usually two per bulb for each receptor type). An odour binds some receptor types and not others, so it activates a set of glomeruli. That set is characteristic of the odour at a given concentration. Raising the concentration often recruits extra, less sensitive glomeruli, though recruitment can level off.

The bulb's layout is therefore a **map of receptor identity**: where a glomerulus sits tells you which receptor feeds it. The layout is only coarsely related to chemistry, so it is not a fine chemical map.

**Mitral and tufted cells** are the principal output neurons of the mouse main olfactory bulb. Each sends a primary dendrite into one glomerulus and an axon toward the cortex. Cells that send their dendrites into the same glomerulus are **sister cells**: they share one input. Around and beneath them sit interneurons: **periglomerular cells**, GABAergic cells that work in the glomerular layer, and **granule cells**, which lack axons and contact the lateral dendrites of mitral and tufted cells.

Mitral and tufted axons reach piriform cortex, the main olfactory area of the cortex, without a thalamic relay. There the tidy map seems to vanish: an odour switches on neurons scattered across the cortex. That raises four puzzles.

1. **Scatter.** Does the output of one glomerulus stay together in piriform cortex, or spread out?
2. **Similarity.** If it spreads out, how can the cortex still carry which odours are chemically alike?
3. **Meaning.** Can a scattered pattern acquire learned meaning, and what would that show?
4. **Stability.** Does an odour's pattern in the cortex stay put from week to week?

The first puzzle is anatomy, taken next. The second needs one term and one idea: a piriform neuron's **glomerular inputs** are the glomeruli whose mitral and tufted axons reach it. It also receives input from other cortical neurons.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Where a piriform neuron sits tells you little about which odours excite it. In the simple feed-forward model used here, its glomerular inputs and its threshold decide its response: each neuron samples several glomeruli scattered across the bulb and fires only when enough of them are active together. So the more glomeruli two odours share, the more neurons they share, wherever those neurons sit. With sparse responses, this random wiring keeps the *order* of similarity on average, but related odours come out *less* alike than in the bulb. Real responses also depend on synaptic strengths, timing and inhibition. Anything tighter, such as related odours clustering more strongly, completion from partial input or learned meaning, is not explained by this model; the cortex's own network and experience are the candidates tested below.

## Following one glomerulus

If piriform cortex kept the bulb's map, the output of one glomerulus should land in one patch of cortex, the way a [topographic map](/lessons/topographic-maps/) keeps neighbours together. Sosulski and colleagues (2011) labelled the output axons of a single glomerulus in adult mice with a tracer and imaged where they went, in piriform cortex and, for comparison, in a second target, the cortical amygdala.

**Predict.** If the cortex kept the map, what would the labelled axons look like in piriform cortex? What would it mean if the two targets looked different?

**What they found.** In piriform cortex the axons projected diffusely with no apparent spatial preference. In the cortical amygdala the same glomerulus gave spatially stereotyped patches. So dispersal belongs to one target, not to the whole bulb.

Recordings of responses tell the same story. Stettler and Axel (2009) imaged odour responses in mouse piriform cortex in vivo and found that an odour activated a **dispersed** ensemble, neurons scattered across the area and not gathered in a patch. A single neuron could respond to chemically dissimilar odours: its **receptive field**, the set of odours that excite it, was discontinuous in chemical space. That does not mean piriform carries no chemical information, only that its arrangement is not a topographic map.

Diffuse is also not the same as random: later projection mapping found spatial structure in how bulb neurons route their axons, though not a map of odour chemistry (Chen and colleagues, 2022). That settles the first puzzle. The second is harder.

## How a scatter keeps similarity

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

If one glomerulus spreads its axons all over piriform cortex, is the chemistry lost? First, what does "similar" mean for a whole population?

**A population pattern.** Record from many piriform neurons while a mouse smells odour A, and write down each neuron's response: this one fired, that one did not, a third fired a little. That list is A's *population pattern*. Do the same for odour B. Two odours are represented similarly if their lists overlap, that is if many of the same neurons respond to both. Real analyses correlate the responses of many neurons, a related measure but not the same number.

**How a piriform neuron is wired.** Tracing back from single piriform neurons found that each receives input from mitral cells of several glomeruli spread across the bulb (Miyamichi and colleagues, 2011). In recordings from anterior piriform cortex, driving one glomerulus with light did not make the cells fire, while combinations of co-active glomeruli did (Davison and Ehlers, 2011). The simple model used here keeps just those two facts: a neuron fires only when enough of its inputs are active together, because one input alone does not bring it to threshold (see [action potential](/lessons/action-potential/)).

**Worked problem: overlap after a scatter.** Take a toy neuron that listens to two glomeruli and fires only if both are active. Wiring is random, so every pair of glomeruli is equally likely to feed a neuron. Odour A activates 10 glomeruli. A related ester, B, also activates 10 and shares 6 of them with A. An unrelated amine, C, shares none (esters and amines are two different chemical families).

*Step 1: which neurons respond to A?* Those whose two inputs both fall among A's 10 glomeruli. There are 10 × 9 / 2 = 45 such input pairs.

*Step 2: which of those also respond to B?* Those whose two inputs both fall among the 6 shared glomeruli: 6 × 5 / 2 = 15 pairs.

*Step 3: overlap.* 15 / 45 = 1/3. On average, in a large randomly wired population, a third of the neurons that respond to A also respond to B. The odours share 60 percent of their glomeruli but only about 33 percent of their responsive neurons.

*Step 4: the unrelated odour.* No glomeruli are shared, so no A-responsive neuron can also respond to C. The overlap is 0.

*Check:* B is still far more like A than C is, so the order survives. The overlap has shrunk because both of a neuron's inputs must be shared: 6/10 × 5/9 = 1/3, close to squaring the shared fraction (0.6² = 0.36).

Where the neurons sit never entered the calculation. A larger toy with 1,000 glomeruli gives the same two results: the order of similarity is kept, and related odours become *less* alike, not more, as long as responses stay sparse (see Going deeper).

**Predict.** Pashkovski and colleagues (2020) imaged two things in mice breathing at a controlled sniff rate, with the same panel of odours for the comparison: the bulb's axon boutons inside piriform cortex (the terminals of mitral and tufted cells, so the cortex's input), and piriform neurons in layers 2 and 3, where its principal cells sit (its output). Which comparisons would test whether the scatter keeps chemistry, and adds anything? If piriform worked like the toy, how should the similarity of related odours compare between boutons and cortical neurons?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Three comparisons do it. *Is there a spatial map?* Ask whether neurons near each other share responses more often than distant ones. If they do not, there is no spatial map. *Is there structure anyway?* Ask whether chemically related odours give more similar population patterns than unrelated ones. *Has the cortex added something?* Compare the boutons' similarity structure with the cortex's on the same panel.

By the toy, piriform should largely keep the bulb's order of similarity, with related odours less alike than in the boutons, not more.

</details>

**What they found.** The first half held: in both the boutons and the piriform neurons, chemically related odours gave more similar patterns than unrelated ones, so the scattered cortex kept the chemistry. The second half did not: piriform neurons clustered related odours *more* tightly than their own input did. To picture it in made-up numbers, related pairs might correlate at about 0.4 in boutons and 0.7 in cortex. The authors also passed their bouton responses through a published random feed-forward model: it largely kept the relationships but made the patterns less alike, as the toy predicts, and the real cortex did not match it. Something in the cortex added structure that this random model did not give.

This is a different kind of map. A *spatial map* says that similar odours activate nearby places. A *representational geometry* says that similar odours evoke similar population patterns, wherever the cells sit. Piriform cortex has the second without the first.

## What the cortex's network adds

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

What could add the structure? Piriform pyramidal cells, its main excitatory neurons, are linked to each other by association fibres, recurrent connections within the cortex, and reviews of the field treat those connections as a place where experience is stored. Strengthening the synapses among neurons that are active together is thought to bind a scattered ensemble into a familiar odour object. That would help an animal tell a familiar odour from similar ones, and let the whole ensemble respond even when part of the input is missing, a process called pattern completion (Wilson and Sullivan, 2011). The same network is a candidate for the extra clustering.

Pashkovski and colleagues tested the network directly. A genetically targeted silencing tool blocked transmitter release from excitatory piriform neurons, attenuating the cortex's associational network. Their input from the bulb still arrives, so they still respond to odours and can still be imaged, but they excite their neighbours, including inhibitory cells, much less.

**Predict.** If piriform's own network reshapes the odour relationships, what should silencing do to the cortical similarity structure?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The relationships should move back toward the input's, closer to the structure in the boutons. That need not mean every correlation falls.

</details>

**What they found.** Responses did not simply weaken: tuning broadened, more neurons responded and odour correlations rose overall, as expected if recurrent excitation normally recruits inhibition. Even so, which odours resembled which moved toward the boutons' pattern. Piriform also sends feedback to the bulb and connects to other olfactory areas, so this shows that piriform network activity is needed for the change, not that local connections alone produce it. Passive odour experience could also reshape the cortical relationships.

| Recording | Odour relationships |
| --- | --- |
| Bulb boutons in piriform cortex (the input) | Related odours more alike than unrelated ones; tracks chemistry |
| Random feed-forward model fed the bouton responses | Relationships largely kept; related patterns less alike |
| Piriform neurons | Related odours clustered more strongly; some pairs regrouped |
| Piriform neurons, excitatory output blocked | Broader, more correlated responses; relationships closer to the boutons' |

**What stays unproven.** A fixed panel does not sample all odours, fluorescence is not a full record of spikes, and similar neural patterns do not show that the mouse perceives the odours as similar; that needs a behavioural test.

## Teaching an arbitrary ensemble

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

If an odour is an ensemble, what happens if you create one without an odour? Choi and colleagues (2011) expressed channelrhodopsin, a light-gated channel, in subsets of layer 2/3 piriform neurons in awake mice and activated them with light. Light drives those neurons whatever the nose is doing. In one group the activation was paired with a reward, and in another with a shock.

**Predict.** What should each group learn? Would learning show that the mice smelled something?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

If an arbitrary pattern can acquire meaning, the reward group should learn an appetitive response to the light and the shock group an aversive one. Learning would show that the pattern became a usable signal, not that it felt like an odour.

</details>

**What they found.** Pairing the activation with a water reward taught the mice to lick when the light came on, and pairing it with a shock taught them to flee: opposite learned behaviours from the same kind of pattern. Small ensembles were enough to drive a learned response, though a weaker one than larger ensembles, and mice could learn to discriminate different activated subpopulations. (For optogenetic tools, see [hacking the cell](/lessons/hacking-the-cell/); for learned associations, [synaptic plasticity](/lessons/synaptic-plasticity/) and [memory consolidation](/lessons/memory-consolidation/).)

So activity in these neurons, even an arbitrary pattern, is **sufficient** as a learned cue: paired with an outcome, it was enough for the mouse to learn an association. It does not show that piriform is **necessary**, that is required, for natural odour learning, nor where the association is stored, in piriform's own synapses or in the areas that piriform feeds. The authors withheld the claim that their artificial network reproduced natural odour processing, and the ensemble sizes tested are not the size of a natural odour representation.

## A code that drifts

The distributed code is also not fixed. Schoonover and colleagues (2021) recorded single units in mouse piriform cortex over days to weeks. To ask whether the responses stayed the same, they used a **decoder** (a classifier): a program trained to guess which odour was given from the recorded activity. Its score is compared with **chance**, the score of random guessing.

Odour-evoked responses drifted: a classifier trained on the first recording day approached chance by 32 days, a measured end point rather than a time constant. Daily exposure to an odour slowed the drift; fear conditioning did not stabilize the responses. This is an active question rather than a settled rule; a 2026 review reads drift on multiple timescales as a sign that piriform cortex may encode temporal context as well as odour identity (Friedrich and Zenke, 2026).

Drift does not mean that an animal's smell changed. A decoder is a tool the experimenter holds, and a downstream circuit may read a moving population consistently. The finding does change what a cortical ensemble can mean: the idea that an odour always lights the same set of cortical neurons is not what these recordings show. That reading assumes the recording itself stayed stable, which Going deeper shows how to check.

## More than a map: inhibition and timing in the bulb

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

So far the bulb's output has been treated as a set of active glomeruli. What the cortex actually receives is richer: the bulb reshapes the pattern with inhibition, and the pattern carries timing.

The key contact is a **reciprocal dendrodendritic synapse**, one dendrite signalling to another and back. A mitral or tufted dendrite releases glutamate onto the interneuron, and the interneuron releases GABA onto the same dendrite. A reviewer of the rodent literature (Burton, 2017) summarized the consequences: the loop can inhibit the cell that excited it (recurrent inhibition), and because one interneuron touches many output cells, it can inhibit neighbours that share it (lateral inhibition). This resembles the lateral inhibition of [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/), built from dendrites, but the bulb is not a retina (see Going deeper).

A sniff is a cycle of inhalation and exhalation, and a spike's **phase** is where in that cycle it falls. A review of bulb coding concluded that the relative timing of activity in the bulb conveys odour information, although whether and how downstream circuits use it has been debated (Uchida and colleagues, 2014). The two output types already differ in timing: tufted and mitral cells lock to opposite phases of the sniff cycle (Fukunaga and colleagues, 2012). They are parallel channels, not copies.

Sister cells make a sharp test of timing: do cells that share one glomerulus send the same message? Dhawale and colleagues (2010) recorded in adult anaesthetized mice and used a light-based method to find which recorded mitral and tufted cells received input from one parent glomerulus, so sisters were defined by their input and not by how they responded to odours.

**Predict.** If the glomerulus fixed the message, what would their mean rates and their spike timing against the breath look like?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Both would match: similar rates and similar timing, like copies of one input line.

</details>

**What they found.** Counting mean spikes, sister responses were correlated. Looking at spike timing relative to the respiration cycle, odours changed the timing of sisters differently. A shared glomerulus is a shared address, not a shared message.

Read the result within its limits. The units were pooled mitral and tufted cells, which fire at different sniff phases, and the mice were anaesthetized, so some timing differences may reflect cell type or state.

So the cortex receives a timed map shaped by inhibition, and turns it into a distributed code that its own network reshapes and that drifts.

## Smell loss in people, and a learned odour map

Two applications sit beside this circuit story. Early in the COVID-19 pandemic, Brann and colleagues (2020) found the ACE2 entry gene in non-neuronal olfactory cells and localized ACE2 protein to sustentacular support cells in mouse dorsal epithelium, not in sensory neurons, which suggested non-neuronal mechanisms. Khan and colleagues (2021) examined adult postmortem tissue from people who had died with COVID-19 and found sustentacular cells to be the main olfactory mucosal target, with no evidence of infection of sensory neurons or the bulb parenchyma in their samples, though they allowed that mechanisms may differ between people. Infection of olfactory neurons and spread along their axons into the brain is therefore not the usual route on current evidence, which suggests neuronal infection is rare.

Poorer smell identification also predicted later disease in cohort studies: Parkinson disease in the Health ABC study (Chen and colleagues, 2017) and Alzheimer dementia in a North Manhattan cohort (Devanand and colleagues, 2015). These are associations, not diagnoses, and smell loss has other causes such as chronic rhinosinusitis.

A different route to odour structure skips the brain. Lee, Mayhew and colleagues (2023) trained a graph neural network on approximately 5,000 labelled molecules and represented every molecule as a point in 256 learned dimensions, a principal odor map. In a test on new molecules rated by a human panel, the frozen model's description profile was closer to the panel's average than the median panellist's was for just over half of the molecules. This is a computational embedding of structure and reported odour, not anatomy of the bulb or cortex.

## Going deeper

### Preparations behind the main results

Sosulski and colleagues electroporated a dextran tracer into a single glomerulus of an adult mouse, waited five days, and imaged the labelled output axons in fixed, flattened cortex and in excised amygdala.

Pashkovski and colleagues imaged male mice 8 to 16 weeks old. The mice were semi-paralysed and ventilated, in a state the authors called wakefulness on EEG criteria, with an imposed 8 Hz sniff cycle, and cortical neurons were tested with three panels of 22 odours (the bulb-bouton comparison used one of them, called the tiled panel).

Dhawale and colleagues recorded in mice 60 to 400 days old, anaesthetized with ketamine and xylazine.

### Diffuse, but not random

Diffuse axons leave open how individual synapses are chosen, because a tracer image is not a connectivity matrix. Barcoded sequencing of the projections of thousands of single bulb neurons (Chen and colleagues, 2022) found that bulb neurons aimed at one anterior-posterior position in piriform also project to matched targets outside it.

### A larger toy, and a published model

The worked problem's rule is a simplification of the tracing and recordings in the main text. In the larger binary toy, our own calculation, each odour activates 100 of 1,000 glomeruli, and each neuron samples 20 different glomeruli at random and fires if at least 5 of them are active. About 4 percent of neurons then respond to an odour. Two odours that share 60 percent of their glomeruli share about 31 percent of their responsive neurons, and two that share none share about 2 percent. The shrinking depends on that sparseness. If the same neurons needed only 2 active inputs, about 61 percent would respond to each odour, and related odours would share about 77 percent of their responders, more than their 60 percent of glomeruli (unrelated ones would still share about 58 percent). The model Pashkovski and colleagues used on their bouton data (from Schaffer and colleagues, 2018) is richer: each model neuron samples many excitatory and inhibitory bulb inputs and gives graded responses. The real cortex kept many of the relationships it predicted, but showed stronger correlation structure and selectively regrouped some pairs, which the model did not capture.

### Inhibition in the bulb is not a retina's

Recordings in rats found that each mitral cell is influenced by a handful of spatially scattered glomeruli, not a centre-surround field of neighbours (Fantana and colleagues, 2008), and glomerular-layer circuits also spread inhibition across the bulb and set its gain (Banerjee and colleagues, 2015). The review of the rodent literature (Burton, 2017) concluded that granule cells alone do not account for all odour-evoked inhibition of mitral and tufted cells.

### Two output channels

In mice, tufted cells respond with shorter latency over a wider range of odour concentrations and project mainly to anterior olfactory cortex, while mitral cells project to all olfactory cortical areas (Igarashi and colleagues, 2012).

### How small an artificial ensemble can be

In Choi and colleagues' experiments, ensembles of as few as about 300 channelrhodopsin-expressing neurons could drive a learned response (flight in about 30 percent of aversive-task trials at about 300 cells and about 95 percent above 500). The figure of 300 counts cells that expressed channelrhodopsin, and only about 40 percent of such cells showed an activity marker after light, so the number of neurons actually driven is uncertain.

### Worked problem, part a: the light-pulse train

The aversive-conditioning photostimulus used 20 Hz pulses of 25 ms each. Suppose a train lasts 3 s (our assumption) with one pulse starting each period.

*Step 1: period.* 1/20 s = 50 ms.
*Step 2: pulses.* 20 s⁻¹ x 3 s = 60 pulses.
*Step 3: off-time per period.* 50 ms - 25 ms = 25 ms.
*Step 4: duty cycle.* 25/50 = 0.50.
*Step 5: light-on time.* 60 x 25 ms = 1,500 ms = 1.5 s.
*Check:* 3 s x 0.50 = 1.5 s.

These are stimulus quantities, not a count of 60 spikes.

### Sisters: further limits, and the awake bulb

Timing differences between sisters do not tell you which synapses produced them, so the Dhawale experiment does not show that granule-cell inhibition did. In awake mice, sisters of one genetically identified glomerulus, M72, gave temporally diverse excitatory and inhibitory responses to most of its ligands and stereotyped early responses only to its strongest ligand (Arneodo and colleagues, 2018), so sisters can differ in more than timing.

### Drift: a changing code or a changing recording

A classifier trained on day 1 piriform responses decodes odours well on day 1 and near chance a month later. Design the experiments that distinguish a changing neural code from a changing recording, and say what the result does not show.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

*Is the recording stable?* Check that the same units are tracked: compare waveform shape, isolation quality and baseline firing across days, and repeat with a subset that is clearly the same cells.

*Is the failure about the decoder?* Train a new decoder on each day and compare it with the frozen day-1 decoder. If daily decoders work and the frozen one fails, odour information is present every day while the mapping changed. If daily decoders also fail late, the odour information is lost or hidden, so check recording quality, odour delivery and the animal's state before reading anything into it.

*Does behaviour drift too?* Test the animal's discrimination of the same odours on the same days. If behaviour holds steady while the decoder fails, the behaviour tolerates the change, though this does not say which cells or readout keep it stable.

*What controls drift?* Compare animals with daily exposure, with fear conditioning and with neither, as the paper did, and ask which factor changes the rate.

*What stays unproven.* A failed decoder alone does not show a changed percept, and a change in code does not show which synapses changed.

</details>

### Worked problem, part b: the odour map's prospective test

A human panel characterized 400 new odorants, and 323 were retained for the final evaluation with no retraining.

*Step 1: fraction retained.* 323/400 = 0.8075, or 80.75 percent.
*Step 2: how many molecules.* The main text's just over half is 53 percent: 0.53 x 323 = about 171 molecules for which the model's profile beat the median panellist's. It is not classification accuracy.

The map did not predict suprathreshold intensity as concentration varied, and the roughly 500,000-molecule picture plots predictions.

### Smell and later disease: the numbers

In the Health ABC study of 2,462 community-dwelling people aged 70 to 79 at enrolment, the lowest tertile of a smell identification test had a Parkinson disease hazard ratio of 4.8 against the highest tertile (95% CI 2.0 to 11.2, from only 42 incident cases; Chen and colleagues, 2017).
