techniques:
  - name: "Two-photon calcium imaging"
    blurb: >-
      A genetically encoded sensor such as GCaMP is expressed in neurons, and a two-photon laser
      scans the tissue to measure each cell's fluorescence over time. It reports calcium, which
      rises after spikes, for hundreds or thousands of identified cells, but spike times must be
      inferred through a calibrated model.
  - name: "High-density extracellular recording and spike sorting"
    blurb: >-
      A silicon probe such as Neuropixels records voltage from hundreds of sites along a shank.
      Spike-sorting software detects spikes and groups them into units by waveform and position,
      giving millisecond timing across many brain regions, with each unit an inference rather
      than a confirmed single neuron.
  - name: "Single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics"
    blurb: >-
      Dissociated cells are sequenced one by one and clustered by gene expression; an imaging
      method such as MERFISH then measures a chosen gene panel in intact sections to place the
      clusters in the anatomy.
  - name: "Serial-section electron microscopy connectomics"
    blurb: >-
      Tissue is cut into thousands of thin sections, each is imaged by electron microscope, and
      software with human proofreading traces neurons and marks chemical synapses through the
      stack. The result is a wiring diagram of one piece of tissue from one animal, with no
      record of activity.

**In one awake mouse, the MICrONS Consortium recorded calcium signals from around 75,000 neurons in visual cortex over a week of imaging sessions, then reconstructed the same tissue by electron microscopy: more than 200,000 cells and half a billion synapses.**

That is a long way from one electrode in one cell. But every number in that sentence is a measurement of something other than what you might assume. A calcium signal is not a spike, a sorted unit is not certainly a neuron, 75,000 neurons are not 75,000 replicates, and a synapse in an electron micrograph is not a strength. This lesson takes each large-scale method in turn and asks what it actually reports.

## A calcium signal is light that follows spikes at a distance

When a neuron fires an [action potential](/lessons/action-potential/), voltage-gated calcium channels open and calcium in the cell body rises for a while after the spike. A genetically encoded sensor turns that rise into light. GCaMP6, like earlier GCaMPs, is one protein made of three parts: a circularly permuted green fluorescent protein, whose ends have been reopened near the light-emitting chromophore; calmodulin, the calcium-binding protein you met in [calcium signaling](/lessons/calcium-signaling/); and M13, a short peptide that calmodulin grips once it has bound calcium. Calcium binding changes the conformation, and the barrel brightens. How the sensor is delivered, by virus or by a transgenic mouse line, belongs to [hacking the cell](/lessons/hacking-the-cell/), and how a two-photon microscope collects the light belongs to [microscopy and images](/lessons/microscopy-and-images/).

So the signal is three steps from a spike: spike, calcium, fluorescence. Think of it as reading a kettle's steam to infer when the stove was switched on. Activity is reported as a fractional change from baseline:

> **ΔF/F0 = (F − F0) / F0**

There is a catch. Around each cell body lies **neuropil**, the dense tangle of axons, dendrites and glial processes, which also glows. Light from it leaks into any region you draw around a soma. The usual fix subtracts a scaled copy of the surrounding signal:

> **F_corrected = F_soma − r × F_neuropil**

**Worked problem.** These numbers are invented for practice. A soma region reads 500 at baseline and 620 at the peak (arbitrary units). The ring of neuropil around it reads 300 and 330.

*Step 1: no correction.* ΔF/F0 = (620 − 500)/500 = 120/500 = 0.24.

*Step 2: subtract neuropil.* Take r = 0.8, the default that Huang and colleagues (2021) used in mouse visual cortex. It is not a universal constant; they adjusted it for some cells. Then F0 = 500 − 0.8 × 300 = 500 − 240 = 260, and F = 620 − 0.8 × 330 = 620 − 264 = 356.

*Step 3: recompute.* ΔF/F0 = (356 − 260)/260 = 96/260 = 0.37.

The correction raised ΔF/F0 by about half, because the neuropil had added a large, nearly steady glow to the baseline. Assumptions: r is the same at baseline and peak, and the neuropil ring samples the same contaminating light as the soma.

How many spikes does a ΔF/F0 mean? Only a calibration against an electrical recording in the same conditions can say. Chen and colleagues (2013) recorded spikes with a loose-seal **cell-attached** pipette, which sits on the outside of one neuron and records its spikes without breaking in, while imaging the same cell. In layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons of primary visual cortex in lightly anesthetized, sedated mice, with virally expressed GCaMP6s, high-magnification imaging and a stimulus adjusted to keep firing moderate, they detected 99 ± 0.2% of isolated spikes at a 1% false-positive rate, in nine cells. Huang and colleagues (2021) did the same in anesthetized transgenic GCaMP6s and GCaMP6f mice. Even at high magnification, a detector tuned to each neuron's known spike times caught fewer single spikes than in Chen's cells, about 40 to 80% on average depending on the mouse line; the authors pointed to weaker transgenic expression and more labelled neuropil. They then downsampled the movies to mimic population imaging, and the tuned detector caught only about 16 to 43% (32 to 43% in the GCaMP6s lines, lower in the faster GCaMP6f lines). At the same 1% false-positive rate, standard blind spike inference often caught fewer than 10%, even before downsampling. Downsampling mimics a larger field of view, which spreads the same imaging time over more pixels and so collects fewer photons from each neuron. The spike-to-fluorescence relation also differs from one neuron to the next. GCaMP6s decays slowly, the fast GCaMP6 variants trade sensitivity for speed, and the jGCaMP8 sensors (Zhang and colleagues, 2023), which swap M13 for a peptide from another calmodulin-binding enzyme, respond more linearly than earlier GCaMPs. None of them reports voltage, and a flat trace does not prove a silent cell.

## Listening to many neurons: sites, channels and units

An **extracellular** electrode sits outside cells and picks up the small voltage changes that nearby spikes cause in the surrounding fluid, unlike the intracellular recordings in [the resting membrane and passive signals](/lessons/resting-membrane-passive/). It loses the membrane potential but keeps millisecond timing.

The original Neuropixels probe (Jun and colleagues, 2017) carried 960 recording sites on one shank but could address 384 of them at a time, and it digitized the action-potential band at 30 kHz, 30,000 voltage samples per second. That is a sampling rate, not a firing rate. With two probes in an awake mouse the team recorded more than 700 well-isolated neurons at once across five brain structures.

Neurons are not read off sites. **Spike sorting** software detects spikes and groups them into **units** by the shape of each spike and the sites where it appears. One neuron shows up on several neighbouring sites; one site hears several neurons. A unit can merge two neurons or split one, and the Allen Institute's documentation for its Neuropixels survey warns that spikes from different neurons can be mixed in one unit, and it does not separate single-unit from multi-unit activity. Treat each unit as an inference with quality scores.

The two methods trade against each other. Imaging tells you which cell, where, and often its genetic type, but it is slow and indirect. Probes give spike timing across many regions, as in the Allen survey of six cortical and two thalamic visual areas in mice (Siegle and colleagues, 2021), but the identity of each unit is uncertain. The codes these populations carry are the subject of [sensory coding](/lessons/sensory-coding/) and [the visual cortex](/lessons/visual-cortex/).

## How many samples do you really have?

Large datasets are nested: trials within units, units within sessions, sessions within animals. Observations inside one level share its conditions, so they are not independent. Counting them as if they were is **pseudoreplication** (Lazic, 2010). Lazic described averaging within each subject as one way to keep subjects as the units of analysis; hierarchical (mixed-effects) models are another.

**Worked problem.** This dataset is invented. Two mice, A and B, each have two sessions. In each session the same stimulus is shown four times, and spikes are counted in a 0.25 s window per presentation. Each unit appears in one session only.

- Mouse A, session 1: U1 (1, 2, 3, 2) and U2 (0, 0, 1, 1)
- Mouse A, session 2: U3 (1, 1, 1, 1) and U4 (2, 2, 2, 2)
- Mouse B, session 3: U5 (0, 1, 0, 1)
- Mouse B, session 4: U6 (1, 0, 1, 0)

*Step 1: count.* 6 units, 4 sessions, 2 animals, 6 × 4 = 24 unit-trials, but only 4 × 4 = 16 stimulus presentations, because units recorded together share the same presentations.

*Step 2: rates.* Each unit is watched for 4 × 0.25 s = 1 s. U1 fired 1 + 2 + 3 + 2 = 8 spikes, so 8 spikes/s. The six rates are 8, 2, 4, 8, 2 and 2 spikes/s.

*Step 3: pooled rate.* 26 spikes over 6 unit-seconds = 4.33 spikes/s.

*Step 4: weight each animal equally.* Session 1 averages (8 + 2)/2 = 5 and session 2 averages (4 + 8)/2 = 6, so mouse A = 5.5 spikes/s. Sessions 3 and 4 each give 2, so mouse B = 2 spikes/s. The animal mean is (5.5 + 2)/2 = 3.75 spikes/s.

The pooled rate is higher because mouse A supplied twice the units. For a claim about mice, n = 2, not 24. Report the hierarchy (animals, sessions, units, trials) and say which level your question is about.

## Atlases and connectomes: parts lists and wiring diagrams

A **transcriptomic atlas** sorts cells by the RNA they express. Yao, Zeng and colleagues (2023) profiled around seven million cells from the adult mouse brain; about four million passed quality control and fell into 5,322 clusters. A spatial dataset of about 4.3 million cells, measured by MERFISH with a 500-gene panel in one male mouse brain, placed the clusters in the anatomy, and transcriptomic identity and location matched to a high degree. The sequencing itself is taught in [sequencing and genomes](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/). The authors cautioned that not every cluster may be a true cell type, and that dissociation probably depletes large, fragile neurons. A cluster is a molecular category, not a measured function.

A **connectome** is a synapse-by-synapse wiring diagram. FlyWire (Dorkenwald and colleagues, 2024) reconstructed 139,255 neurons and 54.5 million chemical synapses from serial-section electron micrographs of one adult female Drosophila brain. A connection from neuron A to B is the set of A's synapses onto B. Electrical synapses are absent, and some connections of only a few synapses were missed. Some models built on it assume that strength is proportional to synapse count; that is an assumption, not a measurement.

MICrONS (2025) joined function and structure in one animal: calcium imaging of excitatory neurons expressing GCaMP6s across primary and higher visual areas of one mouse, then an electron-microscopy reconstruction of the same volume. Responses and wiring can be compared cell by cell for the neurons matched between the two datasets, but still in one mouse and one cubic millimetre.

## What none shows alone

Each method answers a different question. Imaging says which cells changed their calcium; probes say when units spiked; atlases say what molecular kinds of cells exist and where; connectomes say who could talk to whom. None of the datasets here is human, none is a complete brain map with activity and strengths, and none shows that a pathway is needed for a behaviour. That takes a manipulation, the subject of [designing a circuit experiment](/lessons/circuit-experiment-design/).

### Design problem

A lab images 400 neurons in mouse V1 in three mice with GCaMP6s at 30 frames per second over a wide field of view. It reports that 60% of the neurons "did not fire" to a visual stimulus, and cites the connectome of a different mouse showing few thalamic synapses onto those cell types. Critique the claims and redesign the study.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

*The imaging claim.* No fluorescence change is not the same as no spikes. Under wide-field, population-like conditions, single-spike detection with GCaMP6 was only a minority in Huang's calibration, often under 10% with blind spike inference. Calibrate in this preparation: record cell-attached spikes from a sample of neurons while imaging at the same field of view and frame rate, and report detection and false-positive rates. A Neuropixels probe in the same area adds millisecond spike timing, but its units cannot be matched to the imaged cells, and it can miss neurons that rarely fire.

*The statistics.* The 400 neurons come from three mice, so n = 3 for any claim about mice. Report the fraction per mouse and add animals.

*The anatomy.* The connectome is from one other animal, contains chemical synapses only, and counts synapses rather than measuring strength. Few synapses do not mean weak drive. Either register function and structure in the same tissue, imaging first and reconstructing afterward as MICrONS did, or test the input directly: stimulate the thalamic afferents, for example optogenetically, and record the V1 neurons' responses with calibrated imaging or a probe.
