**In a room heated to about 200 degrees F (about 93 degrees C), close to the boiling point of water, a thermometer under Charles Blagden's tongue read 98 degrees F, about one degree above his usual. Ten weeks later the same room passed 260 degrees F (above 126 degrees C) by evening, and eggs set there earlier that day roasted hard; a steak cooked.**

The first film split a 10-degree outside change in half. Here is its rule, with its limits.

> **The one idea.** A **regulated variable**, the quantity a body holds, such as core temperature, sits between two pulls. **Leak**, passive exchange with the surroundings, drags it toward the outside value. **Push-back** from the **effectors**, the muscles, glands and organs that change the variable, drags it toward the **set point**, the value it is held around. Each pull is a fixed **strength** times a gap: leak strength times the gap to the outside, push-back strength times the gap from the set point. Once the variable settles, the two pulls are equal, whatever the strengths; the effectors cancel exactly what leaks. The strengths decide where that balance falls, so the inside follows only a fraction of any outside change:
>
> **inside shift = outside shift × leak strength ÷ (leak strength + push-back strength)**
>
> The stronger the push-back compared with the leak, the less the inside follows the outside. A fraction near 0 makes the animal a **regulator** for that variable, holding it fairly steady; near 1, a **conformer**, letting it follow the outside within limits it can survive.
>
> *Scope:* a simple model, so its numbers are a sketch. It fits a variable that exchanges passively with the outside, over one range of outside conditions, after settling, with the set point fixed and the effectors below their limit. Other disturbances, such as glucose absorbed from a meal or used by cells, need other terms. Each limit returns below: sweat that cannot evaporate, fever, and a signal that acts before any change.

It builds on [what an organism has to do](/lessons/organism-problems/), [size and scaling](/lessons/size-and-scaling/) and [animal body plans](/lessons/animal-body-plans/).

## The loop, part by part

**Homeostasis**, Walter Cannon's word, is the holding of a body variable within a range compatible with staying alive. Cannon built on Claude Bernard's idea that an animal's cells live in an *internal environment* of blood and tissue fluid that changes much less than the world outside.

In the first film's loop, the integrator is a region of the [hypothalamus](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/), and the set point is a reference around which the normal range fluctuates, near, not exactly, 37 degrees C for core temperature ([below](#is-normal-exactly-37-degrees-c)).

A house thermostat is the usual analogy, but its furnace can only heat. The body can use different effectors for each direction, such as sweating and shivering, and turn one effector both ways: skin vessels widen in heat and narrow in cold. Behaviour, such as seeking shade or putting on a coat, is an effector too.

## Four tissues build the loop and the stomach wall

A **tissue** is a group of similar cells, with the material around them, that together do one kind of job. Most animals are built from four types, and the skin's temperature loop uses all four.

- **Epithelial tissue** is a sheet of closely joined cells that covers the body's surfaces and lines its cavities and tubes. It controls what crosses it. Sweat glands are epithelium that secretes. More in [epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/).
- **Connective tissue** is living cells spread through a nonliving **extracellular matrix**, a mesh of protein fibres and other material the cells secrete. Bone, tendon, fat, blood and the skin's deep, vessel-carrying layer are connective tissues. More in [adhesion and the extracellular matrix](/lessons/adhesion-and-ecm/).
- **Muscle tissue** is made of cells that contract and so produce movement or force. Smooth muscle narrows the skin's small arteries to keep heat in.
- **Nervous tissue** is **neurons**, cells that receive and send electrical signals, plus **glial cells**, which support them. Temperature-sensitive nerve endings in the skin, and nerve cells in the brain, are the loop's sensors; the hypothalamic integrator is nervous tissue too.

An **organ** is a structure built from several tissues that work together. A stained section through a stomach wall shows all four, layered as in the figure; the connective tissue also holds the layers together, and the muscle squeezes the contents as well as churning them.

An **organ system** is a set of organs that share a larger job: the stomach belongs to the digestive system, with the esophagus, intestines, liver and pancreas.

Sponges have specialized cells but no true tissues of this kind ([animal body plans](/lessons/animal-body-plans/)). A flowering plant is built from dermal, vascular and ground tissues ([plant body and growth](/lessons/plant-body-and-growth/)), and plants run loops too: as a leaf's water status falls, its stomata, the pores in its surface, close, which slows water loss.

## Negative and positive feedback, and set points that move

The film's push-back toward the set point, from either side, is **negative feedback**, the commonest way homeostasis works. "Negative" names the direction of the push, not of the change: shivering raises temperature and is still negative feedback.

In **positive feedback**, the response pushes the variable further from where it started. The textbook model is human childbirth: stretch of the cervix promotes release of the hormone oxytocin, which strengthens contractions of the uterus, which stretches the cervix further, until something outside the loop, the birth of the baby, ends it.

**Predict.** Flip the push to point away from the set point: inside shift = outside shift × leak strength ÷ (leak strength − push strength). What if the push is half as strong as the leak? Twice as strong?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Half: 1 ÷ (1 − 0.5) = 2, so the shift doubles but still settles. Twice: there is no stable balance, and any change keeps growing. Positive feedback runs away only when the push is stronger than the leak. The model cannot say how strong the labour loop is.

</details>

Treat the labour loop as a model of human labour: mice that lack oxytocin entirely gave birth without obvious problems, though they could not let down milk, so the loop as drawn does not carry over to every mammal. Feedback as circuitry, with gains (how strongly a loop responds) and delays, is in [signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/).

Homeostasis defends a range, and the set point can move. In a fever the set point for core temperature rises, so a person at normal temperature feels cold and shivers. Body temperature also follows a daily rhythm, so normal depends on where and when you measure.

## Regulators and conformers, and the price of constancy

The second film left out two temperature words often mixed up with regulator and conformer. An **endotherm** warms its body mainly with heat from its own metabolism; mammals and birds are endotherms, with partial endothermy in some fish and insects. An **ectotherm** gets its body heat mainly from its surroundings.

**Predict.** A desert lizard gets most of its body heat from the sun rather than from its own metabolism. Is it therefore a conformer?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not necessarily. Endotherm and ectotherm say where the heat comes from; regulator and conformer say how far the body follows the outside. A lizard that shuttles between sun and shade is an ectotherm that regulates, with behaviour as its effector; a typical lake fish is an ectotherm that largely conforms, a different solution, not a lesser one.

</details>

For blood salt, where the variable is **osmolality**, the concentration of dissolved particles in a fluid, the terms are **osmoregulator** and **osmoconformer**; how the mussel and crab manage it is in [osmoregulation](/lessons/osmoregulation/).

Regulation is not free. By the one idea, the inside stays near the set point only when push-back is much stronger than leak, and the effectors cancel what leaks; in a cool room, that means making as much heat as leaks out. A mouse, small and losing heat fast through its large surface for its size, makes a sharp test.

**Predict.** Two mice sit in rooms at 30 degrees C and 22 degrees C. Both hold their core near 37 degrees C. In which room do their effectors work harder, and why should the extra work grow as the room gets colder?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

At 22 degrees C. A colder room means a bigger gap between body and air, so heat leaks out faster, and extra heat production must make it up.

</details>

In a 2022 study, John and colleagues measured energy expenditure by indirect calorimetry (from oxygen used and carbon dioxide produced) in single-housed male C57BL/6J mice, and found it about 30% higher at 22 degrees C than at 30 degrees C.

**Worked problem.** The 30% is measured; the baseline is invented for arithmetic. Assume a mouse of this kind spends 24 kJ per day at 30 degrees C, at steady state, the same mouse in both conditions, and treat the ratio as exactly 1.30. A percentage change uses the starting value as its base:

> **percentage change = 100 x (new - old) / old**

1. *Expenditure at 22 degrees C.* 24 kJ per day x 1.30 = 31.2 kJ per day.
2. *Extra daily cost.* 31.2 - 24 = 7.2 kJ per day. Over 30 days: 7.2 x 30 = 216 kJ.
3. *The reverse move.* Moving the mouse from 22 to 30 degrees C cuts expenditure by 7.2 / 31.2 = 0.231, or 23.1%, not 30%, because the base is now the larger value.
4. *Check.* 31.2 x (1 - 0.231) = 31.2 x 0.769 = 24.0 kJ per day.

Scope: one study, one strain, one sex, two temperatures, and 30 degrees C is not zero cost ([thermoneutral points and a calibration caveat below](#the-mouse-energy-study-scope-and-mouse-thermoneutral-points)). Heat balance comes next, in [thermoregulation](/lessons/thermoregulation/); the signals that run these loops are in [nervous control](/lessons/nervous-control/) and [the endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/).

### Design problem

A thirsty mouse stops drinking long before the water is absorbed and dilutes its blood. You can infuse fluid through a stomach tube, bypassing the mouth, and record thirst-promoting neurons in the subfornical organ, a small brain region. Design an experiment that tests whether drinking stops only after the blood is corrected, or whether a signal from the gut acts first.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

1. *Separate volume from dilution.* Give one group water and another an equal volume of concentrated (hypertonic) salt solution. If stretch alone stopped thirst, both would silence the neurons; if the gut senses dilution, only water should.
2. *Put neurons and blood on one time base.* Sample blood osmolality at short intervals while recording. Neural activity falling before blood changes means a feedforward signal on top of negative feedback from the blood.
3. *Test the route.* Compare vagotomy, cutting the vagus nerves below the diaphragm, with a sham operation, in which the nerves are exposed but not cut. If the effect weakens only after vagotomy, the vagus carries part of the signal.

Zimmerman and colleagues (2019) found that water in the stomach silenced the neurons with a mean latency of about 105 seconds in hydrated mice (107 seconds in water-deprived mice), before any detectable change in blood osmolality; hypertonic saline in the stomach activated them instead; and vagotomy weakened the suppression of drinking.

Limits: this is mice only, "no detectable change" depends on how sensitive the blood assay is, and the 2019 study did not find the gut sensor. Later work in mice (Ichiki and colleagues, 2022) traced the signal to vagal nerve fibres serving the hepatic portal area, where absorbed water passes toward the liver, driven partly by the gut hormone vasoactive intestinal peptide; which cells first detect the dilution is still open.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Blagden's hot rooms in detail

George Fordyce procured a suite of rooms for heating experiments. On 23 January 1775 Blagden entered a stove-heated chamber with Joseph Banks, Daniel Solander and Captain Phipps; his pulse rose while the thermometer under his tongue read 98 degrees. On 3 April, in the room that passed 260 degrees by evening, eggs set there earlier roasted hard in about twenty minutes and a steak was overdone in thirty-three, while Blagden's body thermometer came back to nearly the same point each time he checked.

Blagden credited sweat evaporation with part of the cooling but suspected an extra power of the living body did most, citing Fordyce's trials in steamy rooms, where sweat could not evaporate. Physiology no longer needs that extra power: the brain holds temperature by adjusting sweating and skin blood flow.

### Is normal exactly 37 degrees C?

In a 2020 analysis of US adults, Protsiv and colleagues found recent mean oral temperatures below 37 degrees C, falling about 0.03 degrees C per birth decade, an association whose cause is not settled.

### The mouse energy study: scope and mouse thermoneutral points

The paper's chronic experiment lost its 30 degrees C data for normal-weight mice to a calibration error, so the figure here comes from the short-term comparison. And the thermoneutral point for a mouse, the air temperature at which it spends no extra energy to hold its body temperature, is not one number: Skop and colleagues (2020) found thermoneutral points near 29 degrees C in the light phase and 33 degrees C in the dark.
