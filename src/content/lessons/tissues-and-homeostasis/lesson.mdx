**In a room heated to about 200 degrees F, close to the boiling point of water, a thermometer under Charles Blagden's tongue read 98 degrees, about one degree above his usual. Ten weeks later, in the same room, which by evening passed 260 degrees F, eggs roasted hard and a steak cooked.** An egg in hot air heats until it matches the air. A living person did not. This lesson asks what kept him from heating up like the egg, starting with what a body is made of.

It builds on [what an organism has to do](/lessons/organism-problems/), [size and scaling](/lessons/size-and-scaling/) and [animal body plans](/lessons/animal-body-plans/).

## Four tissues build one stomach wall

A **tissue** is a group of similar cells, with the material around them, that together do one kind of job. Most animals are built from four types.

- **Epithelial tissue** is a sheet of closely joined cells that covers the body's surfaces and lines its cavities and tubes. It decides what crosses from one side to the other. How epithelial cells hold together and move material is in [epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/).
- **Connective tissue** is living cells spread through a nonliving **extracellular matrix**, a mesh of protein fibres and other material the cells secrete. Bone, tendon, fat and blood are all connective tissues. The matrix itself is covered in [adhesion and the extracellular matrix](/lessons/adhesion-and-ecm/).
- **Muscle tissue** is made of cells that contract and so produce movement or force.
- **Nervous tissue** is **neurons**, cells that receive and send electrical signals, plus **glial cells**, which support them.

An **organ** is a structure built from several tissues that work together. A stained section through a stomach wall shows all four. Epithelium lines the inside, where food sits. Beneath it, connective tissue carries blood vessels and holds the layers together. Outside that, layers of smooth muscle churn and squeeze the contents. Networks of nerve cells run between the muscle layers and help control the churning.

An **organ system** is a set of organs that share a larger job. The stomach is one organ of the digestive system, along with the esophagus, intestines, liver and pancreas.

Not every animal is built this way. Sponges have specialized cells but no true tissues of this kind, as you saw in [animal body plans](/lessons/animal-body-plans/). Plants solve the same problem with a different kit. A flowering plant's body is built from dermal, vascular and ground tissues, met in [plant body and growth](/lessons/plant-body-and-growth/).

## The loop: variable, set point, sensor, integrator, effector

**Homeostasis** is the holding of a body variable within a range compatible with staying alive. The word comes from Walter Cannon, building on Claude Bernard's idea that the cells of an animal live in an *internal environment* of blood and tissue fluid that changes much less than the world outside.

The standard model of a homeostatic loop has five parts. Name them in this order.

1. The **regulated variable**: the quantity being held, such as core body temperature or the salt concentration of blood.
2. The **set point**: the reference value around which the normal range fluctuates.
3. The **sensor**: a structure that measures the variable.
4. The **integrator**, or control centre: compares the sensed value with the set point and sends out a command.
5. The **effector**: a muscle, gland or other organ that changes the variable.

A house thermostat is the analogy: room temperature is the variable, the dial the set point, the thermometer the sensor, the circuit the integrator and the furnace the effector.

In an adult human the variable is core temperature. Temperature-sensitive nerve cells in the skin and in the brain are the sensors. A temperature-regulating region of the brain, in the hypothalamus, is the integrator; how it is wired is covered in [the hypothalamus and the autonomic system](/lessons/hypothalamus-autonomic/). When temperature rises, the effectors include blood vessels in the skin, which widen and carry more heat to the surface, and sweat glands, whose sweat removes heat as it evaporates. When it falls, skin vessels narrow to keep heat in, and skeletal muscles shiver and release heat as they use ATP. Behaviour, such as seeking shade or putting on a coat, is an effector too.

Blagden's report shows the loop at work in people. George Fordyce procured a suite of rooms for heating experiments. On 23 January 1775 Blagden went into a stove-heated chamber with Joseph Banks, Daniel Solander and Captain Phipps. The air stood near 200 degrees F for the party and above 211 when Banks went in. If a body behaved like an egg, its temperature should have climbed toward the air's. Instead Blagden's tongue read 98 degrees, about a degree above normal for him, while his pulse rose. On 3 April 1775 the party returned to the same room, which by evening passed 260 degrees. Earlier that day, eggs set in the room roasted hard in about twenty minutes and a steak was overdone in thirty-three, while Blagden's body thermometer came back to nearly the same point each time he checked.

That ruled in an active process holding human temperature against dry heat for minutes, but not its mechanism. Blagden credited evaporation of sweat with part of the cooling but suspected a further power of the living body did most of it, and he cited Fordyce's trials in steamy rooms, where sweat could not evaporate, as support. Physiology no longer needs that extra power. When the air is hotter than the skin, the body gains heat from the air, and evaporation of sweat is the only way left to lose heat. The real finding stands: temperature was held. Today we know the brain holds it by adjusting sweating and skin blood flow. In humid air, where sweat evaporates slowly, that route weakens, and the 1775 trials lasted minutes, not hours.

Plants run loops too. As a leaf's water status falls, its stomata, the pores in its surface, close, which slows water loss.

## Negative and positive feedback, and set points that move

In **negative feedback**, the response opposes the deviation. "Negative" does not mean "makes the variable smaller". If temperature rises, sweating brings it down; if it falls, shivering brings it up. Both are negative feedback, because each response pushes the variable back toward the set point.

In **positive feedback**, the response amplifies the change, and the loop runs until something outside it ends it. The textbook model is human childbirth: stretch of the cervix promotes release of the hormone oxytocin, which strengthens contractions of the uterus, which stretches the cervix further, until the baby is born. Treat that as a model of human labour. Mice that lack oxytocin entirely gave birth without obvious problems, though they could not let down milk, so the loop as drawn does not carry over to every mammal. Feedback as circuitry, with gains and delays, is in [signaling circuits](/lessons/signaling-circuits/).

Homeostasis defends a range, not a single number, and the set point itself can move. In a fever the set point for core temperature rises, so a person at normal temperature feels cold and shivers. Body temperature also follows a daily rhythm. So "normal is exactly 37 degrees C" is wrong. The value depends on where and when you measure, and in a 2020 analysis of US adults, Protsiv and colleagues found recent mean oral temperatures below 37 degrees C, falling about 0.03 degrees C per birth decade, an association whose cause is not settled.

Some responses come before the variable changes. In **feedforward** control, a signal that predicts a disturbance triggers the response early. You will meet one in the design problem at the end.

## Regulators and conformers, and the price of constancy

A **regulator** holds an internal variable fairly steady across a range of outside conditions. A **conformer** lets that variable follow the environment, within limits it can survive. Both labels describe one variable over one range, never a whole animal.

**Temperature.** An **endotherm** warms its body mainly with heat from its own metabolism; mammals and birds are endotherms, with partial endothermy in some fish and insects. An **ectotherm** gets its body heat mainly from its surroundings. An adult human regulates core temperature by sweating and shivering. A typical teleost fish in a lake is an ectotherm whose body temperature largely follows the water. That is a different solution, not a lesser one.

**Blood salt.** **Osmolality** is the concentration of dissolved particles in a fluid. An **osmoconformer** lets its blood osmolality follow the water's; an **osmoregulator** holds it fairly steady as the water's changes. The blue mussel, *Mytilus edulis*, conforms: over the salinities it tolerates, its blood comes close to the water around it. The shore crab, *Carcinus maenas*, is close to seawater in full-strength seawater, but in water diluted to roughly half strength it holds its blood well above the water. So it conforms at one end of its range and regulates at the other. How either animal does it is in [osmoregulation](/lessons/osmoregulation/).

Regulation is not free. In a 2022 study, John and colleagues measured energy expenditure by indirect calorimetry in single-housed male C57BL/6J mice and found it about 30% higher at 22 degrees C than at 30 degrees C. A mouse is small and loses heat fast through its large surface for its size, so at 22 degrees C it spends more to stay warm.

**Worked problem.** The 30% is measured; the baseline here is invented for arithmetic. Assume a mouse of this kind spends 24 kJ per day at 30 degrees C, and treat the ratio as exactly 1.30. Assume steady state and the same mouse in both conditions.

1. *Expenditure at 22 degrees C.* 24 kJ per day x 1.30 = 31.2 kJ per day.
2. *Extra daily cost.* 31.2 - 24 = 7.2 kJ per day. Over 30 days: 7.2 x 30 = 216 kJ.
3. *The reverse move.* Moving the mouse from 22 to 30 degrees C cuts expenditure by 7.2 / 31.2 = 0.231, or 23.1%, not 30%. The base is now the larger value.
4. *Check.* 31.2 x (1 - 0.231) = 31.2 x 0.769 = 24.0 kJ per day.

> **percentage change = 100 x (new - old) / old**

Keep the scope: one strain, one sex, two temperatures. The paper's chronic experiment lost its 30 degrees C data for normal-weight mice to a calibration error, so the figure here comes from the short-term comparison. And the thermoneutral point for a mouse, the air temperature at which it spends no extra energy to hold its body temperature, is not one number: Skop and colleagues (2020) found thermoneutral points near 29 degrees C in the light phase and 33 degrees C in the dark. Heat balance comes next, in [thermoregulation](/lessons/thermoregulation/); the signals that run these loops are in [nervous control](/lessons/nervous-control/) and [the endocrine system](/lessons/endocrine-system/).

### Design problem

A thirsty mouse that drinks stops long before the water is absorbed and dilutes its blood. Zimmerman and colleagues (2019) found that in mice, water infused straight into the stomach silenced thirst-promoting neurons in the subfornical organ, a small brain region, with a mean latency of about 105 seconds in hydrated mice (107 seconds in water-deprived mice), before any detectable change in blood osmolality, and that cutting the vagus nerves below the diaphragm weakened the suppression of drinking. Design an experiment that tests whether drinking stops only after the blood is corrected, or whether a signal from the gut acts first.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Use thirsty mice with a stomach tube, so fluid bypasses the mouth, and record the thirst neurons throughout.

1. *Separate volume from dilution.* Give one group water and another an equal volume of concentrated (hypertonic) salt solution. If stomach stretch alone stopped thirst, both would silence the neurons. If the gut senses dilution, only water should. Zimmerman and colleagues reported that hypertonic saline in the stomach activated these neurons instead.
2. *Put neurons and blood on one time base.* Sample blood osmolality at short intervals while recording, and ask which changes first. Neural activity falling before blood changes means a feedforward signal on top of negative feedback from the blood.
3. *Test the route.* Compare vagotomy with a sham operation, in which the nerves are exposed but not cut. If the effect weakens only after vagotomy, the vagus carries part of the signal.
Limits: this is mice only, "no detectable change" depends on how sensitive the blood assay is, and the 2019 study did not find the gut sensor. Later work in mice (Ichiki and colleagues, 2022) traced the signal to vagal nerve fibres serving the hepatic portal area, where absorbed water passes toward the liver, driven partly by the gut hormone vasoactive intestinal peptide; which cells first detect the dilution is still open.

</details>
