## Explain: what living things are made of

<WordBank terms={['organism', 'cell', 'unicellular', 'multicellular', 'cell theory', 'microscope', 'magnification', 'micrometre', 'theory']} />

An **organism** is one living thing, such as a tree, a mushroom, a dog or a bacterium. A **cell** is the smallest unit that can carry out the processes of life. Every organism is made of one or more cells.

Some organisms are just one cell. They are **unicellular**, which means made of one cell. A Paramecium in pond water is one cell.

Other organisms are **multicellular**, which means made of many cells. You are multicellular, and so is an oak tree. You will sort living things this way in [that lesson](/lessons/levels-of-organization/).

Tiny does not always mean one cell. Some animals in pond water are too small to see well, but they are made of many cells.

The **cell theory** sums up what scientists have learned about cells. It has three parts:
1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of life.
3. All cells come from cells that already exist.

Cells are not all alike. A red blood cell in your body is a small disc, pressed in on both faces. A nerve cell can have long, thin branches. Cells differ in shape and size, depending on their job and the organism they belong to.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/cells-everywhere/cells-differ-in-shape.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: three different cells side by side. A red blood cell is a disc that is thinner in the middle, shown face-on and edge-on; a nerve cell has a round body and one long thin branch; a slipper-shaped Paramecium, fringed with tiny hairs, is a whole living thing made of one cell." caption="Cells come in many shapes and sizes, depending on their job and the organism they belong to. Most cells also have smaller parts inside them, called organelles. You will meet them in [that lesson](/lessons/inside-a-cell/).

## Explain: tools for seeing small things

Your eyes have a limit. Most people cannot make out anything smaller than about 0.1 millimetre (mm) without help. Most cells are smaller than that.

So scientists use a **microscope**, a tool with lenses that makes small things look larger. **Magnification** is how many times larger the image looks than the real thing.

A **micrometre** (µm) is one thousandth of a millimetre. So 1 mm equals 1,000 µm. The eye's limit of about 0.1 mm is about 100 µm.

A school light microscope usually has an eyepiece that magnifies 10 times (10x). It also has objective lenses, often 4x, 10x and 40x. Multiply the eyepiece by the objective to get the total. That gives 40x, 100x and 400x.

<ScaffoldFigure src="/figures/cells-everywhere/school-microscope-parts.webp" width={1200} height={800} keyItems={["eyepiece", "objective lenses", "stage", "focus knob", "light source"]} given={[0, 2]} alt="AI-generated illustration: a school light microscope seen from the side, with a thin line of light running up from a lamp in the base, through a slide on the stage, to the eyepiece at the top. Five numbered circles mark parts for the student to name." caption="A school light microscope sends light up through the slide to your eye. Name the five numbered parts." />

Use it safely and well:
- Carry it with two hands, one under the base and one on the arm.
- Start with the lowest power objective and focus.
- Move what you want to see to the center, then switch to a higher power.
- At higher power you see a smaller circle of the slide. This circle is the field of view.

The best light microscopes can show details down to about 0.2 µm. Even a school microscope shows enough to see most whole cells, but not the smallest parts inside them. Later, scientists built the electron microscope, which uses a beam of electrons instead of light. It showed cell parts far too small for an ordinary light microscope.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Microscopes let us see living things far smaller than about 100 µm, the limit of the eye.</Support>

## Explain: a theory that grew with better tools

In everyday talk, a theory can mean a hunch. In science, a **theory** is a well-tested explanation backed by a great deal of evidence. A theory can still change if strong new evidence appears.

The cell theory grew in steps, often after better tools or more careful looks.
- In the 1600s, early microscopes showed tiny boxes in cork and tiny swimming things in water.
- By about 1840, scientists had looked at many plants and animals and found cells in them.
- At first, some scientists thought new cells formed out of a clear fluid, a bit like crystals. More observations, including work published in the 1850s, showed that new cells form when a cell divides. The theory dropped the wrong idea and kept the rest.

So the cell theory is not a guess. It rests on hundreds of years of observations of many kinds of organisms. You will see how one cell becomes two in [that lesson](/lessons/cells-divide/).

### Design problem

Your class wants to test this claim: living things are made of cells, and nonliving things are not. Plan an investigation with a school light microscope.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Question: Do samples from living things show cells, while nonliving samples do not?
- Independent variable: the type of sample. From living things: onion skin, a thin slice of potato, and a drop of pond water that holds living things. Nonliving: a few grains of sand, a few salt crystals and a scrap of plastic wrap.
- Dependent variable: whether you see cells, and how many cells you can count in one field of view.
- Constants: the same microscope, magnification (100x, then 400x) and light for every slide.
- Control: a slide with only a drop of tap water, so you know what an empty slide looks like.
- Repeated trials: three slides of each sample, each counted by two students.
- Measure: use a clear metric ruler at low power to find the field of view width in mm. Change it to µm. Count how many cells fit across the field, then divide the width by that number to estimate one cell's size.
- Safety: slides and cover slips are glass; tell your teacher about any broken glass. Wash your hands after touching pond water. Wear goggles if a stain is used. Do not taste anything.
- One surprise to think about: cork or paper shows cell walls, even though it is not alive now. Both came from living plants.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The pond drop looked clear because most of the living things in it are smaller than about 100 µm, the limit of your eyes. A microscope makes them look large enough to see, and careful study of such living things shows that each one is made of one or more cells. Better microscopes gave new evidence, and the cell theory grew from it.

**Science practices you used:** using a microscope safely and measuring in metric units, reading a data table and doing math with it, planning a fair test with a control, and explaining why a theory is not a guess.
