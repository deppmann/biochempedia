> **The one idea.** A microscope makes things look bigger: **picture size = real size × magnification**, where picture size is how big a thing looks. At 40x, a cell looks about as big as something 40 times wider seen by eye alone. At a normal reading distance, most people can make out something only when it is bigger than about 100 micrometres (µm), or 0.1 mm. We call that limit the **eye line**.
>
> *Scope:* passing the eye line is needed, but it is not enough. To see a cell's shape, the lens must also show the detail clearly, and the cell must stand out from its background (a stain helps). Light itself sets a limit too; see [Where light runs out](#where-light-runs-out).

## How small is too small to see?

The first film left out the lenses that do the multiplying. A school light microscope has an **eyepiece**, the lens you look into, that magnifies 10 times (10x), and **objective lenses** just above the slide, often 4x, 10x and 40x. Multiply the two to get the total **magnification**: 10 × 4 = 40x, 10 × 10 = 100x and 10 × 40 = 400x. The slide sits on the **stage**, the **light source** shines up through it, and the **focus knob** sharpens the picture.

Use it safely and well:
- Carry it with two hands, one under the base and one on the arm.
- Start with the lowest power objective and focus.
- Move what you want to see to the center, then switch to a higher power.
- At higher power you see a smaller circle of the slide. This circle is the **field of view**.

Try baker's yeast, a cell about 5 µm wide. At 40x its picture is 5 × 40 = 200 µm. It passes the size test but is only a speck; its oval shape shows at 400x.

## What the microscope showed: living things are made of cells

<WordBank terms={['organism', 'cell', 'unicellular', 'multicellular', 'cell theory', 'microscope', 'magnification', 'micrometre', 'theory']} />

An **organism** is one living thing, such as a tree, a mushroom, a dog or a bacterium. A **cell** is the smallest unit that can carry out the processes of life.

Some organisms, such as a Paramecium, are just one cell: they are **unicellular**. Others, such as you and an oak tree, are **multicellular**, made of many cells. Tiny does not always mean one cell: some animals in pond water are too small to see well, but they are made of many cells. You will sort living things this way in [that lesson](/lessons/levels-of-organization/).

Cells are not all alike: a red blood cell is a small disc, and a nerve cell can have long, thin branches.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/cells-everywhere/cells-differ-in-shape.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: three different cells side by side. A red blood cell is a disc that is thinner in the middle, shown face-on and edge-on; a nerve cell has a round body and one long thin branch; a slipper-shaped Paramecium, fringed with tiny hairs, is a whole living thing made of one cell." caption="Cells come in many shapes and sizes, depending on their job and the organism they belong to. These cells are not drawn to the same scale." />

Most cells also have smaller parts inside, called organelles; you will meet them in [that lesson](/lessons/inside-a-cell/).

The **cell theory** sums up what scientists have learned about cells in three parts:
1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of life.
3. All cells come from cells that already exist.

Learning this took about two hundred years and better tools, starting with cork boxes like the ones you just zoomed into.

## What makes it a theory

The second film followed the evidence; why call the result a theory? In everyday talk, a theory can mean a hunch. In science, a **theory** is a well-tested explanation backed by a great deal of evidence. A theory can still change if strong new evidence appears; the clear-fluid idea in the second film is one example.

You will see how one cell becomes two in [that lesson](/lessons/cells-divide/).

## Where light runs out

**Predict.** Both films stopped at the light microscope. If you could keep magnifying far past 400x, would it keep showing new, smaller parts of a cell?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not forever. Light itself blurs details that are very close together. Even the best standard light microscopes, with ordinary glass lenses, cannot show two details apart when they are closer than about 0.2 µm; past that point, more magnification gives a bigger blur, not new detail. Special super-resolution microscopes get around this limit.

</details>

So the size test is only the first check. A school microscope shows most whole cells, but not the smallest parts inside them. For those, scientists built the **electron microscope**, which uses a beam of electrons instead of light; it added new evidence about what cells are made of.

## Evaluate: check your thinking

In two sentences: why did the pond drop look clear, and how do we know the things in it are alive?

<details>
<summary>One good answer</summary>

Most living things in the drop are smaller than about 100 µm, and the few larger ones, such as a Paramecium, are nearly colourless and easy to miss. Careful study of such living things shows that each one is made of one or more cells, and better microscopes gave the evidence the cell theory grew from.

</details>

### Design problem

Compare samples from living things, which should show cells, with materials that are not made of cells. Plan an investigation with a school light microscope.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Question: Do the samples from living things show cells, while the others do not?
- Independent variable: the type of sample. From living things: onion skin, a thin slice of potato, and a drop of pond water that holds living things. Not made of cells: a few grains of clean sand, a few pure salt crystals and a scrap of plastic wrap.
- Dependent variable: whether you see cells, and how many cells you can count in one field of view.
- Constants: the same microscope, magnification (100x, then 400x) and light for every slide.
- Blank control: a clean slide with only the distilled water used on the other slides, so you can tell what comes from the slide or water.
- Repeated trials: three slides of each sample, each counted by two students.
- Measure: find the field of view width in mm with a clear metric ruler at low power, and change it to µm. Divide that width by the number of cells that fit across the field to estimate one cell's size.
- Safety: slides and cover slips are glass; tell your teacher about any broken glass. Wash your hands after touching pond water. Wear goggles if a stain is used. Do not taste anything.
- One surprise to think about: cork and most paper are not alive now, yet cork shows boxes and paper shows long fibres. Both are walls left by plant cells. Not alive now is not the same as never alive.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Where the cell sizes come from

The bacterium, yeast, blood cell and alga values come from the BioNumbers database at Harvard Medical School. Its entries draw on lab studies, a cell size database and a textbook, published between 1982 and 2009. None come from pond samples. The egg and eye values come from an OpenStax textbook (2016). The Paramecium length is the usual size of Paramecium caudatum in species descriptions. The light microscope limit comes from a University of Arizona biology tutorial (2004).

### The exact measurements

The E. coli length in the study was 1.94 to 2.72 micrometres. A second kind of yeast, fission yeast, was about 4 micrometres wide (3.74 micrometres in the study). The usual length of Paramecium caudatum ranges from 170 to 330 micrometres.

**Science practices you used:** using a microscope safely and measuring in metric units, reading a data table and doing math with it, planning a fair test with a control, and explaining why a theory is not a guess.
