Harold Copp."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Into the early 1960s, plasma calcium was explained with one hormone. PTH raised it, and a
      fall in calcium was read as a fall in PTH secretion. Harold Copp, who had come to the
      University of British Columbia in 1950 as the first head of its physiology department,
      tested that picture with Cameron, Cheney, Davidson and Henze. In fasted, anesthetized dogs
      they isolated the thyroid and parathyroid tissue so that it could be perfused through its
      own circulation. If high calcium did nothing but switch PTH off, perfusing the glands with
      high-calcium blood should let systemic calcium drift down only as fast as existing PTH and
      its effects waned. Instead, perfusion with blood containing 12 mg of calcium per 100 mL
      lowered systemic plasma calcium, and the authors argued that withdrawal of PTH could not
      explain the fall. They proposed a calcium-lowering hormone, calcitonin, and placed its
      source in the parathyroid. Later work placed it in the parafollicular C cells of the
      thyroid. The paper held a correct inference and a wrong address, and it opened calcium
      control to a second hormonal signal.

    story: >-

      response was unknown. Edward Brown's group at Brigham and Women's Hospital, working with

      linked to inherited disorders in which calcium is held at the wrong level, and the receptor
      became a target for drugs that act on the parathyroid gland. Credit belongs to the team.

    contribution: >-

    story: >-
      FGF23 entered physiology through rare human disorders in which too much FGF23 activity
      makes the kidney lose phosphate. Those disorders left a question open: does a normal animal
      use FGF23, or is it only a disease product? Shimada and colleagues tested it two ways in
      2004. In one study they injected recombinant FGF23 into normal rodents and into
      parathyroidectomized rats. Serum phosphate fell even in rats without parathyroid glands,
      and serum calcitriol fell too. An injection of calcitriol, in turn, raised serum FGF23 in
      normal mice within four hours. In the other study they deleted the Fgf23 gene in mice.
      Compared with littermates, the null mice had high serum phosphate, increased renal
      phosphate reabsorption and elevated serum calcitriol, with abnormal bone mineralization.
      Together the injection and deletion results made FGF23 a hormone the mouse kidney depends
      on, and they framed the next question, answered in 2006 by Urakawa, Shimada and colleagues,
      of which co-receptor, Klotho, lets FGF23 act on the kidney.

techniques:
  - name: "Isolated gland perfusion in an intact animal"
    blurb: >-
      Separate a gland's blood supply from the rest of the circulation in an anesthetized animal
      and perfuse it with blood of chosen composition, while sampling systemic blood. Copp's group
      used it in dogs to change the calcium reaching the thyroid-parathyroid tissue alone and to
      watch what its venous output did to the rest of the body.
  - name: "Transgenic and cell-specific knockout mice"
    blurb: >-
      Add a gene under a chosen promoter, delete it everywhere, or delete it in one cell type with
      a Cre line and a floxed allele. Overexpression of osteoprotegerin, deletion of Fgf23 and
      osteocyte-specific deletion of RANKL each tested whether one molecule or one cell source is
      needed for normal bone or mineral handling in mice.
  - name: "Ion-selective electrode measurement of ionized calcium"
    blurb: >-
      A calcium-selective membrane electrode develops a potential that depends on the free calcium
      activity of the sample, so it measures the ionized fraction directly rather than total
      calcium. The reading depends on pH and sample handling, because protein binding of calcium
      changes with pH.
  - name: "Fractional excretion from paired plasma and urine"
    blurb: >-
      Measure a solute and creatinine in the same plasma and urine samples; the ratio of the two
      clearances gives the fraction of the filtered solute that is excreted, without a timed urine
      collection. It assumes creatinine clearance approximates GFR and the solute is freely
      filtered.

    choices:
      - "The free ionized fraction, roughly half of the total"
      - "The albumin-bound fraction, about 40 percent of total"
      - "Total calcium, whichever form it happens to be carried in"
      - "Calcium complexed with citrate, phosphate and bicarbonate"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      The receptor binds free calcium ions in the extracellular fluid, and the ionized fraction,
      roughly half of the total, is the one that is sensed and regulated. The albumin-bound
      fraction is held on protein and is not free to bind a receptor; it changes with albumin and
      pH without the parathyroid reading it directly. Complexed calcium is filterable but is
      bound to small anions rather than free. Total calcium is a laboratory measurement that adds
      all three forms together, which is why total and ionized calcium can disagree.

    choices:
      - "More distal calcium reabsorption, less proximal phosphate reabsorption"
      - "More calcium and more phosphate reabsorbed in the proximal tubule"
      - "Less distal calcium reabsorption, more proximal phosphate reabsorption"
      - "Less 1-alpha-hydroxylation and more 24-hydroxylation of vitamin D"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      PTH raises active calcium reabsorption in the distal nephron and removes NaPi-IIa from the

  - stem: "In untreated primary hyperparathyroidism with normal kidney function, which pattern of plasma values is expected?"
    choices:
      - "Calcium high, phosphate high, PTH high"
      - "Calcium low, phosphate high, PTH low"
      - "Calcium high, phosphate low-normal, PTH high"
      - "Calcium high, phosphate low, PTH suppressed"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      An abnormal parathyroid source keeps secreting PTH that calcium does not suppress normally,
      so PTH is high or inappropriately normal; calcium rises through bone resorption, distal
      reabsorption and calcitriol-driven gut absorption, while phosphaturia keeps phosphate
      low-normal or low. High phosphate with high calcium assumes PTH raises both. Low calcium,

      cause, so it points away from the parathyroid.
  - stem: "In a made-up example, an adult at steady state has a GFR of 100 mL per min and a total plasma calcium of 100 mg per L, of which 50 percent is filterable. Urinary calcium is 144 mg per day. What fraction of the filtered calcium is reabsorbed?"
    choices:
      - "52 percent"
      - "99 percent"
      - "2 percent"
      - "98 percent"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Filtered load = 0.100 L per min x 1,440 min per day x 50 mg per L = 7,200 mg per day.
      Fractional excretion = 144 / 7,200 = 0.02, so 98 percent is reabsorbed. 99 percent comes
      from using total calcium (14,400 mg per day filtered), but the protein-bound half is not

      - "Bone takes up the perfusate calcium, so the fall is not hormonal at all"
      - "The glands secrete a factor that lowers plasma calcium when perfused"
      - "High-calcium perfusion withdraws PTH faster than removing the glands"
      - "Removing the glands raises calcitriol, which slows the fall in calcium"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Removing the glands stops all PTH secretion at once, so withdrawal of PTH cannot make
      calcium fall faster in the perfused group than in the group with no glands; the extra
      speed points to something the perfused glands release that actively lowers calcium. That
      was the inference Copp and colleagues drew in 1962. Claiming perfusion withdraws PTH faster
      than removal ignores that removal is the most complete withdrawal possible. Perfusate
      calcium entering the body would raise systemic calcium, not lower it. Calcitriol synthesis
      depends partly on PTH, so removing the glands would tend to lower calcitriol, not raise it.

**About 99 percent of the calcium in an adult human body is in bone, yet the parathyroid glands read only a sliver of the remaining 1 percent: the free calcium ions in plasma.** That sliver stays in a narrow range while bone is dissolved and rebuilt and the kidneys filter about ten grams of calcium a day. How?

## Bone is a living mineral bank

[Muscle, bone and movement](/lessons/muscle-and-movement/) gave the first look, including why vertebrates carry an endoskeleton and arthropods an exoskeleton. In brief: **osteoblasts** lay down matrix, **osteoclasts** resorb it, and **osteocytes**, former osteoblasts walled into the matrix, stay linked to one another by fine processes. The matrix is collagen stiffened by a calcium phosphate mineral, **hydroxyapatite**. **Compact bone** is built of **osteons**, cylinders of matrix around a central canal; **spongy bone** is a lattice of struts called **trabeculae**. Bone ends at joints are covered by **hyaline articular cartilage**, which has no blood vessels and reduces friction. **Ligaments** join bone to bone and **tendons** join muscle to bone. By structure, joints are **fibrous**, **cartilaginous** or **synovial**.

Think of bone as a vault and plasma as your wallet. The vault holds about 99 percent of body calcium and roughly 85 percent of its phosphate. **Remodeling**, osteoclasts resorbing a patch and osteoblasts refilling it, keeps traffic moving between the two.

{/* DIAGRAM: remodeling unit: osteoclast in a resorption pit, osteoblasts behind it, linked osteocytes; RANKL on osteoblast-lineage cells and osteocytes, RANK on a precursor, OPG as free decoy */}

The wallet has three pockets. In adult human plasma, roughly half of total calcium is free **ionized calcium**, about 40 percent is bound to protein, mostly albumin, and about 10 percent is complexed with citrate, phosphate and bicarbonate. Only the ionized fraction acts on cells and is sensed and regulated. In alkalosis albumin binds more calcium, so ionized calcium falls while total calcium stays the same.

Total calcium (mg per dL) and ionized calcium (by ion-selective electrode, mmol per L) are different measurements. Formulas that adjust total calcium for albumin do not reliably reproduce ionized calcium: in 22,658 Alberta adults with both measured in the same samples, albumin-adjusted calcium generally did no better than unadjusted total calcium (Desgagnés and colleagues, 2025).

## Osteoclasts are told what to do: RANKL and OPG

Osteoblast-lineage cells and osteocytes display **RANKL**, a cytokine of the tumor necrosis factor family. Its receptor, **RANK**, sits on osteoclast precursors, and engaging it drives their formation and activation. **Osteoprotegerin (OPG)** is a soluble decoy receptor: it binds RANKL and keeps it off RANK. Picture a key, a lock and a dummy lock that swallows keys. The ratio of RANKL to OPG sets how many osteoclasts form.

The pieces came from three papers, not one. Simonet and colleagues (Cell, 1997) made transgenic mice that expressed OPG from the liver; they developed profound but nonlethal **osteopetrosis**, an excess of dense bone, and OPG blocked ovariectomy-associated bone loss in rats. Lacey and colleagues (Cell, 1998) identified the ligand OPG binds as a cytokine that drives osteoclast differentiation. Hsu and colleagues (PNAS, 1999) showed, in mouse bone-marrow progenitors, that it acts through RANK. Other laboratories described the same molecules independently under other names.

Which cell's RANKL matters? When Nakashima and colleagues (Nature Medicine, 2011) deleted RANKL only in osteocytes of mice, the mice developed severe osteopetrosis.

**Parathyroid hormone (PTH)** reaches osteoclasts mainly through this system. Its receptors sit on osteoblast-lineage cells, including osteocytes, and PTH raises RANKL relative to OPG. Direct PTH effects on osteoclast-lineage cells reported in culture remain an open question in the body. Continuous PTH excess causes net bone loss even though formation also rises; intermittent exposure behaves differently and should not be lumped with it. The ovariectomized-rat result bridges to estrogen and bone in [the ovarian cycle](/lessons/ovarian-cycle-fertility/).

## Four hormones, three organs, one sensor

**The sensor.** The parathyroid glands, usually four, sit on the back of the thyroid. Their chief cells carry the **calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR)**, a G protein-coupled receptor ([G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/)) cloned from bovine parathyroid by Brown, Hebert and colleagues (Nature, 1993). A rise in ionized calcium activates CaSR and inhibits PTH secretion, steeply: a small change in calcium moves secretion a long way. CaSR in the thick ascending limb also lowers calcium reabsorption when calcium is high. ([Calcium signaling](/lessons/calcium-signaling/) covers calcium inside cells; this lesson is about the calcium outside them.)

**PTH**, a peptide, acts through a G protein-coupled receptor that raises cAMP ([cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/)). On the kidney it:

- raises active calcium reabsorption in the distal nephron;
- retrieves the sodium-phosphate cotransporter **NaPi-IIa** from the proximal brush border, so phosphate is lost in urine (Traebert and colleagues, 2000, rat kidney in vivo and isolated perfused mouse tubules);
- stimulates **1-alpha-hydroxylation** of vitamin D; in thyroparathyroidectomized rats on a low-calcium diet, production of active vitamin D appeared to depend on the parathyroid (Garabedian and colleagues, 1972).

With bone resorption added, **plasma calcium rises and plasma phosphate falls**: the phosphaturia outweighs the phosphate released from bone.

**Calcitriol.** Vitamin D is made in skin ([skin and human heat](/lessons/skin-and-human-heat/)), 25-hydroxylated in the liver ([absorption and the liver](/lessons/absorption-and-liver/)) and 1-hydroxylated in the kidney, giving **calcitriol**, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, which acts through the vitamin D receptor ([nuclear receptors](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/)). In mice lacking that receptor, a diet that restored ionized calcium prevented rickets and the rise in PTH, which points to impaired intestinal calcium absorption as the cause (Li and colleagues, 1998). In wild-type mice, two days of calcitriol raised active jejunal phosphate transport while the paracellular route was unchanged (Hernando and colleagues, 2021). In rats, calcitriol inhibits PTH gene transcription (Silver and colleagues, 1986).

**FGF23** is a hormone made by bone, mainly by osteocytes and osteoblasts. It acts on the kidney through an FGF receptor bound to the co-receptor **Klotho** (Urakawa and colleagues, 2006), lowering phosphate reabsorption and calcitriol: in rodents, injected FGF23 lowered renal mRNA for 1-alpha-hydroxylase and raised it for 24-hydroxylase, the enzyme that inactivates vitamin D metabolites. In normal mice, injected calcitriol raised serum FGF23 within four hours. Calcitriol raises FGF23 and FGF23 lowers calcitriol: a closed loop.

**Calcitonin** comes from the parafollicular **C cells** of the thyroid ([thyroid hormone](/lessons/thyroid-hormone/)). In osteoclasts isolated and cultured on glass or plastic, it stops lamellipodial motility within minutes (Chambers and Magnus, 1982), yet its role in normal adult human calcium control appears modest.

### The classic experiment: a hormone that lowers calcium

By the early 1960s, PTH alone explained calcium control: when calcium rose, PTH secretion fell. Copp, Cameron, Cheney, Davidson and Henze (Endocrinology, 1962) isolated the thyroid-parathyroid tissue of fasted, anesthetized dogs and perfused it through its own circulation. If high calcium only switched PTH off, perfusing the glands with high-calcium blood should let systemic calcium fall slowly, as existing PTH was cleared. Perfusion with blood containing 12 mg of calcium per 100 mL lowered systemic plasma calcium, and the authors argued the fall was too fast for PTH withdrawal. They inferred a calcium-lowering factor from the glands and named it calcitonin.

{/* DIAGRAM: schematic Copp preparation: dog thyroid-parathyroid region perfused with high-calcium blood, effluent returning to the circulation, systemic sampling line */}

That ruled in an active, calcium-lowering signal released in response to high calcium, in this dog preparation, and ruled out withdrawal of PTH as the only response. It did not locate the source: Copp's group placed it in the parathyroid, and later work placed it in the thyroid's C cells. Nor did it show that calcitonin matches PTH in importance in adult humans.

## The kidney sets the daily balance

GFR is in [glomerular filtration](/lessons/glomerular-filtration/) and clearance in [the kidney](/lessons/kidney/). Protein-bound calcium is not filtered; the ionized and complexed fractions, roughly 55 to 60 percent of total, are. Roughly 60 to 70 percent of filtered calcium is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule and 20 to 25 percent in the thick ascending limb, largely between cells. The distal convoluted and connecting tubules take back roughly 5 to 10 percent through cells, the step PTH regulates. Phosphate is reabsorbed mainly in the proximal tubule, the step PTH and FGF23 turn down. Why thiazides lower urinary calcium and loop diuretics raise it is in [tubular transport and diuretics](/lessons/tubular-transport-diuretics/).

### Worked problem: calcium and phosphate in a day

Made-up round numbers for a reference adult at steady state. Assume bone formation equals resorption, no sweat losses, and a fixed filterable calcium fraction of 60 percent.

> **filtered load = GFR x filterable plasma concentration; fractional excretion = excreted / filtered load**

1. *Filtered load.* GFR 125 mL per min = 0.125 L per min x 1,440 min per day = 180 L per day. Total plasma calcium 10 mg per dL = 100 mg per L, 60 percent filterable: 60 mg per L. Filtered load = 180 x 60 = 10,800 mg per day.
2. *Fractional reabsorption.* Urinary calcium is 200 mg per day. Fractional excretion = 200 / 10,800 = 0.0185 (1.85 percent); 98.15 percent is reabsorbed.
3. *Balance.* With bone in balance, net intestinal absorption equals urinary excretion, 200 mg per day. With an intake of 1,000 mg per day, fecal calcium = 1,000 - 200 = 800 mg per day.
4. *Sensitivity.* If fractional reabsorption falls from 98.15 to 97.0 percent with the same filtered load, as when PTH falls, excretion = 10,800 x 0.030 = 324 mg per day, 1.62 times the original. A fall of about 1.15 percentage points in reabsorption moves excretion by 62 percent, which is why the small, regulated distal step matters.
5. *Phosphate.* Made-up spot values from a physiology study in which plasma phosphate is below its usual range: plasma phosphate 2.0 mg per dL, urine phosphate 40 mg per dL, plasma creatinine 1.0 mg per dL, urine creatinine 100 mg per dL. Assume creatinine clearance approximates GFR, phosphate is treated as freely filtered, and both come from the same samples, so urine flow cancels.

> **FE(P) = (U_P x P_Cr) / (P_P x U_Cr)**

FE(P) = (40 x 1.0) / (2.0 x 100) = 40 / 200 = 0.20, or 20 percent; 80 percent is reabsorbed. A kidney conserving phosphate at low plasma phosphate would excrete only a small fraction of its filtered load, so a fifth points to a phosphaturic signal, PTH or FGF23. The calculation cannot tell which.

### The modern experiment: mice without FGF23

FGF23 was first tied to rare human disorders in which too much FGF23 activity makes the kidney lose phosphate. Is it needed normally? Shimada and colleagues (Journal of Clinical Investigation, 2004) deleted Fgf23 in mice. If FGF23 is a normal phosphaturic hormone, kidneys without it should keep more phosphate and make more calcitriol. Compared with littermates, the null mice had high serum phosphate, increased renal phosphate reabsorption and elevated calcitriol, with abnormal bone mineralization. In the same group's injection study, recombinant FGF23 lowered serum phosphate even in parathyroidectomized rats, so that effect did not need PTH.

That ruled in endogenous FGF23 as necessary for normal phosphate and vitamin D regulation in the mouse. A lifelong, whole-body deletion cannot separate direct renal effects from those of high calcitriol or development, and says nothing quantitative about humans.

## Predicting the four classic disorders

Start with **untreated primary hyperparathyroidism** and normal kidney function. A parathyroid source secretes PTH that ionized calcium no longer suppresses normally, so PTH is high or inappropriately normal for the calcium ([endocrine axes and testing](/lessons/endocrine-axes-and-testing/)). Bone resorption rises through RANKL. Distal calcium reabsorption rises, yet urinary calcium can still be raised, because plasma calcium and so the filtered load rise. NaPi-IIa leaves the brush border, so plasma phosphate is often low-normal or low. Calcitriol synthesis rises, and so does gut calcium absorption. Net: calcium up, phosphate down or low-normal, PTH up, calcitriol up or high-normal.

The table adds the other disorders, untreated, in adults: directions, not criteria, which stage, treatment and other disease can change.

| Setting | Calcium | Phosphate | PTH | Calcitriol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary hyperparathyroidism | High | Low-normal or low | High or inappropriately normal | High or high-normal |
| Vitamin D deficiency, early | Often normal | Often normal | High | Normal, or even raised |
| Vitamin D deficiency, later | Usually falls | Often falls | High | May fall |
| Early chronic kidney disease (CKD) | May stay normal | May stay normal | Often rising | May begin to fall |
| Advanced CKD | Normal or tending low | Usually high | High | Low |
| Hypoparathyroidism | Low | High | Low or inappropriately low | Low or low-normal |

In early vitamin D deficiency, high PTH stimulates 1-alpha-hydroxylation, so calcitriol can be normal or high while vitamin D stores are low. In CKD, the baseline analysis of 3,879 adults with stages 2 to 4 in the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort found FGF23 elevated while mean phosphate and median PTH were still normal (Isakova and colleagues, 2011), a cross-sectional finding that fits FGF23 rising first without proving the order. Later, the kidney retains phosphate, calcitriol falls and PTH rises. In hypoparathyroidism, losing PTH removes its boost to distal calcium reabsorption, its phosphaturia and its drive to calcitriol.

### Design problem

Osteoblast-lineage cells, osteocytes and other cells can all make RANKL in culture. Design an experiment in mice to find which cell's RANKL is needed for normal bone remodeling in adults, and say what each outcome would mean.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Cross mice carrying a floxed RANKL allele with Cre lines active in osteocytes and, separately, in earlier osteoblast-lineage cells, with floxed-only and Cre-only controls. Confirm specificity with RANKL mRNA and protein in sorted cells and a Cre reporter. Read out bone mass by micro-CT, osteoclast number on bone surfaces by histology, and serum markers. Use an inducible Cre switched on in adults to separate development from adult remodeling, then add a challenge that requires resorption, such as sustained PTH excess or unloading, to ask whether the source changes with the stimulus.

Osteopetrosis after osteocyte deletion, as Nakashima and colleagues found in 2011, means osteocyte RANKL is necessary in that setting, not that osteocytes are the only source at every age, site or in humans. No change after a deletion means that cell's RANKL is dispensable there, or that another source compensates.
