      Into the early 1960s, plasma calcium was explained with one hormone. PTH raised it, and a

      thyroid. The paper held a correct inference and a wrong address, and it opened calcium
      control to a second hormonal signal.

techniques:
  - name: "Isolated gland perfusion in an intact animal"
    blurb: >-
      Separate a gland's blood supply from the rest of the circulation in an anesthetized animal
      and perfuse it with blood of chosen composition, while sampling systemic blood. Copp's group
      used it in dogs to change the calcium reaching the thyroid-parathyroid tissue alone and to
      watch what its venous output did to the rest of the body.
  - name: "Transgenic and cell-specific knockout mice"
    blurb: >-
      Add a gene under a chosen promoter, delete it everywhere, or delete it in one cell type with
      a Cre line and a floxed allele. Overexpression of osteoprotegerin tested what excess OPG can
      do; deletion of Fgf23 and osteocyte-targeted deletion of RANKL tested whether that molecule
      or that cell source is needed for normal bone or mineral handling in mice.
  - name: "Ion-selective electrode measurement of ionized calcium"
    blurb: >-
      A calcium-selective membrane electrode develops a potential that depends on the free calcium
      activity of the sample, so it measures the ionized fraction directly rather than total
      calcium. The reading depends on pH and sample handling, because protein binding of calcium
      changes with pH.
  - name: "Fractional excretion from paired plasma and urine"
    blurb: >-
      Measure a solute and creatinine in the same plasma and urine samples; the ratio of the two
      clearances gives the fraction of the filtered solute that is excreted, without a timed urine
      collection. It assumes creatinine clearance approximates GFR and the solute is freely
      filtered.

    rationale: >-
      At steady state excreted phosphate equals absorbed phosphate, and excretion = GFR x
      filterable plasma phosphate x FE(P). With GFR one-third as large and plasma phosphate held
      at its old value, FE(P) must triple, from 15 to about 45 percent. FGF23 and PTH both pull
      phosphate transporters out of the proximal tubule, which fits FGF23 rising early in CKD.
      Leaving FE(P) at 15 percent would let plasma phosphate climb about threefold before the
      filtered load caught up with intake. Dropping FE(P) to 5 percent moves the wrong way: the
      same intake would pile up faster. A rise in calcitriol would raise gut phosphate absorption
      and make the problem worse, and FGF23 lowers calcitriol rather than raising it.

**About 99 percent of the calcium in an adult human body is in bone, yet the parathyroid glands read only a sliver of the remaining 1 percent: the free calcium ions in plasma.** That sliver stays in a narrow range while bone is dissolved and rebuilt. How?

The pressure on that sliver is easy to miss. In round numbers, the roughly 14 litres of extracellular fluid hold about 840 mg of filterable calcium (14 L × 60 mg per L; filterable means free or bound to small ions, not to protein), and the kidneys filter about 10,800 mg a day: the whole stock passes the filter roughly once every two hours, and almost all of it has to come back. Holding a pool that small and that fast-moving takes a loop that senses it directly.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** Plasma ionized calcium is the regulated variable. The parathyroid calcium-sensing receptor reads it and sets PTH by negative feedback: less calcium, more PTH. PTH opens three doors that raise plasma calcium, each on its own clock: kidney reabsorption (minutes), bone resorption (hours for the first release, days for new osteoclasts), and gut absorption through the calcitriol that PTH has the kidney make (a day or more). At the kidney PTH does the opposite to phosphate and throws it out, so PTH raises calcium and lowers phosphate. Phosphate has its own brake, made by bone: FGF23.

The rule holds for an adult at steady state with working kidneys; chronic kidney disease, the last case here, is where it breaks.

## What is regulated: ionized calcium

Bone holds the reserve: that 99 percent of body calcium, and roughly 85 percent of its phosphate. **Remodeling**, osteoclasts resorbing a patch and osteoblasts refilling it, takes months at each site, a few weeks to resorb and several months to refill, and sets the long-term traffic with blood. The fast exchange with plasma happens at bone surfaces.

Plasma calcium sits in three pockets. Roughly half of the total is free **ionized calcium**, about 40 percent is bound to protein, mostly albumin, and about 10 percent is complexed with citrate, phosphate and bicarbonate. Only the ionized fraction acts on cells and is sensed and regulated.

Labs report total calcium in mg per dL and ionized calcium, read by an ion-selective electrode, in mmol per L. Calcium's molar mass is about 40 g, so 1 mmol/L is about 4 mg/dL: an ionized calcium of 1.2 mmol/L is about 4.8 mg/dL, about half of a total of 9.5 mg/dL. Formulas that adjust total calcium for albumin do not reliably reproduce it: in 22,658 Alberta adults with total and ionized calcium measured in the same samples (albumin in 10,659 of them), albumin-adjusted calcium generally did no better than unadjusted total calcium (Desgagnés and colleagues, 2025).

**Predict.** In alkalosis, fewer hydrogen ions compete for albumin's binding sites, so albumin binds more calcium. What happens to total calcium, and to ionized calcium?

Nerves and muscles respond to ionized calcium, and the drop can make them overexcitable, felt as tingling in the fingers.

</details>

## One sensor, one hormone, three doors

Follow one disturbance through the loop: ionized calcium dips a little, say after a day of meals low in calcium.

**The sensor.** The parathyroid glands, usually four, sit on the back of the thyroid. Their chief cells carry the **calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR)**, a G protein-coupled receptor ([G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/)) that binds ionized calcium outside the cell. High calcium keeps the receptor active and PTH secretion down; a dip takes the brake off. The response is steep, so a small change in calcium moves PTH a long way, which keeps the range narrow. ([Calcium signaling](/lessons/calcium-signaling/) covers calcium inside cells; this lesson is about the calcium outside them.)

**The hormone.** **Parathyroid hormone (PTH)** is a peptide. It acts through a G protein-coupled receptor that raises cAMP ([cAMP and PKA](/lessons/camp-and-pka/)). It opens three doors, on three clocks.

1. **Kidney, within minutes.** PTH raises active calcium reabsorption in the distal convoluted and connecting tubules, so less calcium leaves in urine.
2. **Bone, over hours to days.** PTH's receptors sit on osteoblast-lineage cells, including osteocytes, so PTH reaches osteoclasts mainly through them (direct effects on osteoclast-lineage cells, reported in culture, are unproven in the body): they raise **RANKL** relative to its decoy, **OPG**, and more osteoclasts form (the bone door, below). Some calcium comes off bone surfaces within hours; new osteoclasts take days.
3. **Gut, over a day or more.** PTH stimulates the kidney's **1-alpha-hydroxylase**, the enzyme that makes active vitamin D, **calcitriol**. Calcitriol acts through the vitamin D receptor, a transcription factor ([nuclear receptors](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/)), so its effect waits on new proteins. Then more calcium is absorbed from food.

Calcium rises, CaSR becomes active again, and PTH falls back. That is negative feedback.

**Predict.** Bone mineral is calcium phosphate, and PTH dissolves it. So when PTH rises, which way should plasma *phosphate* go?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Down, which surprises most people. At the kidney PTH does the opposite to phosphate. It pulls the sodium-phosphate cotransporter **NaPi-IIa** out of the proximal tubule's brush border, so less filtered phosphate is reabsorbed and more is lost in urine.

Compare the sizes. Hydroxyapatite, the bone mineral, carries about 2 mg of calcium per 1 mg of phosphate (as phosphorus, the way labs report it), so releasing an extra 200 mg of calcium from bone each day adds only about 90 mg of phosphate from bone alone. The kidneys filter about 5,300 mg of phosphate a day (180 L × 33 mg per L × 90 percent filterable), and raising the fraction excreted from 15 to 20 percent throws out an extra 270 mg or so. The kidney wins: the PTH signature is **calcium up, phosphate down**. It holds because PTH's effect on excretion is large, and only while the kidneys can excrete (chronic kidney disease, at the end, is the test).

</details>

**Calcitriol has two jobs.** Vitamin D is made in skin ([skin and human heat](/lessons/skin-and-human-heat/)), 25-hydroxylated in the liver ([absorption and the liver](/lessons/absorption-and-liver/)) and 1-hydroxylated in the kidney, giving calcitriol, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D. First, it is PTH's slow arm: it raises calcium absorption in the gut, and active phosphate absorption too. Second, it suppresses PTH gene transcription, so a hormone PTH makes turns PTH down.

**Calcitonin**, from the thyroid's parafollicular **C cells** ([thyroid hormone](/lessons/thyroid-hormone/)), stops the crawling of isolated osteoclasts on glass or plastic within minutes (Chambers and Magnus, 1982), yet its role in normal adult human calcium control appears modest.

## The kidney door, in numbers

*The film at the top of the page is this section's worked example: why a small, regulated step sets the day's calcium loss.*

GFR is in [glomerular filtration](/lessons/glomerular-filtration/) and clearance in [the kidney](/lessons/kidney/). Protein-bound calcium is not filtered; the ionized and complexed fractions, roughly 55 to 60 percent of total, are. Of the filtered calcium, roughly 60 to 70 percent is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule and 20 to 25 percent in the thick ascending limb, largely between cells. The distal convoluted and connecting tubules take back roughly 5 to 10 percent through cells, the step where PTH does most of its fine-tuning. Both PTH and CaSR also act upstream: CaSR in the thick ascending limb lowers calcium reabsorption when calcium is high. Phosphate is reabsorbed mainly in the proximal tubule, the step PTH and FGF23 turn down. Why thiazides lower urinary calcium and loop diuretics raise it is in [tubular transport and diuretics](/lessons/tubular-transport-diuretics/).

The distal step handles no more than a tenth of the filtered load (5 to 10 percent), so it sounds minor. But 5 to 10 percent of about 10,800 mg a day is 540 to 1,080 mg, about 2.7 to 5.4 times the day's urinary calcium of about 200 mg. The regulated step is small next to what is filtered and large next to what is lost.

## The bone door: RANKL and OPG

Three cells matter here ([muscle, bone and movement](/lessons/muscle-and-movement/) has more). **Osteoblasts** lay down matrix, **osteoclasts** resorb it, and **osteocytes**, former osteoblasts walled into the matrix, stay linked to one another by fine processes. The matrix is collagen stiffened by a calcium phosphate mineral, **hydroxyapatite**.
Osteoblast-lineage cells and osteocytes display **RANKL**, a cytokine of the tumor necrosis factor family. Its receptor, **RANK**, sits on osteoclast precursors, and engaging it drives their formation and activation. **Osteoprotegerin (OPG)** is a soluble decoy: it binds RANKL and keeps it off RANK. Picture a key, a lock and a dummy lock that swallows keys. RANKL relative to OPG is a main control on how many osteoclasts form; precursor supply, M-CSF and other signals also matter.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/calcium-phosphate-bone/rankl-opg-bone-door.webp" width={1200} height={806} alt="AI-generated illustration: Schematic bone surface. An osteoblast-lineage lining cell on the surface displays RANKL, drawn as keys, and an osteocyte walled into the matrix reaches up to it with fine processes. RANK on an osteoclast precursor is drawn as a lock. OPG, drawn as a dummy lock, swallows keys. PTH acts on the osteoblast-lineage cell. Where a key reaches its lock an osteoclast forms and resorbs bone, releasing calcium into a capillary. OPG is a decoy that swallows the key, so mice making extra OPG resorb too little bone and develop osteopetrosis, an excess of dense bone." />

The decoy's power showed first in mice. Simonet and colleagues (Cell, 1997) made transgenic mice that expressed OPG from the liver; they developed profound but nonlethal **osteopetrosis**, an excess of dense bone, because with the keys swallowed too little bone was resorbed. Which cell supplies the keys? Two studies that deleted RANKL in mouse osteocytes, each with a different genetic tool, got severe osteopetrosis and fewer osteoclasts. So in mice osteocytes are a major source of the RANKL that remodeling needs (the tools' limits are in Going deeper).

This is the door PTH uses (step 2 above). Continuous PTH excess causes net bone loss even though formation also rises; intermittent exposure behaves differently and should not be lumped with it.

## Phosphate's own brake: FGF23

PTH throws phosphate out, but PTH is set by calcium, and calcitriol brings phosphate in with the calcium. Phosphate needs its own control, and bone provides it. Osteocytes and osteoblasts make **FGF23**, which acts on the kidney through an FGF receptor paired with the co-receptor **Klotho**. It does two things there:

- it lowers phosphate reabsorption in the proximal tubule, as PTH does;
- it lowers calcitriol, the opposite of PTH.

The second effect makes sense for a phosphate hormone: calcitriol raises gut phosphate absorption, so lowering calcitriol cuts phosphate intake at the source. Calcitriol, in turn, raises FGF23. The two form a second, smaller loop that runs on phosphate. FGF23 answers mainly to phosphate and calcitriol, not to the calcium-sensing receptor, so it is a phosphate hormone rather than an arm of the calcium response. Calcium still tugs on it: in rodents low calcium holds FGF23 down, which would spare calcitriol and so fits keeping the gut door open.

### The modern experiment: mice without FGF23

FGF23 was first tied to rare disorders in which too much FGF23 activity makes the kidney lose phosphate. Is it needed normally? Shimada and colleagues (Journal of Clinical Investigation, 2004) deleted Fgf23 in mice.

**Predict.** If FGF23 is a normal phosphate brake, what should happen to serum phosphate and calcitriol in mice that lack it?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Both should rise. Compared with littermates, the null mice had high serum phosphate, increased renal phosphate reabsorption and elevated calcitriol, with abnormal bone mineralization. In the same group's injection study, recombinant FGF23 lowered serum phosphate even in parathyroidectomized rats, so that effect did not need PTH.

</details>

So endogenous FGF23 is necessary for normal phosphate and vitamin D handling in the mouse. A lifelong, whole-body deletion cannot separate direct renal effects from those of high calcitriol or development, and says nothing quantitative about humans.

The three hormones side by side, in an adult with working kidneys:

| Hormone | Made by | Plasma calcium | Plasma phosphate | Calcitriol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PTH | Parathyroid chief cells | Up | Down | Up |
| Calcitriol | Kidney (from vitamin D) | Up | Up | (it is calcitriol) |
| FGF23 | Osteocytes, osteoblasts | Little direct effect | Down | Down |

FGF23's effect on calcium is small rather than absent: by lowering calcitriol it slowly reduces gut calcium absorption, and in mice it also raises calcium reabsorption through TRPV5 in the distal tubule (Andrukhova and colleagues, 2014).

## Predicting the disorders from the loop

The rule once more, in short: ionized calcium sets PTH; PTH brings calcium in through kidney, bone and gut and throws phosphate out; FGF23 brakes phosphate. Each setting below (untreated, in adults) is that loop with one part broken. Name the broken part and the arrows follow.

**Primary hyperparathyroidism: PTH stuck high**, with normal kidney function. A parathyroid source secretes PTH that ionized calcium no longer suppresses normally, so PTH is high or inappropriately normal, that is, normal when high calcium should have suppressed it ([endocrine axes and testing](/lessons/endocrine-axes-and-testing/)). All three doors open: bone resorption rises through RANKL, distal calcium reabsorption rises, and calcitriol rises with gut absorption behind it. Urinary calcium can still be raised, because plasma calcium and so the filtered load rise. NaPi-IIa leaves the brush border, so phosphate is often low-normal or low.

**Hypoparathyroidism: PTH missing.**

**Predict.** Calcium, phosphate, PTH and calcitriol: which way does each go?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Calcium low, phosphate high, PTH low or inappropriately low, calcitriol low or low-normal. Losing PTH removes its boost to distal calcium reabsorption, its phosphaturia and its drive to calcitriol: all three doors close, and the kidney keeps phosphate it would have thrown out.

</details>

**Vitamin D deficiency: the gut door short of raw material.** Why might calcitriol be normal early on, with stores low? The loop compensates: PTH rises, and high PTH stimulates 1-alpha-hydroxylation, so calcitriol can be normal or even high. The same PTH throws out phosphate, so phosphate is often normal and can be low. Later calcitriol may fall, and calcium usually, and phosphate often, fall with it.

**Chronic kidney disease (CKD): the kidney door fails.** At steady state the kidneys must excrete the phosphate the gut absorbs, say 800 mg a day, and excretion is GFR × filterable plasma phosphate (about 90 percent of the total) × FE(P). Turned around, plasma phosphate = intake ÷ (GFR × 0.9 × FE(P)); at 180 L per day and 15 percent, 800 ÷ (180 × 0.9 × 0.15) = 33 mg per L, about 3.3 mg/dL. Three cases in round numbers:

- GFR 180 L per day, FE(P) 15 percent: plasma phosphate of about 3.3 mg/dL does it.
- GFR 60 L per day: at the same FE(P), plasma phosphate must reach about 9.9 mg/dL; if FGF23 and PTH push FE(P) to 50 percent, about 3.0 mg/dL is enough.
- GFR 15 L per day: even at FE(P) 50 percent, plasma phosphate must reach about 11.9 mg/dL.

The amount excreted is 800 mg a day in every case; what has to rise is the fraction. So moderate CKD keeps phosphate normal largely by raising FE(P). A large cross-sectional cohort found FGF23 elevated while phosphate and PTH were still normal (Isakova and colleagues, 2011), which fits FGF23 rising first without proving the order. Later, the kidney retains phosphate, calcitriol falls and PTH rises. In advanced CKD even the largest possible rise cannot keep phosphate normal: FE(P) stops at 100 percent, and at a GFR of 15 L per day excreting every filtered milligram still leaves plasma phosphate near 5.9 mg/dL. Phosphate settles high and climbs from there despite high PTH: PTH's signature fails where the rule said it would.

| Setting | Calcium | Phosphate | PTH | Calcitriol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary hyperparathyroidism | High | Low-normal or low | High or inappropriately normal | High or high-normal |
| Vitamin D deficiency, early | Often normal | Often normal, can be low (PTH phosphaturia) | High | Normal, or even raised |
| Vitamin D deficiency, later | Usually falls | Often falls | High | May fall |
| Early chronic kidney disease (CKD) | May stay normal | May stay normal | Often rising | May begin to fall |

### Design problem

PTH raises RANKL relative to OPG, and in mice osteocytes are a major source of RANKL. Design an experiment in adult mice to test whether PTH needs osteocyte RANKL to raise plasma calcium, and say what each outcome would mean.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Delete a floxed RANKL allele with an inducible osteocyte Cre, switched on in adults so bone develops normally, with floxed-only and Cre-only controls on the same inducer. Confirm the deletion in sorted cells. Then give continuous PTH to every group and read plasma ionized calcium over hours and days, osteoclast number on bone surfaces, and urine calcium and calcitriol, since the kidney and gut doors are still open.

If calcium rises in the first hours in both groups but the deleted mice add no osteoclasts and fall behind over days, the slow part of the bone door needs osteocyte RANKL and the early release does not. If calcium rises with no new osteoclasts, the kidney and gut doors may be carrying the load. If everything rises normally, osteocyte RANKL is dispensable here or another source compensates. Any result is about mice in this setting.

</details>

## Going deeper

The main line above does not depend on these details.

### Bone as a tissue, and the joints

**Compact bone** is built of **osteons**, cylinders of matrix around a central canal; **spongy bone** is a lattice of struts called **trabeculae**. Bone ends at most synovial joints are covered by **hyaline articular cartilage**, which has no blood vessels and reduces friction. **Ligaments** join bone to bone and **tendons** join muscle to bone. By structure, joints are **fibrous**, **cartilaginous** or **synovial**.

### How large PTH's phosphate effect must be

PTH lowers phosphate because its effect on excretion is large, not automatically. Take the steady-state relation used in the CKD case, plasma phosphate = input ÷ (GFR × filterable fraction × FE(P)), with 180 L per day, 90 percent filterable, 800 mg per day of input and FE(P) 15 percent: about 3.3 mg/dL. If PTH adds 200 mg per day of input (bone plus gut) and raises FE(P) to 25 percent, phosphate falls to about 2.5 mg/dL. At 18 percent it would not fall (about 3.4 mg/dL). Break-even here is about 19 percent.

### How RANKL and OPG were found

The pieces came from three papers, not one. Simonet and colleagues' 1997 OPG mice (above) came with a second result: OPG blocked ovariectomy-associated bone loss in rats. Lacey and colleagues (Cell, 1998) identified the ligand OPG binds as a cytokine that drives osteoclast differentiation. Hsu and colleagues (PNAS, 1999) showed, in mouse bone-marrow progenitors, that it acts through RANK. Other laboratories described the same molecules independently under other names. The ovariectomized-rat result bridges to estrogen and bone in [the ovarian cycle](/lessons/ovarian-cycle-fertility/).

### Which cell's RANKL: the genetic tools

Nakashima and colleagues (Nature Medicine, 2011) deleted RANKL with a Dmp1-Cre transgene aimed at osteocytes, and the mice developed severe osteopetrosis. That Cre also acts in some osteoblasts and lining cells, so Xiong and colleagues (PLoS ONE, 2015) repeated it with a Sost-Cre that spares those cells, though it is also active in some marrow hematopoietic cells, and saw a threefold fall in osteoclast number in cancellous bone.

Either result carries a limit worth keeping in mind: osteopetrosis after an osteocyte deletion means osteocyte RANKL is necessary in that setting, not that osteocytes are the only source at every age, site or in humans.

### The experiments behind the kidney and gut steps

- The calcium-sensing receptor was cloned from bovine parathyroid by Brown, Hebert and colleagues (Nature, 1993).
- PTH's retrieval of NaPi-IIa from the proximal brush border was shown in rat kidney in vivo and in isolated perfused mouse tubules (Traebert and colleagues, 2000).
- In thyroparathyroidectomized rats on a low-calcium diet, production of active vitamin D appeared to depend on the parathyroid (Garabedian and colleagues, 1972).
- In mice lacking the vitamin D receptor, a high-calcium, high-phosphate rescue diet with lactose, which restored ionized calcium, prevented rickets and the rise in PTH. That points to impaired intestinal calcium absorption as the cause (Li and colleagues, 1998).
- In wild-type mice, two days of calcitriol raised active jejunal phosphate transport while the paracellular route was unchanged (Hernando and colleagues, 2021).
- In rats, calcitriol inhibits PTH gene transcription (Silver and colleagues, 1986).

### FGF23 in more detail

Klotho was identified as the co-receptor that lets FGF23 act on the kidney by Urakawa and colleagues (2006). In rodents, injected FGF23 lowered renal mRNA for 1-alpha-hydroxylase and raised it for 24-hydroxylase, the enzyme that inactivates vitamin D metabolites. In normal mice, injected calcitriol raised serum FGF23 within four hours. Calcium matters too: calcium-deficient rats had lower circulating FGF23 (Rodriguez-Ortiz and colleagues, 2012), and in mice low calcium blunted the rise in FGF23 that calcitriol and PTH otherwise cause (David and colleagues, 2013).

The cohort cited for FGF23 rising first is the baseline analysis of 3,879 adults in the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort, nearly all in CKD stages 2 to 4; "still normal" there means mean phosphate and median PTH.

### Calcitonin: a hormone that lowers calcium

Calcitonin's discovery came from dogs. By the early 1960s PTH alone explained calcium control: when calcium rose, PTH secretion fell. Copp, Cameron, Cheney, Davidson and Henze (Endocrinology, 1962) isolated the thyroid-parathyroid tissue of fasted, anesthetized dogs and perfused it through its own circulation. If high calcium only switched PTH off, systemic calcium should fall no faster than existing PTH and its effects waned. Perfusion with blood containing 12 mg of calcium per 100 mL did lower systemic plasma calcium. The group compared it with removing the glands, the most complete PTH withdrawal possible, and calcium fell faster after high-calcium perfusion, so the authors argued the fall could not be PTH withdrawal alone. They inferred a calcium-lowering factor and named it calcitonin.

{/* DIAGRAM: schematic Copp preparation: dog thyroid-parathyroid region perfused */}

That ruled in an active, calcium-lowering signal in this dog preparation and ruled out PTH withdrawal as the only response. It did not locate the source: Copp's group placed it in the parathyroid, and later work in the thyroid's C cells.
