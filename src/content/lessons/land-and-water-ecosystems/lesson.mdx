## Explain: the living and nonliving parts of an ecosystem

<WordBank terms={['ecosystem', 'biotic factor', 'abiotic factor', 'climate', 'terrestrial ecosystem', 'freshwater ecosystem', 'marine ecosystem', 'estuary', 'salinity']} />

An **ecosystem** is all the living things in a place, together with their nonliving surroundings.

A **biotic factor** is a living part of an ecosystem. Biotic factors include plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms, and the ways they interact. A deer eating acorns is biotic. You saw in [Food Webs and Energy](/lessons/food-webs-and-energy/) that dead parts and wastes of living things are often counted as biotic too.

An **abiotic factor** is a nonliving part of an ecosystem. The main ones are:
- climate
- sunlight
- water
- soil
- air
- nutrients

Soil is mixed. Its sand, clay and minerals are abiotic. The worms, fungi and bacteria living in it are biotic.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/land-and-water-ecosystems/biotic-and-abiotic-in-a-forest.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a shaded oak forest with sun rays blocked by the canopy, rain falling and a deer eating acorns, with labels marking each as living or nonliving, and a soil zoom showing sand, clay and minerals beside an earthworm, fungal threads and bacteria." caption="Every ecosystem has living and nonliving parts. Soil holds both: its sand and minerals are nonliving, and the worms, fungi and bacteria in it are alive." />

Abiotic factors decide which organisms can survive in a place. Few plants can grow on a forest floor that gets little sunlight. Water and floating mud block light, so few plants grow on the floor of deep or muddy water.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Every ecosystem has living (biotic) and nonliving (abiotic) parts, and the nonliving parts help decide who can live there.</Support>

## Explain: what shapes climate

**Climate** is the usual pattern of temperature and rainfall in a place over many years. Earth’s axis is tilted, about 23.4 degrees. As Earth travels around the sun each year, the tilt changes the angle of the sunlight and the length of the day. That is the main reason Virginia has hot summers and cold winters. It is not because Earth moves closer to the sun.

Temperature and precipitation (rain and snow) are the two main parts of climate. These things shape them:
- Latitude. Places farther from the equator usually get less direct sunlight and are colder.
- Elevation. The air usually gets cooler as you climb. A mountaintop in western Virginia is often cooler than the coast on the same day.
- Topography, the shape of the land. Mountains can make one side wet and the far side dry.
- Human actions. Burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide to the air, and it traps heat.

Climate, population sizes and the kinds of species all change over time, so ecosystems change too. You will see how living things fit their places in Adaptations for Survival (a later lesson).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Earth’s tilt, along with latitude, elevation, the shape of the land and people, shapes the climate that an ecosystem lives with.</Support>

## Explain: land, fresh water and salt water

Scientists often sort ecosystems into three broad groups. A forest is terrestrial and a mountain stream is freshwater. The Bay sits between the last two:
- A **terrestrial ecosystem** is on land, such as a forest or a field.
- A **freshwater ecosystem** has water with very little salt, such as a mountain stream, a lake or the upper James.
- A **marine ecosystem** has salty ocean water.

**Salinity** is the amount of salt dissolved in water. An **estuary** is a place where fresh river water mixes with salty ocean water. The Chesapeake Bay is an estuary. Its salinity is in between, and it shifts with rain and season.

| Abiotic factor | Terrestrial (Virginia forest) | Freshwater (mountain stream) | Estuary (Chesapeake Bay) | Marine (open ocean) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Salt | Little in most soils | Very little, under about 0.5 parts per thousand | In between; changes with rain | High, about 35 parts per thousand |
| Sunlight | Blocked by tree leaves | Reaches the bottom of shallow, clear water | Fades fast in muddy water | Fades with depth |
| Temperature | Changes a lot by season | Cool, often shaded | Changes by season | Changes less |
| Water movement | Rain and runoff | Flows one way, downhill | Tides and river flow mix | Waves and currents |
| Soil | Main source of nutrients | Rocks and gravel on the bottom | Mud on the bottom | Sand and mud far below |

### Design problem

Brine shrimp are tiny animals sold as dried eggs for fish food. Plan a fair test of how salinity affects how many brine shrimp eggs hatch.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Question: How does the amount of salt in water affect how many brine shrimp eggs hatch?
- Independent variable: salt per liter of water: 0, 10, 20, 30 and 40 grams.
- Dependent variable: number of hatched shrimp counted in a 1-milliliter sample after 48 hours.
- Constants: the same amount of the same bottled water, same kind of salt, the same small scoop of eggs, same temperature, same light and same cup size.
- Control: the cup with no salt shows what happens in fresh water.
- Trials: three cups at each salt level. Find the mean for each level.
- Safety: wear goggles, wash your hands, use eggs from a store, and do not pour shrimp into a stream or pond.

If hatching changes a lot from one salt level to another, salinity affects this one stage of the brine shrimp life cycle. In the wild, brine shrimp live in salt lakes that are much saltier than seawater. A cup is a model, not a real salt lake.

</details>
