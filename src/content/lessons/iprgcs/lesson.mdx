  A mouse with no working rods or cones still sets its body clock and narrows its pupils. The
  cells responsible are a small set of retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) that make their own slow,
  sustained light response with melanopsin and also relay the fast rod and cone signal. One idea
  carries the lesson: each ipRGC pools those two light signals into one output, and in mice that
  output is the main route by which light reaches the clock and the pupil. You see the signature

  test the idea by comparing mice that lack the pigment, the rods and cones, or the cells
  themselves. Then come separate clock and pupil outputs, the M1 to M6 subtypes, mouse
  experiments on mood and learning, two blind human participants and a smaller extension, coarse
  pattern and contrast vision. Going deeper holds the discovery dates, the channel dispute and a
  photocurrent recording.

    story: >-
      Berson and his co-authors Felice Dunn and Motoharu Takao asked whether the ganglion cells that
      project to the brain's circadian clock were only passing on signals from rods and cones. In the
      isolated, flat-mounted retina of adult rats, they labelled ganglion cells from the
      suprachiasmatic nucleus and recorded from them while blocking synaptic transmission from the
      photoreceptors. The labelled cells still depolarized to light. The action spectrum of the
      response was consistent with a pigment that peaks near 484 nm. In the same 2002 volume of
      Science, Hattar, Liao, Takao, Berson and Yau reported that all 18 of the rat cells they recorded
      as intrinsically photosensitive carried melanopsin, and that mouse axons marked through the
      melanopsin locus reached the suprachiasmatic nucleus. Together the two papers tied a light
      response, a pigment and a brain target to one set of ganglion cells. That made it possible to ask,
      cell type by cell type, what each is for.

      Hattar's papers can be read as a series of questions about where ipRGC signals go and what they

techniques:
  - name: "In situ hybridization"
    blurb: >-
      A labelled nucleic-acid probe binds a messenger RNA in a tissue section, showing which cells
      transcribe the gene. For melanopsin it located the transcripts in inner retina, but transcript
      location alone says nothing about whether those cells respond to light.
  - name: "Patch clamp with synaptic blockade"
    blurb: >-
      Whole-cell recording from a labelled ganglion cell in isolated retina, with drugs applied that
      stop transmission from the photoreceptors. A light response that survives a verified block points to an
      intrinsic response; Berson and colleagues also recorded from a cell body removed from the
      retina, a stronger test. It shows where the response is made, not which protein makes it.
  - name: "Cre-based reporters and genetic ablation"
    blurb: >-
      A Cre driver line expresses a recombinase in cells that use a chosen gene, so that a reporter
      labels them or a gene is deleted. With a permanent reporter, the label marks cells that expressed the driver at any time, including during development (an inducible driver narrows this to the induction window),
      and an ablation shows necessity only for the assay tested.
  - name: "Human pupillometry and psychophysics"
    blurb: >-
      The pupil's constriction is measured at different wavelengths and irradiances to build an action
      spectrum, and a forced-choice test asks whether a person can detect a light. In blind
      participants these separate an inner-retinal contribution from rod and cone signalling.

**A mouse with no working rods or cones still sets its body clock to the day and still narrows its pupils in bright light. Which cells are measuring the light, and what pigment do they use?**

Those mice were real. In 1999 two papers in *Science* studied mice that lack both rods and cones, each with a different mouse model. Freedman and colleagues found that light still shifted the timing of the mice's daily running, and Lucas and colleagues found that light still suppressed melatonin from their pineal gland. Work on the pupil followed in 2001. Something in the eye other than rods and cones was still measuring light.

Responses like these use light without forming an image, so they are called **non-image-forming**. The two best established are setting the clock and narrowing the pupil.

- **The clock.** The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) is the brain's master clock. Light reaching the SCN shifts the clock so that it stays aligned with day and night. This is **photoentrainment**. To watch it in a mouse, watch its running wheel. A mouse is nocturnal, so it runs in the dark. A running record has one line per day. Stacked, the days show whether running starts at a fixed time relative to lights-off, as it does when the clock is locked to the light cycle.
- **The pupil.** The olivary pretectal nucleus (OPN) drives the pupil light reflex.

In those mice, the light for both turned out to be measured by a small set of retinal ganglion cells. Ganglion cells are the retina's output neurons; their axons carry its signals to the brain. An **intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cell (ipRGC)** is a ganglion cell that makes its own light response with the pigment melanopsin and also relays signals that rods and cones send through bipolar cells. The core lesson on [sleep and rhythms](/lessons/sleep-and-rhythms/) introduced melanopsin as the light input to the clock, and [the retina](/lessons/retina/) described the photoreceptors without mentioning these cells at all.

Three questions come first: what the cells' own light response looks like, how light becomes a voltage inside them, and how we know that the clock and the pupil depend on them. Then come their separate outputs, their subtypes, mood and learning in mice, what people show and, more modestly, pattern vision.

## A light response that survives the block

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

The first step was to show that the response is made inside the ganglion cell, not handed on by rods and cones. Block synaptic transmission from the photoreceptors with drugs. If a ganglion cell still responds to light once that block is verified, the response is its own.

In 2002 Berson, Dunn and Takao did this in isolated, flat-mounted adult rat retina. They labelled ganglion cells from the SCN, so they knew which cells project to the clock, and recorded from those cells while the blockers were applied.

**Predict.** Before the block, a labelled cell responds to light through rods, cones and bipolar cells. If that relay were the cell's only source of light information, what would the recording show once synaptic transmission is blocked?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No light-evoked change would remain, though baseline activity and noise could. But one remained: the labelled cells still depolarized to light, so the response was **intrinsic**, made within the ganglion cell. Berson and colleagues also recorded from a cell body removed from the retina, a stronger test, because a cell on its own has no synapses left to block.

</details>

### The signature response

So one cell can give two kinds of light response. Picture both on one time axis, with a steady light switched on and, later, off.

| | Relayed from rods and cones | The cell's own melanopsin response |
|---|---|---|
| Start | fast | slow to start |
| Sensitivity | high: works in dim light | low: needs brighter light |
| In steady light | can have brief and strongly sustained parts, depending on the circuit and the light | often sustained, though it adapts too; depends on subtype and light |
| After the light goes off | falls back quickly | decays slowly, so the cell stays depolarized for a while |

These are typical differences under the same light, not absolute rules. The melanopsin column, above all its slow decay, is the signature of these cells. In people the same cells drive the **post-illumination pupil response**, in which the pupil stays small for a while after a light goes off; it is being studied as a clinical test.

Why would a slow, insensitive, sustained response be useful? The clock and the pupil need to know how bright it is, and how bright it has stayed, not where the edges are. A camera must follow fast changes; a light meter needs a steady reading. In that sense the ipRGCs that serve the clock and the pupil, mostly a subtype called M1, are the retina's light meter: they report how bright the world is, not what is in it.

The action spectrum of the rat response, its sensitivity plotted against wavelength, was consistent with a pigment peaking near 484 nm. In the same volume of *Science*, Hattar and colleagues reported that the rat cells they recorded as intrinsically photosensitive carried melanopsin, and that melanopsin-marked mouse axons reached the SCN. A response, a pigment and a target now pointed to one set of cells.

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** An ipRGC pools two light signals into one output. One is the usually faster, more sensitive signal that rods and cones send through bipolar cells. The other is its own typically slower, less sensitive melanopsin response. Both can be sustained. In mice these cells are the main route by which either signal reaches the clock and the pupil. So removing the pigment, or the rods and cones, leaves the other input working alone, and removing the cell removes both.

The idea covers the mouse clock and pupil and, later, the mouse mood and learning routes. It does not describe the image-forming roles of some subtypes, and it says nothing about necessity in people, where no one can remove the cells.

## Two inputs, one exit: how light becomes a voltage

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Start with the wiring. Rods and cones signal to bipolar cells, and bipolar cells signal to the ipRGC's dendrites. Melanopsin sits in the ipRGC's own membrane. So light reaches the cell by two routes, but each cell has one axon, so both signals leave by one exit. Across the population those axons reach the SCN and the OPN.

Now the polarity, which is opposite to a rod's. Light closes cation channels in a rod, through the cGMP cascade in [senses and cGMP](/lessons/senses-and-cgmp/), so less positive charge enters and the rod hyperpolarizes. Light opens channels in an M1 cell and depolarizes it.

Melanopsin is an opsin, but it is closer to the visual opsins of insects and many other invertebrates than to rod and cone opsins. In mouse M1 cells the pathway is melanopsin, a G protein, probably of the Gq family (inferred; Xue and colleagues did not identify the G-alpha), phospholipase C beta 4 (PLCB4) and the opening of **TRPC6 and TRPC7** channels, the signalling pattern covered in [G proteins](/lessons/gpcrs-and-g-proteins/) and [PLC, IP3 and DAG](/lessons/plc-ip3-dag-pkc/). A fly photoreceptor works much the same way, with Gq, PLC and TRP channels. In Xue and colleagues (2011), loss of *Plcb4* essentially eliminated the intrinsic light response of M1 cells, and M1 cells lacking both TRPC6 and TRPC7 kept almost none of it under the conditions tested.

**Predict.** TRPC channels are nonselective cation channels, and their current reverses near 0 mV. The cell sits near rest, where potassium dominates. Recall the balance-point rule from [the action potential](/lessons/action-potential/): when the voltage is steady and no current is injected, it sits at the average of the reversal potentials, weighted by each conductance. Which way does opening TRPC channels move the voltage?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Opening channels gives more weight to their reversal potential, so the balance point moves up from near E_K toward 0 mV, and the cell depolarizes. With illustrative values in a passive model (not ipRGC measurements), a potassium conductance of 1 and a cation conductance of 0.2, with E_K at −90 mV, give a balance point of (1 × −90 + 0.2 × 0) / 1.2 = −75 mV. Raise the cation conductance to 1 and it moves to (1 × −90 + 1 × 0) / 2 = −45 mV.

</details>

That pathway does not describe every subtype. In M4 cells, studies report that melanopsin closes potassium leak channels as well as opening TRPC channels; closing potassium channels in a cell sitting above E_K also depolarizes it, by the same rule. Whether HCN channels contribute is disputed (Going deeper).

## Remove the pigment, or remove the cell

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[2]} />

The 2002 papers did not show what the pigment, or the cells, are needed for. For that, you take something away and watch what fails, and the one idea gives three things to remove: the pigment, the rods and cones, or the cell itself.

First, the readouts. For the **clock**, a mouse is kept on a light-dark cycle to see whether it **entrains**, keeping a fixed timing relative to the cycle, or **free-runs**, drifting on its own internal period. Separately, a brief light pulse at night tests how far the rhythm **phase-shifts**. A third readout is **masking**: bright light at night commonly suppresses a nocturnal mouse's running acutely, independently of any clock shift. The same light may also shift the clock, so the two are measured separately, and both depend on the stimulus and the time of night. Because masking can make running look locked to the cycle, releasing the mouse into constant darkness shows where the clock itself was set. For the **pupil**, constriction is filmed over seconds to minutes of steady light.

Here are four kinds of mouse in which the cells are intact (Panda and colleagues, 2002; Hattar and colleagues, 2003; pupil phenotypes reviewed by Do, 2019):

| Mouse | Rod and cone relay | Melanopsin | Clock | Pupil in steady bright light |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wild type | yes | yes | entrains; full phase shifts | fast, full and held |
| No melanopsin | yes | no | entrains; smaller phase shifts to brief pulses | constricts, but the constriction fades over minutes |
| No rod or cone signalling | no | yes | entrains | slower and less sensitive, but sustained, and reaches its normal maximum in bright light |
| **Triple mutant**: no melanopsin (*Opn4*), no rod transducin (*Gnat1*), no cone channel subunit CNGA3 (*Cnga3*) | no | no | does not entrain; free-runs; no masking | no significant pupil reflex |

Read down the table with the two inputs in mind. Either input alone is enough for the clock to entrain and for the pupil to constrict. Each still leaves its own mark. The rod and cone signals make constriction quick and sensitive, especially in dim light and at the start of a light, so without them the pupil is slow and needs brighter light. Melanopsin holds constriction steady over minutes in bright light, so without it the constriction fades, and the pupil reflex is only partly impaired. The melanopsin knockout's phase shifts to brief pulses are reduced, by an amount that differs between studies and irradiances, so do not memorize one size (Going deeper). Lose both inputs, and nothing is left.

**Predict.** Now keep rods and cones working, but kill the ipRGCs themselves. If rods and cones had a strong route of their own to the clock and the pupil, what would you see? If the ipRGCs are the principal route, what would you see?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

With a strong separate route, the ablated mice would keep much of their clock and pupil responses, as the melanopsin knockouts do. If the ipRGCs are the principal route, ablation would cost more than losing melanopsin alone and approach the triple mutants, even though rods and cones still work. Güler and colleagues (2008) found the second: pupil-reflex and entrainment deficits larger than those of melanopsin knockouts and similar to those of the triple mutants. Pattern vision, which runs through other ganglion cells, remained. So in mice the ipRGCs are the main route by which rod, cone and melanopsin signals reach the clock and the pupil.

</details>

Three details qualify this ablation. An attenuated toxin gene placed at the melanopsin locus kills the cells progressively and incompletely, with more lost as the mice age, and some pupil response remained, so a weak bypass cannot be excluded. The most strongly affected mice carried the toxin gene in both copies of the melanopsin gene, so they had no intact melanopsin gene; that is why melanopsin knockouts are the right comparison. And losing cells during development can let targets rewire, so when a manipulation can be timed, an adult ablation is better (Going deeper).

Two lessons come out of this, and both apply to every later experiment.

**Removing the cell and removing the pigment answer different questions.** An ablation shows that the cells are needed, but because it removes both inputs it cannot say whether the melanopsin response or the rod and cone relay supplied what was lost.

**Single-loss deficits need not add up.** Take some made-up phase shifts (they return in the first MCAT question): 2.0 h in wild type, 0.6 h without melanopsin, 1.8 h without rods and cones, and none after ablation.

**Predict.** If each input supplied a fixed share of the wild-type shift, the two inputs working alone would add up to the wild-type shift. Do they?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Each input working alone gives 0.6 h and 1.8 h, which sum to 2.4 h, more than the wild type's 2.0 h. Seen as losses, losing melanopsin costs 2.0 − 0.6 = 1.4 h and losing rods and cones costs 2.0 − 1.8 = 0.2 h. Together that is 1.6 h, but losing both costs the full 2.0 h.

One possible reading is that the pooled output saturates. A pupil cannot get smaller than its minimum, and a phase shift has a ceiling. Then the stronger input alone (here melanopsin, 1.8 of 2.0 h) comes near the ceiling, and adding the other gains little. These numbers do not say where the saturation happens, in the cell or downstream, and other explanations are possible. The real knockout studies show complementary inputs (mice without rods and cones still phase-shift to light, mice without melanopsin shift less, and mice without both do not entrain), but they used different readouts, so they do not test this saturation model. A single knockout shows what the *other* input can do alone, not each input's share.

</details>

## Separate cells for the clock and the pupil

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[3]} />

Do the same ipRGCs set the clock and narrow the pupil, or do separate cells do each job? To ask, you need a way to mark one set of cells. **Brn3b** is a transcription factor made by most ganglion cells but absent from a subset of M1 cells. Chen, Badea and Hattar (2011) found that Brn3b-negative M1 cells innervated the SCN and that Brn3b-positive ipRGCs, a mixed set of more than one type, innervated the olivary pretectal nucleus.

**Predict.** Kill only the Brn3b-positive ipRGCs, leaving other ganglion cells intact. What happens to the pupil reflex, and what happens to photoentrainment?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Genetic ablation of the Brn3b-positive cells severely impaired the pupil light reflex and spared circadian photoentrainment, because the Brn3b-negative cells that reach the SCN remained. The two behaviours depend on partly different cells. Because Brn3b marks a mixed output set, the result separates two behaviours without assigning either to one subtype.

</details>

Those outputs come from a varied population. Mouse ipRGCs are named M1 to M6, and they differ in shape, in where their dendrites sit in the inner plexiform layer (the layer where bipolar cells contact ganglion cells) and in where their axons go. ipRGC dendrites gather in two bands of that layer, an outer one and an inner one. The letters describe mouse cells, not six homologous human types.

| Subtype | What to know |
|---|---|
| **M1** | The classic clock and pupil cells, mainly non-image-forming. Dendrites in the outer band, the outermost stratum, conventionally called OFF; the name gives position, not the sign of the response (Going deeper). |
| **M2** | Dendrites in the inner band. |
| **M3** | Dendrites in both bands. |
| **M4** | The mouse sustained ON alpha ganglion cell (the ON alpha cell of [retinal cell types](/lessons/retinal-cell-types/)), intrinsically photosensitive. |
| **M5** | Compact, highly branched arbors; axons reach the dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus (dLGN), the thalamic relay for image-forming vision. |
| **M6** | Small-field and bistratified; also project to the dLGN. |

The M5 and M6 projections to the dLGN are one reason to ask whether ipRGCs also help with seeing patterns, a smaller question taken up near the end. Subtype counts depend on method (Going deeper).

## Beyond the clock: light, mood and learning in mice

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[4]} />

ipRGC signals reach brain areas beyond the clock and the pupil. Mouse experiments suggest that light acts on ipRGCs to affect mood and learning, apart from sleep. LeGates and colleagues (2012) imposed a **T7 cycle**, 3.5 hours of light followed by 3.5 hours of dark, so that 24 / 7 is about 3.4 cycles per day, against a control 24-hour cycle. T7 mice showed depression-like behaviour and impaired long-term potentiation in hippocampal slices ([synaptic plasticity](/lessons/synaptic-plasticity/)), and these changes were absent in animals lacking ipRGCs.

Two controls matter. The authors cite earlier work showing that total sleep and sleep architecture are preserved under T7, and they found the clock still rhythmic, with intact PER2 rhythms in the SCN and liver, although its circadian period lengthened. As with any ablation, the result implicates the cells, not the pigment, and a mouse assay of depression-like behaviour is not a diagnosis (see [emotion and stress](/lessons/emotion-and-stress/)).

<TCycleScheduleReader />

Fernandez and colleagues (2018) then separated the effects by route in mice, using genetic ablation to leave only Brn3b-negative ipRGCs. These SCN-projecting cells were sufficient for the learning deficits under T7, although minor non-SCN innervation remained in this model and its contribution was not excluded. An SCN-independent path from ipRGCs to the **perihabenular (PHb) region**, next to the habenula, carried the affective effects. In the tested paradigms, silencing PHb prevented the mood-like change, and activating it was enough to produce one. Learning and mood therefore appear to run through different outputs in mice. Whether a homologous circuit exists in humans was not tested.

## What people show: two blind participants and an evening screen

Human evidence comes from smaller studies. Zaidi and colleagues (2007) studied two profoundly blind adults without functional rods or cones. In one woman, the pupil's action spectrum peaked at 480 nm after correction for absorption by the lens, and she detected a 481-nm stimulus above chance in a forced-choice test. In one man, 460-nm light suppressed melatonin. The two results come from two different people and must not be merged. Together they are an existence proof that human inner-retinal photoreception can drive a pupil response and melatonin suppression, not a population result, and they do not show restored image vision.

For screens, the best-known human evidence is a single protocol. Chang and colleagues (2015) compared reading a light-emitting device at maximum brightness with reading a printed book, for about four hours on each of five consecutive evenings. After the fifth evening of device reading, melatonin was suppressed compared with a dim-light assessment of the same time window, and melatonin onset came later than after book reading. The study did not manipulate ipRGCs and did not separate the device light's spectrum from its brightness, so its link to these cells is an inference, and it is one protocol, not a dose-response curve for phones, filters or brief use.

## A smaller extension: coarse patterns and contrast

A smaller line of work asks whether ipRGCs also help with seeing patterns. Ecker and colleagues (2010) used a Cre-based melanopsin reporter and found that the labelled cells heavily innervated the dLGN. Mice lacking rod and cone signalling (*Gnat1* and *Cnga3* knockouts) discriminated a striped grating from a grey field of the same average luminance. Schmidt and colleagues (2014) found behavioural contrast-sensitivity deficits in mice lacking melanopsin. Some later work, on M4 cells and on human vision, has followed up the contrast result, but this remains a secondary role next to the clock and the pupil. Discriminating a grating is very coarse vision, not normal acuity, and these results come from mice.

### Design problem

Mice lacking melanopsin still entrain and keep a partial pupil reflex, while mice whose ipRGCs are killed lose much more of both, close to mice that lack melanopsin and rod and cone signalling together. Interpret these data: what does each manipulation remove, and what do the cells and the pigment each contribute to the clock and the pupil? Say how you would design the next experiments, and how far a human study can go.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with what each manipulation removes. The melanopsin knockout removes one input and leaves the relay, so the cells still carry light to the clock and the pupil; the ablation removes both inputs, which is why its deficits resemble the triple mutant's. So the cells are the main route, and melanopsin adds to it rather than being required. Because single-loss deficits need not add, do not read the knockout's deficit as melanopsin's share.

Next, match the readout to the input. Measure phase shifts to brief light pulses as well as entrainment, since the knockout entrains but shifts less. For the pupil, measure constriction over minutes in steady bright light, where melanopsin holds the response, and quick responses in dim light, where rods and cones dominate.

Then refine the manipulation. Use an adult, timed ablation rather than a germline one, so that developmental rewiring cannot explain the result, and check how complete it is. Use an output-restricted ablation, such as removing the Brn3b-positive cells, to separate the pupil route from the clock route, remembering that the marker defines an output set and not a subtype.

Finally, state what the human evidence reaches. Pupil action spectra, the post-illumination pupil response and melatonin suppression in blind participants without functional rods or cones can show that inner-retinal photoreception drives these responses in people. They cannot remove the cells, so they cannot test necessity the way a mouse ablation does.

</details>

## Going deeper

### Four discoveries, four dates

A common retelling says melanopsin cells were discovered in 2000. The papers separate four steps.

In 1998, Provencio and colleagues identified melanopsin in cultured, light-sensitive dermal melanophores of the frog *Xenopus laevis*. That was a pigment first found in a skin cell, with mRNA also reported in frog retina, brain and iris, and it was not a mammalian ganglion-cell result.

In 1999, the two companion *Science* papers used different rodless, coneless mice: Freedman and colleagues measured phase shifts in *rdta/cl* mice, and Lucas and colleagues measured pineal melatonin suppression in *rd/rd cl* mice. They showed that the mouse eye holds a photoreceptor other than rods and cones, without saying which cells.

In 2000, Provencio and colleagues reported the human melanopsin gene, cloned from human retina, and used **in situ hybridization**, which marks where a messenger RNA is present, on retina from adult macaque and from adult and 10-day-old mice. Melanopsin transcripts lay in cells of the inner retina, in the ganglion and amacrine cell layers. It located a gene's expression and recorded no light response; a transcript alone does not make a cell light-sensitive.

In 2002 the rat action spectrum was derived from intensity-response curves at several wavelengths; 484 nm is the value later reviews cite for this paper, and it is a rat estimate, not a human one. In the companion paper by Hattar, Liao, Takao, Berson and Yau, all 18 of the rat cells they recorded as intrinsically photosensitive were melanopsin-immunopositive, and in a mouse in which a reporter gene replaced one copy of the melanopsin gene, labelled axons reached the SCN.

### How large is the knockout's phase-shift deficit?

Panda and colleagues (2002) described the melanopsin knockout's phase shifts as severely reduced. Ruby and colleagues (2002) reported a more modest reduction, and the size depends on irradiance and wavelength. Rods alone can drive entrainment over a wide range of irradiances (Altimus and colleagues, 2010; Lall and colleagues, 2010).

### An ablation timed in adults

Hatori and colleagues (2008) used an inducible design: a diphtheria-toxin receptor made in melanopsin cells, so that the cells are killed only when the toxin is given, which can be done in adults. That design avoids developmental rewiring as an explanation.

### Subtype counts and how each was assigned

A 2023 count describes six morphologically characterized subtypes, and the counts depend on the data set and method. Ecker and colleagues (2010) identified at least five subtypes, which is not six. A 2019 anatomical study cautioned that it remains unclear whether M3 is a bona fide type.

M1 dendrites sit in the OFF stratum, yet M1 cells receive ON bipolar input. Some ON bipolar cell axons make ribbon synapses in passing as they cross the OFF layer, and M1 dendrites are among their targets (Dumitrescu and colleagues, 2009; Hoshi and colleagues, 2009).

M4 was recorded as intrinsically photosensitive by Ecker and colleagues in 2010, linked to ON alpha cells by Estevez and colleagues in 2012, and shown to be an intrinsically photosensitive ON alpha cell by Schmidt and colleagues in 2014.

In M5 cells, Stabio and colleagues recorded UV-excitatory, green-inhibitory colour opponency and traced M5 axons to the dLGN. That is a circuit result, and it does not show that melanopsin alone distinguishes colours. For M6, retrograde tracing from the dLGN confirmed a projection, which the authors took to suggest a role in pattern vision. Quattrochi and colleagues did not test M6-specific behaviour.

### Channels beyond M1: an open disagreement

The M1 double-knockout result has exact conditions. M1 cells lacking both TRPC6 and TRPC7 had no detectable response at 23 degrees C, but kept a response of 1% or less of the wild-type response to strong flashes at 35 degrees C. Even for M1, the temperature and the flash strength belong to the claim.

Whether HCN channels contribute in M2 and M4 cells is an open disagreement between laboratories (see [ion channels as receptors](/lessons/ion-channels-as-receptors/)). Sonoda and colleagues (2018) studied M4 cells in isolated retina from mice aged one to three months and reported that melanopsin raised excitability by closing potassium leak channels. The two accounts have been in tension since 2018, when Jiang and colleagues in the Yau laboratory proposed a cyclic-nucleotide and HCN mechanism for M4 cells, and two 2023 papers then disagreed about HCN channels, which open when the membrane hyperpolarizes and open more readily when cAMP binds them. Li and colleagues, extending earlier work from their laboratory on M1, M2 and M4 cells, reported that PLCB4-TRPC6/7 and cAMP-HCN components coexist in individual cells of all six subtypes, at least for bright flashes, so the two papers conflict directly over M2 and M4. Contreras and colleagues recorded M4 cells in retina from mice aged 30 to 90 days at 30 to 32 degrees C and found that the photocurrent persisted when HCN currents were eliminated, and their data supported potassium-channel closure together with TRPC channel opening; they also concluded that M2 phototransduction requires TRPC channels together with T-type calcium channels. Both used 30 to 32 degrees C; the difference may reflect flash strength, blocker incubation time or other recording conditions.

### Worked problem: reading a photocurrent

Contreras and colleagues recorded from a representative mouse M4 cell in isolated retina, at 30 to 32 degrees C, with synaptic transmission blocked. The holding current at a fixed voltage was +31.28 pA in the dark and -27.48 pA in light.

1. **Change on light.** Light minus dark is (-27.48) - (+31.28) = -58.76 pA. This is an inward shift under that recording convention, and it matches the figure legend.
2. **What the subtraction establishes.** It gives the size of a current change in one cell, not a group average. An inward shift can mean more inward current (cation channels opening) or less outward current (potassium channels closing), and the subtraction cannot tell them apart, so a current trace alone cannot settle which channels carry the M4 response. Here the voltage was held fixed, so the recording shows a current, not a depolarization. In an unclamped cell either change would depolarize. In the passive model of the Predict box, besides the cation case shown there, halving the potassium conductance (cation conductance kept at 0.2) moves the balance point from −75 to about −64 mV.
3. **Use the controls.** After HCN blockade that was verified, the M4 light response was not reduced in the short-incubation condition, and in four cells the authors found no significant light-evoked change in HCN tail-current amplitude. That argues against HCN opening being necessary for the M4 response in this preparation. A non-significant difference is not proof of equality, and the result does not settle the competing study.

### The mouse mood and learning experiments in detail

LeGates and colleagues (2012) used male wild-type mice aged 4 to 8 months and ipRGC-ablated mice aged 6 to 8 months.

Fernandez and colleagues (2018) made their model by Cre-driven genetic ablation (Opn4-Cre with Brn3b-zDTA), and they concluded that the learning effect did not depend on the SCN's pacemaker function.

### The human studies in detail

In Zaidi and colleagues (2007), the woman was 87 years old. Her pupil action spectrum was built from irradiance-response curves at eight wavelengths. The man was 56 years old, and he had 57% melatonin suppression, measured as the area under the curve, during a 6.5-hour 460-nm exposure.

Chang and colleagues (2015) studied 12 healthy young adults, mean age 24.92 years, in an inpatient crossover design. Eleven contributed melatonin data. After the fifth evening of device reading, mean suppression was 55.12% relative to the same 4-hour window in a dim-light constant-posture assessment 24 hours later.

### Pattern vision: the numbers

The rod- and cone-deficient mice in Ecker and colleagues (2010) were aged 4 to 16 months. They managed 0.16 cycles per degree against 0.55 in wild type.
