techniques:
  - name: "Watching infection in a transparent living animal"
    blurb: >-
      Choose an animal you can see through, such as Daphnia or a starfish larva, and follow its
      cells and the invading microbe under a microscope over time. It shows which cells act and
      when, though it cannot by itself show that those cells are necessary for survival.
  - name: "Heat inactivation and serum mixing"
    blurb: >-
      Heat serum to destroy its heat-labile parts, then add back fresh serum from an untreated
      animal that cannot kill on its own. If the mixture kills, the activity needs at least two components with different heat
      sensitivity, as Bordet found for complement and antibody.
  - name: "Loss-of-function mutants with a survival readout"
    blurb: >-
      Compare wild-type and mutant organisms (flies lacking Toll pathway parts, mice lacking a
      working Tlr4, Arabidopsis lacking several surface receptors) for a molecular output and for
      survival or resistance after infection. It tests whether a part is needed, not how it
      recognizes the microbe.
  - name: "Ex vivo restimulation of blood cells"
    blurb: >-
      Take blood cells from a person or animal before and after a treatment, challenge them in a
      dish with an unrelated microbe, and measure what they release, such as the signal IL-1β.
      Counting the cells in the same samples helps tell a change in cell numbers from a change in
      each cell's response.

Innate defenses are inherited and ready before an organism meets a particular microbe. [Adaptive immunity](/lessons/adaptive-immunity/) builds new antibodies and T cells against each microbe, which takes days the first time. Animals and plants meet microbes constantly, and those days belong to innate defenses. They are not "nonspecific": their receptors recognize particular classes of microbial molecules.

> **The one idea.** Early in an infection, innate defense is a race against doubling. If defenses remove a fraction k of the bacteria in each **doubling time** (the time one bacterium takes to become two), each doubling time multiplies the count by 2 × (1 − k): 2 for the doubling, (1 − k) for the survivors. Once removal starts, the count shrinks only when that factor is below 1, so defenses must remove more than half every doubling time. Starting early keeps the peak smaller and the time to clear it shorter. In this model of a bacterial wound, the defenses this lesson follows do three jobs: **fewer get in** (barriers), **the clock starts early** (ready-made receptors sense at once), or **more are removed per doubling** (inflammation brings eating cells, which complement speeds).
>
> *Scope:* an illustrative model of expected counts in one well-mixed wound with constant rates, not a measured infection; real infections add tissue damage it omits. The three jobs are not a complete list: some defenses slow growth or spread instead of removing microbes, as interferons do against viruses.

## Near the threshold

| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.40 | 1.2 | about 892 |
| 0.45 | 1.1 | about 314 |
| 0.55 | 0.9 | about 28 |

Moving k by 0.1, from 0.45 to 0.55, turns about 314 into about 28. Near the threshold a small loss, such as one missing complement protein, can decide the outcome. Each row comes from one line (N0 is the starting number, n the number of doubling times):

> **N after n doubling times = N0 × [2 × (1 − k)]^n**

## Fewer get in: barriers

The cheapest defense lowers the starting number. In humans, skin and the linings of the airways and gut do this ([epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/)). The outer skin is dead, tightly packed cells that few microbes can cross unbroken.

Where a lining must stay thin and moist, it relies on moving fluids. In mammalian airways, a sheet of **mucus**, a sticky gel, traps inhaled particles and microbes, and beating **cilia**, hair-like projections on the lining cells, carry it up and out. In the human stomach, strong acid kills many swallowed microbes (see [digestive systems](/lessons/digestive-systems/)). Lining cells, such as the airways', release **antimicrobial peptides**, short proteins, many positively charged, that damage microbial membranes, some all the time and more when they sense microbes. The mostly harmless **resident microbes** of skin and gut take up space and nutrients that a newcomer would need.

Other organisms build walls too: an insect's tough cuticle, a leaf's waxy cuticle over cell walls. Many microbes enter through a wound or a natural opening, though some fungi can push straight through an intact cuticle.

## Starting the clock: sensing and inflammation

Resident cells such as **macrophages** (long-lived eating cells in tissues that also clear debris) carry **pattern-recognition receptors**, which bind molecules typical of microbes and molecules released by damaged host cells. The first film's sensor, TLR4, is a Toll-like receptor (named after the fly protein Toll, met below) for bacterial lipopolysaccharide. The bound receptor activates NF-κB, a protein that turns on inflammatory genes in the nucleus ([TLRs and NF-κB](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb/)).

In mammals those genes start **inflammation**, which gathers the other defenses. The signals sit in the middle two of its four steps:

- *Vessels widen, leak and turn sticky.* **Mast cells**, tissue cells packed with granules of signals, release mediators such as histamine, which widen small vessels and briefly loosen their lining's junctions. Fluid and plasma proteins, complement among them (met below), leak into the tissue (see [blood and vessels](/lessons/blood-and-vessels/)). TNF-α, a signal protein made by activated macrophages, makes the lining display adhesion molecules that passing white cells can grab.
- *Cells arrive.* **Neutrophils**, short-lived white blood cells, roll along the vessel wall, stick, crawl and squeeze through; the macrophages the monocytes become also clear spent neutrophils as the tissue rebuilds.

In human medicine the visible signs are redness, heat, swelling and pain. Fever, a raised set point for body temperature, is treated in [thermoregulation](/lessons/thermoregulation/).

## Eating the invader: phagocytes

A **phagocyte** engulfs particles; in mammals the main ones are neutrophils and macrophages, the cells inflammation gathers. The engulfed microbe sits in a membrane bubble, the **phagosome**, which fuses with **lysosomes**, sacs of digestive enzymes, to form a **phagolysosome**, where the microbe is usually killed, though some pathogens survive there.

Do such cells defend the body at all? In the 1880s and 1890s that was open, and proteins dissolved in the blood were the rival answer. The second film follows one experiment on each side; each supplied part of the answer.

## Complement: mark, alarm, puncture

**Complement**, Bordet's heat-sensitive factor, is a set of proteins, most made in the liver, that circulate inactive in blood plasma. The second film showed it coating a bacterium; it has three jobs.

Three routes switch it on (see Going deeper), and all converge on one protein, **C3**, by cutting it in two. For C3's two pieces, remember: **C3b binds, C3a alarms.**

1. **Mark (C3b).** The large piece, C3b, and its trimmed form iC3b stick to the microbe, and phagocytes carry receptors for both. Coating a microbe for eating is **opsonization**, the coat the film showed raising k.
2. **Alarm (C3a and C5a).** The small piece, C3a, drifts away, as does C5a, cut a step later from the next protein, C5. Both promote inflammation, and C5a also draws neutrophils to the site.
3. **Puncture (C5b-9).** C5's larger piece, C5b, starts the **membrane attack complex**, C5b to C9, a ring that can kill some microbes by punching a hole in their membrane. Bacteria with an outer membrane over a thin wall are its targets, Neisseria especially; bacteria with a thick wall and no outer membrane mostly resist it.

**Predict.** A person makes normal C3 and C5 but lacks C6, C7 or C8, late proteins of the C5b-9 ring. Which jobs still work, and which infections should trouble this person most?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Marking and alarm still work, because C3b, C3a and C5a are made before the late proteins act. Puncture fails, so the gap shows with the microbes the ring kills best: people lacking a late protein are prone to repeated Neisseria infections. Losing C9, the last protein, is milder: C5b-8 alone still makes small holes and kills Neisseria, only more slowly.

</details>

Complement also helps clear dying cells quietly (see [cell death](/lessons/cell-death/)) and prunes synapses in the brain ([microglia and complement](/lessons/microglia-complement-pruning/)).

## Viruses: interferons and natural killer cells

A virus multiplies inside a cell, out of easy reach of phagocytes and complement.

**Interferons** are signals released by virus-infected cells, and by some immune cells, that put nearby cells into an antiviral state, so each infected cell infects fewer new ones. Alick Isaacs and Jean Lindenmann named them in 1957 after finding that chick membrane exposed to heat-killed influenza virus released a factor that protected fresh membrane. Their receptors signal through JAK and STAT proteins ([JAK-STAT](/lessons/jak-stat/)).

**Natural killer (NK) cells** are lymphocytes, the white-cell family of B and T cells, but they kill infected or abnormal body cells without B and T cells' rearranged antigen receptors. They weigh activating against inhibitory signals; MHC class I, a surface protein most body cells display, is an inhibitory one, so a cell that loses it releases a brake. NK cells then release granules of perforin and granzymes, which can trigger the target's self-destruction.

## Three organisms, one problem: fly, plant, mouse

Every lineage has ready-made recognition; the receptors and responses differ.

**The fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster** makes no antibodies and has no lymphocytes. Its blood cells, hemocytes, can engulf bacteria, and infection switches on genes for antimicrobial peptides such as drosomycin (mainly through the Toll pathway) and diptericin (mainly through the Imd pathway). Fly Toll is activated by Spätzle, a fly protein cut during infection, not by fungal molecules directly ([TLRs and NF-κB](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb/) tells how Toll's role in defense was found).

**The plant Arabidopsis thaliana** has no mobile defender cells; every cell defends itself. Surface pattern receptors start pattern-triggered immunity (PTI). Mostly inside the cell, NLR receptors detect **effectors**, molecules a pathogen delivers into plant cells, many of which suppress defense, directly or by the changes they make to plant proteins, and start effector-triggered immunity (ETI). In Arabidopsis infected with Pseudomonas syringae, the two cooperate. A strong response often includes the **hypersensitive response**, death of plant cells at the infection site, which can limit the pathogen's spread. Signals that spread resistance through the plant are in [plant signals](/lessons/plant-signals/).

**The mouse**, like humans, has barriers, complement, neutrophils, macrophages, NK cells, interferons and fever, and on top of them T and B lymphocytes. None of these three is more primitive than the others; each lineage has met microbes for the same length of time, and the arms race is in [mutualism, parasitism and coevolution](/lessons/mutualism-parasitism-coevolution/).

## What innate immunity lacks, and how it can change

Innate immunity lacks receptors built anew in each lymphocyte, so it does not make the receptor-based memory of B and T cells. What it has is a head start: remove more than half every doubling time, and start before the pile grows.

Its responses can still change: in a 2020 study, human blood cells responded more strongly to an unrelated fungus 90 days after BCG vaccination. This **trained immunity** is not specific to one microbe, and a stronger response in a dish is not protection from infection. How it works is not fully understood; Going deeper looks at the evidence.

### Design problem

Lipopolysaccharide provokes strong inflammation in most mice. Design a test of the claim that mice need the receptor TLR4 to sense lipopolysaccharide and start inflammation.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with a loss-of-function comparison: give mice lacking a working Tlr4 gene and normal mice the same dose of purified lipopolysaccharide, with a salt-solution control injection.

Measure steps of the sequence, not only survival: inflammatory signals from resident cells, and neutrophils arriving at the injection site. If TLR4 is needed, mutants should show little of either after lipopolysaccharide, and match normal mice after the control.

Rule out a general defect: if mutants respond normally to a microbial molecule sensed by another receptor, the defect is specific to sensing lipopolysaccharide.

Use more than one mutation: the strains C3H/HeJ and C57BL/10ScCr barely respond to lipopolysaccharide and carry different Tlr4 defects, and two independent mutations in one gene beat one strain, which could differ in other genes too.

State the limits: a loss-of-function test shows that TLR4 is needed, not how it recognizes the molecule; showing that it binds lipopolysaccharide takes a different experiment.

</details>

## Going deeper

### What the race model leaves out

Real tissues break the model's constant rates and mixing: recruitment ramps up, and some microbes hide. The threshold is not an artefact of whole doubling times: with smooth growth against smooth killing, break-even is still exactly one-half per doubling time.

In the model, delay does not change whether removal above one-half wins. It costs more when killing is weak: at k = 0.75 each doubling lost before killing starts takes one more doubling time to undo; at k = 0.6, a factor of 0.8, it takes about three.

### Receptor and complement details

Most Toll-like receptors signal to NF-κB through the adaptor MyD88, though TLR3 uses TRIF ([TLRs and NF-κB](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb/)). The three complement routes are the classical (usually started by antibody, Bordet's route), lectin (mannose-binding lectin, which binds microbial sugars) and alternative (constant low activation, switched off on host cells by host regulators) pathways. Many pathogens have evolved ways to block complement on their surfaces. C3 alone is around 1 mg per mL of plasma.

### Memory-like innate responses

Some mouse NK cells show a memory-like response to cytomegalovirus: after one infection, a subset that recognizes a viral protein expands and responds more strongly next time. Unlike trained immunity, this special case is specific to one virus.

**Exercise.** Cirovic and colleagues gave the tuberculosis vaccine BCG to 15 healthy adults and a placebo to 5. Ninety days later, blood cells from the vaccinated group responded more strongly in a dish to an unrelated fungus, monocytes showed changes in which parts of their DNA were accessible, and marrow progenitor cells showed changes in which genes were active. Design a test of the claim that innate cells "remember".

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Compare each volunteer with their own blood before vaccination, and keep a placebo group handled identically, so seasons or sampling stress are less likely to explain a change.

Use a heterologous (unrelated) stimulus such as the fungus Candida albicans, since a stronger response to BCG itself could be ordinary adaptive memory. Mixed blood cells contain T cells, so rule out cross-reactive ones, for example by testing purified monocytes.

Separate numbers from behaviour: count each cell population in the same samples, since more monocytes would release more signal with no cell changing. Cirovic and colleagues found no significant change in the measured myeloid population counts. The study was small, so this makes a change per cell likely rather than proven.

Ask how long it lasts by sampling at several times beyond 90 days, which that study did not do.

State the limits: no antigen receptor was rebuilt, the response is not specific, and showing protection needs infections measured in vaccinated and placebo groups.

</details>
