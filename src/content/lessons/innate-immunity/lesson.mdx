**Innate immunity is the set of defenses an organism has before it meets a particular microbe. Barriers keep many microbes out. Ready-made receptors recognize the ones that get in, and that recognition sets off a fast, coordinated response that brings eating cells and blood proteins to the site.**

Animals and plants meet microbes constantly, and the first hours of an infection pass before new antibodies against the microbe could be made. A primary [adaptive response](/lessons/adaptive-immunity/) takes days, so these first hours belong to innate defenses. They are inherited ready to act. They are not "nonspecific": their receptors recognize particular classes of microbial molecules.

A water flea makes this easy to see, because it is transparent. Put one infected with yeast under a microscope, and you can watch its blood cells swallow the invading spores. Whether the cells or the spores win decides whether the flea lives.

## Sense, then respond: inflammation

Once a microbe is past the barriers, the innate response starts with recognition. Resident cells in tissues, such as macrophages, carry **pattern-recognition receptors**. These bind molecules typical of microbes and molecules released by damaged host cells. Mammalian TLR4, a Toll-like receptor, recognizes bacterial lipopolysaccharide, a part of the outer membrane of many bacteria. Toll-like receptors switch on inflammatory genes through NF-κB ([TLRs and NF-κB](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb/)).

In mammals, **inflammation** is how that sensing becomes a local response that gathers the other defenses at the site:

1. *Sensing.* Resident cells such as macrophages detect microbes or damage with pattern-recognition receptors and switch on inflammatory genes.
2. *Leaky vessels.* Mediators such as histamine, released by mast cells, widen small vessels and briefly loosen the junctions of their lining, and TNF-α leads lining cells to display adhesion molecules.
3. *Leak.* Fluid and plasma proteins, complement among them, pass into the tissue (see [blood and vessels](/lessons/blood-and-vessels/)).
4. *Recruitment.* Neutrophils, short-lived white blood cells, roll along the vessel wall, stick, crawl and squeeze through it. Monocytes follow and mature into macrophages.
5. *Repair.* Macrophages clear microbes, debris and spent neutrophils, and the tissue rebuilds.

In human medicine the visible signs are redness, heat, swelling and pain. Fever, a raised set point for body temperature, is treated in [thermoregulation](/lessons/thermoregulation/).

Speed matters, because bacteria can multiply quickly and early killing has to keep pace. Going deeper works through an illustrative model of that race.

The rest of this lesson takes the parts in turn: the barriers that keep microbes out, the cells that eat them, the blood proteins that mark and puncture them, and the defenses against viruses.

## Keeping microbes out: barriers

The cheapest defense keeps microbes out. In humans, skin and the linings of the airways and gut all take part in innate defense. The outer skin is a layer of dead, tightly packed cells that few microbes can cross unbroken. The barrier logic of these linings belongs to [epithelia](/lessons/epithelia/).

Where a lining must stay thin and moist, it relies on moving fluids. In mammalian airways, a sheet of **mucus**, a sticky gel, traps inhaled particles and microbes, and beating **cilia**, hair-like projections on the lining cells, carry it up and out. In the human stomach, strong acid kills many swallowed microbes (see [digestive systems](/lessons/digestive-systems/)). Airway lining cells can also release **antimicrobial peptides**, short proteins that damage microbial membranes, when they sense microbes. And the mostly harmless **resident microbes** of skin and gut take up space and nutrients that a newcomer would need.

Other organisms build walls too. An insect is wrapped in a tough cuticle, and a plant leaf in a waxy cuticle over cell walls. The problem is the same: many microbes enter through a wound or a natural opening, though some fungi can push straight through an intact cuticle.

## Eating the invader: phagocytes

Neutrophils and macrophages, the cells that inflammation gathers, do much of the killing. A **phagocyte** is a cell that engulfs particles, and in mammals the main professional phagocytes are **neutrophils**, short-lived white blood cells that arrive first in large numbers, and **macrophages**, long-lived cells that sit in tissues and also clear debris. An engulfed microbe sits in a bubble of membrane, the phagosome, which fuses with lysosomes, sacs of digestive enzymes, to form a phagolysosome where the microbe is usually killed, though some pathogens survive there.

The evidence that such cells defend the body came from an invertebrate. Elie Metchnikoff, in a paper published in 1884, studied the water flea Daphnia, a small transparent freshwater crustacean. Some fleas carried a budding yeast. Its needle-shaped spores, swallowed with food, pierce the gut wall into the body cavity.

If blood cells were passive, or carried the infection, they should ignore the spores or spread them. Instead Metchnikoff saw mobile body-cavity cells surround and engulf the spores. In fleas whose cells destroyed the spores, the infection stopped; where spores escaped and grew into budding yeast, the flea died.

That showed phagocytes are an active defense in this crustacean. It did not show that they are enough on their own, and defenses dissolved in the blood turn out to work with them, as the next section shows.

## Complement: mark, alarm, puncture

**Complement** is a set of proteins in blood plasma that work with phagocytes. Three routes of activation all converge on cutting C3. The large piece, C3b, and its product iC3b stick to the microbe and mark it for eating, a job called **opsonization**. The small pieces C3a and C5a (cut from C5 a step later) diffuse away and promote inflammation. Later components assemble the membrane attack complex, C5b-9, which can kill susceptible microbes by punching holes in them.

In 1895, Jules Bordet showed that a heat-sensitive factor in all fresh serum works with antibody to kill bacteria. Complement also helps clear dying cells quietly (see [cell death](/lessons/cell-death/)) and prunes synapses in the brain ([microglia and complement](/lessons/microglia-complement-pruning/)).

## Interferons and natural killer cells

Against viruses, two more innate defenses join phagocytes and complement.

**Interferons** are signals released by virus-infected cells that put nearby cells into an antiviral state. Alick Isaacs and Jean Lindenmann named them in 1957 after finding that chick membrane exposed to killed influenza virus released a factor that protected fresh membrane. Their receptors signal through JAK and STAT proteins ([JAK-STAT](/lessons/jak-stat/)).

**Natural killer (NK) cells** are lymphocytes that kill infected or abnormal body cells without the rearranged antigen receptors of B and T cells. They weigh activating and inhibitory signals, and a cell that has lost normal MHC class I, a surface protein most body cells display, removes an inhibitory signal. NK cells then release granules of perforin and granzymes, which can trigger the target's self-destruction.

## Three organisms, one problem: fly, plant, mouse

Every lineage has ready-made recognition, but the receptors and responses differ.

**The fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster** makes no antibodies and has no lymphocytes. Its blood cells, hemocytes, can engulf bacteria, and infection switches on genes for antimicrobial peptides such as drosomycin (mainly through the Toll pathway) and diptericin (mainly through the Imd pathway). Fly Toll is activated by Spätzle, a fly protein cut during infection, not by fungal molecules directly ([TLRs and NF-κB](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb/) tells how Toll's role in defense was found).

**The plant Arabidopsis thaliana** has no mobile defender cells, so every cell defends itself. Surface pattern receptors start pattern-triggered immunity (PTI), and mostly intracellular NLR receptors detect a pathogen's injected effector proteins and start effector-triggered immunity (ETI). In Arabidopsis infected with Pseudomonas syringae, the two cooperate. A strong response often includes the **hypersensitive response**, death of plant cells at the infection site, which can limit the pathogen's spread. Signals that spread resistance through the plant are in [plant signals](/lessons/plant-signals/).

**The mouse**, like humans, has barriers, complement, neutrophils, macrophages, NK cells, interferons and fever, and on top of them T and B lymphocytes. None of these three is more primitive than the others; each lineage has met microbes for the same length of time, and the arms race is in [mutualism, parasitism and coevolution](/lessons/mutualism-parasitism-coevolution/).

## What innate immunity lacks, and how it can change

What innate immunity lacks is receptors built anew in each lymphocyte, so it does not make the receptor-based memory of B and T cells. But its responses can change. In a 2020 study, 90 days after BCG vaccination, human blood cells responded more strongly to an unrelated fungus. This is called **trained immunity**. It is not specific to one microbe, and a stronger response in a dish is not the same as protection from infection. How it works is not fully understood; Going deeper looks at the evidence.

### Design problem

Lipopolysaccharide, a part of the outer membrane of many bacteria, provokes strong inflammation in most mice. Design a test of the claim that mice need the receptor TLR4 to sense lipopolysaccharide and start inflammation.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with a loss-of-function comparison. Use mice that lack a working Tlr4 gene and normal mice, and give both the same dose of purified lipopolysaccharide. Give a harmless salt solution as a control injection.

Measure steps of the sequence, not only survival. Look for inflammatory signals made by resident cells, and count neutrophils that have left the blood at the injection site. If TLR4 is needed, the mutants should show little of either after lipopolysaccharide, while their response to the control injection matches normal mice.

Rule out a general defect. Give both groups a different microbial molecule that is sensed by another receptor. If the mutants respond normally to it, their cells can still mount inflammation, and the defect is specific to sensing lipopolysaccharide.

Use more than one mutation. Two mouse strains, C3H/HeJ and C57BL/10ScCr, barely respond to lipopolysaccharide, and each carries a different defect in Tlr4. Two independent mutations in the same gene make a much stronger case than one strain, which could differ in other genes too.

State the limits. A loss-of-function test shows that TLR4 is needed, not how it recognizes the molecule. Showing that it binds lipopolysaccharide takes a different experiment.

</details>

## Going deeper

These sections hold details and exercises that build on the main line.

### The race between killing and doubling

How fast must innate defenses kill? A simple model shows why speed matters.

**Worked problem.** These numbers are illustrative, not a measured infection. Suppose 100 bacteria enter a wound and each doubles every 20 minutes. Assume constant rates and a well-mixed wound.

1. *No defense, 4 hours.* Four hours is 12 doublings: 100 x 2^12 = 100 x 4,096 = 409,600 bacteria.
2. *Defenses kill 75% per 20-minute interval.* Survivors (25%) still double, so the multiplier is 2 x 0.25 = 0.5 per interval. After 12 intervals: 100 x 0.5^12 = 100 / 4,096, about 0.024. The infection is cleared.
3. *Defenses kill only 40%.* Multiplier 2 x 0.60 = 1.2. After 12 intervals: 100 x 1.2^12 = 100 x 8.92, about 892, still growing.
4. *The threshold.* The population shrinks only if 2 x (1 - k) is below 1, so the fraction killed, k, must exceed 50% per doubling time.

> **N after n intervals = N0 x [2 x (1 - k)]^n**

The model assumes constant rates and a well-mixed site. Real tissues break both assumptions: recruitment ramps up over time, and some microbes hide.

### Receptor and complement details

Most Toll-like receptors signal to NF-κB through the adaptor MyD88, though TLR3 uses TRIF ([TLRs and NF-κB](/lessons/tlr-and-nf-kb/)). Complement proteins are abundant in plasma: C3 alone is around 1 mg per mL.

### Cells or serum? A century-old argument

By his own later account, Metchnikoff got the idea in Messina in the early 1880s, when he pushed splinters into transparent starfish larvae and saw mobile cells gather around them. His 1884 paper named the flea's yeast Monospora bicuspidata. Paul Ehrlich and others championed defenses dissolved in the blood. The two views turned out to be parts of one system, and in 1908 Metchnikoff and Ehrlich shared the Nobel Prize.

### Memory-like innate responses

Some mouse NK cells show a memory-like response to cytomegalovirus, a special case. The BCG study mentioned above is one example of trained immunity in people.

**Exercise.** Cirovic and colleagues gave the tuberculosis vaccine BCG to 15 healthy adults and a placebo to 5. Ninety days later, blood cells from the vaccinated group responded more strongly in a dish to an unrelated fungus, monocytes showed changes in which parts of their DNA were accessible, and marrow progenitor cells showed changes in which genes were active. Design a test of the claim that innate cells "remember".

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Start with the comparisons. Take blood from every volunteer before vaccination as a baseline, so each person is compared with themselves, and keep a placebo group handled identically, so seasonal infections or the stress of sampling are less likely to explain a change.

Use a heterologous stimulus, a microbe unrelated to BCG such as the fungus Candida albicans. A stronger response to BCG itself could be ordinary adaptive memory; a stronger response to something unrelated is unlikely to be antigen-specific. Mixed blood cells also contain T cells, though, so cross-reactive T cells still need to be ruled out, for example by testing purified monocytes.

Separate numbers from behaviour. Count each cell population in the same samples. If there were simply more monocytes, the dish would release more signal without any cell changing. Cirovic and colleagues found no significant change in the measured myeloid population counts. The study was small, so this makes a change per cell likely rather than proven.

Ask how long it lasts by sampling at several times beyond 90 days, which that study did not do.

State the limits. This is not antigen-receptor memory: no receptor was rebuilt and the response is not specific. And a stronger dish response is not protection; to claim that, you would need to measure infections in vaccinated and placebo groups.

</details>
