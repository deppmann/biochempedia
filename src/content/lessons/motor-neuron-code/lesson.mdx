    quotes: []
    story: >-
      In 1994 Jessell's laboratory, working with Samuel Pfaff, cloned a family of LIM homeobox
      genes from chick and found that four of them (Islet-1, Islet-2, Lim-1, Lim-3) are expressed
      in overlapping sets in embryonic motor neurons. The sets appeared before motor columns were
      visible and before axons sorted into separate pathways, and each set matched a column and a
      pathway. That turned motor-neuron identity into something you could read off a cell. Later
      work from the same laboratory pushed the logic in both directions. Novitch, Chen and Jessell
      reported in 2001 that the bHLH repressor Olig2 marks the progenitors that make motor
      neurons and acts with Neurogenin2 to couple subtype identity to becoming a neuron at all.
      Kania, Johnson and Jessell showed in mouse that Lim1 in one half of the limb-innervating
      column sends axons dorsally. The 1994 map became a mechanism you could test.
  - name: "Samuel L. Pfaff"
    contribution: >-

    story: >-
      Pfaff's 1996 paper asked the plainest question about a marker: is it needed? In mice in
      which Isl1 expression had been eliminated by gene targeting, motor neurons were not
      generated, while much of the rest of neural tube differentiation went on normally. A class

      and a way to make either on purpose.

      A plasmid is injected into the lumen of the neural tube of a chick embryo and a brief

  - name: "Targeted gene deletion in mouse"
    blurb: >-
      A gene is disrupted in the germ line, and the embryo is stained for markers of each cell
      class. This asks whether the factor is necessary, which is a different question from the
      electroporation experiment and often has a different answer.
  - name: "Antibody staining of transverse sections"
    blurb: >-
      Antibodies against transcription factors label nuclei in a section through the cord, and
      several can be read in one section in different colours. Combinatorial codes were found
      this way, because the information is in which markers coincide in one cell.
  - name: "Directed differentiation of stem cells"
    blurb: >-
      Embryonic stem cells are treated with the signals that pattern the embryo, in the order the
      embryo uses them, and the cells are then scored for markers or transplanted. It tests
      whether a proposed pathway is sufficient outside the embryo.

**Put the protein Lhx3 into cells of a chick spinal cord that make no Isl1, and they turn on interneuron genes. Put Lhx3 in together with Isl1, and the same protein turns on motor-neuron genes instead.**

No gene in a motor-neuron progenitor says "motor neuron". The cell expresses a combination of transcription factors, none of which is specific to motor neurons on its own. The code does two jobs. First it makes a cell a motor neuron. Then, along the cord and in the limb, it helps decide which muscle that neuron will reach, though how much of that choice the code sets on its own is still open. Run the same steps in a dish and you can make motor neurons.

## One protein, two cell types

Meet the factors. Lhx3 and Isl1 are **LIM-homeodomain** proteins. Each has a homeodomain, which binds DNA, and two LIM domains, which bind other proteins, not DNA. Think of the LIM domains as handles: whoever holds Lhx3's handles decides what Lhx3 does. The third player, the LIM cofactor NLI (also called Ldb1), has no DNA-binding domain. It grips LIM domains, and it pairs with itself, so the complexes it builds come in pairs.

Lhx3 is made in two neighbouring cell types: newly born motor neurons, and the V2 interneurons just dorsal to them, local-circuit neurons of the ventral cord. Isl1 is made in the motor neurons, not in V2 interneurons.

**Predict.** You electroporate the chick neural tube: inject a plasmid into it and drive the plasmid into the cells on one side with a brief electric pulse, so the other side is the control. In condition A, Lhx3 goes into cells that make no Isl1. In condition B, Lhx3 and Isl1 go into the same cells. Which genes come up in each: motor-neuron genes such as Mnx1, V2 interneuron genes such as Chx10, or neither?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

A gives V2 interneuron genes. B gives motor-neuron genes, and V2 genes are largely suppressed (the Design problem below gives the catch). The same protein, Lhx3, is in both. Thaler and colleagues in Pfaff's laboratory explained why in 2002.

Picture Isl1 as a bridge. It holds NLI with its own LIM domains and holds Lhx3 with a short tail, so Lhx3 hangs off Isl1 instead of off NLI.

- **V2 interneuron.** NLI grips Lhx3's LIM domains directly, and that complex triggers V2 differentiation. Two NLI·Lhx3 units, joined through NLI, bind V2 genes.
- **Motor neuron.** Isl1 grips NLI with its own LIM domains, taking the place Lhx3 would use. Lhx3 then binds a short, high-affinity segment in Isl1's C-terminal region. Two NLI·Isl1·Lhx3 units, joined through NLI, promote motor-neuron genes.

NLI is in both complexes. What differs is whether Isl1 bridges NLI to Lhx3. The first two structures in this lesson show the same surface of Lhx3 held once by the Isl1 segment and once by NLI.

</details>

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** No single factor says "motor neuron". In this example, what the cell becomes depends on which complexes its transcription factors form, and that depends on which partners are present and in what ratio. Later layers (Hox proteins with FoxP1, then Lim1 against Isl1) refine which kind of motor neuron it becomes. Their timing overlaps: Hox patterning starts in progenitors.

**Scope.** This covers motor neurons of the chick and mouse spinal cord and their V2 neighbours; many cranial motor neurons of the hindbrain use other factors, such as Phox2 proteins. It is about specifying a cell's class and subtype. Keeping the identity is a separate job (Mnx1, below), and so is keeping the cell alive. And presence may not be the whole story: the binding model below, and the excess-Lhx3 caveat in the Design problem, suggest that how much Isl1 there is relative to Lhx3 matters too.

### Why the ratio matters

The short answer: even with equal amounts of Isl1 and Lhx3, binding alone leaves some Lhx3 in the V2 complex. A simple model shows it.

Picture an ideal binding model, not a measurement. NLI holds Lhx3 or Isl1, not both, and Lhx3 binds NLI or Isl1, not both; Isl1 can hold NLI and Lhx3 at once, on separate sites. Every pair binds equally tightly, and Lhx3 and NLI are each present at ten times the concentration at which half would be bound (ten times the dissociation constant). The model ignores NLI pairing, DNA and other partners, so it counts single units, not the paired complexes on DNA. Hold Lhx3 and NLI fixed, and vary Isl1.

| Isl1 relative to Lhx3 | Lhx3 in V2 units (NLI·Lhx3) | Lhx3 in motor-neuron units (NLI·Isl1·Lhx3) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| none | 73% | 0% |
| half as much | 45% | 23% |
| equal | 24% | 37% |
| twice as much | 6% | 37% |
| four times as much | 1% | 23% |

The rest of the Lhx3 is free, or held by Isl1 without NLI. The exact numbers depend on these choices (at a hundred times the dissociation constant, equal amounts leave 16 percent in V2 units rather than 24), but the pattern holds. Read the table in three steps.

1. **A mixture, not a switch.** At equal amounts, about a quarter of the Lhx3 is still in the V2 complex. Of the Lhx3 in either complex, the motor-neuron complex holds about 61 percent. If Isl1 binds NLI ten times more tightly than Lhx3 does, that share rises to about 84 percent: better, but still a mixture.
2. **Too little Isl1 leaves V2 complexes.** That is why the Design problem below warns that two separate plasmids do not fix the ratio. A fused Isl1-Lhx3 protein is a cleaner experiment: it fixes the ratio and tethers Lhx3 to its own Isl1 segment, which this simple model does not include (see [Going deeper](#a-fused-isl1-lhx3-protein)).
3. **Too much Isl1 lowers the motor-neuron complex too**, because spare Isl1 pairs with Lhx3 or with NLI on its own. This last point comes from the model, not from a reported experiment.

So in this model binding alone gives a mixture. The model says nothing about cell fate; real cells add DNA, other partners and downstream regulators. The mouse deletions below show one such regulator, Mnx1, which helps hold the motor-neuron choice in place.

## Before the code: a stripe of progenitors

The motor neurons in that Lhx3 choice come from one progenitor domain, and their V2 neighbours from the domain next to it. Both start with position across the neural tube.

Sonic hedgehog from the notochord and then the floor plate sets up a gradient across the neural tube, opposed by BMPs from above, and progenitors read their position in it; [the Hedgehog lesson](/lessons/hedgehog-and-cilium/) covers how that signal is received. The read-out is a set of stacked domains, each with its own transcription-factor profile. One of them, called pMN (p for progenitor, MN for motor neuron), makes motor neurons; the V2 interneurons come from p2, just dorsal to it.

A pMN progenitor is still dividing. It carries the bHLH repressor Olig2 along with Nkx6.1 and Pax6, and Olig2 is its tag. Novitch, Chen and Jessell reported in 2001 that Olig2 marks these progenitors and works with Neurogenin2, a factor that makes progenitors leave the cell cycle and become neurons. Together they tie motor-neuron character to the step of becoming a neuron. So the cell becomes a neuron and a motor neuron at the same time. Mice lacking both Olig1 and Olig2 largely lose their motor neurons (the necessity table below has the details). The same pMN domain later turns to making oligodendrocytes, which [the glia lesson](/lessons/origins-of-glia/) takes up.

Around its final division, as it becomes postmitotic (no longer dividing), a motor neuron turns on a new set: Isl1 or its relative Isl2, Lhx3, and Mnx1, the gene also called Hb9. These are the markers you would stain for to find a newly differentiated motor neuron in a section; Lhx3 is transient, and later only the medial motor column keeps it.

## Enough, needed, and kept

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

Two words first. A factor, or a combination, is **sufficient** if adding it produces the effect in the cells and stage tested, using the rest of their machinery (the Lhx3 test above used the cells' own NLI). It is **necessary** there if the effect does not happen without it. Each of two techniques tests one of these.

- **Electroporation in chick tests sufficiency.** Expressing a factor ectopically, in cells that would not normally make it, shows what it can impose.
- **Gene deletion in mouse tests necessity.** Delete the gene and see what fails to form.

The two ask different questions, and they can give different answers. Note which animal and which manipulation each claim rests on.

Mnx1, or its chick relative MNR2, electroporated into the cord is sufficient to trigger motor-neuron differentiation and to repress the neighbouring V2 programme.

**Predict.** Now delete Mnx1 in a mouse. Mnx1 is enough to start the programme in chick, but is it needed? Are motor neurons made without it? If they are, what would you look for to see whether anything went wrong?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

They are made, on schedule and in normal numbers. But they transiently take on molecular features of V2 interneurons, such as Chx10, and their columns and axon projections are disordered. Two laboratories reported this in the same issue of Neuron in 1999. Mnx1 is not needed to make a motor neuron. It is needed to keep one: a motor neuron has to keep suppressing the interneuron programme.

</details>

Mnx1 is one of the regulators that hold the choice in place. Being needed to make a cell and being needed to keep it are different jobs. The mouse deletions sort the factors into those jobs.

| Mouse lacking | Motor neurons made? | What changes | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Olig1 and Olig2 | largely no | oligodendrocyte differentiation is abolished; lineage tracing suggested the pMN cells make V2 interneurons and then astrocytes | the progenitor layer decides what the domain makes |
| Isl1 | no | much of the rest of the tube differentiates normally; a class of Engrailed1 interneurons also fails | needed to make a motor neuron |
| Mnx1 (Hb9) | yes, on schedule and in normal numbers | cells transiently show V2 features; columns and axons disordered | needed to keep the identity, not to make it |
| Lhx3 and Lhx4 | yes | which subclass they become, and which way their axons leave the tube | not needed to make a motor neuron, though enough with Isl1 in chick |

The last row looks like a contradiction: Lhx3 is in the motor-neuron complex, yet motor neurons are made without it. It is not one, because the two tests ask different questions. In chick, Isl1 plus Lhx3 is enough to start the motor-neuron programme. In mouse, motor neurons still form without Lhx3 and Lhx4, but they become a different kind of motor neuron, with a different axon exit. A factor can be sufficient in one test without being necessary in another. These results do not show what lets the cells become motor neurons without Lhx3 and Lhx4.

## Which motor neuron: Hox proteins and FoxP1 along the cord

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

A motor neuron in your neck and one in your lower back were built by the same code, yet one drives a neck muscle and the other a muscle of the leg. The difference is rostrocaudal, along the length of the body, and it is written in Hox proteins, the same family you met setting rhombomere identity in [the hindbrain](/lessons/hox-and-hindbrain/).

First, the groups being sorted. A motor **column** is defined by where the cell bodies sit and where the axons go. Among the muscle-innervating columns, a **pool** is the set of neurons that innervates one muscle, such as the biceps.

| Column | Where along the cord | Target | Markers in this lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Medial motor column (MMC) | every level | axial muscles of the back | keeps Lhx3; no FoxP1 |
| Lateral motor column (LMC) | limb levels | limb muscles | FoxP1 high; lateral cells Lim1, medial cells Isl1 |
| Preganglionic column (PGC) | mainly thoracic | autonomic ganglia | FoxP1 low |
| Hypaxial motor column (HMC) | thoracic | body-wall (hypaxial) muscles | no FoxP1 |

In mammals a phrenic motor column also innervates the diaphragm. Do not expect a tidy count of pools; the number depends on the species and on which muscles you count.

How do Hox proteins sort cells into these columns? Dasen, Liu and Jessell reported in 2003 that sequential phases of Hox-c activity control columnar fate in motor neurons. Graded fibroblast growth factor signalling establishes Hox expression in progenitors, which is translated into a Hox pattern in motor neurons. There, Hox proteins act cell autonomously, inside each cell, as repressors and activators that sort cells into columns. One pair shows the logic. Hoxc6 is made at brachial (forelimb) levels and Hoxc9 at thoracic levels, and each represses the other.

**Predict.** In chick, you electroporate Hoxc9 early into the brachial neural tube, so that both the progenitors and the motor neurons they make carry it. These motor neurons would normally form the LMC. What should happen to their column identity?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

LMC identity is suppressed, and the cells take on a preganglionic identity (in chick, the column of Terni), a thoracic column. The thoracic Hox protein represses the brachial one and imposes its own level's column. Timing matters: Hoxc9 given only to motor neurons after their last division suppresses LMC identity but does not impose the preganglionic one, which needs Hoxc9 already in the progenitors. Cross-repression keeps the identities of neighbouring levels apart.

</details>

A 2005 paper from the same laboratory described the network in more detail: one set of Hox interactions fixes position along the cord, and another generates diversity among pools within a single segment, with the pattern of connections to target muscles as the read-out.

Hox proteins need help to do this in some columns. FoxP1 is the gate for the Hox-dependent columns: the limb column (LMC) and the autonomic column (PGC). Dasen and colleagues reported in 2008 that FoxP1 acts as a Hox accessory factor, a partner the Hox proteins need to produce their column output. It is expressed in the Hox-sensitive columns, and acting in a dose-dependent way, high in the LMC and low in the PGC. Rousso and colleagues in Novitch's laboratory showed in the same year that Foxp1 defines columnar identity and sets the pattern of LIM homeodomain proteins.

**Predict.** Delete Foxp1 in a mouse. Are motor neurons still made at limb level? If so, what do they become, and where do their axons go in the limb?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Motor neurons are still made; what fails is the assignment of which one each is. The output of the motor-neuron Hox network is abolished in the limb and autonomic columns. Prospective limb and autonomic neurons instead take on a molecular identity like that of the thoracic hypaxial motor column, which Dasen and colleagues read as the ancestral motor neuron state. In the limb, motor axons appear to select trajectories and muscle targets at random.

</details>

FoxP1 sorts cells that are already motor neurons. It does not make them.

## Which half of the limb: the LIM code sets the first axon choice

The lateral motor column splits again, and the split is a clear case of a transcription factor code handing off to axon guidance. Lateral LMC neurons express Lim1 (Lhx1); medial ones retain Isl1.

Part of the split is set inside the column. Sockanathan and Jessell reported in 1998 that a retinoid made by early-born LMC neurons helps later-born neurons become lateral LMC, so some of the information comes from the motor neurons themselves. For how retinoic acid is received, see [nuclear receptors](/lessons/nuclear-receptors/).

**Predict.** Lateral LMC axons normally take a dorsal path in the limb, and medial LMC axons a ventral one. Kania, Johnson and Jessell removed Lim1 in mouse. What do the lateral LMC axons do without it?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

They lose their dorsal preference and select paths at random. Lim1 in the lateral set ensures that their axons take a dorsal trajectory in the limb (Kania, Johnson and Jessell, 2000). The limb has its say too: Lmx1b in dorsal limb mesenchyme controls both trajectories from outside, and without mesenchymal Lmx1b the choices of both divisions are disturbed.

</details>

Much of the downstream machinery is a set of Eph receptors and ephrins, working alongside other guidance systems, which [guidance cues and pioneers](/lessons/guidance-cues-and-pioneers/) and [topographic maps](/lessons/topographic-maps/) take up. Once the axons reach muscle, survival during the period of developmental cell death depends partly on support from the target, along with other cells such as glia, and NGF is not the general survival signal for motor neurons; see [the neurotrophic hypothesis](/lessons/neurotrophic-hypothesis/).

The order is worth holding onto. Position in the tube sets the progenitor domain. The domain sets the postmitotic code. Position along the cord sets the column. The column sets which receptors the axon carries. Only then does the axon meet the cues that steer it. The order is a simplification: the stages overlap (Hox patterning starts in progenitors), an axon meets several cues at once, and signals from the periphery can feed back on a motor neuron's gene expression.

## Making motor neurons in a dish

Run the layers forward and you have a protocol. Wichterle and colleagues reported in 2002 that mouse embryonic stem cells, given the signals that pattern the embryonic cord, became spinal progenitors and then motor neurons along a route that recapitulated the embryonic one. The cells populated an embryonic spinal cord after transplantation, extended axons and formed synapses with target muscles.

Two added molecules do most of the patterning work, roughly one for each axis. Retinoic acid pushes the cells to a caudal, spinal character rather than a forebrain one. A Hedgehog agonist, either Shh protein or a small molecule such as purmorphamine or SAG, ventralises them toward pMN. Then you wait, and Mnx1-positive neurons appear. Retinoic acid is not a dial for axial level, though. On its own it carries cells only as far as hindbrain and cervical Hox profiles, and more caudal identity needs earlier signals (fibroblast growth factor, Wnt and growth differentiation factor) before the retinoid is added. So this basic protocol gives mostly medial motor column cells, the one column in the table above that needs no FoxP1.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/motor-neuron-code/dish-protocol.webp" width={1200} height={584} alt="AI-generated illustration: a left-to-right strip in which mouse embryonic stem cells, treated with retinoic acid and a Hedgehog agonist, become spinal progenitors and then Mnx1-positive motor neurons, with an inset of a motor neuron reaching a muscle after transplantation into an embryo." caption="Mouse embryonic stem cells can be patterned toward spinal pMN progenitors with retinoic acid and Hedgehog signalling, then differentiate into Mnx1-positive motor neurons. Retinoic acid alone gives relatively rostral hindbrain and cervical Hox profiles; more caudal identities need earlier signals. The basic protocol yields mostly medial motor column neurons. The inset summarises a separate transplantation test: the neurons populated an embryonic spinal cord and formed synapses with muscle." />

Later work added control over subtype: manipulating Foxp1 can bias stem-cell-derived motor neurons toward limb-innervating LMC identity (Adams and colleagues, 2015; details in [Going deeper](#programming-limb-motor-neurons)). That is the Hox-and-FoxP1 layer, applied on purpose.

What the dish still does badly is the fine grain. You can get motor neurons, and you can bias them toward a column. Getting one named pool, the cells that innervate one muscle, is another matter, which is a fair measure of how much of the code is still unwritten.

## Putting it together

The guiding idea, once more in short: in this example, identity depends on which complexes form and in what ratio, and later, overlapping layers refine which kind of motor neuron the cell becomes. Here is the evidence, layer by layer.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lhx3 alone, or Lhx3 with Isl1, electroporated | chick | V2 genes alone; motor-neuron genes with Isl1 | the partner decides Lhx3's output | that Lhx3 is needed in mouse |
| Mnx1 or MNR2 electroporated | chick | motor-neuron differentiation; V2 programme repressed | Mnx1 is enough to start the programme | that it is needed |
| Olig1 and Olig2 deleted | mouse | motor neurons largely lost; no oligodendrocytes | the progenitor layer comes first | the separate roles of Olig1 and Olig2 |
| Isl1 deleted | mouse | no motor neurons | Isl1 is needed to make one | how Isl1 acts |
| Mnx1 deleted | mouse | made in normal numbers; transient V2 features | Mnx1 keeps the identity | — |

### Design problem

You can electroporate chick dorsal spinal cord, where Lhx3 is absent and Isl1 is confined to a small intermediate population of interneurons, and you can also look at an Isl1 knockout mouse. Predict the marker result for each of three conditions: (a) Lhx3 alone; (b) Isl1 plus Lhx3; (c) the ventral spinal cord of the Isl1 knockout mouse, where motor neurons would normally form.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

(a) Lhx3 alone leaves Lhx3 free to bind NLI, so you expect ectopic V2 interneuron genes such as Chx10, and no motor-neuron genes. (b) Isl1 plus Lhx3 allows the Isl1-Lhx3 complex to form, so you expect ectopic motor-neuron genes such as Mnx1. Interneuron genes should fall, but two separate constructs do not fix the ratio, and excess Lhx3 can still raise V2 genes. (c) Without Isl1 the complex cannot form, and motor neurons are not generated. One prediction ties all three together: the cell responds to the combination, not to either factor alone.

</details>

## Going deeper

### A fused Isl1-Lhx3 protein

A single fused Isl1-Lhx3 protein holds the two factors at a fixed one-to-one ratio in one molecule, with Lhx3 tethered to its Isl1 segment. Expect the same outcome as Isl1 plus Lhx3 given as separate constructs, and more reliably, since the ratio cannot drift. Lee and colleagues reported in 2012 that an Isl1-Lhx3 fusion induces motor-neuron genes while suppressing interneuron programmes, in the cord and in stem cells.

### The progenitor code in more detail

Mizuguchi and colleagues, misexpressing Olig2 and Neurogenin2 in chick, reached a conclusion similar to Novitch, Chen and Jessell's in the same year, 2001.

### Who reported the Olig mutants

Two groups reported Olig loss-of-function mice in 2002; the double-mutant lineage tracing in the lesson is from Zhou and Anderson. Keep the scope in mind: this is the mouse spinal cord, and Olig2 is the one required for motor-neuron and oligodendrocyte specification in the cord, while Olig1 contributes to oligodendrocyte development and maturation, especially in the brain.

### Programming limb motor neurons

Adams and colleagues in 2015 programmed limb-innervating LMC neurons from mouse and human embryonic stem cells by manipulating Foxp1. The mouse-derived cells preferentially projected toward limb muscle in explant and transplant assays; the human result was at the marker level.

### Is the code big enough?

Capacity is probably not the hard part. In principle, four LIM genes, each on or off, give 16 combinations, and six on-off factors would give 64, enough to label even 50 pools. Which combinations cells actually use, and how they are read into pool identity, is open, as is how much of pool identity is transcriptional at all.
