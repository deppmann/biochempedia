    profileSourceAnchor: "Henderson, Concerning the relationship between the strength of acids and their capacity to preserve neutrality, American Journal of Physiology 21:173-179 (1908); Sgambato, Prozzo and Sgambato's history of the equation, Giornale Italiano di Nefrologia 36(1) (2019)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      By the start of the twentieth century it was clear that blood stays close to neutral while
      the body keeps making acid, but not how a weak acid and its salt could hold it there.
      Henderson, at Harvard, treated the problem as one of mass action. For a weak acid and its
      salt in solution, mass action gives the hydrogen ion concentration as a constant multiplied
      by the ratio of acid to salt. In blood the acid side is set by the carbon dioxide dissolved
      in it and the salt is bicarbonate, so what fixes acidity is a ratio, not the amount of
      either partner. A later account describes Henderson as a theoretical analyst who used
      mathematics and graphs to describe how systems are organised. In 1916 Karl Hasselbalch rewrote the relation in Sorensen's pH notation, giving
      the logarithmic form used today. Henderson's relation made blood acidity something that
      could be calculated from two measurable quantities, and every blood-gas calculation in this
      lesson starts from it.

    story: >-
      Henderson's relation has a catch for anyone reading a blood sample. Bicarbonate moves with
      carbon dioxide, so a low bicarbonate on its own cannot say whether non-volatile acid was
      added or carbon dioxide was blown off. Astrup's equilibration method made carbon dioxide
      tension something the laboratory set rather than something it was handed: the blood was
      brought to equilibrium with gas of known carbon dioxide tension and its pH measured, so the
      sample's behaviour could be read at a chosen tension. Siggaard-Andersen, working with
      Astrup's method in the late 1950s, used this to define base excess: the amount of acid or
      base that would have to be added to the whole blood in vitro to bring its pH to 7.40 with
      carbon dioxide tension held at 40 mmHg. Because carbon dioxide is fixed by the definition,
      what is left is the non-respiratory part. The measure was debated for years, but it gave
      laboratories a single figure for the metabolic side of a sample, and later comparisons
      found bicarbonate, base-excess and strong-ion methods quantify that component virtually
      identically.

techniques:
  - name: "Blood-gas analysis with equilibration"
    blurb: >-
      Electrodes measure the pH and carbon dioxide tension of a blood sample, and bicarbonate is
      calculated from the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation. Equilibrating the sample with gas of
      known carbon dioxide tension, Astrup's method, separates the respiratory part of a pH change
      from the non-respiratory part and gives base excess.
  - name: "Clearance and timed urine collection"
    blurb: >-
      Measure plasma and urine concentrations together with urine flow, and compare what was
      filtered with what was excreted. Timed and 24-hour collections give titratable acid,
      ammonium and bicarbonate excretion, and so net acid excretion.
  - name: "Tubule microperfusion"
    blurb: >-
      Perfuse a single proximal tubule in situ, or an isolated collecting duct held between
      pipettes, with fluid of known composition, and measure bicarbonate, fluid or ammonia flux.
      Adding inhibitors one at a time divides a flux among the transporters that carry it.
  - name: "Knockout mice under acid or alkali loading"
    blurb: >-
      Delete one transporter gene and compare blood gases and urine with wild-type littermates,
      at baseline and after an acid or alkali load. A phenotype that appears only under loading
      shows what the protein is needed for when the system is pushed.

      0.03 mmol/L per mmHg, which converts pCO2 into dissolved CO2; it is not the pK.

**Adult human arterial plasma at rest holds about 24 millimoles of bicarbonate per litre and about 40 nanomoles of free hydrogen ions per litre.** Metabolism in a US adult on a typical diet adds roughly 50 mEq of non-volatile acid a day, yet pH stays near 7.40, held on three clocks: buffers in seconds, lungs in minutes, kidneys over days. [Water, pH and weak bonds](/lessons/water-weak-bonds/) gave you pH = pKa + log([A-]/[HA]), and [the kidney](/lessons/kidney/) the nephron; here both get numbers.

## A buffer that would be unremarkable in a bottle

A **volatile acid** leaves as a gas: carbon dioxide, removed by the lungs. A **non-volatile acid**, such as sulfuric acid from sulfur-containing amino acids or an organic acid such as lactic acid or a ketoacid, cannot be breathed off; it is buffered, then excreted by the kidney. A disturbance that starts with pCO2 is **respiratory**; one that starts with non-volatile acid or base is **metabolic**.

For human plasma at 37 C, with pCO2 in mmHg and bicarbonate in mmol/L:

> **pH = 6.1 + log10( [HCO3-] / (0.03 x pCO2) )**

The 0.03 is the solubility of CO2 in plasma, in mmol/L per mmHg. The 6.1 is an apparent pK for the combined reaction CO2 + H2O = HCO3- + H+ in plasma at 37 C, hydration and ionization together; carbonic acid by itself is a much stronger acid. Henderson set out the mass-action relation in 1908, and in 1916 Hasselbalch rewrote it in Sorensen's pH notation ([carrying oxygen and carbon dioxide](/lessons/oxygen-transport/) tells his work with Bohr and Krogh). At a pCO2 of 40 mmHg, dissolved CO2 is 0.03 x 40 = 1.2 mmol/L, the ratio is 24 / 1.2 = 20, and pH = 6.1 + 1.30 = 7.40. pH follows the ratio, not either number alone.

Now the puzzle. A pK of 6.1 sits 1.3 units below 7.40, and in a closed container the pair buffers no better than any other weak acid pair. Its power comes from being open to CO2: added acid turns bicarbonate into CO2, the lungs remove it, and the denominator does not climb. Add 10 mmol/L of strong acid to a made-up plasma with no other buffers. Sealed in a syringe, bicarbonate falls to 14 and dissolved CO2 rises to 11.2 mmol/L, so pH falls to about 6.20. With pCO2 held at 40 mmHg, dissolved CO2 stays at 1.2 and pH falls only to about 7.17. Alveolar ventilation holds that denominator ([ventilation and perfusion](/lessons/ventilation-perfusion/)).

{/* SIMULATION */}

Uncatalysed interconversion of CO2 and bicarbonate takes over a minute in vitro, while blood spends about one second in a pulmonary capillary; carbonic anhydrase closes that gap.

Two limits follow. Plasma bicarbonate cannot be set independently of pCO2, so it mixes respiratory and metabolic effects, and the equation says nothing about other acids. **Base excess**, introduced in the late 1950s by Siggaard-Andersen with Astrup's equilibration method, measures the metabolic part directly: the acid or base needed to bring whole blood in vitro to pH 7.40 at a pCO2 of 40 mmHg. Bicarbonate, base-excess and Stewart strong-ion methods quantify that component virtually identically but explain it differently, and comparisons of Stewart's approach with the traditional one have been inconsistent.

## Where the protons actually go

Follow an acute load of non-volatile acid. Outside cells, bicarbonate takes up protons and becomes CO2 for the lungs, with plasma proteins and phosphate taking a smaller share; over hours cells and bone take up a further share. In a tissue capillary, CO2 entering a red cell is hydrated by carbonic anhydrase, most of the bicarbonate leaves on the Cl-/HCO3- exchanger AE1 as chloride enters, and hemoglobin buffers nearly all of the H+ formed. The Bohr and Haldane effects that couple this to oxygen are in [carrying oxygen and carbon dioxide](/lessons/oxygen-transport/).

{/* DIAGRAM: red cell in a systemic capillary: CO2 in, carbonic anhydrase, HCO3- out on AE1 as Cl- enters, H+ on hemoglobin */}

Intracellular buffering only limits how far and how fast intracellular pH moves; returning it to its set point takes acid-base transporters ([membrane transport](/lessons/membrane-transport/)). In cultured neonatal mouse calvariae (skull bones) in vitro, acute metabolic acidosis led bone to take up hydrogen ions into its mineral phase ([calcium, phosphate and bone](/lessons/calcium-phosphate-bone/)).

Buffering is not removal. Every proton a buffer holds is still in the body, and every bicarbonate consumed is gone until the kidney makes another. In a study of 1,082 US adults aged 50 to 74, mean net endogenous acid production from 24-hour urine was about 47 to 48 mEq per day (interquartile range about 36 to 64); ketoacids add to such loads ([insulin, glucagon and glucose](/lessons/insulin-glucagon-glucose/)). Whether lactate formation itself releases protons in working muscle is contested, and [exercise physiology](/lessons/exercise-physiology/) takes up that debate.

## The proximal tubule gets the bicarbonate back

Human kidneys make about 160 to 170 L of ultrafiltrate a day ([glomerular filtration](/lessons/glomerular-filtration/)). At 24 mmol/L, 165 L carries about 4,000 mmol of bicarbonate, roughly eighty times the day's non-volatile acid; losing 3 percent would cost more base than that acid consumes. The proximal tubule takes back 60 to 70 percent of filtered water and NaCl and a greater proportion of the NaHCO3.

Reclaiming is indirect. Apical NHE3 exchanges Na+ in for H+ out, and a V-type H+-ATPase secretes more H+. In the lumen, carbonic anhydrase IV, anchored on the luminal membrane, turns H+ and filtered HCO3- into CO2 and water, fast enough for fluid that touches the cells for under a second. CO2 enters the cell, cytosolic carbonic anhydrase II rehydrates it, the H+ is secreted again, and the HCO3- leaves across the basolateral membrane on the Na+-dependent cotransporter NBCe1. One bicarbonate vanishes from the lumen and one appears in the blood; no new base is made. The segment table and carbonic anhydrase inhibition as a probe are in [tubular transport and diuretics](/lessons/tubular-transport-diuretics/).

{/* DIAGRAM: proximal tubule cell, lumen to interstitium: apical NHE3 and V-type pump, luminal carbonic anhydrase IV, CO2 entering, cytosolic carbonic anhydrase II, basolateral NBCe1 */}

NHE3 knockout mice survive mildly acidotic; their in situ microperfused proximal tubules absorbed 54 percent less bicarbonate and 63 percent less fluid, and a significant part of the remainder was carried by a bafilomycin-sensitive H+-ATPase. In humans, William Sly and colleagues identified carbonic anhydrase II deficiency (1983) as the defect in an inherited syndrome that includes failed renal acidification. Boron's group has reported that two Na+-coupled "bicarbonate" transporters, expressed in cultured cells, carry carbonate (CO3 2-).

That the tubule secretes acid at all was once in doubt. An older view held that the tubule acidified urine by reabsorbing buffer base, filtered bicarbonate and dibasic phosphate, leaving the acid forms behind; then titratable acid excreted could not exceed what that reabsorption allows. Robert F. Pitts and colleagues tested this in 1945 to 1949, in clearance studies of dogs and of people (as summarised in 1956 by Orloff), setting filtered loads against excretion. In metabolic acidosis, titratable acid excretion exceeded what reabsorbing filtered bicarbonate or dibasic phosphate could explain. H+ was being added to tubular fluid, and Pitts proposed it was extruded in exchange for filtered sodium. Orloff stressed there was no direct evidence yet for ion exchange in tubule cells; these were whole-kidney measurements, and the molecular exchanger came decades later.

## New bicarbonate: ammonium and titratable acid

Replacing bicarbonate that acid consumed takes new bicarbonate, made in proportion to acid excreted:

> **net acid excretion = titratable acid + NH4+ - HCO3-**

About one-third to one-half is normally **titratable acid**, protons carried on urinary buffers, mainly phosphate. The other half to two-thirds is ammonium: proximal cells metabolise each glutamine to 2 NH4+ and 2 HCO3-, the bicarbonate going to the blood. Only excreted ammonium counts, because ammonium returned to the blood is handled by the liver in a bicarbonate-consuming step ([protein turnover](/lessons/protein-turnover-amino-acid-catabolism/)); here ammonia is an acid-base carrier, not the nitrogen waste of [osmoregulation](/lessons/osmoregulation/). People on a typical Western diet excrete 30 to 40 mmol of ammonium a day. In acidosis the proximal tubule takes up more glutamine and citrate and recovers less phosphate, raising both terms.

In the collecting duct, **type-A intercalated cells** pump H+ into the lumen through a V-type H+-ATPase and return new HCO3- to the blood on basolateral AE1. **Non-type-A cells**, near mirror images, secrete bicarbonate through luminal pendrin; isolated cortical collecting ducts from alkali-loaded pendrin-knockout mice failed to secrete it (Royaux and colleagues, 2001). Urine pH bottoms out near 4.5, a 1000-to-1 H+ gradient from plasma, so buffers must carry the acid. In acute acidosis distal H+ secretion rises at once while ammonium production lags; in chronic acidosis ammonium excretion is high and urine pH can rise to about 6. Urine pH is a poor index of ammonium excretion, and no master renal pH sensor has been identified.

The textbook account was diffusion and trapping: NH3 crosses membranes freely and is trapped as NH4+ in acid fluid, so deleting an ammonia transporter should matter little. Sophie Biver and colleagues (2008) acid-loaded Rhcg knockout mice beside wild-type controls and microperfused their collecting ducts. The knockouts could not acidify urine normally because ammonium excretion was impaired, and their ducts showed reduced apical NH3 permeability and impaired transepithelial NH3 transport. Protein-mediated transport is required in the mouse collecting duct. It did not show whether Rhcg carries NH3 or NH4+, says nothing about humans, and, tested under acid loading, does not show what sets baseline excretion.

The human counterpart is the pump's B1 subunit, whose gene Karet and colleagues found mutated in families with distal renal tubular acidosis and hearing loss. Predict the chain. Apical H+ pumping falls, so the duct builds a smaller gradient and urine pH stays higher; less phosphate is titrated and less ammonium held in the lumen, so net acid excretion, new bicarbonate, plasma bicarbonate and pH fall. Rising: retained acid buffered elsewhere, bone included; ventilation, so pCO2 falls; and plasma chloride, replacing lost bicarbonate, so the anion gap is unchanged. In carbonic anhydrase II deficiency, by contrast, the acidosis is proximal, distal or combined, so proximal reclamation can fail as well.

## Reading the numbers, and what the rules are worth

**Worked problem.** In this made-up example for human plasma at 37 C, take pK 6.1, solubility 0.03 and steady state, and compare against bicarbonate 24 mmol/L, pCO2 40 mmHg, pH 7.40 and albumin 4.0 g/dL. The sample: pCO2 27 mmHg, HCO3- 12, Na+ 140 and Cl- 104 mmol/L, albumin 2.0 g/dL.

1. Dissolved CO2 = 0.03 x 27 = 0.81 mmol/L; 12 / 0.81 = 14.8; pH = 6.1 + 1.17 = 7.27. [H+] = 10^(9 - 7.27) = about 54 nmol/L, against 40 at pH 7.40.
2. pH is low with a low bicarbonate. A primary rise in pCO2 would raise bicarbonate, so non-volatile acid moved first, and the low pCO2 is moving pH back toward 7.40.
3. An empirical fit gives expected pCO2 = 1.5 x 12 + 8 = 26 mmHg; the measured 27 lies within the scatter one review attaches to it. A pCO2 of 36 would mean ventilation below the fit, pointing to a second, respiratory process.
4. Anion gap = [Na+] - [Cl-] - [HCO3-] = 140 - 104 - 12 = 24 mmol/L. An acid whose anion the panel does not measure, such as a ketoacid or lactate, consumes bicarbonate and leaves its anion, so the gap rises; base lost directly is replaced by chloride, leaving the gap unchanged.
5. Albumin is the main unmeasured anion. Corrected gap = 24 + 2.5 x (4.0 - 2.0) = 29 mmol/L, the same as 24 + 0.25 x (40 - 20) with albumin in g/L.
6. Against a made-up comparison gap of 12 (140 - 104 - 24), the gap rose 12 (17 corrected) as bicarbonate fell 12, ratios of 1.0 and 1.4. That ratio genuinely varies, larger for lactate than for ketoacid anions, so alone it cannot establish a second process.

The gap's reference range is not fixed either: it shifted down in one laboratory when ion-selective electrodes raised measured chloride (Winter and colleagues, 1990), so each laboratory's own range applies.

Compensation follows mechanism. In metabolic acidosis pCO2 falls. In respiratory disturbances the kidney moves bicarbonate in the same direction as pCO2, slowly: for a primary rise in pCO2, compiled fits give about 1 mmol/L per 10 mmHg acutely and about 3 per 10 mmHg over days, and altitude's falling bicarbonate is told in [control of breathing](/lessons/control-of-breathing/). Compensation is partial, so pH does not return to 7.40, and the fits are regressions on observed people, not laws.

### Design problem

In the chicken, blood from a leg vein drains directly into the peritubular region of the same-side kidney, so an agent infused into one leg reaches one kidney's tubule cells while the other kidney is a control in the same bird. Orloff's 1956 review reported, as unpublished observations, that acetic acid added to blood perfusing one side raised local CO2 tension (it reacts with plasma bicarbonate) and, on that side only, lowered potassium excretion (already raised by a potassium sulfate infusion) and raised urine H+ concentration; it credits the method to Sperber (1946). (a) What does the paired design buy that an intravenous infusion into a mammal cannot? (b) What shows whole-body acid-base state did not change? (c) Which rival explanation remains, and how would you exclude it?

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

(a) Each bird is its own control: systemic pH and pCO2, filtered load, blood pressure and anaesthesia are shared, and only the peritubular environment differs.

(b) Arterial pH and pCO2 before, during and after, with urine collected simultaneously and separately from each ureter, and flow and electrolytes measured on each side.

(c) The acid also lowers peritubular bicarbonate and may change sodium delivery and local blood flow. A CO2-free acid, an equimolar sodium salt infused the same way, and a flow measurement on each side separate "CO2 and H+ inside the cell" from "less blood or sodium delivered". Even then the potassium result suggests H+ and K+ secretion share a driving force without naming a transporter, and one bird species cannot be generalised to mammals. The modern form of the logic is the isolated perfused tubule, with the bath set directly.
