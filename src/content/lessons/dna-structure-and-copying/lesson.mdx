## Explain: a twisted ladder of four bases

<WordBank terms={['nucleotide', 'base', 'double helix', 'covalent bond', 'hydrogen bond', 'base pairing', 'gene', 'chromosome', 'replication', 'template', 'enzyme', 'nucleoid']} />

DNA is a polymer, a long chain built from small repeating units. Each unit is a **nucleotide**, a building block made of three parts: a sugar called deoxyribose, a phosphate group and a base. A **base** is the part of a nucleotide that carries information, and DNA uses four of them: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G). Nucleotides are named by their base, so you will hear about “an A” or “a G.”

Along one strand, the sugar of one nucleotide is joined to the phosphate of the next by a **covalent bond**, a strong bond in which atoms share electrons. That makes a sugar-phosphate backbone with the bases sticking out to one side.

Two strands line up with their bases facing each other in the middle. A **hydrogen bond** is a weak attraction between molecules or parts of molecules, and hydrogen bonds hold each pair of facing bases together. The two strands then twist around each other into a **double helix**, a shape like a ladder twisted along its length. The backbones are the sides of the ladder, and the base pairs are the rungs.

**Base pairing** is the rule that A always pairs with T and C always pairs with G across the two strands. Picture a zipper whose teeth only close with the right partner. The analogy breaks because a zipper’s teeth carry no message, while the order of DNA’s bases does.

{/* DIAGRAM: a short stretch of double helix untwisted into a ladder. Number: 1 sugar, 2 phosphate, 3 one base, 4 covalent bond along the backbone, 5 hydrogen bonds between an A-T pair, 6 the twisted double helix */}

The two strands also run in opposite directions, like two lanes of a road. Each strand has a distinct “head” end and “tail” end, and the strands lie head to tail.

The order of the bases is the information. A **gene** is a region of DNA whose base order, in most genes, codes for a protein; a few genes code for RNA molecules that do jobs of their own. A **chromosome** is one very long DNA molecule wrapped around proteins, and it carries many genes. Only about 1 to 2 percent of human DNA codes for proteins. Other stretches control genes, help hold chromosomes in shape, or have jobs nobody has found yet. The genetic code that turns base order into protein is almost the same in every living thing, which is why bacteria can be engineered to make some human proteins, such as insulin. [From DNA to protein](/lessons/protein-synthesis/) shows how that code is read.

Every cell that grows and divides carries DNA. In plants, animals and fungi most of it sits inside the nucleus, with small extra pieces in organelles such as mitochondria. Bacteria keep their DNA in a **nucleoid**, a region of the cell with no membrane around it.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** DNA is two strands of nucleotides twisted into a double helix, with sugar-phosphate sides and A-T and C-G base pairs as the rungs.</Support>

## Explain: copying DNA one strand at a time

**Replication** is the copying of a DNA molecule before a cell divides. Because A must pair with T and C with G, each strand carries everything needed to rebuild its partner. Each old strand works as a **template**, a pattern that sets the order of the new strand.

Copying is done by enzymes. An **enzyme** is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up. The steps, in order:

1. An enzyme unwinds the helix and unzips it, breaking the hydrogen bonds between the base pairs. The covalent backbones stay whole.
2. Free nucleotides float nearby in the cell. Each one pairs with its matching base on an open strand: A with T, C with G.
3. Another enzyme, DNA polymerase, joins the new nucleotides into a strand with covalent bonds.
4. The result is two DNA molecules. Each has one old strand and one new strand, and both have the same base order as the original.

Why only one direction? DNA polymerase can attach a new nucleotide only to one particular end of a growing strand, the tail end. So every new strand grows the same way, adding at its tail. The two templates point in opposite directions. One new strand can grow smoothly toward the unzipping point. The other grows away from the unzipping point, so it must be built in short pieces, each started fresh as more template opens up. Another enzyme then joins the pieces.

{/* DIAGRAM: a replication fork. Number: 1 the unzipping point, 2 an old strand used as a template, 3 a free nucleotide pairing with it, 4 a new strand growing at its tail end, 5 short pieces on the other side, 6 two finished molecules each with one old and one new strand */}

How close to “almost perfect” is it? DNA polymerase checks each new base and removes most wrong ones, a step called proofreading. Repair enzymes catch many errors that slip past. A textbook estimate is about one uncorrected mistake for every billion bases copied. Across roughly 6 billion base pairs, that leaves roughly a handful to a dozen changes per copy. Those rare changes are mutations, and [mutations can change proteins](/lessons/protein-synthesis/).

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Enzymes unzip DNA, each old strand serves as a template for a new partner strand built by base pairing, and the copy is two identical molecules, each half old and half new.</Support>

## Elaborate: do the bases really pair up?

### Design problem

Every cell in an animal’s body grows from one fertilized egg by copying DNA. Plan an investigation to test whether DNA from different tissues of one animal has the same base composition, and whether it differs from another species.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- Question and hypothesis: If cells copy their DNA accurately, then liver and muscle DNA from the same chicken will have the same percentages of A, T, G and C, while wheat DNA will differ.
- Independent variable: the tissue (chicken liver, chicken muscle) and the species (chicken, wheat).
- Dependent variable: the percentage of each base, measured by a lab that sequences the DNA and counts every base.
- Constants: the same DNA extraction method, the same lab and machine, the same counting software.
- Control: a DNA sample whose base percentages are already known, run alongside to check the method.
- Repeated trials and sample size: tissue from at least three chickens and three wheat plants, each measured twice, then a mean for each tissue and species.
- Ethics and safety: use tissue from chickens already sold for food, so no animal is harmed for the study, and wear gloves around raw meat.

If the two chicken tissues differ from each other by no more than repeated measurements of the same sample differ, and wheat differs by more, the hypothesis is supported. A large difference between chicken tissues would point to a problem with the samples or the method before anything else.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

The 2 metres of DNA fit because the molecule is extremely thin and is wound tightly around proteins. The near-perfect copying comes from the structure itself. Each strand is a template for the other, base pairing allows only one correct partner at each spot, and proofreading removes most of the rare mistakes.

**Science practices you used:** making predictions and testing them with a model, naming where a model or analogy breaks, reading a data table and comparing values, making a claim from evidence with reasoning, and planning an investigation with controls and repeated trials.
