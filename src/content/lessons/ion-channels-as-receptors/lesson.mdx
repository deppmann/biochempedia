techniques:
  - name: "Patch clamp"
    blurb: >-
      A glass pipette with a tip about a micrometre across is pressed against a cell and sealed
      to the membrane with slight suction. The seal has a resistance above a gigohm, so little current
      leaks around the rim and the picoamp current of one channel can be recorded.
      Variants record from the whole cell or from a patch pulled off the cell.
  - name: "Intracellular microelectrode recording"
    blurb: >-
      A fine glass electrode is pushed into a cell to read the voltage across its membrane. Fatt
      and Katz used it in frog muscle at the spot where the nerve ends, and it showed the small
      spontaneous steps of voltage that led to the idea of transmitter packets.
  - name: "Expression cloning"
    blurb: >-
      A library of DNA copies of a tissue's messenger RNAs is split into pools, and each pool is
      expressed in cells that lack the function you seek. A pool that gives the response is
      divided again until one clone is left. The capsaicin receptor was found this way, with a
      rise in calcium as the readout.
  - name: "Structure determination of channels"
    blurb: >-
      X-ray crystallography and electron cryo-microscopy both give the positions of atoms in a
      channel protein. Crystallography of KcsA showed the selectivity filter. Electron
      cryo-microscopy, which needs no crystal, gave the structure of TRPV1.

    choices:
      - "A channel lets ions run down their gradient in a pore"
      - "A channel spends ATP to push ions up their own gradient"
      - "A channel moves ions more slowly than a transporter does"
      - "A channel binds ions and carries them over a few at a time"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      An open channel is a pore, and ions move through it down their electrochemical gradient at
      millions per second. Spending ATP to push ions uphill is what the pump does. Binding ions
      and changing shape to carry a few across at a time is also how a transporter works, not a
      channel. A channel is far faster than a transporter, not slower.
  - stem: "The sodium ion is smaller than the potassium ion. Why does it pass so poorly through the selectivity filter of a potassium channel?"
    choices:
      - "It needs ATP to cross the filter, and the channel has none"
      - "Its shell of water makes it too wide to enter the filter at all"
      - "Its positive charge is repelled by arginines that line the filter"
      - "It does not sit in the filter's oxygen sites the way potassium does"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Potassium sheds its water to enter the filter, and the backbone carbonyl oxygens stand in
      for that water at four sites in a row. Ions pass in single file, each arrival pushing the
      next one on. Sodium binds differently: in crystal structures it sits in the plane of an
      oxygen ring, still partly wet, and it does not keep this relay going. So sodium can enter
      the filter, but it is not passed along as potassium is. A water shell too wide to enter is
      the sieve idea, and it fails because the filter strips water from the ions it passes. The
      filter is lined by oxygen atoms and not by arginines, which belong to the S4 voltage
      sensor. A channel spends no ATP; ions run through it down their gradient.
  - stem: "Which feature of the S4 helix lets a voltage-gated channel sense the membrane voltage?"
    choices:
      - "A loop that shuts the pore soon after it has opened"
      - "A ring of backbone oxygen atoms that grips one ion"
      - "A bound magnesium ion that leaves on depolarisation"
      - "A run of positive residues that move in the field"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      S4 carries a positively charged residue at about every third position. A change in voltage
      is a change in the electric field, which pushes on those charges and moves the helix. A
      ring of backbone oxygen atoms is the selectivity filter, which chooses the ion and is not
      the voltage sensor. A magnesium ion that leaves on depolarisation describes the block of the
      NMDA receptor, not a voltage sensor. A loop that shuts the pore after opening is the
      inactivation gate of the sodium channel.
  - stem: "A neuron is held at −70 mV while glutamate and glycine are applied. Its AMPA receptors pass current, but its NMDA receptors pass almost none. Which change would let the NMDA receptors conduct?"
    choices:
      - "Adding more glutamate to the bath"
      - "Depolarising the cell to −20 mV"
      - "Hyperpolarising the cell to −90 mV"
      - "Removing calcium from the bath"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      At −70 mV a magnesium ion from outside sits in the NMDA receptor pore. Depolarising the
      cell drives it out, and current flows. Adding more glutamate does not help, because the
      gate is already open and the pore is blocked. Hyperpolarising the cell, which makes the
      inside more negative, pulls magnesium in harder. Removing calcium takes away one of the
      ions that would flow and does nothing about the block.
  - stem: "A nerve fibre is bathed in tetrodotoxin and then given a small depolarising current. What do you expect to record?"
    choices:
      - "A normal resting voltage and a longer action potential"
      - "A normal resting voltage and no action potential"
      - "A resting voltage near zero and no action potential"
      - "A resting voltage near zero and repeated action potentials"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Tetrodotoxin plugs the outer mouth of voltage-gated sodium channels, so the positive
      feedback that makes an action potential cannot start. The resting voltage is set mostly by
      potassium channels and by the gradients that the pumps maintain, which the toxin does not touch, so it stays normal.
      That rules out both answers with a resting voltage near zero. A longer action potential is
      what blocking potassium channels would give, because the return to rest would be slow.

**One open ion channel passes about ten million ions a second, and open channels shift membrane voltage in about a millisecond.** The first film showed why an **ion channel**—a gated pore that skips G proteins and kinases—acts so fast; here we quantify flow, inspect single-channel and structural evidence, and see how gates and pore blocks govern signaling. Channels work in every cell type, well beyond neurons: you already met Orai in the T cell and the IP3 receptor on the calcium store.

> **The one idea.** An open channel pulls membrane voltage toward its ion’s equilibrium potential; signals open the gate, and pore blocks control whether ions pass. **Scope:** This rule covers passive ion flow through channels along pump-built electrochemical gradients. A multi-ion channel pulls voltage toward a value between its ions’ equilibrium potentials; Ca2+ entry also carries a chemical signal, and blockers such as ketamine can reduce opening as well as plug the pore.

## Putting numbers on flow: equilibrium potential and driving force

Transporters such as the Na+/K+ pump bind ions, change shape and spend ATP to move a few hundred uphill each second (lesson `membrane-structure-function`), whereas an open channel lets millions run downhill through a pore.

Flow follows the **electrochemical gradient**, the combined push of concentration difference and membrane voltage. Inside a resting neuron voltage sits near −70 mV (other cells differ). Where concentration push and electrical pull cancel is an ion's **equilibrium potential**, E, given by the Nernst equation:

> **E = (61.5 mV ÷ z) × log10 (concentration outside ÷ concentration inside), at 37 °C**

Here z is the ion's charge (+1 for Na+ and K+, +2 for Ca2+, −1 for Cl−). Typical E values sit near −90 mV for K+, near +60 mV for Na+ and above +120 mV for Ca2+. The electrical push follows from one subtraction, the **driving force**:

> **driving force = membrane voltage − E**

Use a two-step check so charge sign never misleads you. First, an open channel pulls membrane voltage toward E (or, for a multi-ion channel, between its ions' E's), with strength set by the gap between membrane voltage and E. Second, match the ion's charge: cations enter to make the inside less negative and leave to make it more negative; anions move the opposite way.

At −70 mV, Na+ sits 130 mV from its E (+60 mV), so opening Na+ channels drives a strong inward rush that **depolarises** the cell (makes voltage less negative). K+ sits 20 mV from its E (−90 mV): at rest its gentle outward leak balances small inward leaks to hold −70 mV, while opening extra K+ channels pulls voltage toward −90 mV.

Very few ions need to cross to swing voltage, so bulk concentrations barely shift and nothing has to be made. Voltage is the message, and when the ion is calcium it is an intracellular messenger too (lesson `calcium-signaling`).

## Watching one channel switch: patch-clamp evidence

Current through one open channel is a few picoamps (trillionths of an amp; 1.6 pA is about 10⁷ monovalent ions per second). In Göttingen, Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann pressed a glass pipette tip against a frog muscle fibre and recorded current through the membrane patch under it—the **patch clamp**. Their 1976 trace stepped cleanly between zero and one fixed level as a single acetylcholine-gated channel opened and shut.

To a good approximation a channel is either shut or open, and signal strength is set by **open probability**, the share of time spent open. (Going deeper covers their gigohm seal and Bernard Katz's transmitter packets.)

**Predict.** In a patch holding one acetylcholine-gated channel, you leave the acetylcholine unchanged but shift the held voltage so the driving force on the ions is halved. What happens to the height of each square step, and to the time the channel spends open?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Each step is about half as tall, because the current through one open pore is its conductance times the driving force. The share of time spent open stays roughly the same, because acetylcholine, not the driving force, mostly sets it.

</details>

## How a pore passes potassium and excludes smaller sodium

A potassium channel passes up to about a hundred million K+ ions a second yet admits fewer than one Na+ per thousand K+, even though Na+ is smaller (radius 0.95 Å versus 1.33 Å for K+).

**Predict.** If a potassium channel were a rigid sieve wide enough for K+, what would happen to Na+?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Smaller Na+ would slip through at least as easily as K+, so a size sieve fails.

</details>

Selectivity is an energy trade. In water, K+ and Na+ wear coats of water molecules with oxygens turned inward; stripping that coat costs energy, so an ion enters a narrow pore only if the pore repays it.

In 1998 Roderick MacKinnon's laboratory solved KcsA, a potassium channel from a soil bacterium. Four identical subunits surround one pore narrowing near its outer end to a **selectivity filter** 12 Å long, too tight for hydrated ions. Rings of backbone carbonyl oxygens line the filter, replacing K+'s water shell at almost no net cost.

In the 1998 account, a stiff filter could not close around smaller Na+, so Na+ could not touch all oxygens of a ring and paid too high a dehydration cost. Later work refined that model: the filter flexes, and selectivity rests on the electrical character of its carbonyl oxygens plus four sites in a row. Na+ can enter and bind, still partly wet, without being passed along as K+ is.

The same four sites explain speed: K+ ions sit in single file, and repulsion pushes one out as the next arrives. (Going deeper notes the open debate on occupancy and water, and that Na+ and Ca2+ filters differ.)

Once a pore selects its ion, what opens the gate—and what can still block the pore—is the subject of the second film.

## Three keys for one gate

Recall the second half of the guiding rule: signals open the gate, and pore blocks control whether ions pass. The second film outlined three gating keys—ligands, voltage and physical stimuli; here are the receptor families and circuits they run.

**Chemical ligands, outside and inside.** Both **ionotropic** and **metabotropic** receptors use ion channels. An ionotropic receptor *is* the channel: muscle acetylcholine receptors (lesson `receptor-field-guide`) and neuronal glutamate channels let Na+ in and excite, whereas GABA-A receptors pass Cl− and, in most mature neurons, hold voltage down. A metabotropic receptor is a G-protein-coupled receptor that acts on a channel via an intracellular messenger. Four channels from earlier lessons open from inside: the heart's K+ channel by Gβγ (lesson `gpcrs-and-g-proteins`), the rod's channel by cGMP (lesson `senses-and-cgmp`), and the IP3 receptor and Orai (lesson `calcium-signaling`).

**Voltage and the action potential.** Voltage-gated Na+, K+ and Ca2+ channels each have four voltage sensors whose S4 helix carries a positive charge at about every third position; a voltage across a membrane is an electric field, which pushes on charge, so depolarisation shifts S4 outward to open the gate. In neurons this drives the **action potential** (nerve impulse). Depolarisation past **threshold** triggers positive feedback (lesson `signaling-circuits`): Na+ enters, depolarises further and opens more Na+ channels, driving voltage toward E of sodium within about a millisecond. An inner loop on each Na+ channel then shuts the pore (**inactivation**), while slower K+ channels open and restore voltage. Inward Na+ current spreads along the **axon** (the nerve cell's long fibre), while inactivated channels behind keep the impulse from turning back.

**Predict.** If a mutation keeps the inner inactivation loop of voltage-gated Na+ channels from shutting after opening, how does an action potential change?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Depolarisation starts normally, but Na+ keeps flowing in, so the spike is prolonged and repolarisation is slowed. The membrane behind the impulse also lacks its inactivated channels, so it is less protected from being re-excited.

</details>

**Physical stimuli from the world.** TRP-family channels sense surroundings such as heat directly. Work out the skin's heat sensor before reading its name.

### Design problem

Design a skin nerve-ending detector that turns heat above about 43 °C into a nerve impulse within milliseconds. What protein do you need, which ions move and which way, and how does a small local shift become a travelling impulse? Then explain why a chilli pepper feels hot.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Milliseconds favour a direct channel gated by heat that passes cations, calcium most readily. Near −70 mV, Na+ and Ca2+ sit far from their E's, so both flow in and depolarise the ending. Because that local change fades with distance, place voltage-gated Na+ channels nearby: crossing threshold fires a full-height action potential along the axon. Capsaicin opens the same heat-gated channel, sending identical impulses that the brain reads as burning heat even though your mouth is no hotter.

</details>

One real version, and one of the skin's detectors of painful heat, is **TRPV1**, cloned in David Julius's laboratory in 1997 by screening sensory-neuron DNA libraries for capsaicin sensitivity. Capsaicin binds a pocket in TRPV1's membrane-spanning region to open the same gate heat opens.

## Coincidence detection and plugging a pore

As the second film showed, at synapses (junctions between nerve cells) the **NMDA receptor** has a second lock: at rest a Mg2+ ion from outside is drawn into its open pore and sticks there. So it conducts strongly only when two things happen together: the cell before the synapse has released glutamate, and the cell after it is already depolarised. It carries three further features. First, glutamate opens its gate only when glycine or D-serine is also bound. Second, once the cell is depolarised, usually because nearby AMPA receptors have let Na+ in, Mg2+ is expelled and the pore admits Ca2+ with Na+; Ca2+ acts as an intracellular messenger (lesson `calcium-signaling`) linking coincident activity to the nucleus and memory (a later lesson). Third, scaffolds anchor synaptic receptors in place: **PSD-95** grips NMDA receptor tails via PDZ domains (lesson `protein-interaction-modules`), holding them opposite glutamate release.

Because a channel is a hole, toxins and drugs silence it by plugging it. **Tetrodotoxin (TTX)**, carried by pufferfish, lodges in the outer mouth of nerve and skeletal-muscle voltage-gated Na+ channels; Na+ cannot enter, no action potential starts, and paralysis follows. Edmund "Butch" Brodie at the University of Virginia studies rough-skinned newts carrying tetrodotoxin and the garter snakes that eat them: resistant snakes have altered amino acids at the outer pore mouth where the toxin grips poorly.

**Ketamine**, an anaesthetic and drug of abuse, enters the open NMDA pore to block it from within and also makes the channel open less often.

**Predict.** You apply ketamine to a resting neuron without glutamate, then wash ketamine completely away before adding glutamate and depolarising the cell. Does ketamine block the response? What if ketamine is present during glutamate plus depolarisation?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Washout at rest leaves the later response intact: with NMDA gates closed, ketamine cannot enter the pore. Present during glutamate and depolarisation, ketamine enters the open, unblocked pore to stop conduction (and reduces opening).

</details>

## Going deeper

### Gigohm seals and transmitter packets

Early patch recordings were buried in noise from current leaking around the rim between glass and membrane. By Neher's account in his Nobel lecture, around 1980 he and Sakmann "noticed by chance" that slight suction tightened the seal more than a hundredfold—from millions of ohms to billions (gigohms)—so picoamp steps stood clear (fast K+ channels reach ~10⁸ ions/s, ten times a typical 1.6 pA channel). Earlier, in the 1950s, Bernard Katz and colleagues showed at the nerve-muscle junction that acetylcholine is released in packets of roughly constant size, each opening many channels at once.

### Open questions in selectivity filters

How many K+ ions occupy the KcsA filter at once, and whether water sits between them, is still argued. Sodium and calcium channels build their selectivity filters differently.

### Borrowing channels as tools

Güler and colleagues (2012) engineered mice expressing TRPV1 in chosen neurons, so a dose of capsaicin switches those neurons on.
