summary: >-
  In a bipolar cortical neuron's stop-and-go climb, the front goes first, and the cell has moved only when its nucleus catches up, so migration is measured at the nucleus, not the tip. An excitatory cortical neuron is born near the fluid-filled ventricle and must travel outward through the developing wall to reach its layer. This lesson follows that trip. It covers the two ways a neuron moves radially, the glial fibre it climbs, and the "two stroke" cycle in which the centrosome goes ahead and the nucleus is brought up after it. Afterwards you can connect LIS1, dynein, doublecortin and Cdk5 to that cycle and to human lissencephaly, and judge what a live-imaging experiment can and cannot show.

scientists:
  - name: "Pasko Rakic"
    contribution: >-
      Reconstructed young neurons from serial electron micrographs of fetal monkey cerebral
      cortex, found them pressed along radial glial fibres, and inferred that the fibres guide
      neurons outward to the cortical plate.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Rakic, J. Comp. Neurol. 145:61 (1972), 'Mode of cell migration to the superficial layers of fetal monkey neocortex'."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Rakic worked from fixed tissue, so he could never watch a cell move. What he could do was
      follow one cell through many thin sections. In fetal rhesus monkey cerebral cortex he
      combined Golgi staining with serial-section electron microscopy and rebuilt young neurons
      and their surroundings in three dimensions. The neurons lay in the intermediate zone with a
      long leading process pointing toward the surface, and along their length they were pressed
      against slender fibres of radial glia, cells whose processes span the wall from the
      ventricle to the pia. Fixed tissue cannot show motion, so the argument was an inference:
      neurons caught at many depths, pressed against fibres, suggested a climb along the fibre. He
      published the account in 1972. The inference gave later workers a definite hypothesis to
      test in living cells, and it made the radial glial fibre the reference line for thinking
      about how a neuron's birthplace relates to its final column in the cortex.
  - name: "Mary E. Hatten"
    contribution: >-
      With James Edmondson, recorded living mouse cerebellar granule neurons moving along
      astroglial fibres in culture, turning Rakic's anatomical inference into an observed
      behaviour that could be measured and perturbed.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Edmondson & Hatten, J. Neurosci. 7:1928 (1987); Hatten, in The History of Neuroscience in Autobiography, vol. 10 (Society for Neuroscience)."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      A still image cannot say which cell is moving. Hatten and her graduate student James
      Edmondson set out to film the behaviour. They purified granule neurons and astroglia from
      the cerebella of early postnatal mice, put the two populations back together on a glass
      coverslip, and recorded them with video-enhanced differential interference contrast
      microscopy, which makes unstained living cells visible. After recording they fixed the
      cultures and used an antibody to confirm that the long process each neuron had followed
      was glial. Their 1987 paper showed neurons moving along glial processes in a stop-and-go
      rhythm, with the cell body closely apposed to the fibre and a leading tip that extended
      and retracted on its own schedule. The preparation was a simplified culture, not an
      intact brain, and the authors did not test whether glia were required. The assay
      separated the tip's probing from the soma's advance, and it became the system in which
      her laboratory and others later looked for the adhesion molecules and motors of the climb.
  - name: "Orly Reiner"
    contribution: >-
      Led the 1993 isolation of LIS1, the gene deleted in Miller-Dieker lissencephaly, giving the
      field a human gene for a disorder of neuronal migration.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Reiner et al., Nature 364:717 (1993), 'Isolation of a Miller-Dieker lissencephaly gene containing G protein beta-subunit-like repeats'."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Children with Miller-Dieker syndrome have a cortex with few or no folds, and many carry a
      deletion near the tip of the short arm of chromosome 17, in the band called
      17p13.3. Reiner, working with David Ledbetter, William Dobyns, Thomas Caskey and
      colleagues, searched that region for the gene responsible. In 1993 they reported a gene
      they named LIS1 and showed that it was deleted in patients with the disorder. Its protein
      sequence contained repeats resembling those of the beta subunit of heterotrimeric G
      proteins, now called WD40 repeats. The paper did not say what LIS1 did in a neuron. It did
      something more useful for the field: it gave a dose-sensitive human gene, in which losing
      one copy was enough to disturb cortical development. Two years later a match between LIS1
      and a fungal gene for moving nuclei pointed the search toward the motor dynein, and the
      gene became a handle on the machinery of migration itself.
  - name: "Susan K. McConnell"
    contribution: >-
      With Bruce Schaar, filmed neuroblasts from the anterior subventricular zone of newborn rats
      and showed that a swelling forms in the leading process before the nucleus moves, that the
      centrosome lies ahead of the nucleus in cells fixed mid-cycle, and that blocking myosin II
      stops the cell body while the leading process keeps growing.
    profileSourceAnchor: "Schaar & McConnell, Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 102:13652 (2005), 'Cytoskeletal coordination during neuronal migration'."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      To see how the parts of a migrating neuron move relative to one another, Schaar and
      McConnell used neurons from the anterior subventricular zone of newborn rats (precursors of
      olfactory bulb interneurons, not cortical neurons), which
      migrate readily out of small explants in a gel. They filmed the cells and then fixed a
      chosen cell at a chosen moment of its cycle, found that same cell again, and cut it into
      thin sections for the electron microscope. A swelling formed in the leading process ahead
      of the nucleus, the centrosome sat in front of the nucleus, and bundles of microtubules
      wrapped around the nucleus. The swelling marked where the nucleus would go next. When they
      added blebbistatin, a drug that blocks the motor activity of myosin II, leading processes
      could keep extending while the cell bodies stopped. The 2005 paper gave a concrete
      sequence of steps for nucleokinesis and a way to separate the front of the cell from its
      body with a single drug.

techniques:
  - name: "Serial-section electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Fixed tissue is cut into a long series of very thin sections, each is photographed in the
      electron microscope, and a cell is traced from section to section to rebuild its shape in
      three dimensions. It shows which membranes touch, but because the tissue is dead it cannot
      show which cell moved.
  - name: "Time-lapse imaging of slices and cultures"
    blurb: >-
      Living brain slices or cultured cells, labelled with a dye or a fluorescent protein, are
      photographed every few minutes for hours. The movie shows the order of events (process
      extends, swelling forms, nucleus jumps), but the dissection and the culture conditions can
      change speed and behaviour.
  - name: "Retroviral and electroporation labelling"
    blurb: >-
      A retrovirus or a plasmid pushed into cells by an electric pulse delivers a gene for a
      fluorescent protein, and in the retrovirus case every daughter of the infected cell
      inherits it. The same delivery can carry an RNA that knocks down a gene, so a labelled
      cell with less of the protein of interest can be compared with control cells.
  - name: "Acute drug inhibition"
    blurb: >-
      A small molecule that blocks one protein, such as blebbistatin for myosin II or
      nocodazole for microtubules, is added while a cell is being filmed. If one movement stops
      and another continues, the two depend on different machinery, although every drug can
      act on more than its intended target.

  - stem: "In a fixed section of fetal monkey cortex, a young neuron lies along a radial glial fibre in the intermediate zone. What can this image alone establish?"
    choices:
      - "That the fibre is required for the neuron to migrate"
      - "That the neuron and the fibre are in close contact"
      - "That the neuron is moving outward at a steady speed"
      - "That the neuron will stop in the deepest cortical layer"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      A fixed image shows position and contact, so it can establish that the neuron is pressed
      against the fibre. Speed and direction need a series of images over time, which fixed tissue
      cannot give. Whether the fibre is required needs an experiment that removes or disables it.
      The final layer depends on the neuron's birth date and on later stop signals, and nothing in
      one image of a cell in transit says where it will stop.
  - stem: "Which statement best distinguishes somal translocation from locomotion in the developing mouse cortex?"
    choices:
      - "In translocation only glia move, while in locomotion only the neuron itself moves"
      - "In locomotion the nucleus stays still, while in translocation the process shortens"
      - "In locomotion the neuron moves sideways, while in translocation it moves outward"
      - "In translocation a long process reaches the surface, while locomotion uses a short one"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      In somal translocation the neuron already has a long leading process attached near the pial
      surface and moves its soma up inside it, smoothly; in locomotion a shorter free leading
      process leads and the soma advances in steps along a glial fibre. Both modes are radial, so
      the sideways choice describes tangential migration of interneurons. The nucleus does move in
      locomotion, in jumps. Glia do not carry neurons in either mode; the neuron moves itself.
  - stem: "In the two-stroke cycle of nucleokinesis, what happens in the first stroke, before the nucleus moves?"
    choices:
      - "The centrosome moves forward into a swelling of the leading process"
      - "The nucleus divides so that one daughter can enter the leading process first"
      - "Myosin II at the front pushes the leading process back toward the cell body"
      - "The trailing process detaches and becomes the axon at the ventricular surface"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      In the first stroke the leading process extends and the centrosome moves ahead into a
      swelling; in the second, the nucleus is brought up into the swelling. (In interneurons from
      the ganglionic eminence the Golgi was also shown to move into the swelling.) A migrating
      neuron is post-mitotic, so the nucleus does not divide. The trailing process of a cortical
      neuron can become the axon, and some observed neurons keep an axon-like process in the
      ventricular zone, but forming or detaching an axon is not a stroke of the cycle. Myosin II contributes force that moves the soma forward, not force that
      shortens the leading process back toward the cell.
  - stem: In slices of embryonic rat cortex, filmed late-born neurons do not climb straight to the cortical plate. Which route fits the observations?
    choices:
    - They move to the subventricular zone, become multipolar and linger, often move back toward the ventricle, then turn bipolar and migrate radially to the cortical plate
    - They climb straight from the ventricular zone to the cortical plate as bipolar cells and never pause
    - They stay multipolar all the way and reach the cortical plate by somal translocation alone
    - They reach the cortical plate first and then move back to the subventricular zone before becoming multipolar
    answer: 0
    rationale: Newborn neurons first moved to the subventricular zone, became multipolar and spent about a day or longer there or in the lower intermediate zone with little net outward progress; many then moved back toward the ventricle for a time before turning bipolar and migrating radially to the cortical plate. A straight climb with no stop misses the multipolar stage that the movies revealed. Staying multipolar and using only somal translocation confuses an early mode with the late-born route. Reaching the cortical plate first reverses the order. This is an example trajectory, not a required route for every neuron.
  - stem: "A girl has seizures, and her MRI shows a band of grey matter lying in the white matter beneath an otherwise ordinary cortex. Her brother, with a mutation in the same gene, has a smooth cortex. Which gene is most likely mutated?"
    choices:
      - "DCX, on the X chromosome, because the girl is a mosaic of two cell types"
      - "Reelin, on chromosome 7, because neurons fail to pass the older layers"
      - "LIS1, on chromosome 17, because one copy is lost in both children"
      - "Cdk5, on chromosome 7, because the boy lacks the kinase in all neurons"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Doublecortin is on the X chromosome. In a heterozygous girl, X inactivation makes some
      neurons use the normal copy and migrate, while others use the mutant copy and stall in the
      white matter, forming a second band of grey matter (subcortical band heterotopia, or double
      cortex). A boy has one X, so all his neurons are affected and the cortex has few or no
      folds. LIS1 is not on a sex chromosome, so it would not explain the sex difference. Reelin
      defects disorganize the cortex (inverted in the reeler mouse) with an underdeveloped cerebellum (cerebellar hypoplasia), not a band. Human
      Cdk5 loss is not the classic cause of this sex-linked pattern.

  - stem: "Neurons from newborn rat forebrain are filmed migrating in a gel. In control cells the nucleus advances about 6 micrometres in each cycle, and one cycle is completed every 15 minutes on average. Over 2 hours of continuous migration, what mean speed does this give for the nucleus?"
    choices:
      - "90 micrometres per hour"
      - "6 micrometres per hour"
      - "24 micrometres per hour"
      - "12 micrometres per hour"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      Four cycles fit in an hour at one per 15 minutes, and 4 times 6 micrometres is 24
      micrometres per hour; the 2-hour window gives 48 micrometres in 2 hours, the same speed.
      Six micrometres per hour treats one stroke as an hour's progress. Twelve per hour divides
      the 2-hour distance by 4 instead of by 2. Ninety per hour multiplies 6 by 15 minutes as if
      minutes were cycles.
  - stem: "In slices of embryonic rat cortex, neurons expressing a short hairpin RNA against LIS1 are filmed alongside control neurons. Knockdown neurons extend their leading processes at nearly the control rate, but their cell bodies barely move. Which conclusion do these data best support?"
    choices:
      - "LIS1 is needed for the neuron to leave the cell cycle and migrate"
      - "LIS1 is needed for actin assembly at the tip of the leading process"
      - "LIS1 is needed for the motor-driven advance of the soma, not for process growth"
      - "LIS1 is needed for the radial glial fibre to support the neuron"
    answer: 2
    rationale: >-
      The front of the cell keeps growing while the soma stalls, so the defect is in the
      dynein-driven advance of the soma, which LIS1 regulates. If
      actin assembly at the tip failed, the leading process would stop growing, and it does not.
      The knockdown is in the neurons, not the glia, and the process still extends along its
      route. Neurons that extend a leading process and are filmed migrating have already left
      the cell cycle, so a failure to exit cannot explain a stalled soma.
  - stem: "Neurons are filmed migrating, and then blebbistatin, a myosin II inhibitor, is added. Within 30 minutes the cell bodies stop in most neurons while the leading processes continue to lengthen. A second dish is given nocodazole, which disassembles microtubules, only after the swelling has formed, and the nucleus still moves forward by about a normal step while the cell surface blebs. What is the most defensible interpretation?"
    choices:
      - "Microtubules play no part in nucleokinesis, since nocodazole had little effect"
      - "Myosin II acts only at the rear of the cell, since the soma stopped moving"
      - "Myosin II drives leading-process growth, since the drug acted within minutes"
      - "Myosin II is needed for soma advance, while process growth uses other machinery"
    answer: 3
    rationale: >-
      Blocking myosin II stops the soma but not the process, so soma advance needs myosin II and
      process extension does not depend on it in the same way. The timed nocodazole result, from
      Schaar and McConnell's neuroblasts migrating in a gel, shows that, once a swelling exists, the nucleus can still advance after microtubules are disrupted;
      blebbistatin blocked that movement, so myosin II can drive it. It does not make microtubules
      irrelevant, and LIS1 and dynein experiments show they matter.
      A drug acting everywhere in the cell cannot say where myosin II acts, front or rear, which
      is still argued. These data do not support myosin II as the driver of process growth, because the process kept
      growing with myosin II blocked.

    text: >-
      Excitatory cortical neurons born in the ventricular and subventricular zones migrate
      radially to the cortical plate. Early-born neurons, when the wall is thin, mainly use

  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: >-
      Nucleokinesis couples a microtubule system to an actomyosin system. A perinuclear array of
      microtubules runs from the centrosome around the nucleus, and dynein, regulated by LIS1
      and NDE1 or NDEL1, acts in two places: at a swelling in the leading process it pulls the

**Knock down one protein in a migrating cortical neuron, and its front keeps reaching toward the surface while its cell body, nucleus and all, barely moves.**

The protein is LIS1, and losing one copy of its gene is enough to give a child a cortex with reduced or absent folding. To see why, you need to know how a neuron moves at all. A newborn cortical neuron is about ten micrometres across, and the wall it has to cross can be many times that thick. It makes the trip with no legs, commonly climbing a radial glial fibre, and it moves its own nucleus forward one stroke at a time.

## Across the wall

You have seen in [the lesson on birth order and layers](/lessons/birth-order-and-layers/) that a neuron's birth date predicts its layer: early-born neurons settle deep, and later-born neurons settle in more superficial layers. That rule needs travel.

The main zones, from the inside out (the subplate, between the intermediate zone and the cortical plate, is left out):

| Zone | Where it lies | What happens there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ventricular zone** | the inner surface, beside the fluid-filled ventricle | progenitors divide; excitatory neurons are born |
| **Subventricular zone** | just outside the ventricular zone | more neurons are born; many newborn neurons linger here as multipolar cells |
| **Intermediate zone** | the middle of the wall | axons run; migrating neurons cross it |
| **Cortical plate** | near the outer surface, which the pia, a thin membrane, covers | neurons settle into their layers |
| **Marginal zone** | above the cortical plate, just under the pia | the leading process of a locomoting neuron can reach it, and some cells then finish with a terminal translocation |

In a mouse the wall is thin at first; in a primate late in gestation it is far thicker. Either way the problem is the same: how does a cell move its bulky nucleus outward through a crowded wall?

During the climb, a migrating neuron has one **leading process**, a long extension pointing the way it is going, and a cell body, or **soma**, that holds the nucleus. The **centrosome**, the cell's main organizing centre for microtubules, sits near the nucleus or a little ahead of it in the leading process.

**Predict.** You film a neuron in a slice. Over an hour the tip of its leading process advances steadily toward the cortical plate. Has the neuron migrated? What would you need to see before you said yes?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not necessarily. A process can grow while the cell body stays put, which is exactly what the LIS1 knockdown in the opening does. You would need to see the soma, and so its nucleus, move relative to the surrounding tissue.

</details>

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson.** The front goes first, and the cell has moved only when its nucleus catches up. Migration is measured at the nucleus, relative to the tissue, not at the tip.

**What the rule covers.**

- **Which cells.** Bipolar neurons that advance in stop-and-go steps: cortical neurons climbing glia, cerebellar granule neurons on glia, and subventricular-zone neuroblasts (young migrating neurons) and interneurons, which can move the same way without glia. Somal translocation appears fairly smooth; the two-stroke description was worked out for stop-and-go locomotion. Cells in the multipolar stage (several short processes, met later) move differently.
- **Which claim.** The order of events, and where to measure them. It does not say where the force comes from: whether myosin II squeezes from behind the nucleus or pulls from the front is still argued, so the rule says "catches up" and names no force.
- **Typical, not universal.** In some cerebellar granule neurons the nucleus even passes the centrosome.

## Two ways up

In 2001 Bagirathy Nadarajah, Alan Pearlman, Rachel Wong and colleagues filmed neurons in acute slices of embryonic mouse cortex and named the two ways they climb.

| Stage | Leading process | How the soma moves | Name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Early, when the wall is thin | in many cells, long and already attached near the pial surface | moves up within the process as it shortens, fairly smoothly | **somal translocation** |
| Later, when the wall is thick | shorter, ending freely | advances in jumps; from the cells' shape, the authors inferred that radial glia guide it | **locomotion** |
| The final approach | reaches the marginal zone, above the cortical plate | in some cells, switches to a final translocation | **terminal translocation** |

Picture translocation as a person on a rope that is already tied to the ceiling, simply reeling in. Locomotion, the harder case, is where the front and the nucleus come apart, and most of this lesson is about it.

## The glial ladder: from an inference to a movie

The fibres the neurons climb belong to radial glia. As [the lesson on radial glia](/lessons/radial-glia-progenitors/) explains, these same cells are the main progenitors of the cortex. Each one that still touches the ventricle keeps a process running to the pia, so the wall is strung with parallel fibres.

Pasko Rakic proposed in 1972, for the cerebral cortex, that these fibres are the guide; he had made the same case for the cerebellum in 1971. He rebuilt young neurons from serial electron micrographs of fetal rhesus monkey cortex and found them pressed along radial glial fibres at every depth of the intermediate zone. That was fixed tissue. It showed contact, and it suggested a climb, but it could not show which cell moved, or how.

**Predict.** Suppose you could film living neurons and glia together in a dish. What could such a movie show that Rakic's sections could not? And what would it still leave open about the living brain?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In 1987 James Edmondson and Mary Hatten supplied the movie, in a different animal and a different brain region. They purified granule neurons and astroglia from the cerebella of early postnatal mice, recombined them on a coverslip, and filmed them. Neurons moved along glial processes in a stop-and-go rhythm, with the cell body closely apposed to the fibre and a leading tip that extended and retracted out of step with the soma. A later antibody stain confirmed that the guides were glial.

The experiment showed that a neuron can migrate on a glial fibre and how it moves when it does. It did not show that glia are required in the living brain, or what tells a neuron to get off.

</details>

Notice "out of step with the soma": even in this early movie, the tip and the cell body kept separate schedules.

## The front goes first

Nucleokinesis, the forward movement of the nucleus, works as a **"two stroke"** cycle.

1. **Stroke one.** The leading process extends. A swelling forms in it, ahead of the nucleus, and the centrosome moves forward into the swelling.
2. **Stroke two.** The nucleus is brought forward into the swelling. The trailing part of the cell follows.

Then the cycle repeats. The analogy is an inchworm: the front reaches and grips, then the back catches up.

Bruce Schaar and Susan McConnell watched this cycle in neurons from the anterior subventricular zone of newborn rats, migrating in a gel; they filmed the swelling and the nucleus, and located the centrosome in cells fixed mid-cycle. These are not cortical neurons but precursors of olfactory bulb interneurons, which normally travel without radial glia. Later imaging of rat cortical slices found the same order in radially migrating cells: centrosomes moving ahead of nuclei, and dynein concentrated at a swelling.

**Predict.** A swelling has just formed in the leading process, a short way ahead of the nucleus. Where will the nucleus be after its next jump? And if you fixed the cell at that moment and looked inside, where would you expect to find the centrosome?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

In Schaar and McConnell's cells, the swelling predicted where the nucleus would go next. Electron micrographs of cells fixed mid-cycle showed the centrosome ahead of the nucleus and bundles of microtubules wrapped around the nucleus like a cage, with the microtubules running forward toward the centrosome.

So the proposed order is front, then centrosome, then nucleus.

</details>

## The engine: what brings the nucleus up

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[0]} />

So what makes the nucleus catch up?

Start with the track, the microtubule cage. Microtubules have a direction. In the usual picture, drawn from imaging of radially migrating rat cortical neurons, their **minus ends** gather at the centrosome, and their **plus ends** point away from it, back around the nucleus and forward into the leading process. Some other neurons arrange them differently: in some cerebellar granule neurons the microtubules around the nucleus need not meet at the centrosome.

The motor is cytoplasmic **dynein**, which walks toward minus ends, that is, toward the centrosome. What it moves depends on what holds it.

- **If dynein is held in place**, it cannot walk anywhere, so it drags the microtubule instead, and the centrosome at the microtubule's minus end comes toward the dynein.
- **If dynein is attached to something that can move**, it carries that thing along the microtubule toward the centrosome.

Think of hauling hand over hand on a rope. Tied to a post, you pull the far end of the rope toward you; free on a fixed rope, you climb toward its far end. In the usual model, a migrating neuron uses both cases, one in each stroke.

1. **Stroke one, at the swelling.** Dynein concentrated in the swelling ahead of the nucleus pulls on microtubules whose minus ends sit at the centrosome. The microtubule network, and with it the centrosome, is drawn forward into the swelling.
2. **Stroke two, at the nucleus.** Dynein on the surface of the nucleus walks along the cage and carries the nucleus toward the centrosome, which now sits ahead.

LIS1 binds dynein and regulates it in both places; it is not the motor itself.

A second force comes from actin and myosin II, the contractile system of [the lesson on integrins and Rho GTPases](/lessons/integrins-and-rho-gtpases/). Blebbistatin is a drug that blocks myosin II. When Schaar and McConnell added it, leading processes could keep growing while the cell bodies stopped.

**Predict.** In slices of embryonic rat cortex, the centrosome and the nucleus of migrating neurons were labelled and filmed. For control neurons, neurons with LIS1 or dynein knocked down, and neurons given blebbistatin, what do you expect the leading-process tip, the centrosome and the nucleus to do?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

| | Leading-process tip | Centrosome | Nucleus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Control | extends | moves ahead into the swelling | follows |
| LIS1 or dynein knocked down | keeps extending | stalls | stalls |
| Blebbistatin (myosin II blocked) | keeps extending | keeps moving | stops, so the gap to the centrosome grows |

Knocking down LIS1 or dynein impairs both of dynein's proposed jobs, and both the centrosome and the nucleus stall. Blebbistatin leaves centrosome movement in place but stops the nucleus, which is why the two are thought to depend on different forces. In every row the tip keeps growing.

</details>

That last column is the reason for the rule. A cell can keep reaching without arriving.

### Measure migration at the nucleus

A movie gives speed directly, as long as you follow the nucleus. If each cycle moves the nucleus a fixed step,

> **mean speed = step per cycle × cycles per hour**

A nucleus that advances 6 micrometres per cycle, with one cycle every 15 minutes, travels 6 × 4 = 24 micrometres an hour.

Because the movement is stop-and-go, the mean is lower than the speed in a burst. In Edmondson and Hatten's culture the cell body advanced in bursts of about 56 micrometres an hour, but about 33 an hour overall. If a cell moved only at burst speed and stood still the rest of the time, it would be moving 33 ÷ 56, about 60 percent of the time.

**Predict.** Take a toy model. Over 2 hours, the tip of a control neuron and the tip of a LIS1 knockdown neuron each advance 24 micrometres an hour relative to the tissue. The control nucleus steps 6 micrometres every 15 minutes; the knockdown nucleus does not step. How far does each tip move, and each nucleus? If you tracked only the tips, what would you conclude?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

| Over 2 hours | Tip moves | Nucleus moves | Tip-to-nucleus distance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Control | 48 micrometres | 8 steps × 6 = 48 micrometres | no net change (it stretches and shrinks within each cycle) |
| LIS1 knocked down | 48 micrometres | 0 | 48 micrometres longer |

Tracked at the tip, the two cells look the same, and you would score the knockdown as a normal migrator. Tracked at the nucleus, one cell has crossed 48 micrometres of wall and the other has not moved.

</details>

## When the engine breaks: lissencephaly

<LessonFilm film={frontmatter.films[1]} />

Disrupt the machinery that brings the nucleus up, and neurons can stall short of their layers. Each of the main genes below touches the two-stroke machinery, or sits upstream of it.

**Lissencephaly** means a cortex with reduced or absent folding; in the classic form the cortex is also abnormally thick and poorly layered, because neurons failed to reach their places.

**LIS1, the regulator of dynein.** Losing one copy of *LIS1* is enough to cause lissencephaly. *LIS1* is deleted in Miller-Dieker lissencephaly, but that deletion also removes neighbouring genes, among them *YWHAE*, which encodes 14-3-3ε, and these make the syndrome more severe than lissencephaly caused by *LIS1* alone. In rat cortical neurons LIS1 knockdown stalls both strokes.

**Predict.** A mouse cortex is smooth to begin with. How could a mouse with a defect in this machinery show that something is wrong, if not by losing folds?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Not by folds. In a mouse you look at how fast neurons move and where they end up, for example whether the cortical layers are disordered. The readout depends on the gene, the dose and the brain region: mice lacking doublecortin alone have a nearly normal neocortex, though their hippocampus is abnormal.

</details>

**Doublecortin, a microtubule stabilizer.** In 1998 two groups (Gleeson, Walsh and colleagues; des Portes, Chelly and colleagues) identified *DCX* on the X chromosome. It encodes a protein that binds and stabilizes microtubules, the tracks the engine runs on. Boys with a mutation typically have lissencephaly. Girls with one mutant copy are mosaics, because each cell switches off one of its two X chromosomes at random: neurons using the normal copy migrate, and some using the mutant copy stall. The stalled neurons form a band of grey matter in the white matter below the cortex, called subcortical band heterotopia or "double cortex".

**Cdk5, the kinase upstream.** Cdk5 is a kinase (an enzyme that adds phosphate groups to proteins) of the cell-cycle family that, in neurons, is activated by p35 (or the related p39) instead of a cyclin. Mice lacking Cdk5 or p35 have disordered cortical layers. Among its targets are NDEL1, a dynein regulator that works with LIS1, and doublecortin, so Cdk5 sits upstream of the same nuclear machinery.

These patients and mice show what the motor system is for, but each gene also acts at other stages, as the next section shows.

## Not a straight climb

The two-stroke climb is not the whole trip.

In 2004 Stephen Noctor, Arnold Kriegstein and colleagues labelled progenitors in embryonic rat cortex with a GFP retrovirus and filmed their daughters in slices. Newborn neurons first moved to the subventricular zone, then became **multipolar**, with several short processes, and spent about a day or longer there or in the lower intermediate zone with little net outward progress. Many then moved back toward the ventricle for a time before turning bipolar and migrating radially to the cortical plate. Only a movie could show this.

It moves from the ventricular zone to the subventricular zone, becomes multipolar and lingers near the lower intermediate zone, moves back toward the ventricle, then becomes bipolar and migrates toward the cortical plate. The return occurs in many cells." caption="One route observed in embryonic rat cortical slices: (1) a newborn neuron in the ventricular zone moves outward to (2) the subventricular zone, then (3) becomes multipolar and lingers, with little net progress, in the subventricular zone or lower intermediate zone. Many cells (4) move back toward the ventricle before (5) becoming bipolar and migrating radially toward the cortical plate. This is an example trajectory, not a required route for every neuron." />

A multipolar cell is still migrating. It extends and retracts several processes and can move slowly, changing direction, between pauses. What it lacks is the one steady leading process of the bipolar climb, so the sustained two-stroke climb begins only after the cell turns bipolar. Why some cells first move back toward the ventricle is not settled.

LIS1 matters at this turn too. LIS1 knockdown in embryonic rat cortex blocks the nuclear movement of progenitors, the multipolar-to-bipolar change and the movement of the soma, so a single defect can stall cells at more than one step.

### Design problem

You image a migrating cortical neuron in a slice and see that its leading process keeps extending but the nucleus stays put. Name two molecular defects that could cause this and one experiment that tells them apart.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Defect one: the dynein system fails.** Loss of LIS1 or of dynein function impairs dynein's pull in both strokes.

**Defect two: the actomyosin push or pull fails.** Loss of myosin II activity stops the soma while the process extends.

**The experiment.** Label the centrosome (for example with a fluorescent centrin) and the nucleus, and film the cell. Track the leading process, the centrosome and the nucleus separately over time, and measure migration at the nucleus. As in the engine prediction, LIS1 or dynein knockdown stalls both the centrosome and the nucleus, while a myosin II block stops only the nucleus, so the centrosome-nucleus distance grows. As a control, add blebbistatin to normal cells and check that it reproduces the second pattern, and rescue the first with a knockdown-resistant LIS1.

Be careful with the interpretation. The two systems interact, and a long block in either may slow the other. The readout also depends on the cell type and on how much LIS1 is lost; see [Going deeper](#where-the-force-is-applied).

</details>

## Going deeper

### Where the force is applied

Rho and its kinase ROCK switch on myosin II, and different preparations put actomyosin activity in different places. In Schaar and McConnell's neuroblasts, once a swelling had formed, the nucleus still advanced after nocodazole disrupted microtubules, and blebbistatin blocked that step; microtubules and dynein remain essential in the knockdown work. The force distribution likely depends on cell type and stage.

The LIS1 readout depends on cell type and on how much LIS1 is lost. LIS1 or dynein knockdown stalls both the centrosome and the nucleus in rat cortical slices, but in cultured mouse cerebellar granule neurons with one copy of *Lis1* the distance between the nucleus and the centrosome grows instead, and becomes more variable.

### Which cells showed the swelling

Schaar and McConnell argued that the swelling is common to many kinds of migrating neuron. The Golgi's move into the swelling was shown in interneurons from the ganglionic eminence.

### Outer radial glia

In the large primate cortex, outer radial glia, which have no ventricular contact, add more fibres.

### The longer list of mutants

The list of mutants runs from LIS1, NudE and Ndel (NDE1 and NDEL1 in mammals, dynein regulators that work with LIS1), doublecortin, Cdk5 and dynein to myosin II, ROCK and many more. Most of the names fit one of the two strokes.

### Interkinetic nuclear migration in progenitors

Motor-driven nuclear movement also occurs earlier, in the progenitors. In **interkinetic nuclear migration** the nucleus of a radial glial cell moves away from the ventricle in G1 and back to it in G2, dividing at the ventricular surface. In embryonic rat cortex the kinesin Kif1a, a plus-end motor, carries it basally, and dynein carries it apically. So progenitors use two motors to move the nucleus in two directions, while a migrating neuron uses dynein, helped by myosin II, to move both its centrosome and its nucleus the same way.

### How LIS1 was linked to dynein

In 1993 Orly Reiner, David Ledbetter, William Dobyns and colleagues isolated *LIS1* from chromosome band 17p13.3 and showed it was deleted in Miller-Dieker lissencephaly. The clue to its job came from a fungus: in 1995 the laboratory of N. Ronald Morris found that *nudF*, a gene needed to spread nuclei along a filament of *Aspergillus*, encodes a protein similar to LIS1. Other genes from the same screen encode parts of the dynein system. By 2000 several groups had linked mammalian LIS1 to dynein and found LIS1, NDEL1 and dynein together in the nervous system.
