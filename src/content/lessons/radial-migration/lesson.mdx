    profileSourceAnchor: "Rakic, J. Comp. Neurol. 145:61 (1972), 'Mode of cell migration to the superficial layers of fetal monkey neocortex'."
    quotes: []
    story: >-
      Rakic worked from fixed tissue, so he could never watch a cell move. What he could do was
      follow one cell through many thin sections. In fetal rhesus monkey cerebral cortex he
      combined Golgi staining with serial-section electron microscopy and rebuilt young neurons
      and their surroundings in three dimensions. The neurons lay in the intermediate zone with a
      long leading process pointing toward the surface, and along their length they were pressed
      against slender fibres of radial glia, cells whose processes span the wall from the
      ventricle to the pia. Fixed tissue cannot show motion, so the argument was an inference:
      neurons caught at many depths, pressed against fibres, suggested a climb along the fibre. He
      published the account in 1972. The inference gave later workers a definite hypothesis to
      test in living cells, and it made the radial glial fibre the reference line for thinking
      about how a neuron's birthplace relates to its final column in the cortex.

    story: >-

      Edmondson set out to film the behaviour. They purified granule neurons and astroglia from
      the cerebella of early postnatal mice, put the two populations back together on a glass

      rhythm, with the cell body closely apposed to the fibre and a leading tip that extended
      and retracted on its own schedule. The preparation was a simplified culture, not an
      intact brain, and the authors did not test whether glia were required. The assay
      separated the tip's probing from the soma's advance, and it became the system in which
      her laboratory and others later looked for the adhesion molecules and motors of the climb.

      McConnell used neurons from the anterior subventricular zone of newborn rats (precursors of
      olfactory bulb interneurons, not cortical neurons), which
      migrate readily out of small explants in a gel. They filmed the cells and then fixed a
      chosen cell at a chosen moment of its cycle, found that same cell again, and cut it into
      thin sections for the electron microscope. A swelling formed in the leading process ahead
      of the nucleus, the centrosome sat in front of the nucleus, and bundles of microtubules

      body with a single drug.

techniques:
  - name: "Serial-section electron microscopy"
    blurb: >-
      Fixed tissue is cut into a long series of very thin sections, each is photographed in the
      electron microscope, and a cell is traced from section to section to rebuild its shape in
      three dimensions. It shows which membranes touch, but because the tissue is dead it cannot
      show which cell moved.
  - name: "Time-lapse imaging of slices and cultures"
    blurb: >-
      Living brain slices or cultured cells, labelled with a dye or a fluorescent protein, are
      photographed every few minutes for hours. The movie shows the order of events (process
      extends, swelling forms, nucleus jumps), but the dissection and the culture conditions can
      change speed and behaviour.
  - name: "Retroviral and electroporation labelling"
    blurb: >-
      A retrovirus or a plasmid pushed into cells by an electric pulse delivers a gene for a
      fluorescent protein, and in the retrovirus case every daughter of the infected cell
      inherits it. The same delivery can carry an RNA that knocks down a gene, so a labelled
      cell with less of the protein of interest can be compared with control cells.
  - name: "Acute drug inhibition"
    blurb: >-
      A small molecule that blocks one protein, such as blebbistatin for myosin II or
      nocodazole for microtubules, is added while a cell is being filmed. If one movement stops
      and another continues, the two depend on different machinery, although every drug can
      act on more than its intended target.

  - stem: "In the two-stroke cycle of nucleokinesis, what happens in the first stroke, before the nucleus moves?"
    choices:

      - "The nucleus divides so that one daughter can enter the leading process first"
      - "Myosin II at the front pushes the leading process back toward the cell body"
      - "The trailing process detaches and becomes the axon at the ventricular surface"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      In the first stroke the leading process extends and the centrosome, with the Golgi, moves
      ahead into a swelling; in the second, the nucleus is pulled into the swelling. A migrating
      neuron is post-mitotic, so the nucleus does not divide. The trailing process in cortical
      neurons can become the axon, but that is not a stroke of the cycle and does not happen at
      the ventricle. Myosin II contributes force that moves the soma forward, not force that
      shortens the leading process back toward the cell.
  - stem: "In progenitors of the ventricular zone, the nucleus moves basally during G1 and apically during G2. Which motor pairing fits this interkinetic nuclear migration in the rat?"
    choices:
      - "Dynein in both directions, switched by the activator Cdk5"
      - "The kinesin Kif1a basally in G1 and dynein apically in G2"
      - "Myosin II basally in G1 and actin polymerization apically in G2"
      - "Dynein basally in G1 and the kinesin Kif1a apically in G2"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Microtubules in these elongated progenitors have their minus ends toward the apical
      surface, so dynein, a minus-end motor, carries the nucleus apically in G2, and the

**A newborn cortical neuron is about ten micrometres across, and the wall it has to cross can be many times that thick. It makes the trip with no legs, pulling its own nucleus forward one stroke at a time.**

You have seen in [the lesson on birth order and layers](/lessons/birth-order-and-layers/) that a neuron's birth date predicts its layer: early-born neurons settle deep, and later-born neurons settle in more superficial layers. That rule needs travel. Excitatory neurons of the cortex are born near the inner surface of the wall, in the ventricular zone beside the fluid-filled ventricle and in the subventricular zone just outside it. Their layers lie near the outer, pial surface. In between is the intermediate zone, where axons run. In a mouse the wall is thin at first; in a primate late in gestation it is far thicker. So every neuron faces the same physical problem: how does a cell move its bulky nucleus outward through a crowded wall, over a distance many times its own length?

This lesson answers in three parts: the route, the ladder, and the engine.

## The idea: two ways to go up

Chris frames this as "Modes of radial migration: translocation versus locomotion."

In 2001 Bagirathy Nadarajah, Alan Pearlman, Rachel Wong and colleagues filmed cells in acute slices of embryonic mouse cortex by time-lapse imaging. Early in corticogenesis, many cells had a long leading process already attached near the pial surface. The process shortened and the cell body moved up within it, fairly smoothly. They called this **somal translocation**. Picture a person on a rope that is already tied to the ceiling, simply reeling in.

Other cells had a shorter leading process that ended freely, and their cell bodies advanced in jumps. They called this **locomotion**, and from the cells' shape they inferred that it was guided by radial glia. Locomotion becomes the main mode later, when the wall is thick. They also saw some locomoting cells switch to a final translocation once the leading process reached the marginal zone at the top of the cortical plate.


The route of a late-born neuron is not a straight climb. In 2004 Stephen Noctor, Arnold Kriegstein and colleagues labelled progenitors in embryonic rat cortex with a GFP retrovirus and filmed their daughters in slices. Newborn neurons first moved to the subventricular zone, then became **multipolar**, with several short processes, and paused there or in the lower intermediate zone, in Chris's summary for up to about a day. Most then moved back toward the ventricle for a time before turning bipolar and migrating radially to the cortical plate. Only a movie could show it.

## The glial ladder: from an inference to a movie

The fibres the neurons climb belong to radial glia. As [the lesson on radial glia](/lessons/radial-glia-progenitors/) explains, these same cells are the main progenitors of the cortex. Each one that still touches the ventricle keeps a process running to the pia, so the wall is strung with parallel fibres. In the large primate cortex, outer radial glia, which have no ventricular contact, add more fibres.

Pasko Rakic proposed in 1972, for the cerebral cortex, that these fibres are the ladder; he had made the same case for the cerebellum in 1971. He rebuilt young neurons from serial electron micrographs of fetal rhesus monkey cortex and found them pressed along radial glial fibres at every depth of the intermediate zone. That was fixed tissue. It showed contact, and it suggested a climb, but it could not show which cell moved, or how.

In 1987 James Edmondson and Mary Hatten supplied the movie, in a different animal and a different brain region. They purified granule neurons and astroglia from the cerebella of early postnatal mice, recombined them on a coverslip, and filmed them. Neurons moved along glial processes in a stop-and-go rhythm, with the cell body closely apposed to the fibre and a leading tip that extended and retracted out of step with the soma. A later antibody stain confirmed that the guides were glial. The experiment showed that a neuron can migrate on a glial fibre and how it moves when it does. It did not show that glia are required in the living brain, or what tells a neuron to get off.

## The "two stroke" cycle: how the nucleus is pulled

Here is the engine. Chris calls nucleokinesis, the forward movement of the nucleus, a **"two stroke"** cycle.

1. **Stroke one.** The leading process extends. A swelling forms in it, ahead of the nucleus, and the centrosome and Golgi move forward into the swelling.
2. **Stroke two.** The nucleus is pulled forward into the swelling. The trailing part of the cell follows.

Then the cycle repeats. The analogy is an inchworm: the front reaches and grips, then the back catches up.

Bruce Schaar and Susan McConnell watched this cycle in neurons from the anterior subventricular zone of newborn rats, migrating in a gel. These are not cortical neurons: they are precursors of olfactory bulb interneurons, which normally travel without radial glia. The authors argued that the swelling is common to many kinds of migrating neuron, and later imaging of rat cortical slices found centrosomes moving ahead of nuclei and dynein concentrated at a swelling in radially migrating cells. The Golgi's move into the swelling was shown in interneurons from the ganglionic eminence. In Schaar and McConnell's cells, the swelling predicted where the nucleus would go next. Electron micrographs of cells fixed mid-cycle showed the centrosome ahead of the nucleus and bundles of microtubules wrapped around the nucleus like a cage, with the microtubules running forward toward the centrosome.

That cage is the track. Cytoplasmic dynein is a motor that walks toward the minus ends of microtubules, which sit at the centrosome. Dynein works in two places. Concentrated at the swelling, it pulls the microtubule network, and with it the centrosome, forward. Around the nucleus, it pulls on the cage and draws the nucleus toward the centrosome. A second force comes from actin and myosin II, the contractile system of [the lesson on integrins and Rho GTPases](/lessons/integrins-and-rho-gtpases/), where Rho and its kinase ROCK switch on myosin. When Schaar and McConnell added blebbistatin, which blocks myosin II, leading processes could keep growing while the cell bodies stopped. Whether myosin squeezes from behind the nucleus or pulls from the front is still argued. Different preparations put actomyosin activity in different places, and in some cerebellar granule neurons the nucleus even passes the centrosome. The force distribution likely depends on cell type and stage.

A movie gives speed directly. If each stroke moves the nucleus a fixed step:

> **mean speed = step per cycle × cycles per hour**

Motor-driven nuclear movement also occurs earlier, in the progenitors themselves. In **"Interkinetic nuclear migration"** the nucleus of a radial glial cell moves away from the ventricle in G1 and back to it in G2, dividing at the ventricular surface. In embryonic rat cortex the kinesin Kif1a, a plus-end motor, carries it basally, and dynein carries it apically. That is a contrast worth keeping: progenitors use two motors to move the nucleus in two directions, while a migrating neuron uses dynein, helped by myosin II, to move both its centrosome and its nucleus the same way.

### Design problem

You image a migrating cortical neuron in a slice and see that its leading process keeps extending but the nucleus stays put. Name two molecular defects that could cause this and one experiment that tells them apart.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

**Defect one: the dynein system fails.** Loss of LIS1 or of dynein function removes the pull on the microtubules. In live slices of embryonic rat cortex this stalls both the centrosome and the nucleus, while the leading process keeps growing.

**Defect two: the actomyosin push or pull fails.** Loss of myosin II activity stops the soma while the process extends.

**The experiment.** Label the centrosome (for example with a fluorescent centrin) and the nucleus, and film the cell. Track the leading process, the centrosome and the nucleus separately over time. In slices of embryonic rat cortex, LIS1 or dynein knockdown stalls both the centrosome and the nucleus, because dynein also pulls the centrosome forward from the swelling. A myosin II block stops the nucleus but lets the centrosome keep moving, so the centrosome-nucleus distance grows. In mouse neurons with one copy of Lis1 the distance grows instead, so the readout depends on the system and on how much LIS1 is lost. As a control, add blebbistatin to normal cells and check that it reproduces the second pattern, and rescue the first with a knockdown-resistant LIS1.

Be careful with the interpretation. The two systems interact, and a long block in either may slow the other.

</details>

## "The mutants" and "Human lissencephaly"

Chris's list of "The mutants" runs from LIS1, NudE and Ndel, doublecortin, Cdk5 and dynein to myosin II, ROCK and many more. Most of the names fit one of the two strokes.

**LIS1.** In 1993 Orly Reiner, David Ledbetter, William Dobyns and colleagues isolated *LIS1* from chromosome band 17p13.3 and showed it was deleted in Miller-Dieker lissencephaly. Lissencephaly means a cortex with reduced or absent folding; in the classic form the cortex is also abnormally thick and poorly layered, because neurons failed to reach their places. Losing one copy of *LIS1* is enough. The clue to its job came from a fungus: in 1995 the laboratory of N. Ronald Morris found that *nudF*, a gene needed to spread nuclei along a filament of *Aspergillus*, encodes a protein similar to LIS1. Other genes from the same screen encode parts of the dynein system. By 2000 several groups had linked mammalian LIS1 to dynein and found LIS1, NDEL1 and dynein together in the nervous system. LIS1 is now understood as a regulator of dynein, not the motor itself.

**Doublecortin.** In 1998 two groups (Gleeson, Walsh and colleagues; des Portes, Chelly and colleagues) identified *DCX* on the X chromosome. It encodes a protein that binds and stabilizes microtubules. Boys with a mutation typically have lissencephaly. Girls with one mutant copy are mosaics, because each cell inactivates one X: neurons using the normal copy migrate, and some using the mutant copy stall. The stalled neurons form a band of grey matter in the white matter below the cortex, called subcortical band heterotopia or "double cortex".

**Cdk5.** Cdk5 is a kinase of the cell-cycle family that, in neurons, is activated by p35 (or the related p39) instead of a cyclin. Mice lacking Cdk5 or p35 have disordered cortical layers. Among its targets are NDEL1 and doublecortin, so Cdk5 sits upstream of the same nuclear machinery.

These patients and mice show what the motor system is for, but each gene also acts at other stages. LIS1 knockdown in embryonic rat cortex blocks the nuclear movement of progenitors, the multipolar-to-bipolar change and the movement of the soma, so a single defect can stall cells at more than one step.
