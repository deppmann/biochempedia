## Explain: the parts all these cells share

<WordBank terms={['organelle', 'nucleus', 'cell membrane', 'cytoplasm', 'endoplasmic reticulum', 'mitochondria', 'vacuole', 'cell wall', 'chloroplast', 'central vacuole']} />

An **organelle** is a small part inside a cell that has its own special jobs. You met the parts of a cell in [that lesson](/lessons/inside-a-cell/). Plant cells and animal cells share most of them.

- The **nucleus** holds the cell’s DNA, its instructions, and controls what the cell does.
- The **cell membrane** is a thin outer layer that controls what goes in and out.
- The **cytoplasm** is the jelly-like part between the membrane and the nucleus.
- The **endoplasmic reticulum** is a folded network of membranes that helps make and move materials.
- The **mitochondria** release energy from sugar (glucose) in a form the cell can use.
- A **vacuole** is a sac that stores water and other materials. Animal cells may have small ones.

Most plant and animal cells have a nucleus. A few do not; mature red blood cells in mammals have none.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Plant and animal cells share a nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm, endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria and vacuoles.</Support>

## Explain: three parts plants add

Plant cells add three parts. Each one has a job.

| Part | Job | Plant cells | Animal cells |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | Supports and protects the cell | Yes, outside the membrane | No |
| Chloroplasts | Capture radiant energy to make food | In green cells | No |
| Large central vacuole | Stores water; presses out on the wall when full | Yes, in most mature cells | Usually no; most have only small vacuoles |

The **cell wall** is a stiff layer outside the cell membrane that supports and protects the cell. A plant cell has both a wall and a membrane. The wall contains a tough material called cellulose, along with other sugar-based materials.

A **chloroplast** is a green organelle that captures radiant energy, the energy in light, for making food. Inside it, the plant makes sugar (glucose) from carbon dioxide and water. You will study this in [that lesson](/lessons/photosynthesis-feeds-the-world/). Plant cells have mitochondria too, so they can release energy from that sugar, day and night. Cells that get no light, like most root cells and onion bulb cells, usually have no chloroplasts.

The **central vacuole** is one large sac that fills most of a mature plant cell and stores water. Young cells, such as those near the tips of roots, have smaller vacuoles that grow as the cell grows. Now back to the celery. When water moves into a cell, the vacuole fills and presses out against the wall. The wall is stiff, so it pushes back, and the cell gets firm. Many firm cells side by side make a firm stalk. When a cell loses water, the vacuole shrinks and the stalk goes limp. Water moves into cells by osmosis, which you will study in [that lesson](/lessons/diffusion-and-osmosis/). Water pressure matters most in soft parts of a plant, like celery stalks and leaves. A tree trunk is held up mainly by thick, hard cell walls, which keep working even after the cells inside them die.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/plant-and-animal-cells/celery-limp-and-firm.webp" width={1200} height={800} alt="AI-generated illustration: a limp celery stalk drooping in an empty glass and a firm stalk standing in water, with zooms of their cells: small shrunken vacuoles in the limp stalk, and full vacuoles pushing out on the cell walls in the firm stalk." caption="When water fills each large central vacuole, it presses on the stiff cell wall, and many firm cells make a firm stalk." />

A water balloon in a cardboard box is a good model. Fill the balloon, and it presses on the box until the box is tight. The model is not exactly like a real cell: a real wall can stretch a little, and water moves in and out of a cell all the time.

You are different. Your body is held up by a skeleton of bones. Your cells have no walls, so water inside them does not hold you up the way it holds up a plant. Your body also controls how much water stays inside you.

## Explain: shape and size fit the job

Animal and plant cells can differ in shape, size and parts. Most often, these differences fit the cell’s job. Plant cells often look boxy, because the wall gives them straight sides. Animal cells are often rounder or flatter. A cheek cell is thin and flat, like a tile on a floor.

A green leaf cell is packed with chloroplasts, and it gets light. An onion bulb cell usually has none, because the inside of the bulb gets no light. Both still have a wall that holds the plant up.

### Design problem

Does the amount of water change how firm celery gets? Plan an investigation you could do in class. Name your question, variables, constants, control, trials, measurements and safety steps.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Does limp celery get firmer when it stands in water than when it stands in an empty cup?
- **Independent variable:** water or no water in the cup.
- **Dependent variable:** how far each stalk bends. Clamp the thick end to a table edge, hang a 20-gram mass on the thin end, and measure the bend in centimetres with a ruler.
- **Constants:** all stalks left out on a counter for a day first so they start limp; stalks from the same bunch, cut to the same length (15 cm), same room, same time.
- **Control:** stalks in empty cups, measured the same way.
- **Trials:** at least five stalks in each group. Measure each one at the start and after 3 hours. Find the mean bend for each group.
- **Safety:** your teacher cuts the stalks. Wipe up spills. Do not eat lab celery.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

Limp celery gets crisp in water because water fills the large central vacuoles in its cells. Each full vacuole presses on a stiff cell wall, so the cells and the stalk stand firm. Your cells have no walls, so water inside them does not hold you up the way it does a plant; your skeleton holds you up.

**Science practices you used:** planning a fair test with a control, reading and comparing measurements in a table, and using a model and saying how it differs from a real cell.
