summary: "The citric acid cycle makes almost no ATP itself, just one GTP (or ATP) per turn. Its real product is high-energy electrons — three NADH and one FADH2 per acetyl-CoA — carried off to the electron transport chain where most of the cell's ATP is actually minted. Its intermediates also serve as raw material for building fatty acids, amino acids, heme and glucose, so the cell must refill them. Watch one carbon, count the cofactors, and notice that every turn ends by rebuilding oxaloacetate: in net terms oxaloacetate is a catalyst, not a fuel, a misconception that costs many exam points."

  - name: "Warburg manometry (respirometry)"
    blurb: "A sealed flask on a manometer measures the gas a tissue exchanges — O2 consumed, CO2 released — in real time. Krebs used minced pigeon-breast muscle on Warburg's manometer to watch respiration speed up when he added an intermediate, the assay that built the cycle. Its modern descendant is the Seahorse analyzer, which reads cellular oxygen consumption to fingerprint mitochondrial function."
  - name: "Selective enzyme inhibition (pathway dissection)"
    blurb: "Jam one step with a specific poison, then see which intermediate floods and which runs dry — that pattern reveals the order of the steps. Malonate blocks succinate dehydrogenase and backs up succinate; fluoroacetate, once the cell converts it to fluorocitrate, blocks aconitase and backs up citrate. Krebs used this 'poison and watch the traffic jam' logic with malonate, alongside evidence that citrate forms from oxaloacetate and that small amounts of intermediates boost respiration far beyond their own oxidation, to show the sequence was a cycle."
  - name: "Isotopic carbon tracking"
    blurb: "Label a carbon with ¹³C or ¹⁴C and trace where it lands turn by turn.     choices:

      - "The cycle makes most of the cell's ATP through substrate-level phosphorylation at four separate steps"
      - "The cycle's main product is CO2, which is later converted to ATP in the lungs"
      - "The cycle produces no ATP or GTP at all; all energy leaves as heat"
    answer: 0
    rationale: "Per acetyl-CoA the cycle yields 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP, and 2 CO2. Its real job is harvesting high-energy electrons onto carriers for the ETC; only the single GTP is direct high-energy phosphate, so the claim about ATP being the main direct product is wrong. The 'no ATP/GTP at all' choice ignores the one GTP; the 'four substrate-level steps' choice is false (there is only one); CO2 is not turned into ATP."
  - stem: "Malonate competitively inhibits succinate dehydrogenase. What pattern of intermediates would you expect shortly after adding malonate to actively respiring mitochondria?"
    choices:
      - "Fumarate accumulates while succinate is depleted"
      - "Succinate accumulates while fumarate and downstream intermediates are depleted"
      - "All eight intermediates rise equally because the cycle is a closed loop"
      - "Oxaloacetate accumulates because it can no longer be consumed"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "Succinate dehydrogenase converts succinate to fumarate. Block it and its substrate (succinate) backs up while everything downstream (fumarate, malate, oxaloacetate) runs dry — the 'traffic jam' logic Krebs used to order the cycle. Fumarate would fall, not rise; a closed loop does not mean uniform accumulation when one step is blocked; oxaloacetate is downstream of the block, so it is depleted, not accumulated."
  - stem: "Why does the citric acid cycle stall when oxygen is removed, even though no enzyme of the cycle uses O2 as a substrate?"
    choices:
      - "CO2 cannot be released without O2, so the cycle is blocked at isocitrate dehydrogenase"
      - "O2 is a direct substrate of malate dehydrogenase, so that step fails first"
      - "Without O2 the ETC cannot reoxidize NADH back to NAD+, so the cycle's NAD+ supply runs out"
      - "O2 normally inhibits citrate synthase, and its loss locks the enzyme open"
    answer: 2
    rationale: "O2 appears in none of the eight steps, but it is the terminal electron acceptor of the ETC. Without it the ETC backs up, NADH cannot be reoxidized to NAD+, and the cycle's small fixed NAD+ pool is consumed — the dehydrogenase steps then have no oxidized carrier to use. O2 is not a substrate of malate dehydrogenase; O2 does not inhibit citrate synthase; CO2 release does not require O2."
  - stem: "Oxaloacetate is described as catalytic in the citric acid cycle. Which statement best supports that label?"
    choices:
      - "Oxaloacetate is the molecule oxidized to the two CO2 released each turn"
      - "Oxaloacetate donates the high-energy phosphate that becomes GTP"
      - "Oxaloacetate is consumed each turn and must be replaced from glucose"
      - "Oxaloacetate is regenerated at the end of each turn, so a small pool of it can support many turns"
    answer: 3
    rationale: "A catalyst is regenerated at the end of its reaction. Oxaloacetate is consumed at citrate synthase and a new oxaloacetate is formed at malate dehydrogenase, so a small oxaloacetate pool can support many turns; the fuel actually consumed is acetyl-CoA. (The rebuilt oxaloacetate is not made of the same carbon atoms: tracing shows its carbons are gradually replaced by acetyl carbons.) Turning the cycle does not use up oxaloacetate in net terms; it needs refilling only when intermediates are drawn off for biosynthesis. The CO2 is released by isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, not by oxidizing oxaloacetate itself. The GTP is paid for by succinyl-CoA's thioester, not by oxaloacetate."
  - stem: "Citrate leaves the mitochondrion to supply fatty-acid synthesis, and α-ketoglutarate is converted to glutamate for amino-acid synthesis. What must the cell do to keep the cycle running?"
    choices:
      - "Nothing; oxaloacetate is a catalyst, so withdrawing other intermediates never matters"
      - "Refill the pool by anaplerosis, for example pyruvate carboxylase adding CO2 to pyruvate to make oxaloacetate"
      - "Stop the electron transport chain so that NADH accumulates"
      - "Convert the withdrawn intermediates back to acetyl-CoA"
    answer: 1
    rationale: "The cycle is amphibolic: intermediates are drawn off for building fatty acids, amino acids, heme and glucose. If they were not replaced, the oxaloacetate pool would shrink and the cycle would slow. Anaplerosis refills it; in many tissues pyruvate carboxylase makes oxaloacetate from pyruvate and CO2, and it is switched on by acetyl-CoA. Oxaloacetate is regenerated each turn only when nothing is withdrawn, so the catalyst label does not remove the need for refilling."

  - level: "highschool"
    label: "High school"
    text: "The citric acid cycle (also called the Krebs cycle) happens in your mitochondria after glycolysis. It takes a 2-carbon piece called acetyl-CoA and runs it around a loop of eight enzymes. Each loop releases 2 CO2 (the carbons you breathe out) and, more importantly, charges up electron carriers: 3 NADH and 1 FADH2, plus 1 GTP. Those carriers are the real product — they carry electrons to the next stage, the electron transport chain, where most of your ATP gets made. The starting molecule, oxaloacetate, comes back at the end every single time, so the cycle keeps reusing it instead of getting used up. Oxygen isn't actually in any step of the cycle, but you still need it: without it, the carriers can't be emptied and refilled, so the cycle stops."
  - level: "college"
    label: "College"
    text: "The TCA cycle oxidizes one acetyl-CoA to 2 CO2, capturing the energy as 3 NADH + 1 FADH2 + 1 GTP. It splits conceptually into two stages: Stage One (citrate synthase, aconitase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, α-KG dehydrogenase) releases two carbons (an acetyl group's worth) as CO2 and banks 2 NADH; Stage Two (succinyl-CoA synthetase, succinate dehydrogenase, fumarase, malate dehydrogenase) regenerates oxaloacetate and banks GTP, FADH2, and the third NADH. Oxaloacetate is catalytic — regenerated each turn. Three features to know cold: citrate synthase ('synthase,' no ATP) is a strongly exergonic, effectively irreversible committing step; succinate dehydrogenase is Complex II of the ETC and uses FAD because taking two hydrogens off succinate's CH2–CH2 is too low-energy for NAD+; and succinyl-CoA synthetase is the only substrate-level phosphorylation. Control sits mainly at citrate synthase and the two oxidative decarboxylations (IDH and α-KGDH): inhibited by ATP/NADH, activated by ADP/NAD+/Ca2+. Net ATP yield ≈ 10 per acetyl-CoA (NADH ≈ 2.5, FADH2 ≈ 1.5)."
  - level: "grad"
    label: "Graduate"
    text: "The cycle is best read as electron logistics under tight thermodynamic and regulatory constraint. Three reactions are held far from equilibrium and carry flux control — citrate synthase (ΔG°′ ≈ −31.4 kJ/mol; ordered induced fit with in-situ hydrolysis of enzyme-bound citryl-CoA to capture thioester energy), IDH, and the α-KGDH complex (a PDH-homologous E1/E2/E3 megacomplex employing lipoyl-arm substrate channeling). The remaining steps run near equilibrium and are pulled vectorially, most notably the strongly endergonic malate→OAA step, which is dragged forward only because citrate synthase keeps [OAA] vanishingly low. Cofactor choice is mechanistically dictated: SDH uses covalently bound FAD because the fumarate/succinate couple (E°′ ≈ +0.03 V) is far too positive to reduce NAD⁺ (E°′ ≈ −0.32 V), and SDH's identity as Complex II couples the cycle physically to the respiratory chain. Regulation integrates energy charge (ATP/ADP, NADH/NAD+) with the Ca2+ signal that co-activates IDH, α-KGDH, and PDH during contraction. The amphibolic role demands cataplerosis/anaplerosis balance (pyruvate carboxylase, feedforward-activated by acetyl-CoA, as a major OAA refill, with glutamine a major input in proliferating cells). Two oncometabolite lesions converge on the same target. Gain-of-function IDH1 or IDH2 (the NADP⁺-dependent isoforms, not the cycle's IDH3) makes D-2-hydroxyglutarate; loss-of-function SDH or FH lets succinate or fumarate pile up. All three competitively inhibit α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases, remodeling the epigenome — and that axis is now druggable."

**Add a pinch of a four-carbon acid to minced pigeon breast muscle, and the muscle burns far more oxygen than oxidizing that pinch could ever explain.**

That is what Szent-Györgyi and then Krebs saw when they measured the muscle's oxygen use. The acid was not being used up as fuel. It was being reused, over and over, to burn something else. That reuse is what makes the citric acid cycle a cycle.

## Where we are, and what the cycle is for

Glycolysis handed off two pyruvates and warned us the real bottleneck was NAD⁺. Here that warning comes due. In the mitochondrial matrix, pyruvate dehydrogenase turns each pyruvate into acetyl-CoA, releasing one CO₂ and one NADH. One glucose, two acetyl-CoA, two turns of the cycle. Everything below is per acetyl-CoA.

Most students arrive believing this cycle's job is to make ATP. It is not: per turn it makes exactly one high-energy phosphate, a GTP. Its real product is electrons, loaded onto three NADH and one FADH₂ and shipped to the electron transport chain, where the bulk of your ATP is made. One NADH's electron pair, handed to oxygen, releases about 220 kJ/mol; the GTP is worth about 30. **The citric acid cycle harvests electrons; the ETC spends them.**

**Predict.** An oxidation state counts the electrons a carbon has lost to its bonding partners (negative if it has gained them): each bond to O or S counts +1, each bond to H −1, a bond to C 0. Carbon in CO₂ is at +4. In the acetyl group, the methyl carbon (CH₃) is at −3 and the carbonyl carbon (bonded to CoA) at +3. How many electrons must come off to make both into CO₂? If each dehydrogenase removes one pair, how many dehydrogenases should the cycle have?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Eight electrons: 7 from the methyl carbon (−3 to +4) and 1 from the carbonyl carbon (+3 to +4). Four pairs, so four dehydrogenases, and the cycle has exactly four.

</details>

> **The one idea to carry through this lesson: follow the electrons.** Each turn uses up only one fuel: an acetyl group. Oxidizing its two carbons to CO₂ frees exactly eight electrons. Four dehydrogenases (steps 3, 4, 6 and 8) each load one pair onto a carrier: 3 NADH + 1 FADH₂. Oxaloacetate comes back in the amount it went in, so it cancels out of the net equation. Carriers must be emptied before they can be loaded again, so, in a respiring cell, the cycle can turn no faster than the electron transport chain, and so oxygen, can empty them.

The count gives you the number of pairs, four, without memorizing. It does not say which carrier takes each pair (step 6's chemistry does) or which atoms leave as CO₂ (the carbon section), and it holds only while nothing is drawn off (last section).

## Burn the fuel, then rebuild the machine

Hold the eight enzymes as two stages, like a fireplace: Stage One burns the firewood down to CO₂, and Stage Two sweeps the ashes into a new fireplace, rebuilding oxaloacetate. Each stage loads two carriers. (The film calls Stage One the "oxidation" stage; two of the four oxidations actually come in Stage Two.)

### Stage One: the carbons leave (steps 1–4)

**1 · Citrate synthase** joins 4-carbon oxaloacetate (OAA) to 2-carbon acetyl-CoA, making 6-carbon citrate. A *synthase* uses no ATP; the energy comes from cleaving acetyl-CoA's high-energy thioester.

**2 · Aconitase** turns citrate into isocitrate by moving its hydroxyl (–OH) to a neighboring carbon. No redox happens here.

**Predict.** Citrate already has an –OH. Why can't the next enzyme just oxidize citrate?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

Citrate's –OH sits on a carbon with no hydrogen (a tertiary alcohol). Making C–OH into C=O means removing that carbon's hydrogen, and there is none to remove without breaking a carbon–carbon bond. Aconitase moves the –OH to a carbon that has one.

</details>

**3 · Isocitrate dehydrogenase** carries out the first oxidative decarboxylation: oxidize, then remove a carboxyl as CO₂. Out come **CO₂ #1** and **NADH #1**. Textbooks often call it the cycle's **rate-limiting step**, and that is the answer exams usually expect. In living tissue, control is shared with citrate synthase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase.

**4 · The α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex** carries out the second: **CO₂ #2** and **NADH #2**. It is the twin of pyruvate dehydrogenase.

Which two carbons left is a puzzle for later.

### Stage Two: oxaloacetate is rebuilt (steps 5–8)

**5 · Succinyl-CoA synthetase** is the cycle's only substrate-level phosphorylation, a phosphate made directly by the reaction rather than by the chain. Cleaving succinyl-CoA's thioester drives GDP to GTP (or ADP to ATP, depending on the enzyme isoform).

**6 · Succinate dehydrogenase** is the odd one out. It sits in the inner mitochondrial membrane and *is* Complex II of the ETC, feeding electrons straight into the chain.

**Predict.** Step 6 takes two hydrogens off succinate's CH₂–CH₂ to make fumarate's C=C. Which carrier gets the pair, and why not NAD⁺?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

FAD. Those electrons are too low in energy to reduce NAD⁺: handing them to NAD⁺ would cost about 68 kJ/mol under standard conditions. The FAD held inside the enzyme sits at a far more positive potential than NAD⁺, so accepting the pair costs little, roughly 6 to 20 kJ/mol depending on the value used, and ubiquinone, the chain's next carrier, pulls them on.

</details>

**8 · Malate dehydrogenase** regenerates OAA and loads **NADH #3**, closing the ring. This step is unfavorable on its own, but citrate synthase uses up OAA the instant it appears, pulling it forward. The steps are coupled, not independent.

## Add it up: oxaloacetate cancels

The last column sums to **3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 GTP, 2 CO₂**. Write the turn as one reaction. One OAA goes in at step 1 and one comes out at step 8, so OAA appears on both sides and cancels:

> **acetyl-CoA + 3 NAD⁺ + FAD + GDP + Pᵢ + 2 H₂O → 2 CO₂ + 3 NADH + FADH₂ + GTP + CoA-SH + 2 H⁺**

That answers the pigeon-muscle puzzle. The added acid joins the cycle's pool of four-carbon intermediates, and every turn hands the acceptor back, so each added molecule carries many acetyl groups through, each sending its electrons to oxygen.

It also clears up a misconception that costs many exam points: OAA is not "used up" in net terms, so a small pool supports many turns. The fuel is acetyl-CoA; OAA is the reusable platform each acetyl group is built onto. (The rebuilt OAA is not made of the same atoms, as the next section shows.)

## Which carbons leave? A puzzle the net equation cannot answer

Two carbons come in as acetyl and two leave as CO₂, but are they the same two? OAA has a carboxyl group (–COO⁻) at each end and two middle carbons, one carrying a C=O. In the carbon tracker below, acetyl's carbons (methyl and carbonyl) are orange and OAA's are navy.

Citrate synthase bonds acetyl's methyl carbon to OAA's C=O carbon. Citrate's central carbon (OAA's old C=O carbon) then carries three branches: an orange arm from acetyl (ending in the old carbonyl carbon, now a carboxyl), a navy arm from OAA (ending in a carboxyl), and a navy carboxyl. Every CO₂ leaves as a carboxyl, so which carboxyls do steps 3 and 4 remove?

**Predict.** You add acetyl-CoA labeled at both carbons and let exactly one turn finish. Is any label in the CO₂?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

None. Aconitase moves the –OH onto the navy, OAA-derived arm. Isocitrate dehydrogenase oxidizes it and removes the carboxyl beside it, the navy one on the central carbon: CO₂ #1. α-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenase removes the carboxyl at the far end of the same navy arm: CO₂ #2. The orange carbons are untouched in the first turn. The atoms that leave are older ones.

</details>

Citrate's two CH₂–COO⁻ arms look identical on paper. How does aconitase tell them apart? The arms are chemically identical but sit in mirror-image positions around the central carbon, so an enzyme that grips citrate at three points holds it in only one orientation, the way a right glove fits only one hand. Citrate *looks* symmetric, but to the enzyme it is not. Alexander Ogston pointed this out in 1948, after tracer results first seemed to rule citrate out as an intermediate.

**Then real symmetry takes over.** At step 5 the chain becomes succinate, ⁻OOC–CH₂–CH₂–COO⁻, which reads the same from either end, so no enzyme can tell the ends apart. The old carbonyl carbon is now one of the two end carboxyls, with even odds for each; the old methyl is one of the two middle carbons. That is why the tracker turns every carbon half orange at succinate. Fumarate is symmetric too, so the odds carry into the new OAA, ends to ends and middles to middles. Each turn removes the two end carboxyls of the OAA it starts with:

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

## Oxygen and the throttle: one demand story

Recall the guiding idea: the cycle turns no faster than its carriers are emptied. Think of NADH and FADH₂ as rechargeable batteries. The electron transport chain drains them (NADH hands its electrons to Complex I and returns as NAD⁺), and oxygen keeps that going by accepting the spent electrons at the very end.

So, a favorite trick question: *is O₂ a substrate of the citric acid cycle?* No. Oxygen appears in none of the eight reactions; it is needed only to empty the carriers: *required, but not a substrate*.

**Predict.** Cut off the oxygen. Which steps stall, and why?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

The four dehydrogenases stall first, because each needs an empty carrier. Without O₂ the chain backs up and the small, fixed NAD⁺ pool fills up as NADH, leaving steps 3, 4 and 8 nothing to load. Step 6's FAD empties only into ubiquinone, and with the chain backed up that pool is already full of electrons. The other steps back up behind them; with little OAA coming out of step 8, step 1 slows to a crawl too.

</details>

The cell reads two gauges: how full its carriers are (NADH/NAD⁺) and how full its ATP supply is (ATP/ADP). The best-known control points are the two irreversible oxidative decarboxylations, **isocitrate dehydrogenase** and **α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase** (held back by its products, NADH and succinyl-CoA), with citrate synthase (limited by OAA supply, slowed by its products) as a third. The rule: **ATP and NADH = STOP** (the cell is rich; high NADH means full carriers). **ADP, NAD⁺ and Ca²⁺ = GO** (calcium works differently, below). Rising ADP speeds the respiratory chain, so NAD⁺ comes back faster, and ADP also activates isocitrate dehydrogenase directly. Pyruvate dehydrogenase gates entry upstream by the same logic.

**Calcium is a separate signal, a push rather than a gauge.** When a muscle contracts, some of the Ca²⁺ released to drive contraction is taken up into mitochondria. There it activates isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, and pyruvate dehydrogenase through PDH phosphatase, its best-established targets. Its main effect is not on emptying carriers: it raises NADH production the moment work starts.

## Where the catalyst rule stops: amphibolic and anaplerotic

In a real cell, intermediates are drawn off. The cycle is **amphibolic**: it serves both breakdown and building. Citrate is exported for fatty-acid synthesis, α-ketoglutarate becomes glutamate and other amino acids, succinyl-CoA builds heme, and OAA feeds gluconeogenesis. Each withdrawal takes carbon that would have become OAA, so the pool would shrink. The cell refills it by **anaplerosis**: in many tissues chiefly pyruvate carboxylase (glutamine is a major source in dividing cells), which adds CO₂ to pyruvate to make OAA and is switched on by acetyl-CoA, a feedforward signal ("fuel is arriving; build more of the platform").

When the cycle's enzymes break, its intermediates can drive cancer. Mutations in succinate dehydrogenase and fumarase let succinate and fumarate pile up as **oncometabolites** in paragangliomas and kidney cancer. Mutant IDH1 or IDH2, the NADP⁺-dependent cousins of the cycle's NAD⁺-dependent IDH3, gain a new activity: they make 2-hydroxyglutarate, scrambling the epigenome in gliomas and leukemias, while IDH3 still runs step 3. IDH inhibitors are now real drugs.

## Going deeper

The main line above does not depend on these details.

### Citrate synthase's pull

Citrate synthase works by ordered induced fit: binding OAA makes the enzyme clamp shut, creating the acetyl-CoA site. The citryl-CoA intermediate is hydrolyzed while still bound, so the thioester energy is not wasted. The step is one of the cycle's most exergonic (ΔG°′ ≈ −31.4 kJ/mol) and effectively irreversible: the committed step that pulls the whole cycle forward.

### Pyruvate dehydrogenase's twin

The α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex shares pyruvate dehydrogenase's E1/E2/E3 architecture, its five cofactors (TPP, lipoic acid, CoA, FAD, NAD⁺) and its substrate channeling, which tunnels intermediates from one active site to the next without ever letting them diffuse free.

### Synthase versus synthetase

A synthetase, unlike a synthase, uses or makes a nucleotide triphosphate. Succinyl-CoA synthetase (step 5) runs in the direction that makes GTP, through a phosphohistidine intermediate: a histidine in its active site is phosphorylated on the way.

### The Warburg effect

In the Warburg effect, tumors run glycolysis hard even with oxygen available. It is a related rewiring at the cycle's entrance: more pyruvate is turned into lactate rather than entering the cycle as acetyl-CoA, although many tumors keep a working cycle, often fed by glutamine.

### Krebs and the urea cycle

Krebs had already found the urea cycle in 1932, five years before this one. That work taught him what to look for in the pigeon-muscle experiments: find the molecule that comes out unchanged, and you have found the catalyst at the heart of a cycle.
