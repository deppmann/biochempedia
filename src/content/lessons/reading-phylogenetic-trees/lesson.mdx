      Felsenstein, a population geneticist at the University of Washington, worked on a problem that every tree raises: a tree estimated from data is a
      single best guess, with no statement of how firmly the data support each branch. In 1985,
      in the journal Evolution, he applied the bootstrap, a statistical resampling method
      introduced by Bradley Efron, to phylogenies. You build many new data sets of the same size

techniques:
  - name: "Multiple sequence alignment"
    blurb: >-
      Sequences of the same gene from each species are lined up so that each column holds
      positions thought to descend from one ancestral position. The alignment is itself an
      inference of homology, and errors in it pass into every tree built from it.
  - name: "Outgroup comparison and parsimony mapping"
    blurb: >-
      A taxon known to lie outside the group of interest is used to root the tree and to judge
      which state of a character is ancestral. Character changes are then placed on branches so
      that the total number of changes is as small as possible.
  - name: "Maximum-likelihood and Bayesian tree inference"
    blurb: >-
      A model of how sequences change is used to calculate the probability of the alignment on
      each candidate tree. Likelihood methods search for the tree that makes the data most
      probable; Bayesian methods estimate a distribution of trees given the data, the model and a
      prior.
  - name: "Nonparametric bootstrap"
    blurb: >-
      Alignment columns are resampled with replacement to make many replicate data sets, a tree is
      inferred from each, and each clade is labelled with the percentage of replicate trees that
      contain it. The number measures how consistently the data recover a clade, not the
      probability that the clade is true.

      species sit only at the tips, never between them. A node is not a particular fossil: a fossil

      - "Trout is now more closely related to frog"

      Swapping the two branches that leave a node is a rotation, and it keeps every clade with the

  - stem: "Hair is present in mice and humans and absent in lizards, frogs and the outgroup. On a tree in which mice and humans are sister taxa, what does parsimony infer about hair?"
    choices:
      - "One gain on the branch leading to their clade"
      - "A gain at the root followed by three losses"
      - "Hair as the ancestral state for all tetrapods"
      - "Two separate gains of hair, one in each lineage"
    answer: 0
    rationale: >-
      Taking the outgroup's state as ancestral, absence is the starting state, and a single gain on
      the branch to the mouse-human clade explains the data with one change, the fewest possible.
      Two separate gains would need two changes, so parsimony prefers the single gain. A gain at the
      root followed by losses in lizards, frogs and the outgroup would need four changes. Treating hair as
      ancestral for all tetrapods needs one gain on the tetrapod stem and losses in frogs and lizards,
      three changes, so it is less parsimonious than the single gain.
  - stem: "A clade on a tree inferred from one gene has a bootstrap support value of 95. Which statement does this value justify?"
    choices:

      - "It appeared in 95% of trees from resampled data"
      - "The clade will appear in 95% of other gene trees"
      - "The clade has a 95% probability of being true"
    answer: 1
    rationale: >-
      Bootstrap support is the percentage of trees, inferred from data sets made by resampling the

**Is a trout more closely related to a shark, or to you? The two fish look alike.**

The first film answers it from a tree. Below is why the look misleads and how far to trust a tree.

> **The one idea.** Relatedness lives at the nodes; change lives on the branches. Trout is closer to you than to a shark because the ancestor it shares with you (node B) descends from, and so is more recent than, the one it shares with a shark (node J). Anything a node rotation can change, such as tip order, says nothing about relationships, and neither does the number of nodes between two tips. Characters change state along branches. Two taxa share a state by homology when both inherit it from one gain on a shared branch, even if other lineages later lose it; separate gains are convergence. Only a shared derived state, one gained inside the group being tested, shows which taxa are closest relatives.
>
> *Scope:* rooted trees, read as hypotheses. A cladogram ranks two nodes in time only when one descends from the other; it cannot date nodes on separate branches. How firmly the data support each node is a separate question (the last section), and a tree built from one gene shows that gene's history, which can differ from the history of the species.

## The parts of a tree

The first film named the parts: **taxa** (species or larger groups) sit at the **tips**; each **internal node** is an inferred common ancestor of every lineage descending from it (to its right in the drawing below); the **root** is the ancestor of everything on the tree; and the **most recent common ancestor** (MRCA) of a set of tips is the latest node from which all of them descend; and a **clade** is an ancestor and all of its descendants. One term it did not give: two clades that are each other's closest relatives are **sister groups**. It is like a family tree drawn for cousins only: everyone shown is alive, and the grandparents are inferred.

Because a tree is a hypothesis, estimated from characters (see [sequencing and genomes](/lessons/sequencing-and-genomes/)), it can be tested and overturned like any other historical explanation, as [testing evolutionary explanations](/lessons/testing-evolutionary-explanations/) describes.

The first film's **"more evolved"** misreading has one exception: a fossil tip, whose lineage is sampled earlier, has not had the same time since the root. The lamprey feature it pointed to is the rasping, tooth-lined oral disc of the parasitic species.

On a **cladogram**, only the branching order means anything. On a molecular **phylogram**, branch lengths are scaled to estimated sequence change, usually substitutions per site, not years. A tree scaled in time is a **chronogram**, and converting change into time needs a molecular clock and a calibration, the subject of [molecular evolution](/lessons/molecular-evolution/). Visible features change along branches too, as the second film shows.

## Characters on branches: shared derived and shared ancestral

The second film left open the lesson's first question: if trout is closer to you, why does it look like a shark?

Call the outgroup's state **ancestral** and a state that arose on a branch inside the tree **derived**. That is an inference, not a guarantee: the outgroup's own lineage can change too, so careful studies compare several outgroups. A **shared derived** state traces to one gain inside the group, even if some descendants later lose it, as frog loses X in the second film's one-origin mapping. A **shared ancestral** state was already present in an older ancestor; it is homologous too, but it cannot show which descendants are closer to each other.

Which label a state gets depends on the group you are testing. Jaws and paired fins, gained on the branch into J, are shared derived for the jawed vertebrates as a whole. But trout and shark share gills and paired fins because their ancestor at node J already had them; frog and human lost or changed them. So for "is trout closer to shark or to you?" those states are shared ancestral. Trout shares a derived state with you instead: one air-filled organ, a lung in you and a swim bladder in trout, which a shark lacks. Only a gain inside the group being tested groups species.

Real convergence is inferred this way. On a molecular tree of Greater Antillean Anolis lizards from Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica and Puerto Rico, species of the same habitat type, or **ecomorph** (such as twig or trunk-ground specialists), on different islands were usually not each other's closest relatives, with two possible exceptions. Losos and colleagues (1998) inferred that ecomorphs arose independently on different islands. Not every type occurs on every island; Jamaica has no grass-bush species.

One caution: each gene is inherited along its own path, so a single-gene tree can disagree with the species tree without any convergence. One reason is **incomplete lineage sorting**: when lineages split quickly, an ancestral gene variant can sort into descendants in an order that differs from the species' splits.

## How trees are built and how confident to be

A molecular tree usually starts with a **multiple sequence alignment**: sequences of one or more genes lined up so that each column holds one position across all the species. The alignment is itself a hypothesis that each column's positions share an origin, and its variants arise by [mutation](/lessons/mutation/). Four common families of methods then build or choose among trees:

- **Distance methods**, such as neighbor-joining, turn the alignment into pairwise differences between taxa and build a tree from them.
- **Maximum parsimony** finds the tree needing the fewest character changes.
- **Maximum likelihood** uses an explicit model of sequence change and finds the tree under which the observed alignment is most probable.
- **Bayesian inference** combines the data with prior assumptions and reports how probable each tree is under the model (its posterior probability).

Then comes support. Joseph Felsenstein, in 1985, applied the **bootstrap** to trees; resampling with replacement means a column can be drawn twice and another left out.

**Predict.** Suppose the vertebrate tree carries bootstrap values of 98 on (frog, human) and 72 on (trout, (frog, human)). Is the frog-human clade 98% likely to be true?

<details>
<summary>Check your prediction</summary>

No. Those clades appeared in 98% and 72% of the replicate trees. A bootstrap value tells you how consistently the characters in hand recover a clade under the method used. If the model is wrong in a systematic way, the same wrong clade can come back from every replicate.

</details>

A simulation with a known true tree showed a poorly fitting model doing exactly that (see Going deeper).

A node with more than two descendant branches, a **polytomy**, usually means the data were not strong enough to resolve the order of splits. It is rarely evidence that three lineages split at once.

### Design problem

Similar twig-dwelling lizards, with short limbs and slender bodies, occur on four Caribbean islands. Design a test that distinguishes one origin of the twig form followed by dispersal between islands from repeated independent origins on each island.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

Sample every lizard species you can on all four islands, plus mainland outgroups, and sequence several genes, not genes or traits used to define the twig ecomorph, or the test becomes circular. Infer the tree by maximum likelihood or Bayesian methods, with bootstrap or posterior support.

One origin plus dispersal, with no later shifts from the twig form to other forms, predicts that the twig species form a single clade. Repeated origins predict that each island's twig species is nested among species from its own island, with different islands' twig species in different clades. Map the twig form with the outgroup's state as ancestral and count the changes each hypothesis needs.

Decide in advance what support you require: a twig clade at bootstrap 50 settles nothing, and strong support that is consistent across genes and methods comes closer. Even then, convergence on a tree is an inference with uncertainty. This is the logic of Losos and colleagues (1998) for Greater Antillean Anolis.

</details>

## Going deeper

### When strong support is wrong

Kapli and Telford (2020) simulated protein data on a known animal tree with sponges branching first, letting the amino-acid mix differ among sites. A model assuming one mix at every site returned the wrong tree in 98 of 100 data sets, usually with high support. The error ran one way: data simulated with comb jellies branching first were recovered correctly every time, even by that simpler model, so the authors argued that strong support for comb jellies first in real data may be this kind of artefact.

### The root of the animal tree

Schultz and colleagues (2023) mapped the linkage of genes on chromosomes, using unicellular relatives of animals as outgroups, and reported shared derived linkages grouping sponges with cnidarians, placozoans and bilaterians, which places comb jellies as the sister group of all other animals. Copley (2025) argued that their statistical test overstated the significance of some of those linkages. Read this as a live dispute.

### Methods in more detail

Formally, Bayesian inference estimates a posterior distribution of trees and model parameters given the data, the model and a prior. An outgroup is the usual way to root a tree, not the only one: midpoint and clock rooting also exist. Gene-family histories, in which a gene's tree differs from the species tree, are covered in [how signaling evolved](/lessons/how-signaling-evolved/).
