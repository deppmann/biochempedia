## Explain: daily cycles and growing toward light and water

<WordBank terms={['circadian rhythm', 'tropism', 'migration', 'hibernation', 'dormancy', 'deciduous', 'instinct']} />

Think about your own day. You get sleepy at night and hungry around mealtimes. Earth spins once about every 24 hours, so most places have day and night.

A **circadian rhythm** is a pattern in a living thing that repeats about every 24 hours. Light and darkness have the biggest influence in keeping these rhythms in step with the day. In many animals, the rhythm keeps going for a while even in steady darkness. That shows part of the timing comes from inside the body.

Different animals are active at different times. Many owls and many bats hunt at night and rest in the day. Tree squirrels and most songbirds do the opposite.

Plants keep time too. Many have their own daily clock: bean leaves, for example, lift during the day and droop at night. Plants also respond to their surroundings by growing. A **tropism** is growth of a plant toward or away from something in its surroundings.

- **Toward light.** Cells on the shaded side of a stem grow longer than cells on the lit side. The longer side pushes the stem into a bend toward the light.
- **Toward water.** In lab tests, young roots bent toward the wetter side of their dish.

The bend is made of new growth, so it builds up slowly, from about half an hour to days.

## Explain: getting through the seasons

In a Virginia winter, days are short, nights are cold, and many foods are hard to find.

- **Migration** is the seasonal movement of animals from one region to another and back. Monarchs fly south in fall. In spring, monarchs move north again over several generations.
- **Dormancy** is a resting state in which a living thing slows or stops growing but stays alive until conditions improve. Seeds and tree buds wait out winter this way.
- **Hibernation** is a kind of dormancy in animals. The body temperature drops and the heart and breathing slow, so the animal uses less of its stored fat. Groundhogs hibernate. It is not one long sleep: groundhogs and their relatives the ground squirrels warm up for short times, then cool down again.

Virginia’s **deciduous** trees, such as oaks and maples, drop their leaves each fall. Where each leaf stalk joins the twig, there is a thin band of cells that formed when the leaf grew. As days get shorter and the air cools, the cells in this band start to come apart. This weakens the joint, and the leaf falls. A protective layer then seals the scar. Without leaves, the tree loses less water in winter, when frozen soil makes water hard to get.

| Season | Common cues (they differ by species) | Some responses |
|---|---|---|
| Fall | Shorter days, cooler air | Leaves drop, buds go dormant, monarchs fly south, groundhogs hibernate |
| Winter | A long time of cold | Dormant buds become ready to open |
| Spring | Warmer days | Buds open, seeds sprout, groundhogs come out |

Not every seasonal response waits for a cue from outside. Groundhogs also have an inner yearly clock. In rooms where the day length never changed, their cycles of eating and weight gain kept repeating about once a year for up to 4 years.

Buds of many trees need cold before warmth. In a 2018 experiment, young aspen trees got 10 weeks of short, 8-hour days, and their buds went dormant. Then the trees spent 5 weeks at 4 °C. Back in warm, long days, the buds swelled and green leaves came out.

<LessonFigure src="/figures/daily-and-seasonal-rhythms/aspen-buds-need-cold.webp" width={1200} height={676} alt="AI-generated illustration: three steps with an aspen twig tip. First, short days and a closed, dormant bud. Second, cold, and the bud stays closed. Third, warm, long days, and the bud swells and opens with small green leaves." caption="In the aspen experiment, buds went dormant in short days, spent weeks in the cold, and only then opened when warm, long days returned." />

Are these behaviors learned? For monarchs and ground squirrels, mostly not. An **instinct** is a behavior an animal inherits and can do without being taught. A monarch flying south has never made the trip before. In a lab study of ground squirrels, most were young animals hibernating for the first time, and genes explained much of when they started. Some animals do learn: young whooping cranes flew straighter routes when older cranes were in the group.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Cues such as day length and temperature help time migration, hibernation, leaf drop and dormancy, and some animals also have an inner yearly clock. Many seasonal behaviors of animals, such as a monarch’s first trip south, are inherited instincts.</Support>

## Explain: when the long-term pattern shifts

Some changes last for years. They can be natural, such as wildfires, floods and climate shifts. They can be caused by people, such as pollution, habitat destruction and overharvesting. Long-term changes can change how many organisms live in a place, where each species lives, and how a whole ecosystem fits together. As the climate has warmed, many land species now live farther toward the poles or higher up mountains than before. You will study these changes in [Populations Rise and Fall](/lessons/populations-rise-and-fall/) and Big Changes (a later lesson).

Timing can shift too. If winters and early springs warm, a tree whose buds respond to warmth may bloom earlier. But buds that need cold first may open late or unevenly if winters become too warm to give them enough cold.

<Support min={2}>**Key idea:** Long-term changes can shift how many organisms live in a place, where they live, and when seasonal events happen.</Support>

### Design problem

Does the direction of light change which way a bean seedling grows? Plan an investigation.

<details>
<summary>One way to do it</summary>

- **Question:** Do bean seedlings bend toward light that comes from one side?
- **Independent variable:** the direction of light. Some boxes have a hole on one side. Others have the hole on top.
- **Dependent variable:** the angle of each stem from straight up, in degrees, measured with a protractor after 5 days.
- **Constants:** same kind of bean, soil, pot, daily water, room and amount of light.
- **Control:** seedlings in boxes lit from the top. Their stems should grow straight up.
- **Repeated trials:** five seedlings for each kind of box. Find the mean angle.
- **Safety:** use a window or a cool LED lamp, not a heat lamp. Wash your hands after handling soil, and do not eat the seeds.

If stems lit from the side bend toward the hole and the control stems grow straight, the data support the idea that shoots grow toward light.

</details>

## Evaluate: check your thinking

Monarchs and cherry trees respond to cues such as day length and temperature, and many of their responses are inherited. Because a cherry bud opens after it warms, a long-term change in climate can move the bloom day, and the Tidal Basin record shows the mean moving earlier since the 1960s, though not in a steady line.
